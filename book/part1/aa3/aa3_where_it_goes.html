Where the Money Goes

Most of the aid never leaves the United States as cash. Israel buys American weapons through Foreign Military Sales cases, the Pentagon signs the contract with the American maker, and the purchase is paid for from Israel's military financing. [cite:aa_mil_f15] The One-Way Mirror on this shelf sets out the steps of that pipeline.

One example shows the mechanism. On 29 December 2025 the Pentagon awarded Boeing a contract with a ceiling of about $8.58 billion for 25 new F-15IA fighters for Israel, with an option for 25 more, to be built in St Louis, Missouri, with work running to the end of 2035; about $840 million was obligated at award. [cite:aa_mil_f15] Israel has also bought 50 F-35s. [cite:aa_crs_2023]

Missile defence, built together

The second stream is missile defence. By 2023 the United States had contributed more than $4.5 billion to the Arrow systems, more than $2.4 billion to David's Sling and nearly $3 billion to Iron Dome. [cite:aa_crs_2023] The 2016 agreement sets a goal of producing missile-defence components in the United States at a level of at least half of American-funded production, and in October 2026 the first Tamir interceptors for Iron Dome built outside Israel were delivered from a plant in Camden, Arkansas. [cite:us19_crs_md] [cite:ow8_rtx_camden]

Israeli industry

The third stream, off-shore procurement, went to Israeli firms: about $2 billion of the military aid between October 2023 and September 2025 was spent this way. [cite:aa_hartung_2025]

Supporters of the aid point to these factories as evidence that it is an American jobs and technology programme as much as a grant. Critics answer that the same money would create work if spent on domestic needs, and that a subsidy to arms makers is still a subsidy. The figures above are not in dispute; what they mean is.
