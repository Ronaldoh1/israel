Truman Before the Money

In April 1943, a Missouri senator named Harry Truman stood before a mass rally in Chicago Stadium and spoke of Europe's Jews. Truman is often remembered as a lifelong, uncomplicated champion of a Jewish state. His documented words and acts between 1943 and May 1948, before the campaign money of the 1948 election, reveal a more complicated man. They show public conviction, open awareness of voters and private irritation at the pressure he was under, all in the same person and the same years.

The rally is the earliest of these records. It urged help for Europe's Jews. According to David McCullough's biography, Truman told the crowd that under "mad Hitler" Jews were being "herded like animals" into camps. [cite:csm_truman_diary_2003] It was a public statement of humanitarian concern, made two years before he reached the White House.

The second record is more contested in its details. On 10 November 1945, now president, Truman met his chiefs of mission, the senior American diplomats, from the Arab world. The State Department's official record documents that the meeting took place. [cite:frus_1945_chiefs_mission] The words most often attributed to him there come from a single witness. William Eddy, the American minister to Saudi Arabia, attended, and later wrote that Truman said: "I'm sorry, gentlemen, but I have to answer to hundreds of thousands who are anxious for the success of Zionism; I do not have hundreds of thousands of Arabs among my constituents." The quotation rests on Eddy's 1954 pamphlet, quoted by the historian John Judis, and Eddy was himself a State Department opponent of Zionism. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_globe] The meeting is documented. The exact words rest on one interested account and should be read that way.

Less than a year later, on 4 October 1946, came the Yom Kippur statement, issued a month before the midterm elections ([scene:wh_s01|the Yom Kippur statement]). The following summer produced the most revealing document of all. On 21 July 1947, after Henry Morgenthau called him about the refugee ship Exodus, Truman wrote in his private diary: "The Jews, I find are very, very selfish." He added that once underdogs gain power, "neither Hitler nor Stalin has anything on them for cruelty." The diary was found at the Truman Library and released by the National Archives in 2003. [cite:jvl_truman_diary_1947] [cite:aljazeera_truman_diary] [cite:cbs_truman_diary] It shows a president privately resentful of the pressure he felt.

The electoral arithmetic was on paper too. In November 1947, a memo to Truman titled "The Politics of 1948 and the Jews" analyzed the Jewish vote in the coming election. [cite:jvl_truman_index] In March 1948, having refused to see Zionist leaders, Truman met Chaim Weizmann at the urging of his old business partner, Eddie Jacobson. On 14 May 1948 he granted de facto recognition to Israel eleven minutes after its proclamation, against the opposition of Secretary of State Marshall. [cite:jvl_dejure_1949] [cite:jvl_truman_index] Four days later, on 18 May, he described American Jews as "very emotional."

No single one of these records explains Truman's decisions, and they should not be forced into a single motive. The 1943 speech shows genuine humanitarian conviction. The memo, and Eddy's account with its limits, show awareness of domestic politics. The diary shows irritation. All three are documented, and all three belonged to the same president in the years before he recognized the new state.
