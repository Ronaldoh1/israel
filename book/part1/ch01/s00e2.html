The Exodus of 1948–1972 and Its Aftermath

Between 1948 and 1972, roughly 850,000 Jews left Arab and Muslim countries. The familiar account tells this as a single, simple story of rescue: Jews were persecuted throughout Arab lands until Israel gave them refuge. The familiar version stops there. The documents do not. The departures had several causes, operating at the same time and documented separately: violence and legal persecution by Arab states, organized and named Israeli recruitment operations, and, in specific and contested cases, sabotage. What happened to many of the arrivals once they reached Israel is a second, distinct story, and in places a grave one.

The turning point in Iraqi Jewish memory came seven years before Israel existed. On 1–2 June 1941, after the collapse of a pro-Nazi coup in Iraq, rioters in Baghdad killed at least 128 Jews. That is the official count of an Iraqi government commission of inquiry; some estimates run as high as 180. More than 1,000 people were injured, and roughly 900 Jewish homes and more than 1,500 businesses were looted or destroyed.[cite:ushmm_farhud] This event, the Farhud, cannot be explained as a reaction to the 1948 war or the Nakba, the Palestinian displacement of that year. The dates do not permit it.

Law and organization set the Iraqi exodus itself in motion. On 2 March 1950, Iraq's parliament passed a law permitting Jews to leave on condition that they surrender their Iraqi citizenship. A later law required departing Jews to sell their property and liquidate their businesses before going, so that economic loss was built into the exit itself. Israel and the Jewish Agency organized a mass airlift, Operation Ezra and Nehemiah. Shlomo Hillel, an Iraqi-born agent of Mossad LeAliyah Bet, negotiated personally with Iraqi authorities and later described the operation in a memoir, Operation Babylon. Flights ran through Cyprus and Iran via the Near East Transport Company and El Al. Israeli authorities had planned for about 300 arrivals a day; at the peak, 1,400 came. By the end of 1951 more than 107,000 Iraqi Jews had been airlifted, and about 16,000 more had left by other routes. By early 1952, of Iraq's roughly 130,000 Jews, only about 6,000 remained.[cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:wiki_shlomo_hillel]

Iraq was not the only case. Operation Magic Carpet airlifted approximately 48,000 to 49,000 Yemenite Jews to Israel in 1949–50. Operation Yachin, conducted in secret from 1961 to 1964, brought Moroccan Jews. These were organized operations of the Israeli state and the Jewish Agency, not spontaneous flight, though the hardships in the countries of origin were real.[cite:wiki_idf_operations_list] Each country had its own circumstances and its own timeline.

Egypt supplies the documented case of sabotage. In 1954 Israeli military intelligence recruited a cell of Egyptian Jews to plant bombs at Western targets in Egypt, including American and British facilities. The aim was to have the attacks blamed on Egyptian nationalists or the Muslim Brotherhood, and to damage Western confidence in Nasser's government. The operation was discovered, its members were arrested and tried, and Defense Minister Pinhas Lavon was forced to resign.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] That the operation took place is not in dispute. Who authorized it, and at what level, has been argued over for decades. It is a specific case, not a pattern the record documents for every country in this account.

Arrival did not end the story. New arrivals, disproportionately Mizrahi, were housed in crowded transit camps called ma'abarot, first in tents and later in basic shacks. Ashkenazi immigrants of the same period tended to arrive better educated and were more likely to assimilate into the existing, largely Ashkenazi secular establishment. They also qualified for German war-reparations payments, which Mizrahi immigrants, never having lived under Nazi occupation, could not receive.[cite:honestreporting_forgotten_refugees] The gap in starting conditions was structural. Individual lives varied, but the pattern is documented.

Two episodes stand out. Between 1948 and 1954, hundreds of infants and small children of Yemenite and other Mizrahi immigrant families disappeared from Israeli state hospitals and transit camps. Families were often told the children had died, frequently without a body, a death certificate or a burial place. That much is not disputed. What lay behind it is. The Cohen-Kedmi Commission investigated from 1995 to 2001. It concluded that claims of a deliberate, government-organized kidnapping plot were not substantiated, while acknowledging that 42 children remained unaccounted for.[cite:wiki_kedmi_commission] The researcher Shoshana Madmoni-Gerber, drawing on hundreds of family testimonies, has argued that the disappearances reflect a coordinated pattern. The activist Uzi Meshulam, who campaigned for a fuller inquiry, was imprisoned in connection with his campaign.[cite:wiki_uzi_meshulam] [cite:nytimes_disappeared_children] Both positions rest on evidence, and the disagreement remains unresolved.

The second episode is the Ringworm Affair. Through the 1950s, Israeli medical authorities treated scalp ringworm in immigrant children, disproportionately Mizrahi and North African, with X-ray irradiation. One frequently cited figure puts the campaign at roughly 27,000 children; broader estimates run considerably higher. Part of the motive was practical: ringworm was grounds for exclusion under United States and other immigration rules, and irradiation was then a treatment used elsewhere as well. A 2003 Israeli documentary, The Ringworm Children, presented the episode as a grave state-linked scandal.[cite:wiki_ringworm_affair]

No single cause cancels the others. Persecution, recruitment and sabotage all operated, and the departure, though large, did not end Jewish life in the region at once or everywhere. Baghdad's community, once very large, is functionally gone today. The full story is neither "they all fled persecution" nor "they were all lured away." It is both, in proportions that varied from place to place, followed by an arrival that was a rescue without being an equal welcome.
