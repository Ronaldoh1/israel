What Is Passed Down: Inheritance, the Estate Tax and "Buy, Borrow, Die"

The Dream promises that each generation can do better than the last by its own effort. It says less about what each generation is handed. Inheritance is the transfer of wealth at death; gifts are transfers made while the giver is alive; together economists call them intergenerational transfers. This section sets out who receives them, how much they explain of the gaps described in the previous section, and the tax rules that decide how much of a large fortune passes on untaxed: the estate tax, the "step-up in basis" and the borrowing strategy that combines them. [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|The wealth gap]

Who inherits

The Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond used the 2019 Survey of Consumer Finances, which asks families about every inheritance or large gift they have received, to compare Americans born between 1949 and 1972. Among white respondents, 34.8 per cent had received a transfer; among Black respondents, 13.2 per cent. When a transfer was received, the average white amount was $303,100 and the average Black amount $154,260, in 2019 dollars; the medians were closer, $100,830 and $74,460, because the white average is pulled up by a small number of very large transfers. Almost 5 per cent of white recipients reported $1 million or more; virtually no Black recipients did. Nearly one-third of white recipients had received more than one transfer, against 7 per cent of Black recipients. [cite:ad12_richmond_inherit] Most transfers arrive late: at least half of recipients get their first after age 40, which means inheritance more often pays off a mortgage or funds a retirement than buys a first home. [cite:ad12_richmond_inherit]

The Richmond authors also found that receiving a transfer rose steadily with parental education for white families, from 29.8 per cent where parents had not finished high school to 41.2 per cent where a parent held a bachelor's degree, while for Black families it varied only between 8.3 and 15.5 per cent with no steady rise. They call the reason unclear and suggest that parental education is a weak stand-in for accumulated family wealth among Black families. [cite:ad12_richmond_inherit] The history of how that wealth was built and lost, from the exclusions written into federal mortgage insurance to the loss of land and homes, is set out in Part Six and Part Thirteen. [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six: from redlining to the suburbs] [scene:ad13_black_americans|Part Thirteen: Black Americans]

How much inheritance explains, and the dispute

How much of the wealth gap these transfers account for is disputed among economists, and the range is wide. Earlier studies summarised by the Richmond Fed put inheritances at 10 to 20 per cent of the white–Black gap (a 1997 paper) and intergenerational transfers at 26 per cent (a 2020 simulation). [cite:ad12_richmond_inherit] A 2023 study by Jeffrey Thompson of the Boston Fed and John Sabelhaus of the Brookings Institution found that transfers explain 14 per cent of the gap without other controls, and that lifetime earnings, pension assets and education together explain about 80 per cent. About a third of white families and about one in ten Black families had received any inheritance, and more than half of all inheritances and gifts were under $50,000. [cite:ad12_bostonfed_inherit]

This is the part of the record where the taught version largely holds for the typical family: most family wealth in the middle is built from earnings and the pensions and homes those earnings buy, not from bequests. The two findings are compatible. Transfers matter a great deal at the top of the distribution, where the multi-million-dollar bequests are, and they matter in the timing and security they give, but for the median family the larger drivers are what a working life pays and whether it comes with a pension. [cite:ad12_bostonfed_inherit] [cite:ad12_richmond_inherit] Those drivers are the subject of Parts Five and Ten. The estate tax: from 2 per cent of deaths to fewer than 1 in 1,000

The estate tax is a federal tax on the value of what a person leaves at death above an exempt amount, paid by the estate before heirs receive it. Gifts during life count against the same exemption, and anything left to a spouse or to charity is deducted. In 2001 the exemption was $675,000 and the top rate 55 per cent; by 2009 the exemption had risen to $3.5 million and the rate fallen to 45 per cent; in 2010 the tax lapsed for a year. Congress then set a $5 million exemption and a 35 per cent rate, made the tax permanent in the American Taxpayer Relief Act of 2012 at a 40 per cent rate with the exemption indexed to inflation, doubled the exemption for 2018–2025 in the 2017 tax law, and in the 2025 reconciliation law (Public Law 119-21) set it at $15 million per person from 2026, indexed and without an end date. [cite:ad12_crs_estate] As of October 2026 that $15 million figure is the law in force.

The effect on who pays has been large. In 2001 about 2.1 per cent of people who died left a taxable estate; in 2016 it was 5,467 estates, 0.21 per cent of deaths; after the 2017 doubling it was 2,129 estates in 2019, 0.07 per cent, about one death in 1,400. The tax raised $32 billion in fiscal 2023, 0.7 per cent of federal revenue. Of what it collects, 90 per cent is paid by estates of people in the top tenth of the income distribution and 65 per cent by the top 1 per cent. [cite:ad12_crs_estate] It is one of the most progressive taxes in the federal system, and it now reaches almost no one.

The case against the tax is made on the record too. Opponents describe it as a second tax on money already taxed as income, argue that it harms family farms and businesses, and say it discourages saving. The Congressional Research Service notes that the double-taxation point does not apply to gains never taxed during life, that only about 300 estates made up mostly of farm or business assets were expected to pay, and that the evidence mostly finds small effects on the saving of those who leave estates. [cite:ad12_crs_estate]

The step-up in basis: gains that are never taxed

The larger rule for most inherited wealth is not the estate tax but the treatment of capital gains. A capital gain is the rise in an asset's value; it is taxed as income only when the asset is sold, and the tax is on the difference between the sale price and the "basis," usually what the owner paid. When an owner dies, the heir's basis is "stepped up" to the asset's value at the date of death. The Congressional Research Service gives the arithmetic: shares bought for $100,000 and worth $200,000 at death, later sold by the heir for $250,000, produce a taxable gain of $50,000; the $100,000 gain that built up during the original owner's life is never taxed by anyone. Under "carryover basis," the rule that applies to gifts made during life, the heir would be taxed on $150,000. [cite:ad12_crs_estate] The step-up is a long-standing provision of the income tax code; it is designed friction, a deliberate rule, not a loophole that appeared by accident. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that replacing it with carryover basis would raise $7.2 billion in fiscal 2024, rising to $26.6 billion by 2032. [cite:ad12_crs_estate]

"Buy, borrow, die"

The phrase "buy, borrow, die," attributed to the University of Southern California law professor Edward McCaffery, describes how the step-up combines with borrowing. [cite:ad12_propublica] An owner buys assets that rise in value and does not sell them, so no gain is taxed. To pay for living costs, the owner borrows against the assets; a loan is not income, because it must be repaid, so the cash arrives untaxed. At death, the heirs inherit with a stepped-up basis, sell enough to repay the loans, and the gain accumulated over a lifetime escapes income tax entirely. The Budget Lab at Yale calls it "a tax planning technique which allows wealthy Americans to pay low tax rates on consumed income." [cite:ad12_yale_bbd]

How much this matters is contested, and the strongest evidence cuts in two directions. ProPublica, using IRS records obtained from an undisclosed source, reported in June 2021 that the 25 richest Americans' wealth rose by $401 billion between 2014 and 2018, by Forbes's estimates, while they paid $13.6 billion in federal income tax, a "true tax rate" of 3.4 per cent; the typical household paid about 14 per cent of its income. [cite:ad12_propublica] That rate is ProPublica's own measure, comparing taxes with wealth growth rather than with income as the law defines it. The National Taxpayers Union Foundation answered that the comparison "knowingly compares apples to oranges" because the US taxes income, not paper wealth, while conceding that borrowing against assets is "a tax avoidance strategy that some wealthy individuals use." [cite:ad12_ntu_bbd] The Yale Budget Lab, using the SCF and Forbes data, found that borrowing is a "relatively minor source of cash income" for wealthy Americans in aggregate, about 1 per cent of the income of the top 0.1 per cent by net worth in one study it cites, and estimated that reform options would raise $102 billion to $147 billion over ten years. [cite:ad12_yale_bbd] The documented finding is the rule, the step-up, which lets lifetime gains pass untaxed whether or not the owner borrows; the borrowing strategy is real but narrower than its fame suggests.
