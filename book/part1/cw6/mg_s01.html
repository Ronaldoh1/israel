The End of Military Government, 1963–1966

Six days after the Security Council censured the raid on Samu, Israel ended a regime of its own making inside its borders. For most of Israel's first two decades the majority of its Arab citizens lived under a military administration that required permits to leave their localities, imposed curfews and designated closed areas; it was abolished in 1966. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] How that regime was built and run from 1949 is told in Volume II, in [scene:rd6_military_government|the military government], and its worst single event, at Kafr Qasim on 29 October 1956, in Stage Three. This section takes up only its ending: how it was eased from 1963, how it was dismantled in 1966, and what remained in place when the military governors left.

The common claim that Arab citizens of Israel have enjoyed equal civil rights since 1948 does not survive the dates. A separate administrative regime applied to one category of citizens until December 1966. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] But the ending itself is also less simple than a single date suggests, and the file kept by the Prime Minister's Adviser on Arab Affairs, now in the Israel State Archives and published by the Akevot Institute, shows why. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005]

The legal basis, 1963. The regime rested not on a statute of the Knesset but on the Defence (Emergency) Regulations of 1945. In March 1963 the Military Advocate General, Meir Shamgar, circulated a secret thirteen-page manual on those regulations, printed in 30 copies. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The manual is a reminder that, whatever changed in the years that followed, the legal instrument on which control rested was being codified for internal use at the very moment the easing began.

The easing, October–November 1963. The first large change came in the autumn of 1963. On 21 October 1963 Eshkol cancelled the general requirement that Arab citizens carry personal travel permits. A directive of 3 November 1963 by the Chief of Staff, Zvi Tzur, then created a general permit for most residents of the Triangle and Wadi 'Ara, excluding the Negev and six border villages; cut the military zones in the Galilee from 17 to nine; and kept eleven "abandoned villages" closed. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005]

The link between closure and land is explicit in the file. At a meeting chaired by the Adviser on Arab Affairs, Shmuel Toledano, on 18 November 1965, nine villages were reviewed for opening; those with unresolved "land issues" stayed closed. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The review was conducted under the civilian adviser, and the test the file records for keeping a village closed was an unresolved question of land.

The decision, January 1966. On 12 January 1966 the Prime Minister told the Knesset that the government planned to abolish the military government within a year. The army did not agree: the Chief of Staff had argued for keeping it in eased form. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The decision was therefore a civilian one, taken over military objection. Arab citizens' own political organizing in these years, and the state's response to it, are the subject of [scene:cw6_al_ard|al-Ard and the courts].

The directives of 27 February 1966. How the regime would be replaced was settled at a meeting on 27 February 1966 chaired by the Prime Minister's adviser Isser Harel. Its directives drew the essential line: the apparatus of military government would be dismantled, but the Defence Regulations would stay in force. Enforcement would pass to the Israel Police and, in some cases, to the Shin Bet. The Shin Bet was to be reinforced; new police stations were to be built; a police reserve unit was to be formed for "mixed communities"; and a demolition unit was to be created for "illegal construction." [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The powers were not abolished. They were transferred, and the bodies that received them were strengthened to hold them.

The announcement and the date. The public account of the change reached the press at midyear. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported on 30 June 1966 that the military governorships would end on 1 December 1966 for about 150,000 Arab citizens, mainly along the Jordanian border and around Nazareth, and that a special police headquarters for the Arab minority had already been set up. [cite:rs_cw6_jta_jun1966] Eshkol gave the Knesset formal notice of abolition on 8 November 1966. Because the regime rested on military regulations rather than on a law, no legislation was needed to end it, according to the Economic Cooperation Foundation's account. [cite:rs_cw6_ecf_abolition] On 1 December 1966 the governorships ended.

What remained. After abolition the police, under the Chief of Staff's supervision, were tasked with enforcing the Emergency Regulations, according to the Institute for Palestine Studies' encyclopedia. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_mg_e] The regulations that stayed in force included the power to declare closed areas (Regulation 125), restriction orders (Regulation 109), police supervision (Regulation 110), administrative detention (Regulation 111, replaced in 1979) and deportation (Regulation 112). [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_mg_e] [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] After December 1966 these powers could be exercised by the police under the same legal text.

The men who planned the change described it in their own words. Toledano called it mainly "psychological." A 1963 document on the regime's aims, in the same file, put it more fully: "the main change is external-psychological that gives a feeling of equality." [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The phrase is the adviser's office describing its own policy, and it is central to one side of the dispute over what the abolition meant.

Did 1 December 1966 end military rule? Two readings of the record stand, and each rests on documents. On the first, the answer is yes, formally and in substance: the military governorships were abolished and their powers passed to civilian bodies, for some 150,000 citizens who had lived under them. [cite:rs_cw6_jta_jun1966] [cite:rs_cw6_ecf_abolition] The general requirement to carry personal travel permits had been cancelled in 1963. On this reading that change, at least, was more than a matter of perception. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005]

On the second reading, what ended was the military apparatus, not the regime of control. The state's own archive file shows the Emergency Regulations kept in force and their enforcement transferred to the police and the Shin Bet, which were reinforced for the purpose; Toledano himself called the change a mainly "psychological" one. [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] The Institute for Palestine Studies' encyclopedia places the full legal abolition of military rule later, in late 1968. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_mg_e] The two readings are not wholly in conflict. Both accept that the governorships ended on 1 December 1966; they differ on whether the governorships were the thing that mattered.

One interpretive reading carries the argument further. It holds that before Israel governed a single Palestinian in the West Bank or Gaza, it had already run an eighteen-year separate regime for Palestinian citizens inside its own borders, and that the occupation which began in 1967 inherited a model rather than inventing one. That is a reading of the sequence, not a documented finding; the sources consulted here document the regime and its transfer of powers, not any decision to carry the model across the line in 1967.

What the record does not settle. The documents consulted for this section do not show how often the retained regulations were used against Arab citizens after December 1966, or whether their use rose or fell once the police took them over. They do not say which villages among the eleven closed in 1963 and the nine reviewed in 1965 were opened, or when. The date of full legal abolition, late 1968 in the Institute for Palestine Studies' account, is not confirmed by an Israeli government source in this chapter. And the Chief of Staff's arguments for keeping the regime in eased form are recorded only as a position, not in his own words.
