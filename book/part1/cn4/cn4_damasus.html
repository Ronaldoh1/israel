A Bishopric Worth Killing For: Damasus and Jerome's Bible

The Bible that Western Christians read for more than a thousand years, the Latin Vulgate, was commissioned in 382 by a bishop of Rome. The circumstances in which that bishop took office are recorded by one of the most careful historians of antiquity, and they are not edifying. They also explain, better than any theological treatise, what the Roman see had become in the half-century since Constantine began to endow it. [cite:cn_brit_vulgate]

When the see fell vacant in 366, two men claimed it: Damasus and Ursinus. Ammianus Marcellinus, a pagan army officer who wrote his history in Rome a generation later, describes them as "burning with a superhuman desire of seizing the bishopric". Their supporters fought in the streets and in the churches, and in the basilica of Sicininus, he reports, "in a single day a hundred and thirty-seven corpses of the slain were found". [cite:cn_amm_27_3] The civil authority sided with Damasus, who became bishop on 1 October 366, and Ursinus and his followers were banished. [cite:cn_brit_damasus]

Ammianus then does something unusual for a pagan writer: he explains why the office was worth such a fight. Looking at the splendour of life in the city, he writes, he did not wonder that men ambitious for it should struggle with all their strength, since once they obtained it they would be "so free from care that they are enriched from the offerings of matrons, ride seated in carriages", dress splendidly and "serve banquets so lavish that their entertainments outdo the tables of kings". [cite:cn_amm_27_3] He adds that such men could be truly happy if they lived instead "after the manner of some provincial bishops", whose plain food and modest dress commended them to the deity "as pure and reverent men". [cite:cn_amm_27_3] The contrast is the point. By the 360s the bishop of Rome was a figure of wealth on the scale of the city's great families, and his income came in large part from the gifts of rich Roman women.

The emperors noticed. A law issued on 30 July 370 in the names of Valentinian I and Valens barred clergy, former clergy and those vowed to celibacy from visiting the houses of widows and female wards, and declared void any gift or legacy such women tried to make to them, the property passing instead to their nearest kin. [cite:cn_fc_laws364] The law does not name Damasus. It shows that within four years of his election the Roman state thought the flow of inheritances from wealthy women to churchmen serious enough to legislate against. The same state that had given the clergy their immunities now had to fence in their fortune-hunting.

This was the bishop whose faith the edict of 380 made the legal measure of orthodoxy, as Part Three showed. [cite:vt21_wiki_thessalonica] Two years later he called a synod in Rome, which, according to Britannica, officially pronounced Rome's primacy. [cite:cn_brit_damasus] A list of scriptural books, part of a document later known as the Gelasian Decree, is often attributed to this synod of 382; the scholar who edited it, Ernst von Dobschütz, judged it a pseudonymous compilation of the sixth century, several later scholars agree that it is not a decree of 382, since Jerome never mentions one, and a minority hold the list of books itself to be authentic. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt23_wiki_gelasian] [cite:vt14_wiki_rome382]

What is not disputed is the commission. In 382 Damasus asked Jerome, the leading biblical scholar of the day, who had attended the synod and become his secretary, to revise the Latin translations of the Bible. The revised gospels appeared around 383, and Jerome went on to translate the Old Testament from the Hebrew, finishing around 405. [cite:cn_brit_vulgate] [cite:cn_brit_damasus] Jerome's own scholarship pulled against parts of the received canon: working from the Hebrew, he doubted the books that the Greek-speaking churches read but the Hebrew Bible did not contain, the books later called deuterocanonical. [cite:vt23_ccel_jerome_solomon] [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero] His doubts were overruled by the councils of the following decades, and the books stayed in the Latin Bible. Eleven centuries later the Council of Trent declared Jerome's Vulgate the authentic Latin text of scripture, and Martin Luther, following Jerome's doubts, moved the same books into an appendix. [cite:cn_brit_vulgate] [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon]

The story of the Vulgate is usually told as a scholar's triumph, and Jerome's achievement was real. It is also the story of a translation commissioned by an office that had become one of the richest prizes in the empire, held by a man installed after a street battle and confirmed by the civil power. The Latin Bible was the institution's Bible from the day it was ordered.
