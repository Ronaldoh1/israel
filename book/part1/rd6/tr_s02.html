Truman After Recognition: A Retreat, a Pledge, and a Threat Not Kept, 1948–1950

American recognition of Israel in May 1948 did not settle Washington's policy toward the new state. It opened a two-year sequence in which Harry Truman's decisions moved sometimes toward Israel's position and sometimes against it. Two accounts of that sequence circulate, and they are mirror images. One holds that once the donors had paid, Truman did whatever Israel wanted. The other holds that money had nothing to do with it, and that his policy followed from conviction and from the interests of the United States. Each has a strong form. The first can point to a named donor, a named sum and a historian's account of a decision that changed. The second can point to a president who signed legislation he called discriminatory against Jewish refugees and later threatened Israel in writing. The way to weigh them is to put the decisions in the order they were made, with their dates.

The decision that came first. Recognition itself had been made against the advice of the government's own specialists. Declassified CIA and State Department documents from 1947 and 1948 record consistent warnings against recognizing Israel on strategic grounds. The CIA's assessments noted the Zionist arms-procurement networks operating across Europe and the United States, predicted that recognition would immediately produce armed conflict with the Arab states, and warned of damage to American access to oil. One CIA report held that an arms embargo would lead to peaceful coexistence, the opposite of what followed. The State Department under George Marshall opposed recognition on strategic grounds as well. Truman overruled both. He recognized Israel at 6:11 pm on 14 May 1948, eleven minutes after its declaration of independence, a recognition so fast that his own State Department was caught unprepared. It has been called the fastest diplomatic recognition in American history.

The familiar explanation is that Truman acted from moral conviction about Jewish statehood after the Holocaust, and that the CIA's concerns were one input among many. The documents support a narrower statement: the strategic advice was clear and was set aside, and the decision was political rather than strategic. One reading, made with the advantage of hindsight, holds that this set a pattern in which political decisions overrode intelligence assessments, and that the intelligence relationship which followed began on terms Israel could shape. That is an interpretation of later history, not a finding about 1948, and the later relationship, from James Jesus Angleton's tenure as the CIA's counterintelligence chief from 1954 to the Khrushchev speech of 1956, is told in [scene:cia_s02|the CIA and Israel after 1948].

25 June 1948: the Displaced Persons Act. The first decision of the summer concerned Jewish refugees in Europe rather than the new state itself. On 25 June 1948 Truman signed the Displaced Persons Act "with very great reluctance," saying that it "discriminates in callous fashion against displaced persons of the Jewish faith." [cite:truman_institute_dpact] He did not leave the matter there. He pressed Congress for two years, until the act was amended in 1950. [cite:wjc_dpact] [cite:truman_institute_dpact] The episode does not fit a president acting on donors' instructions, nor one indifferent to Jewish refugees: he signed a law he condemned and then spent two years trying to change it.

Summer 1948: what Truman wanted and did not press. Through the summer, Truman privately wanted Israel to stop taking territory beyond the partition lines, and he was disturbed by its refugee policy. He backed away from pressing either point. The historian John Judis documents that his nearly broke whistle-stop campaign needed $100,000 from two donors, Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann, at that moment, and that this need was part of the reason he stopped pushing. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] The donors, the sum and the way Judis frames it are the subject of [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg]. The point for this sequence is its date: the retreat came in the summer and autumn of an election year, when the campaign was short of money.

21–28 September 1948: the Bernadotte plan. On 16 September the UN mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, completed a report that would have given the Negev to the Arab side; the next day he was murdered in Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_progress_report] [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte] On 21 September 1948 Secretary of State Marshall endorsed the plan. On 28 September the White House aide Clark Clifford was upset that Marshall had approved it without the President's endorsement, and the camp of Truman's opponent, Thomas Dewey, sought to exploit the episode. [cite:jvl_clifford_bernadotte] [cite:djn_marshall_bernadotte_1948] Truman backed away from the plan. The episode shows that the American government was not of one mind. The Secretary of State and his department pressed a settlement that cut Israel's territory; the President's political staff, alert to the campaign, objected; and the President sided with his staff.

24 October 1948: the pledge. Ten days before the election, and two days after a statement by Dewey, Truman went further. He quoted the Democratic platform, which pledged full recognition of Israel and revision of the arms embargo "to accord to the State of Israel the right of self-defense," and he directed government agencies to expedite Israel's applications for loans. [cite:ucsb_truman_oct24_1948] The timing tells its own story. The statement answered an opponent's statement within forty-eight hours and came in the campaign's last fortnight.

29 November 1948: the Negev. After winning, on 29 November, Truman reassured Weizmann that Israel would keep the Negev. Two months earlier his Secretary of State had endorsed a plan that would have taken it away.

January 1949: recognition and a loan. The October pledges were then kept. An Export-Import Bank loan of $100 million was earmarked for Israel, and on 31 January 1949 the United States granted de jure recognition, full legal recognition of the state. [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:docsteach_dejure_1949] [cite:djn_1977_truman_note]

The loan's size is best seen against the other dollar sums in this story. The library's money dashboard sets every documented American dollar flow of the pre-state and founding years side by side, in nominal dollars of the year reported. The $100 million loan is the largest bar on it. The $100,000 that Feinberg and Kaufmann raised for the campaign train is the smallest, one-thousandth of the loan. [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman]

The contrast cuts both ways. Those who argue that donors bought policy point to the sequence: the campaign took the smaller sum in 1948, and the state received a government loan a thousand times larger in 1949. Those who argue that money played no role point to the scale: a government loan of $100 million was a decision of the United States made through the Export-Import Bank after an election had been won, not a return on a campaign contribution. The documents do not show a link between the two sums. What they show is that the $100,000 was raised at the moment a policy was in play, and that the loan followed a public pledge made in the campaign's last days.

28 May 1949: the threat. Then the pressure returned. On 28 May 1949, while the Lausanne talks were under way, Truman had a secret note delivered to David Ben-Gurion. It said the United States was "seriously disturbed" by Israel's position on territory and refugees. It said Israel should offer territorial compensation for the land it held beyond the partition lines. And it warned that if Israel continued to reject the UN resolution of December 1948, "a revision of its attitude toward Israel has become unavoidable." [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] [cite:djn_1977_truman_note] The note took up exactly the two questions, territory and refugees, on which Truman had stopped pressing in the summer of 1948. With the election behind him, he raised them again, in writing.

June 1949: rejection, and no revision. Israel rejected the demands in June. The threatened revision was not carried through. [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] [cite:djn_1977_truman_note] The documents gathered here do not explain why Washington did not follow its warning with action. What came at Lausanne after the note is told in [scene:lau_s01|the Lausanne conference].

May 1950: the Tripartite Declaration. In May 1950 the United States joined Britain and France in the Tripartite Declaration on the region. Its text has not been researched for this book, and its terms are not described here.

What the dated record supports. Laid out in order, the decisions have a clear shape. The two moves most favourable to Israel in 1948, the retreat from the Bernadotte plan and the October pledges, came when money and votes were most needed. After the election Truman both delivered and pushed back: he kept his pledges on recognition and the loan, and then, six months later, threatened a revision of policy over the very issues he had set aside. He did not enforce the threat. That record fits neither simple account. It is not the record of a president who did whatever Israel's supporters asked, since in May 1949 he sent a note threatening a revision of policy over territory and refugees. Nor is it the record of a president untouched by his campaign's finances, since a historian names financial need as part of the reason he stopped pressing a point he cared about, at the moment he stopped. Put in a sentence: the shift clustered around the election; then the pressure returned, and faded.

Several things remain unsettled. The documents establish timing, not motive: they show when Truman moved, not how much weight he gave to money, votes, conviction or advice at each step. Judis's account attributes the summer retreat to financial need "in part," and no source here measures that part. No document here links the campaign contribution to the 1949 loan. And the record gathered for this book does not say why the threatened revision of May 1949 was never carried out, which is perhaps the most consequential question of the sequence, since it was the moment at which Washington had stated its terms and then let them go.
