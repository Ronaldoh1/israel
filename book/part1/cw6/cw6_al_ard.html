Al-Ard and the Yeredor Case: Organizing Under Military Government, 1959–1965

While Israel and its neighbours fought over the Jordan's water, Palestinian citizens inside Israel were testing what political room the state would allow them. The regime of military government over Israel's Arab citizens is described in Volume II in [scene:rd6_military_government|military government], and its end in 1966 in [scene:cw6_military_rule_end|the next stage of that story]. This section concerns something the regime's structure does not show on its own: what happened when Palestinian citizens of Israel tried to organize politically outside the existing parties, between 1959 and 1965. The answer came in three stages, from the administration, from the Minister of Defence, and finally from the Supreme Court.

A vote lost by one, 1963. The political setting is shown by a vote in the Knesset in 1963. An opposition motion to end military rule failed by a single vote. Two Arab members linked to Mapai, Jabr Muadi and Thiab Ubaid, voted with the government. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_mg] The episode is recorded in the Institute for Palestine Studies encyclopedia. One reading is that it shows the two channels through which Arab citizens could act in parliament at the time. One ran through members linked to Mapai and voting with the government, whose votes could decide a question as close as this one. The other ran outside it, and that was the channel al-Ard tried to open.

From the Popular Front to al-Ard. According to the journalist Edo Konrad, writing for +972 Magazine in 2015, al-Ard grew out of a "Popular Front" formed by nationalists and communists in 1959. Konrad names Fouzi El-Asmar as one of its founders. [cite:rs_cw6_konrad_972] Konrad's essay is a work of advocacy journalism, and it is the source here for most of what is known about the group's early life; its account of events is reported as his, and its interpretation is marked as such below.

On his account, the authorities closed successive versions of al-Ard's newspaper, and they refused to register the group as an economic company. Its members were placed under surveillance, and some were jailed or banished to the Negev. [cite:rs_cw6_konrad_972] The pattern he describes is one of administrative obstruction rather than a single prohibition: each attempt to publish or to incorporate was met by a refusal, and the group's members were subject to the powers of a state that still governed its Arab citizens under military government.

The courts and the ban, 1964. The decisive legal step came in 1964. In the case of Sabri Jiryis v. District Commissioner of Haifa, HCJ 253/64, the Supreme Court held that al-Ard sought to deny Israel's existence. The Minister of Defence declared it an unlawful association the same year. [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] [cite:rs_cw6_konrad_972] The two steps were distinct: the court made a finding about al-Ard's aims; the ban was an act of the executive, made by the Minister responsible for defence, not by a court. From 1964 al-Ard as such could not lawfully exist.

The Socialists' List, 29 September 1965. Its members then tried to stand for parliament. For the elections to the Sixth Knesset, an Arab list of ten candidates, called the Socialists' List, was submitted to the Central Elections Committee. On 29 September 1965 the Committee refused to confirm it. Its grounds were that most of the candidates were members of al-Ard and that "its initiators deny the integrity of the State of Israel and its very existence." [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] The list's supporters appealed to the Supreme Court. The case is known by the name of its appellant: Yeredor v. Chairman of the Central Elections Committee for the Sixth Knesset, EA 1/65.

The majority: "defensive democracy." The Court dismissed the appeal by a majority. President Agranat and Justice Sussman formed the majority; according to Justice Sussman's opinion, the decision was given on 12 October 1965. They held that the Knesset could not be used as an instrument to pursue the destruction of the state. Sussman described the approach as a "defensive democracy." [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] The strongest form of the majority's case is this. A democracy is not obliged to lend its own parliament to those who seek to abolish the state the parliament serves; the Court had already found, in the Jiryis case, that al-Ard sought to deny Israel's existence; and a list made up mostly of members of a body already declared unlawful was, on that finding, an attempt to carry an unlawful association's aims into the legislature. The majority held that such a list could be barred even without an express statute: a state may defend itself against its own destruction even where the law has not spelled the power out.

The dissent: Justice Cohn. Justice Haim Cohn dissented, and his reasoning was textual. Section 23 of the Knesset Elections Law said that a duly submitted list "shall be confirmed." No statute empowered the Committee to reject one. And there was, in his view, no "real, clear and present danger" that might justify going beyond the statute. He wrote that the right to vote and to be elected "is one of the fundamental rights of the citizen." [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] The strongest form of the dissent is that the case was not about al-Ard's views at all, but about who may take a citizen's right to stand for election away: on Cohn's reading, only the legislature could do so, by statute, and a committee that did so without one acted beyond its power, whatever it thought of the candidates.

Defence of the state, or suppression of organizing? The two opinions frame a dispute that has outlasted the case. On one side stands the majority's reasoning: a list whose initiators deny the state's existence may be barred even without an express statute, because a democracy may defend itself. [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] Its supporters can point to the Court's own prior finding in the Jiryis case, and to the limit built into the principle as the majority stated it: it reached a list whose initiators denied the state's existence and sought its destruction.

On the other side stand Justice Cohn's dissent and, in a different register, Konrad's essay. Cohn's objection was legal: the rejection had no statutory basis. [cite:rs_cw6_yeredor] Konrad's is an interpretation of the whole period, made in advocacy journalism fifty years later: that the state treated secular Palestinian organizing as a threat regardless of its ideology. [cite:rs_cw6_konrad_972] The strongest form of this side's case joins the two. The obstruction of al-Ard, on Konrad's account, took the form of closed newspapers and a refused registration as well as the ban; the 1965 decision barred ten candidates on the ground that most of them belonged to a banned body and that the list's initiators denied the state's existence; and the right being withdrawn was, in a dissenting justice's words, among the citizen's fundamental rights. Konrad's broader claim, that ideology did not matter to the state's response, is his interpretation and is not established by the judgment itself, which rested expressly on al-Ard's denial of Israel's existence.

The two cases rest on different questions. The majority asked whether al-Ard's aims could be carried into the Knesset; the dissent asked whether the Committee had the power to stop them. An answer to the first does not answer the second, which is why the judgment has remained both an authority for "defensive democracy" and a reference point for its critics.

What the record does not settle. The sources consulted here leave much of al-Ard's own history open. They do not give the text of its programme or of the newspapers that were closed, so the reader cannot check the Court's characterization of its aims against its own words. They do not give the number of members surveilled, jailed or banished, or the dates of those measures, beyond Konrad's general account. They do not name the ten candidates of the Socialists' List, or say what became of them after October 1965. And they do not record how Arab citizens voted in the election from which the list was barred.
