Between 1922 and 1936 the Zionist project in Palestine changed character. It stopped being chiefly a matter of declarations and diplomacy and became a matter of building. The British Mandate, the arrangement under which Britain now governed the country, supplied the frame. Inside it the movement assembled the working parts of a state that did not yet exist: a labor federation with its own bank, construction firm and health fund; a Jewish Agency widened to take in wealthy American non-Zionists; a banking channel chartered in London; an electricity monopoly whose grid followed Jewish settlement. Over the same years Britain counted the country's people and investigated its violence. Once it tried to slow the pace of immigration and land transfer, and within months it reversed itself.

These were not years of unity. Inside Palestine, Jewish opponents of Zionism made their case to the British, and in 1924 one of their spokesmen was shot dead in Jerusalem. The movement itself split. The Revisionist break of 1925, the departure of the movement's right wing, produced both the paramilitary organizations of the 1930s and 1940s and the institutional line that later became Likud. In 1929 violence at Hebron removed a Jewish community that had lived there for centuries; organized Jewish life in the city did not resume until 1968. And from 1933 Germany cast its shadow over everything. There a democracy was voted out of existence, a transfer agreement with the new regime divided the Jewish world, and the Nuremberg Laws gave persecution the form of statute.

The chapters that follow ask a single question. In the years when the state-in-waiting took shape, what was actually built, by whom, and on what terms?
