Montségur and the Inquisitors

The war did not end the Cathars. The papal Inquisition, established in the thirteenth century, did more: it "had virtually eliminated" Catharism in Languedoc within a century. [cite:hr_brit_crusade] The inquisitors were themselves targets. On 28 May 1242 about fifty men from the fortress of Montségur and dispossessed lords killed two representatives of the Inquisition at Avignonet, and the killing led to the decision to send a royal army against the fortress. [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur]

At the fortress of Montségur, its lord, Pierre-Roger de Mirepoix, "protected a community of Cathars". [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur] Royal forces under the seneschal Hugues des Arcis, about ten thousand troops against roughly a hundred defenders, besieged it from May 1243. After nine months it fell, on 16 March 1244. Everyone in the castle was allowed to leave "except those who would not renounce their Cathar faith". About two hundred and ten of the perfect and of believers who would not recant were burned on a pyre at the foot of the mountain. [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur]

The Cathar hierarchy faded in the 1270s; the heresy lingered through the fourteenth century and disappeared early in the fifteenth. [cite:hr_brit_cathari] The instrument that achieved what the crusade could not was the inquisitor's register: the questioning of villagers, the recording of names, the summoning of witnesses. Part Six returns to those registers, because they are also the main source for what the Cathars believed, and historians now argue about how far they can be trusted.

The fire at Montségur is the most remembered moment of the story, and the terms of surrender show its logic. Those who renounced their faith walked free; those who would not were burned. [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur] The crime, in the end, was not what one did but what one refused to say. That is the line this shelf has followed from the beginning: the office that holds the truth demands assent, and treats a refusal as the gravest offence.
