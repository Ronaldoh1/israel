Earning the Promise: Spies and Soldiers for Britain, 1915–1918

The Balfour Declaration is often described as a gift, granted by Britain with nothing given in return. The wartime record is more complicated. While the declaration was being negotiated in London, Jews in Palestine were spying on the Ottomans for British intelligence, and Jewish volunteers were fighting in British service at Gallipoli and later in Allenby's Palestine campaign. These services gave the movement standing with the British at the moment the cabinet was deciding.

The intelligence came from NILI, a ring of about 40 members based mainly in Zikhron Ya'akov and Hadera. It formed in 1915 around the Aaronsohn siblings and Avshalom Feinberg, and it passed Ottoman military information to British intelligence, partly by way of a vessel run by the forerunner of MI6. The CIA's own journal has reviewed the ring's role. [cite:wiki_nili] [cite:jc_nili_shindler] [cite:cia_review_spies_palestine]

Aaron Aaronsohn, a noted agronomist, left Palestine in 1916 to brief the British directly. He influenced officials including Sir Mark Sykes and William Ormsby-Gore, who were later pivotal in persuading the cabinet to issue the Balfour Declaration. [cite:jc_nili_shindler] The channel ran, in other words, from an intelligence network inside Ottoman Palestine to men who helped shape the British decision.

The cost to the ring was severe. The Ottomans broke NILI in October 1917. Sarah Aaronsohn took her own life after days of torture. [cite:wiki_nili] [cite:jc_nili_shindler]

The military contribution began earlier and continued later. In 1915 the Zion Mule Corps, 737 volunteers, largely refugees expelled from Palestine, served at Gallipoli. In 1917 and 1918 the 38th to 42nd battalions of the Royal Fusiliers, known as the Jewish Legion, fought in Allenby's campaign in Palestine. One battalion trained in Nova Scotia, and among its men was David Ben-Gurion. [cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion]

The Legion was not the product of a united movement. Much of the British Zionist leadership opposed its formation; Weizmann and Jabotinsky were the exceptions. As with the split in the American movement described in [scene:s04h|the section on Hadassah, Brandeis and the ZOA], the record shows real disagreement over method, not a single plan executed in unison.

Together, the spy ring and the battalions amounted to services rendered: intelligence from inside Ottoman Palestine and soldiers in British uniform, offered at exactly the time the cabinet was weighing the declaration. They were not the only considerations in play. Weizmann's wartime chemistry and restorationist sympathies, treated elsewhere in this book, also played a part. But the familiar picture of a promise given for nothing leaves out what the movement had contributed to Britain's war, and what some of its members had paid for that contribution.
