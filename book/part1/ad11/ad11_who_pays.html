Who Pays

The civic version of American taxation is short: the more you earn, the larger the share of your income you hand over, so that those who gain most from the system carry most of its cost. That description fits the federal system. It fits much less well once the payroll tax, the sales tax and the property tax built into every rent are added in. This section measures the whole bill. An average (or effective) tax rate is the total tax a household pays divided by its income; it is the number that tells you how heavy the bill is. A marginal rate is the tax on the next dollar earned; it matters for the benefits cliff at the end of this part. Households are grouped into quintiles, five equal fifths ranked by income, with the top 1 per cent often shown on its own. For the difference between income and wealth, and between median and mean, see [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two].

The federal system: progressive, and taxing the bottom less than in 1979

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO), Congress's nonpartisan scorekeeper, publishes the standard measure. Its latest report, released on 24 September 2026 and covering 2023, found that average federal tax rates fell for every income group between 1979 and 2023, and fell most for the lowest fifth. The individual income tax alone ranged from minus 11 per cent of income for the lowest fifth to 16 per cent for the highest; the minus sign means that refundable credits, chiefly the Earned Income Tax Credit and the Child Tax Credit, paid out more to low-income working families than they owed. The top fifth paid 70 per cent of all federal taxes in 2023, against 55 per cent in 1979, and the top 1 per cent paid 26 per cent, against 14 per cent. [cite:ad11_cbo_dist2023]

For 2022, the year before, the Tax Foundation, a research group that generally favours lower taxes, drew the quintile figures out of CBO's data: all federal taxes took 1.4 per cent of the lowest fifth's income, 23.2 per cent of the highest fifth's, and 31.5 per cent of the top 1 per cent's. The lowest fifth's rate had been minus 16.5 per cent in 2020 and minus 22.5 per cent in 2021, when pandemic relief paid out through the tax system, and it rose when that relief ended. [cite:ad11_tf_cbo2022] [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] The same data carry a second point that the shares alone hide: the top fifth pays more of the taxes partly because it takes more of the income. The bottom 80 per cent paid 44.8 per cent of federal taxes in 1979 and 29.6 per cent in 2022, but its share of income also fell, from 54.6 to 45.1 per cent; the top 1 per cent's share of income before taxes roughly doubled over the same years. [cite:ad11_tf_cbo2022] [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022]

The long view runs the same way. On CBO's older method (not directly comparable with the 2026 reports), the lowest fifth's average federal rate was 8.0 per cent in 1979 and 4.0 per cent in 2007; the middle fifth's fell from 18.6 to 14.3 per cent; the top fifth's from 27.5 to 25.1 per cent; and the top 1 per cent's from 37.0 to 29.5 per cent, with a low of 25.5 per cent in 1986 and a rebound to 36.0 per cent in 1996 after the 1993 rate increase. [cite:ad11_tpc_cbo7907] On this measure too, the federal side of the taught version holds.

State and local taxes: the other half of the bill

Federal figures leave out roughly a third of what Americans pay: in ITEP's estimate below, state and local taxes were 10.3 of the 29.2 per cent of income that all taxes took in 2024. [cite:ad11_itep_wp2024] The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP), a research group that generally favours more progressive taxation, publishes the main estimate of state and local burdens, Who Pays?, now in its seventh edition, which applies 2024 state and local tax law to 2023 incomes. Averaged across the country, state and local taxes took 11.3 per cent of family income for the lowest fifth, 10.5 per cent for the middle fifth, 9.5 per cent for the next 15 per cent, 8.3 per cent for the next 4 per cent and 7.2 per cent for the top 1 per cent. The pattern runs the opposite way from the federal one: the lower the income, the higher the rate. [cite:ad11_itep_wp7]

The reason is what each group buys and owns. The lowest fifth spends most of what it earns, so sales and excise taxes alone took 7 per cent of its income, and property taxes, paid directly by owners and passed through in rent by landlords, took another 4.4 per cent; state income taxes, net of credits, were slightly negative. For the top 1 per cent, sales and excise taxes took 1 per cent, property taxes 1.9 per cent and income taxes 4.1 per cent. [cite:ad11_itep_wp7] The combined state and local sales tax rate averaged 7.53 per cent nationally in mid-2026, above 9.5 per cent in Louisiana, Tennessee and Washington. [cite:us22_tf_sales26]

Adding all levels together changes the picture of the whole. ITEP's 2024 estimate of every federal, state and local tax found total rates of 17.1 per cent for the lowest fifth, 26.4 per cent for the middle fifth, 28.8 per cent for the next 15 per cent and 34.8 per cent for the top 1 per cent. The top 1 per cent received 20.1 per cent of all income and paid 23.9 per cent of all taxes; the lowest fifth received 2.6 per cent of income and paid 1.5 per cent of taxes. ITEP's own summary is that the system is "just barely progressive." [cite:ad11_itep_wp2024] ITEP's combined study puts the lowest fifth's state and local rate at 13.5 per cent, above the 11.3 per cent in its Who Pays? edition, because the two use different years and methods. [cite:ad11_itep_wp2024] [cite:ad11_itep_wp7]

The payroll tax and its cap

For most working families the largest federal tax is not the income tax. Employees pay 6.2 per cent of wages for Social Security and 1.45 per cent for Medicare, 7.65 per cent in all, and employers pay the same again; the self-employed pay both halves, 15.3 per cent. The Medicare part applies to every dollar. The Social Security part stops at a ceiling, $184,500 of earnings in 2026, up from $176,100 in 2025. [cite:ad11_ssa_2026] The arithmetic of the cap is simple. A worker earning $50,000 pays $3,100 in employee Social Security tax, 6.2 per cent of pay. A worker earning ten times the cap, $1,845,000, pays the same tax as someone at the cap: 6.2 per cent of $184,500, or $11,439, which is 0.62 per cent of $1,845,000. That is why CBO's data show payroll taxes taking 9.5 per cent of the lowest fifth's income and 6.1 per cent of the highest fifth's. [cite:ad11_tf_cbo2022]

The cap is designed friction, not a loophole. Benefits are also calculated only on earnings up to the cap, and the benefit formula returns a higher share of past earnings to lower earners, so the program redistributes downward even though its tax does not. Whether the cap should rise is taken up in [scene:ad10_social_security|Part Ten]. [cite:ad11_ssa_2026]

A dollar earned two ways

The tax code treats income from work and income from owning assets differently. In 2026 the top federal rate on ordinary income, which includes wages, is 37 per cent on taxable income above $640,600 for a single filer. [cite:us22_irs_ty26] Gains on assets held longer than a year are taxed at 0, 15 or 20 per cent; in 2025 the 20 per cent rate began at $533,400 for a single filer, and a 3.8 per cent net investment income tax brings the top rate on such gains to 23.8 per cent. [cite:ad11_bti_cg2025] A wage dollar also carries Medicare tax; an asset sale does not. The gap matters because the very top of the income scale earns mostly from assets: the last published IRS data on the 400 highest-income returns, for 2014, reported about $77 billion from asset sales and $14 billion in dividends against under $6 billion in salaries and wages, and effective income tax rates flattening at the top for that reason. [cite:ad11_kenan_davids] [cite:ad11_tpc_sz2019]

The top 400: a disputed headline

A widely repeated claim holds that America's richest families pay a lower tax rate than its working class. Its source is the economists Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, who estimated in October 2019 that the 400 richest families paid 23 per cent of their income in all taxes in 2018, against 24.2 per cent for the bottom half of households. The Tax Policy Center's Howard Gleckman questioned the comparison: it treated refundable credits as government spending rather than as negative taxes, which raises the measured rate at the bottom; it extrapolated 2018 from older data; and it rested on a group of 400 out of about 150 million taxpayers, where small errors compound. The Tax Policy Center's own estimate put the federal rate at 2.9 per cent for the lowest fifth and 30.6 per cent for the top 0.1 per cent. [cite:ad11_tpc_sz2019]

The dispute did not end there. A 2025 study by Saez, Zucman and two co-authors using IRS data estimated that roughly the 400 wealthiest paid about 24 per cent of their economic income in tax in 2018–2020, against about 30 per cent for the population as a whole. The economist David Splinter, who with Gerald Auten has tested how far such estimates depend on design choices, contends that correcting for double-counted unrealised gains, dynastic wealth spread across related returns and under-reported state taxes raises the top group's rate to about 38 per cent, and that counting transfers as income lowers the population's to about 25 per cent. The Cato Institute's Adam Michel, citing these adjustments and adding charitable giving, puts the range at 38 to 73 per cent. [cite:ad11_cato_forbes400] The finding that survives on both sides is narrower than the headline: the very richest, whose income is mostly unrealised gains on assets, pay a far smaller share of their economic gains each year than their statutory rates suggest, because gains are taxed only when sold. Whether that leaves them below working families depends on the definitions, and the claim is contested, not settled.

The 2025 law

On 4 July 2025 the President signed the budget reconciliation law, Public Law 119-21, which made permanent most of the 2017 individual tax cuts and cut projected spending on Medicaid and food assistance. In its analysis of 11 August 2025, CBO found that over 2026–2034 resources would fall for households toward the bottom of the income scale and rise for the middle and top. [cite:ad11_cbo_pl11921] As reported from CBO's tables, the lowest tenth of households would lose about $1,200 a year on average, 3.1 per cent of income, the fifth and sixth tenths would gain $800 to $1,200, and the top tenth would gain about $13,600 a year. [cite:ad11_tr_pl11921] CBO's totals show where the losses come from: federal and state in-kind transfers fall by about $900 billion over the period, mainly through lower spending on Medicaid and food assistance, which go chiefly to lower-income households. [cite:ad11_cbo_pl11921] As of October 2026 the law is in force; its Medicaid work requirements begin on 1 January 2027. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] Laws under both parties have moved these numbers before: the 1993 increase raised the top 1 per cent's rate, the 2001–03 cuts lowered rates across the scale, and pandemic relief pushed the bottom fifth's below zero. [cite:ad11_tpc_cbo7907] [cite:ad11_tf_cbo2022]

Where it works as promised

The federal tax system does what the civics description says. Every fifth pays a lower federal rate than in 1979, the lowest fifth pays almost nothing on net, and the income tax returns money to low-income working families through refundable credits. CBO finds that federal taxes and transfers together reduce income inequality in every year it measures, and reduced it by more in 2023 than in most years since 1979. [cite:ad11_cbo_dist2023] The regressive part of the bill sits in state and local taxes and in the payroll tax. The next section turns to charges that are not called taxes at all.
