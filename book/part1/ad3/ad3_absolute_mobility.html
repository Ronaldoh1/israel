The Share Who Out-Earn Their Parents

The simplest promise in the American Dream to check is the one most families use: that a child who works will end up better off than the parents who raised them. Economists call the measure that tests it absolute mobility. It asks one question of each person: is your household income, adjusted for inflation, higher than your parents' household income was at the same age? Absolute mobility is different from relative mobility, which asks whether a child climbs or falls in the national ranking, and which the next section takes up. [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|How to measure the Dream] Absolute mobility is the measure closest to the promise as families tell it, and the best record of it shows a fall from almost everyone to about half. [cite:ad3_oi_fading]

How the number was built

The standard estimate comes from a 2017 paper in the journal Science by Raj Chetty, David Grusky, Maximilian Hell, Nathaniel Hendren, Robert Manduca and Jimmy Narang, whose research group is now Opportunity Insights. [cite:ad3_oi_fading] They compared children's family income at about age 30 with their parents' family income at about the same age. Family income here means the pre-tax income of a person and any spouse added together, before taxes are taken out and before government benefits such as food assistance are added. All amounts were converted into 2014 dollars using the CPI-U-RS, a research version of the consumer price index that the Bureau of Labor Statistics recalculated backwards with today's methods; for years before 1977 the ordinary consumer price index was linked to it. [cite:ad3_fading_paper]

No single survey follows American families from 1940 to today, so the authors combined three: children's incomes from the Current Population Survey, the government's household survey, for 1970 to 2014; parents' incomes from the decennial census; and, for the link between a child's rank and the parent's rank, tax records for children born from 1980 to 1982, assumed to hold for earlier generations too. Whatever that link, the 1940 figure lies between 84 and 98 per cent, and assuming no link at all still gives 50 per cent for the 1980 cohort. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] A cohort is everyone born in the same year.

The record: ninety-two to fifty

For children born in 1940, 92 per cent grew up to have a higher family income at 30 than their parents had at 30. For children born in 1980, the figure was 50 per cent, and it was still 50 per cent for those born in 1984, the last group old enough to measure when the paper was written. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad3_fading_slides] A child born just before the Second World War was almost certain to out-earn the household that raised them; one born in the early 1980s had the odds of a coin toss. The fall was not steady. It was steepest between the 1940 and 1964 cohorts, paused for children born in the late 1960s and early 1970s, and then resumed. [cite:ad3_fading_paper]

Comparing individuals rather than households makes the fall sharper for men. Sons whose own earnings exceeded their fathers' earnings fell from 95 per cent for the 1940 cohort to 41 per cent for the 1984 cohort. Daughters compared with fathers start far lower, at 43 per cent in 1940, because few women of the mothers' generation worked for pay; the figure fell to 22 per cent for the 1960 cohort and recovered to 26 per cent by 1984 as women's earnings rose. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] The decline reached every state. It was largest in the industrial Midwest: about 48 percentage points in Michigan and about 45 in Indiana, Illinois and Ohio, against about 35 in Massachusetts, New York and Montana. [cite:ad3_fading_science]

Where on the ladder it fell

The odds always depend on where the parents started: a child of very poor parents needs only a modest income to beat them, a child of very rich parents faces a high bar. For the 1940 cohort, the share who out-earned their parents was 94 per cent for children of parents at the 10th percentile of income (poorer than nine in ten families), 93 per cent at the median and 88 per cent at the 90th percentile. For the 1980 cohort, those figures were 70, 45 and 33 per cent. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] The researchers Richard Reeves and Katherine Guyot of the Brookings Institution, working from the same published data, found that the largest single drop came between the 1940 and 1950 cohorts and was concentrated at the top, but that since 1950 the losses have fallen most heavily on the broad middle. [cite:ad3_brookings_middle] The promise stopped describing the typical outcome most clearly not for the poorest but for children of middle-income households.

Why it fell: how growth was shared

Two forces decide how many children out-earn their parents: how fast the economy grows, and how that growth is divided among households. The authors tested each by asking what absolute mobility for the 1980 cohort would have been under different conditions. If the economy had grown as fast as it did when the 1940 cohort was young, about 2.5 per cent a year in real output per family against about 1.5 per cent, but its gains had been divided as they actually were in the 1980s and after, absolute mobility would have risen only from 50 to 62 per cent. If the economy had grown at its actual later pace but its gains had been divided across income groups as they were for the 1940 cohort, absolute mobility would have reached 80 per cent, closing 71 per cent of the gap. Under the actual division, getting back to 1940 levels would have needed real growth above 6 per cent a year. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad3_fading_slides]

This is a counterfactual about how the gains of growth were spread across all income groups. It does not attribute a share of the decline to any named group, and a widely repeated summary that rising wealth at the very top "explains 70 per cent" of the decline misreads it: the 71 per cent is the share of the gap that would close if the whole distribution of growth looked as it did for the 1940 cohort. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] Census income data show the same pattern from another angle. Between 1967 and 2025, real household income at the 10th and 50th percentiles rose about 56 per cent, while income at the 90th percentile rose about 121 per cent. [cite:us14_census_p60]

The critics, and what survives them

The 50 per cent figure has been challenged on its measures, chiefly by Scott Winship of the Archbridge Institute and Michael Strain of the American Enterprise Institute. Households have shrunk; adjusting for household size raises the share of 1980s-born adults better off than their parents from 50 to 60 per cent. Using a different survey, the Panel Study of Income Dynamics, which follows the same families over decades, and a different price index, the PCE deflator, which shows lower inflation than the CPI-U-RS, raises it to 67 per cent. Counting government cash benefits brings it to 68 per cent. [cite:ad3_brookings_winship] Winship's own summary, averaging incomes over many years, is that roughly three in four adults, and the overwhelming majority of children raised poor, live better off than their parents. [cite:ad3_winship_archbridge] Strain, in The American Dream Is Not Dead (2020), puts the share at nearly three-quarters on a household-size-adjusted measure; his reviewer in National Review, Robert VerBruggen, questioned that adjustment on the ground that it would count having fewer children as upward mobility. [cite:ad3_nr_strain]

The Chetty team reported its own tests of most of these choices. Adjusting for household size gives a fall from 93 to 60 per cent; assuming inflation was 0.8 points a year lower than measured gives 93 to 59; assuming it was 2 points lower still leaves a 26-point decline; counting taxes and benefits raises every cohort by about 3 points without changing the trend; and measuring at age 40 instead of 30 gives 86 per cent for the 1940 cohort and 56 per cent for the 1970 cohort. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] Winship accepts that the share fell. [cite:ad3_brookings_winship] The dispute is over the level today, roughly half on one set of measures and two-thirds to three-quarters on another, not over the direction.

Where it works as promised

For children raised near the bottom, the promise in its plainest form still largely holds. Seven in ten children of parents at the 10th percentile in the 1980 cohort had higher family incomes than their parents at the same age, and a child born at the 5th percentile in 1980 needed only to reach about the 12th percentile of their own generation to do so. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad3_brookings_middle] On the critics' measures, most adults of every recent generation live better than their parents did. [cite:ad3_winship_archbridge]

What is not yet known

The series stops with children born in 1984, who were 30 in 2014. No estimate on the same method for later cohorts had been published as of October 2026, so whether the share recovered for children born in the late 1980s and 1990s, who entered work during or after the 2008 recession, cannot be stated from this record. [cite:ad3_oi_fading]

Interpretation, not finding: the promise kept alive by its best generation

The Sophia platform reads this record as the core of its "illusion" thesis: the Dream, in this reading, was a true description of one generation's experience, roughly those born in the 1940s, that went on being taught as a general law after the conditions that produced it had changed, so that later generations measured their lives against a standard the economy no longer delivered to half of them. The strongest counter-readings are three. The first is the critics': on household-size-adjusted, multi-year or post-transfer measures, most Americans still out-earn their parents, so "illusion" overstates a fall from near-universal to a clear majority. The second is that the 1940 cohort's parents were measured at the end of the Depression and their children grew up in fast postwar growth, so 92 per cent may be the outlier, not the norm. The third is that the Chetty team's counterfactual locates the cause in a measurable pattern, the division of growth, which policy can change. The record establishes the fall; whether to call it an illusion is a judgment it leaves to the reader.
