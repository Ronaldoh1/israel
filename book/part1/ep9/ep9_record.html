How the Record Was Built

For a decade after the 2008 agreement, no federal agency reopened the case. The record that exists today was built in that decade by people outside government. Victims and their lawyers took depositions and collected exhibits in civil suits, including the case against the government over the hidden agreement, filed in 2008 and decided in 2019. [cite:marra] Virginia Giuffre sued Ghislaine Maxwell for defamation in 2015, and that case, settled in 2017, produced much of the sworn material the public would later read. [cite:ep9_pbs_unseal2024]

Then a reporter went looking for the girls. Julie K. Brown of the Miami Herald identified eighty women who said Epstein had abused them, and four agreed to be interviewed on video. Her series, "Perversion of Justice", published in November 2018, reconstructed the agreement and the way it was kept from the victims. Federal prosecutors in New York reopened the case, and his arrest in July 2019 was, as NPR later put it, largely the result of her reporting. [cite:ep9_npr_brown2021]

The Herald also went to court to open the sealed files in the Giuffre case. About 2,000 pages were unsealed in 2019, more in 2020, 2021 and 2022, and in January 2024 Judge Loretta Preska released the remaining records, with a handful of names kept hidden to protect victims. They named mostly people already known to have been around Epstein, and they were not a "client list". [cite:ep9_pbs_unseal2024] Every release of Epstein material the public could read before 2025 came this way, through courts and the press, under administrations of both parties. The Justice Department's own first release came in February 2025, and it consisted of binders of documents that were already public. [cite:ep9_abc_bondi_fired]
