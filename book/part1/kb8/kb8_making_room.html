Making Room: the Withdrawal

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

Luria's account of creation begins with an absence. Before anything can exist, God, who fills everything, must withdraw into Himself to make room. That withdrawal is tzimtzum. [cite:kb_brit_luria] It is a strange beginning for a story of creation. Most accounts start with a word or an act of making; this one starts with a stepping back.

The inner reading takes the withdrawal as the first movement of any inner life. A self that fills all its own space, with its plans, opinions and fears, has no room for anything to come into being: no new understanding, no other person, no presence of God. The first act of the inner work is the same as the first act of creation in Luria's account: to withdraw, to make a space. In this reading, humility is not a moral rule but a structural necessity. Nothing can be created in a place that is already full.

The same movement appears elsewhere on this shelf. The Gnostic ruler declared "there is no other God beside me", and the inner reading in The Gnostics took him as the self that thinks it is the whole. [cite:vt24_apjohn] Luria's God does the opposite: the one who truly is everything steps back so that something else can be. Read side by side, the two myths give two pictures of power. One fills all space and tolerates no other; the other withdraws so that others can exist.

The record limits the reading. Luria's tzimtzum is a doctrine about God and creation, worked out in a technical system. The application to the human self is the reader's.
