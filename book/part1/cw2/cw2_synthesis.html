The Architecture on 26 July 1956

When Nasser announced the nationalization of the Suez Canal Company on 26 July 1956, the structure that the next stage's crisis would test had been built in the three years before. It was not built by one government or to one design. It was assembled from separate decisions taken in Jerusalem, Cairo, Washington, London, Paris, Moscow and Prague, and at the United Nations, each for its own reasons, and each contested by someone inside the same government. This section sets out how those threads stood on that day: who held power, through what instrument, for what stated interest, and who opposed it from within.

The first thread was retaliation as Israeli policy. It had an institutional birth date. In August 1953 Ariel Sharon was appointed to establish and command Unit 101, created, in the words of Israel's Ministry of Defense archive, "to carry out reprisal operations in enemy territory"; in January 1954 the unit was folded into paratrooper battalion 890, and the cross-border operations continued under the paratroopers. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_sharon] The operations that followed form a line through the stage: Qibya in October 1953, the Gaza raid of 28 February 1955, Khan Yunis at the end of August 1955, Lake Tiberias in December 1955, Qalqilya in October 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_mod_black_arrow] [cite:rs_cw2_un_a3157] [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] Israel presented the raids as answers to attacks across the line that it named and documented, from the killing of a mother and her two children at Yehud in October 1953 to the bus at the Scorpion Pass in March 1954 and the four civilians killed near Kfar Menahem in August 1955; on the Scorpion Pass, the UN chairman of the armistice commission found the evidence "far from being conclusive" as to who was responsible. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3196] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3427] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] Three of the raids drew the Security Council's censure: Resolution 101 on Qibya, adopted 9–0 with Lebanon and the Soviet Union abstaining; Resolution 106 on Gaza, unanimous; Resolution 111 on the lake, unanimous, warning of "further measures under the Charter". [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res106] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res111] The Council could censure, and could send its Secretary-General, who came back in May 1956 with cease-fire assurances that every government made conditional on the others' conduct. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] Neither censure nor the mission stopped the raids or the attacks across the line.

The policy had its opponents inside Israel's own cabinet. Avi Shlaim describes two schools, Sharett's "negotiation" school and Ben-Gurion's "retaliation" school: Sharett "sought to avoid escalation in order to prevent war", while Dayan "needed escalation to bring about war". [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] Neil Caplan records that Sharett led cabinet majorities for restraint and blocked proposals to capture Gaza or the Straits of Tiran. [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] The contest had a price in Washington, too. Dulles offered a mutual defence pact in February 1955 on two conditions, that Israel not expand its borders by force and not carry out military retaliation; Ben-Gurion and Sharett accepted the first and refused the second. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] Sharett's case, in Shlaim's account, was that avoiding escalation was the way to prevent war. By 26 July 1956, on one reading, the argument had been settled by office, not by persuasion. Ben-Gurion had returned as defence minister on 21 February 1955 and as prime minister on 3 November 1955; Sharett had resigned as foreign minister on 18 June 1956, and two days after the nationalization, according to a dealer's description of the letter, Ben-Gurion wrote to him that his continuing at the Foreign Ministry "was not for the good of the country". [cite:rs_cw2_mod_bg_portfolio] [cite:rs_cw2_pmo_govts] [cite:rs_cw2_kaller_bg] Shlaim's judgment, quoted by Caplan, is that "Ben-Gurion failed to topple Nasser but he succeeded in toppling Sharett." [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia]

The second thread was Washington's attempt to manage the conflict with the instruments it had, and the limits of each. The first was money. In September 1953, on Assistant Secretary Byroade's advice, Dulles withheld Mutual Security funds while, as he put it, Israel was "acting in defiance" of the UN Chief of Staff's decision on the works at Banat Yaacov; the works stopped at midnight on 28 October, and Press Release 604 recommended about $26 million, saying that aid granted while Israel ignored the UN "would tend to undermine the authority" of its truce organization. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_presser_20oct53] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_pr604] It was a lever that worked, on a narrow question, because a UN officer's ruling gave it a fixed point. The second instrument was development. Eric Johnston's water plan was declared technically acceptable to Israel, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon, with the United States offering two-thirds of the costs, and was then not endorsed by the Arab League's political organs, whose Council decided against it in October 1955 by AquaPedia's dating. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_unified_plan] [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] The third was diplomacy. Operation Alpha, begun with Britain on 20 December 1954, put forward a Negev land link, the resettlement of 75,000 refugees in Israel and non-belligerence; Dulles's address of 26 August 1955 offered a loan for compensation and a treaty guarantee of agreed borders; Robert Anderson carried it to Ben-Gurion, Sharett and Nasser from January to March 1956, and, according to the Jewish Virtual Library, both sides rejected it. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_alpha] [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_alpha] [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55] The fourth was arms, and here Washington chose refusal: Israel's requests after the Czech deal were postponed on 13 December 1955 and finally rejected in April 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] The fifth was the Aswan offer, withdrawn on 19 July 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike]

Several of these instruments had opponents within the American government as well as outside it. The decision on Aswan is the clearest case. The memoranda of 19 July give a policy judgment about Egypt; Robert Bowie and Fraser Wilkins later named a hostile Congress, with cotton growers, supporters of Israel and the China lobby among its constituencies, as the real constraint; Parker Hart called Dulles's decision "impulsive". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike] [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] State and Congress pulled in different directions; within State, officers disagreed with their Secretary. Washington's public line was itself contested: Byroade's speeches of April and May 1954 were protested by Israel's government and denounced by Emanuel Neumann as "Byroadeism", and the administration stood by him. [cite:rs_cw2_medoff_byroade] Washington and London did not always speak together either. When Eden proposed on 9 November 1955 that Israel and the Arab states "must make some compromise between these two positions", Dulles replied from Geneva that the "US is in no sense committed". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_uk]

The third thread was the entry of the Soviet bloc. It came through Egypt, by sale, and it came at Egypt's request. On 15 June 1954 Nasser told the Soviet ambassador that Britain had stopped arms shipments and that Egypt could not meet "a hundredth of its army's needs". [cite:rs_cw2_wilson_solod] On 27 September 1955 he announced that Czechoslovakia would supply tanks, MiG-15s and bombers, in a deal the Jewish Virtual Library values at "more than $83 million"; Egypt paid in cotton and rice, with a million Egyptian pounds already sent to Prague "on account". [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_czech] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tel632] Assistant Secretary George Allen explained that American arms talks with Egypt had stalled on Washington's requirement of a military mission. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_allen_czech] Whether Israel's Gaza raid drove Nasser to Moscow, as Shlaim and Allen held, or the Baghdad Pact did, as Eban argued, is disputed; the Soviet record of June 1954 shows the request predated both. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_gaza] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_allen] [cite:rs_cw2_solod_gaza]

The fourth thread answered the third. With Washington refusing, Israel turned to France. Shimon Peres went directly to the French defence establishment, and Shlaim reads the two Western motives against each other: "France offered arms in the hope of inducing Israel to go to war against Egypt," while "America allowed its allies to supply arms to Israel on condition that Israel did not go to war." [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] That is his interpretation. The State Department's own count, in September 1956, was that Israel was believed to hold 24 Mystères and possibly 48, with France proposing to sell 24 more. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_mystere]

The fifth thread was the region choosing sides. Iraq and Turkey signed the Baghdad Pact on 24 February 1955; Britain adhered on 5 April, Pakistan on 23 September and Iran on 23 October 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_baghdad] The United States stayed out, for reasons a State Department position paper of November 1956 listed: renewed Israeli demands for a security guarantee, Senate ratification, Hashemite–Saudi–Egyptian rivalries and Soviet reaction. It paid its share of the budget, $76,350, and sat on the committees all the same. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_baghdad_us] At Bandung in April 1955, twenty-nine Asian and African states met without Israel, which was not invited despite Burma's U Nu, and declared "support for the rights of the Arab people of Palestine"; Nasser emerged there, in Zachary Karabell's phrase, as an international leader. [cite:rs_cw2_enc_bandung] [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bandung] Jordan showed what the choice cost a government caught between the two: its prime minister's move toward the Pact in December 1955 brought three days of riots and his fall, and in March 1956 King Hussein dismissed Glubb, whose "open endorsement" of the Pact's supporters was among the reasons the Amman report listed. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] [cite:rs_cw2_wilson_glubb]

The sixth thread was Egypt's own consolidation, which had its own opponents and its own violence. The Free Officers' coup of 23 July 1952 produced a contest in 1954 that Nasser won over Naguib; the Suez base agreement of 19 October 1954 provided for British troops to leave the Canal Zone; the Manshiya shooting a week later led to the Muslim Brotherhood's banning and more than a thousand arrests; Naguib was removed in November 1954; and on 23 June 1956 Nasser, the only candidate, was elected president with 99.95 per cent, as HISTORY.com reports. [cite:rs_cw2_ebsco_farouk] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_suez_base] [cite:rs_cw2_origins_mb] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_naguib] [cite:rs_cw2_history_nasser] Israel's covert attempt to damage Egypt's ties with the West during the base talks, Operation Susannah in July 1954, ended with two Egyptian Jews hanged in Cairo on 31 January 1955 and a dispute over who ordered it, followed by Lavon's resignation in February 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged]

Two further threads ran beneath the diplomacy. The first was intelligence. From 1954 James Angleton headed the CIA's new Counterintelligence Staff and controlled its Israel account, which the CIA's own historian calls "an administrative anomaly" and a foundation of his influence; he saw Israel "as a bastion against the Soviet Union". [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] The text of Khrushchev's secret speech reached Washington through that channel in the spring of 1956, by the account Shammai Siskind gives. [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] No formal written liaison agreement for these years is documented in the sources used. [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] The second was oil. After the coup of 1953, Iran's oil was settled in a consortium signed on 5 August 1954: the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company 40 per cent, Royal Dutch Shell 14 per cent, five American companies 8 per cent each, and by arithmetic 6 per cent for the French participant, with operating companies incorporated in the Netherlands and £25 million in compensation to the Anglo-Iranian company from 1957. [cite:rs_cw2_fo_consortium] [cite:rs_cw2_dsb_consortium] [cite:rs_cw2_compensation54]

The last thread was American and domestic. On 14 June 1954 Eisenhower signed the bill adding "under God" to the Pledge of Allegiance, speaking of "those spiritual weapons which forever will be our country's most powerful resource"; in July 1955 Congress required "In God We Trust" on all currency; and on 30 July 1956, four days after the nationalization, he signed the joint resolution making it the national motto. [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_pledge] [cite:rs_cw2_unt_pl140] [cite:rs_cw2_stat70_732] The documented motive was religious revival set against Communism. None of the sources used here links either change to Israel or to any Zionist organization, and this book draws no such link as a finding. [cite:rs_cw2_ffrf_kramnick] [cite:rs_cw2_sconres96]

Set side by side, the threads show who acted, for what stated reason, and who resisted inside the same house.

The pattern these threads make is convergence, not design. Britain negotiated over its Canal base and joined Iraq in the Pact; Egypt sought the British out, arms on its own terms and a dam on its own terms; Israel's activists sought deterrence and arms, and its moderates sought to avoid escalation; Washington weighed its steps against Moscow's reaction, as its own papers show, and found no instrument that bound both sides at once. France's interests, by Shlaim's reading, ran toward a war with Egypt. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] No single government controlled the outcome, and none of the sources used here shows one doing so.

What the record leaves open is the weight of each thread. It does not settle whether the Gaza raid or the Baghdad Pact turned Nasser to Prague, since he had asked Moscow before either; whether Aswan was withdrawn for Egypt's conduct or for Congress's mood; or whether Sharett's restraint could have held had Washington sold Israel arms in December 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_solod_gaza] [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] Those questions run forward into [scene:cw2_aswan|the end of the dam offer] and the crisis of the autumn, told in the next stage.
