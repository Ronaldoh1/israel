The Phrase and the Verses

Most readers meet the phrase in a news story. On 12 August 2025 Benjamin Netanyahu gave an interview to the Israeli channel i24NEWS. Asked by the presenter Sharon Gal whether he felt connected to the vision of a "Greater Israel", he answered "Very much"; asked whether he subscribed to it, he said "absolutely", and he described himself as on a "historic and spiritual mission". [cite:gi1_aje_condemn] [cite:gi1_newarab_i24] He did not say which territory he meant. The territorial lists that ran with the story were supplied by the outlets: Al Jazeera wrote that the concept was "understood to refer to" the West Bank, Gaza and parts of Lebanon, Syria, Egypt and Jordan, and The New Arab gave a longer list in its own voice without a source. [cite:gi1_aje_condemn] [cite:gi1_newarab_i24] Thirty-one Arab and Islamic states, joined by the secretaries-general of the Arab League, the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation and the Gulf Cooperation Council, condemned the remarks as a direct threat to Arab national security, and Jordan's foreign ministry called them "a dangerous and provocative escalation". [cite:gi1_aje_condemn] [cite:gi1_newarab_i24] No on-record Israeli clarification was found. According to Haaretz, as reported by L'Orient Today, the Prime Minister's Office and the Foreign Ministry told Jordan and Egypt, which had asked for clarification, that Netanyahu had not used the words "Greater Israel" and was not responsible for the headlines written after the interview. [cite:gi7_olj_pendant] Part Seven examines the interview, the maps Netanyahu showed at the United Nations and the responses in full. [scene:gi7_interview|Part Seven: the "very much" interview]

The episode shows the problem this volume addresses. A two-word phrase carried a prime minister's assent, a list of countries he did not name and a regional diplomatic protest, and each of the three rested on a different understanding of what the words mean.

What the Hebrew says

The Hebrew phrase is Eretz Yisrael HaShlema, usually translated "the Whole Land of Israel" or "the Complete Land of Israel"; shalem means complete. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] The English "Greater Israel" is a looser rendering. The historian Daniel Pipes, writing in 1994, argued that it is an inexact translation which "implies a geographical expansion not present in the original". [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The Hebrew asks a question rather than answering it: whole compared with what? The answer has changed with the speaker and the decade. To a Revisionist Zionist in 1930 the whole land meant both banks of the Jordan; to the movement founded under that name in the summer of 1967 it meant the territories Israel had just captured; to a minority of religious nationalists it has meant the widest boundaries named in scripture. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:gi1_wiki_mgi] The next section sets these versions out with the people and documents that carried each.

The broad texts and the boundary texts

The widest version draws on a few verses of the Hebrew Bible. In Genesis 15:18 God makes a covenant with Abram: "Unto thy seed have I given this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates" (King James Version). [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] Deuteronomy 11:24 promises the Israelites that "every place whereon the soles of your feet shall tread shall be yours: from the wilderness and Lebanon, from the river, the river Euphrates, even unto the uttermost sea", that is, the Mediterranean. [cite:gi1_bg_deut1124] These are the texts from which the slogan "from the Nile to the Euphrates" was later made.

Other passages draw a different map. Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 describe the land to be divided among the tribes in specific boundary terms, and the area they mark is much narrower: it lies west of the Jordan, and Ezekiel 47:18 makes the Jordan the eastern border. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] Genesis itself complicates the wider reading, since the covenant is made with all of Abram's offspring, who in the biblical narrative include Ishmael, traditionally the ancestor of the Arabs. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Pipes argued that the specific boundary passages govern the general promise and that, because the covenant includes Ishmael's descendants, it cannot be read as a deed for a modern Jewish state. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] That is one argument among several; readers who take the Genesis verse as a literal promise reach other conclusions, and Part Eleven follows the religious currents that do. [scene:gi11_religious|Part Eleven: the religious engine]

Even the southern end of the Genesis promise is disputed. The verse uses the Hebrew nahar, "river", a word applied to the largest rivers, and one standard reference work, the Encyclopedia of The Bible, reads "the river of Egypt" as the Nile and finds no textual basis for changing the word. [cite:gi1_ebg_riveregypt] The boundary passages use a different term, nahal Mitzrayim, the "brook" or "wadi" of Egypt, a seasonal stream bed, which the same encyclopedia says is "highly probably" the Wadi el-Arish, a watercourse in northern Sinai about midway between the Suez Canal and Gaza. [cite:gi1_ebg_brookegypt] Some translators and dictionaries read Genesis 15:18 the same way: the New International Version renders it "from the Wadi of Egypt", and Smith's Bible Dictionary lists both the Nile and the wadi as senses of the phrase. [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] [cite:gi1_bh_riveregypt] Pipes, citing the medieval commentator Rashi, took the wadi reading. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The phrase "from the Nile to the Euphrates" therefore rests on one reading of one word, and the narrower reading has long-standing support in Jewish and Christian scholarship.

A verse is not a programme

Scripture establishes what a text says. It does not establish what any modern government intends. The step from the first to the second needs evidence of its own: a party platform, a law, a coalition agreement, a map put before an audience, an official's dated statement. Pipes's 1994 article catalogued how widely the Nile-to-Euphrates claim had spread, repeated by Yasser Arafat, Gamal Abdel Nasser, Muammar Qadhdhafi, Hafez al-Asad and the Iranian leader Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, and he called it "groundless fantasy". [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Pipes was himself a partisan in the argument, and his article contains a finding that later writers misreported: in 1898 Theodor Herzl did note in his diary a plan to ask the Ottoman sultan for land from the Egyptian frontier to the Euphrates, though he never made the request. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Part Two and Part Ten examine that note, and the stories of a Knesset inscription and of rivers in the flag, which the record does not support. [scene:gi2_herzl|Part Two: Herzl's note] [scene:gi10_myths|Part Ten: the myths]

The record also contains Israeli leaders who rejected the idea outright. At a cabinet meeting in September 2008 the outgoing prime minister, Ehud Olmert, said that "the notion of Greater Israel no longer exists, and anyone who still believes in it is deluding themselves." [cite:gi1_ynet_olmert] Seventeen years later his successor told an interviewer that he felt "very much" connected to the vision. [cite:gi1_aje_condemn] Both men led the same state. Neither statement speaks for Israelis as a whole, and neither can be read from the other.

How this volume reads the record

The rule that follows is the rule of the whole book. Greater Israel is treated here as a set of claims made by named movements, parties, documents and officials, among them the Revisionist Zionists, the Movement for Greater Israel, Gush Emunim, Likud platforms, the Religious Zionism party, and individual ministers and prime ministers. It is never attributed to Jews, to Israelis or to Zionists as a people; many Israelis and several Israeli parties have opposed territorial expansion, and the record of their opposition is given the same care in Part Twelve. [scene:gi12_opposition|Part Twelve: the Israeli opposition] Three kinds of statement are kept apart: documented facts, which rest on a law, a vote, a platform, a map or an official's own words; patterns, which can be shown by setting events side by side over time; and arguments about intent, which are labelled as arguments and given with the case against them. "Greater Israel" counts as a programme in this book only where a named actor has stated it or legislated it.

This volume takes one proposition as a hypothesis to be tested rather than assumed: that the documented record is worse than the rumour. The rumour, a secret plan to rule from the Nile to the Euphrates, has no documentary basis. The record, as later parts show, is narrower and more concrete: platforms, laws, coalition guidelines and ministers' statements claiming the land between the Jordan and the Mediterranean, and the settlement and annexation measures that followed. [cite:gi1_jvl_likud1977] [cite:gi1_jns_guidelines] Whether that record amounts to a programme of expansion beyond the 1967 lines, and who holds it, is the question the remaining twelve parts test.
