The Council That Never Sat: The Legislative Council of 1922–1923

The Mandate obliged Britain to do two things that pulled against each other. Article 2 made the Mandatory responsible for conditions that would secure the Jewish national home and for the development of self-governing institutions, while also "safeguarding the civil and religious rights of all the inhabitants of Palestine." [cite:rs_md_avalon] The first test of how those duties would be reconciled came in 1922, when London offered Palestine a constitution with a partly elected legislature. Within a year the offer had failed. The record of that failure is more specific than either side's later shorthand: a share in government was offered, on terms that the Arab leadership refused and the Zionist leadership accepted, and its rejection left the High Commissioner ruling without any elected legislature for the rest of the Mandate.

The negotiation began before the text was final. Through the first half of 1922 the Palestine Arab Delegation in London, the body sent by the Fourth Palestine Arab Congress, corresponded with the Colonial Office about the draft constitution [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress]; its rejection of the draft in February and the Colonial Office's replies are told in [scene:pp_s01|the account of the Palestinian national movement]. Two of the delegation's specific objections bore directly on the council. An earlier draft had included two nominated members, and the Colonial Office agreed to drop them. The delegation went further: it demanded that every member be elected, and it objected to a quorum of ten. [cite:rs_lc_cmd1700] By the arithmetic of the final text, the ten official members would by themselves have made a quorum of ten.

The Zionist side answered the same correspondence differently. On 18 June 1922 the Executive of the Zionist Organisation assured the government that its activities "will be conducted in conformity with the policy therein set forth," meaning the policy of the statement now known as the Churchill White Paper. [cite:rs_lc_cmd1700] According to the historian Shira Klein, the Zionist leadership did not treat the White Paper as a triumph. Weizmann and others accepted it because they believed its principle of "economic absorptive capacity" could still allow a Jewish majority to be built over time. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_whitepaper] The White Paper itself, and the immigration rule at its centre, are examined in [scene:s06a|the section on the Churchill White Paper].

The constitution was issued in London as the Palestine Order in Council on 10 August 1922, and Herbert Samuel, as High Commissioner, proclaimed it in force on 1 September 1922. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] Article 19 set out the Legislative Council: twenty-two members plus the High Commissioner, of whom ten were to be official members and twelve unofficial, elected members. Article 30 set the quorum at ten. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] The two nominated seats were gone, as the Colonial Office had conceded. The quorum of ten, to which the delegation had objected, remained.

The twelve elected seats were divided by community: eight Muslim, two Christian and two Jewish. The historian Philip Mattar has set that allocation against the population. Palestinian Arabs, by his reckoning, were allotted 43 percent of the seats while forming 88 percent of the population. [cite:rs_lc_mattar] The figure explains the shape of the dispute that followed. Even if every Muslim and Christian seat had been filled, the ten Arab members would have held ten places in a chamber of twenty-three.

The two sides' reasons for accepting and refusing are the heart of the matter, and each deserves its strongest form. The Arab leadership's case was that the council was built on the Mandate and on the Balfour Declaration, which the delegation said could not be a basis for discussion; that it gave the Arabs a minority of the seats although they were the large majority of the people; and that it fell short of what they had asked for, a national government with control over immigration. [cite:rs_lc_cmd1700] [cite:rs_lc_mattar] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_whitepaper] In February 1922 the delegation had already demanded a "national independent Government," and the Colonial Office had replied on 1 March that "the creation at this stage of a national Government would preclude the fulfilment of the pledge" to the Jewish people. [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] To take part in the council, on this reading, was to accept the frame that made the Arab majority a minority in its own legislature. The Zionist leadership's case was that the framework, whatever its limits, kept the national-home commitment intact and tied immigration to the country's economic capacity rather than to Arab consent. [cite:rs_lc_cmd1700] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_whitepaper] Both readings rest on the same documents. They differ on what those documents were for.

The Arab answer came at the Fifth Palestine Arab Congress, meeting in Nablus in August 1922, which resolved to boycott the elections to the Legislative Council. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] [cite:rs_lc_albright] According to the Jewish Virtual Library's account, the congress opened on 22 August and also resolved to reject the new constitution. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress]

The boycott was tested in the primary elections of late February 1923. Voting was in two stages, and the primaries chose secondary electors. Of 809 secondary electors expected, no more than 213 were chosen. The Muslim districts, expected to yield 663, produced only 107. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] The 1947 Yale study of the Mandate by Albright and others records the pattern by community. Jews and Druze took part fully. Muslims filled about one-sixth of their allotment and Christians about one-third. The polling was extended on 7 March 1923, and even then the result was too unrepresentative for a council to be formed. [cite:rs_lc_albright]

Britain then tried twice more within the year. It first proposed a nominated Advisory Council in place of the elected one. The Arab nominees refused their seats, and the idea collapsed. [cite:rs_lc_albright] It next offered an "Arab Agency," a body meant to parallel the Jewish agency recognised under Article 4 of the Mandate, but with its members chosen by the High Commissioner. [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_arabagency] That offer was rejected in October 1923. The president of the Arab Executive, Musa Kazim al-Husseini, wrote that the Arabs' "sole object is independence." [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_arabagency] [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] One difference between the two agencies is plain in the documents: the Zionist Organization had been named in the Mandate itself as the Jewish agency, while the Arab body would have been the High Commissioner's own selection. Britain reported the episode to the League. Its report on the administration for 1923 lists a White Paper of November 1923 on the "Proposed Formation of an Arab Agency" and "Papers relating to the Elections for the Palestine Legislative Council." [cite:rs_lc_report1923]

By the end of 1923 Britain was governing with an advisory council made up of British officials only. The High Commissioner ruled without an elected legislature for the rest of the Mandate. [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] One asymmetry follows from the documents: with no legislature, the Arab leadership held no elected place in government, while the Zionist Organization kept the advisory standing that Article 4 of the Mandate gave it as the recognised Jewish agency. [cite:rs_md_avalon]

The idea did not die at once. A council was proposed again in 1930, under Passfield, and again in 1935, when Britain offered a council of twenty-eight members: fourteen Muslims and Christians, eight Jews, five officials and one commercial member. That proposal was suspended amid Jewish opposition and Arab division, and the idea died with the revolt of 1936–39. [cite:rs_lc_mattar] The terms had shifted since 1922. The 1935 council would have given Muslims and Christians fourteen seats to the Jews' eight, and this time the opposition recorded on the Jewish side was part of the setting in which the proposal was suspended.

Several things the record here does not settle. The sources give the turnout by community, not the reasons of individual voters who stayed away. It does not establish whether a council of this shape, had it sat, could have changed policy on immigration or land, since no such council ever met. The formal instrument by which the 1923 elections were set aside, and the exact membership of the Advisory Council Britain then proposed, are not established in the sources used here and are left out rather than guessed at. Nor do the sources detail the 1930 proposal beyond its date. What the record does show is the sequence: a constitution offered on terms one side accepted and the other refused, three British attempts in a single year to find some form of Arab participation, and, from the end of 1923, a High Commissioner who governed with officials alone.
