Part Thirty-Two established that the mystery cults and the philosophical schools shared a documented idea, that the soul could be prepared by rite or discipline to rise toward the divine, expressed in forms as different as the Orphic grave tablets, the seven planetary grades of a mithraeum at Ostia and Porphyry's report of Plotinus's four unions with God. [cite:vt33_getty_goldtablets] [cite:vt33_ostia_felicissimus] [cite:vt33_voegelin_plotinus] It established that the closings were carried out by named actors: Theodosius's laws of 391–392, Bishop Theophilus acting under imperial order at the Serapeum in 391, Alaric's Goths at Eleusis in 396, a crowd led by the church reader Peter who killed Hypatia in 415, Justinian's decree to Athens around 529 and his general Narses at Philae around 535–537. [cite:vt33_romana_hypatia] [cite:vt33_watts_jrs] [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] [cite:vt33_ou_library] The rites were lost and Porphyry's attack on Christianity was burned by imperial order, but the written doctrine of ascent survived through the Pseudo-Dionysian writings, Arabic versions of Plotinus and Proclus, and Ficino's translations of 1463 onward.
