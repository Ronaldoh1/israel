The Consequence Ladder

A state can do seven broad things to a person it wants to control, each harsher than the last: watch them, deny them things, fine them, prosecute them, remove them, confine them, and make them work. This section climbs that ladder one rung at a time and asks three questions at each: what the theorists prescribe, in their own words; what exists in American law now, and for whom; and what the worst-case extension would be. Two findings run through every rung. No text by Yarvin or Land examined for this book prescribes forced labour, fines or purchase bans for dissenters. And Yarvin's one documented answer for people a sovereign corporation does not want is not labour but confinement. [cite:de35_land_ebook] [cite:de6_tnr_duran]

Watched and denied

The texts examined contain no programme of surveillance. The nearest thing is Land's statement that a resident who takes an interest in politics would "exhibit semi-criminal proclivities," which describes a disposition the sovereign would look on with suspicion without saying what it would do about it. [cite:de35_land_ebook] What exists now is extensive, and most of it was described in Book Five. ICE's Mobile Fortify application lets an officer photograph a face in the street and match it against about 200 million images; a DHS privacy document says people are given no chance to decline. [cite:de26_404_fortify] [cite:de26_reason_fortify] DHS has used administrative subpoenas, which no judge approves in advance, to seek the identities behind social-media accounts critical of immigration agents, and withdrew at least one when challenged. [cite:de28_int_meta] [cite:de28_eff_letter] The worst-case extension is watching as a sorting step, the "identify" and "flag" links of [scene:de34_the_end_to_end_map|the end-to-end map]. The second rung, denial, is the largest, and the next section takes it domain by domain. Its defining feature is that it rarely needs a court.

Fined and prosecuted

The texts treat taxes as "sovereign rent" and say nothing about fines as punishment. [cite:de35_land_ebook] The federal government already takes money without a lawsuit for debts it is owed: the Treasury Offset Program can take up to all of a tax refund and up to 15 per cent of a Social Security payment, after at least 60 days' notice, and defaulted student loans can be collected by garnishing up to 15 per cent of disposable pay without a court order. [cite:de24b_crs_top] [cite:ad7_ci_garnish] These are debt-collection tools, aimed at debts, with notice and a right to dispute. The worst-case extension would key them to something other than a debt, for example a civil penalty for protest conduct; no such use is on the record.

On prosecution, Yarvin's texts are more explicit. CNN reported that in 2022 he described "ninjas" who would "seize all points of power, without respect for paper protections." [cite:de6_cnn_2025] In 2025 he told the Times that a new regime "can't be" about bayoneting professors, and that former officials should be treated as ordinary people who had served a bad regime. [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] What exists is a directive, not a statute: National Security Presidential Memorandum 7 of 25 September 2025 tells joint terrorism task forces to investigate "institutional and individual funders" of groups it describes, and the Attorney General's memorandum of 4 December 2025 orders a running list of groups engaged in acts that "may constitute domestic terrorism." [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] [cite:de30_bondi_memo] The documented prosecutions under it concern violence, not speech ([scene:de34_missing_pieces_and_triggers|Part Thirty-Six]).

Removed

Removal is the rung on which the texts and the record come closest. In the first Patchwork post of 13 November 2008, Yarvin's sovereign corporation would "sell their slums out from under them, obviously," and its surplus residents "can of course emigrate to any other realm in the Patchwork. This presumes, however, that said realm is willing to accept them." [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de6_audit_le] In the United States removal applies only to non-citizens. For them it has been used on speech: more than 1,800 international students had their records terminated or visas revoked by 25 April 2025, and a federal judge found in September 2025 that the government had run an ideological deportation policy against non-citizen academics, vacating it in January 2026; the appeal was pending in October 2026. [cite:de24b_pie_sevis] [cite:de28_ch_aaup] Citizens cannot lawfully be deported, but the record shows the line is not always held in practice: ProPublica counted 506 citizens detained by immigration agents, and about a dozen deported, most of them children. [cite:de32_dn_propublica]

Confined

Here the texts are plain. In "Patchwork 2" of 20 November 2008, after a line about biodiesel that he marked "just kidding," Yarvin set out "a humane alternative to genocide": holding unwanted residents in "permanent solitary confinement," connected to a virtual-reality interface. [cite:de6_tnr_duran] The New Yorker in 2025 summarised the regime he described as one that would "imprison 'decivilized populations.'" [cite:de35_ny_autocracy] What exists is immigration detention on a scale never seen before: $45 billion for detention to 2029, about 90 per cent of detainees held in privately run sites, and a record number of deaths in custody in fiscal 2026 ([scene:de32_the_detention_business|Part Thirty-Four]). [cite:de32_aic_hr1] [cite:de32_trac_private] [cite:de32_npr_deaths] The confined rung also holds the sharpest case abroad. In March and April 2025 the United States sent 252 Venezuelans to El Salvador's CECOT prison. Human Rights Watch and Cristosal documented near-daily beatings, sexual violence and four months of incommunicado detention amounting to enforced disappearance, until their release in a prisoner exchange on 18 July 2025. Their report does not describe forced labour; CECOT belongs on this rung, not the next. [cite:de35_hrw_cecot]

Compelled labour

No text by Yarvin or Land examined for this book calls for forced labour. The passage most often cited as if it did is from "Why Carlyle Matters" of July 2009, in which Yarvin wrote that the "innate character and intelligence of some is more suited to mastery than slavery," and that "a person makes a good slave if he is loyal, patient, and not exceptionally bright or stubborn." [cite:de6_mm_2021] It is a claim, in his own voice, that fitness for servitude is hereditary; it is not a proposal to compel anyone to work, and in 2025 he called abolition "a necessary step." [cite:de6_audit_nyt] The rung is supplied instead by American law. The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted," words adapted from the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. [cite:de35_lii_13th] After the Civil War, every former Confederate state except Virginia leased convicts, most of them Black and many convicted on dubious charges, to mines and plantations; in Alabama their death rate ran about ten times that in states without leasing, and leasing brought in 73 per cent of the state's revenue in 1898. Alabama ended it last, on 1 July 1928. [cite:de35_eh_mancini] The Supreme Court did draw one limit: in Bailey v. Alabama (1911) it held that a state "may not compel one man to labor for another in payment of a debt." [cite:de35_lii_13th]

Today about 800,000 incarcerated people work. Seven states pay nothing for most jobs, others average 15 to 52 cents an hour for non-industry jobs, and 76 per cent of workers surveyed by the ACLU and the University of Chicago reported punishment, such as solitary confinement or lost visits, for refusing. [cite:de35_aclu_captive] Immigration detainees, who are held without criminal conviction, are paid "at least $1 per day" under a rate set by a 1950 law; Congress declined to raise it to $4 in 1982. [cite:de32_pogo_dollar] Courts have resisted that rate. A Washington jury awarded detainees $17.3 million in back pay, and the state $5.9 million more, against GEO Group, which the Ninth Circuit affirmed; GEO's petition, which the Solicitor General urged the Supreme Court to hear, was set for conference on 6 November 2026. [cite:de32_wa_nwauzor] [cite:de32_scotus_25828] [cite:de32_nwauzor_supp] In GEO Group v. Menocal the Court ruled 9–0 on 25 February 2026 that GEO could not appeal early on a contractor defence, sending a forced-labour claim over unpaid cleaning, enforced by solitary confinement, back toward trial. [cite:de32_lii_menocal]

Eight states have removed the exception from their own constitutions: Colorado in 2018, Nebraska and Utah in 2020, Alabama, Oregon, Tennessee and Vermont in 2022, and Nevada in 2024, whose text now reads that "neither Slavery nor involuntary servitude shall ever be tolerated." [cite:de35_ap_13th2022] [cite:de35_ni_q4] Louisiana's measure failed in 2022 after its own sponsors urged a "no" vote over ambiguous wording, and California voters rejected Proposition 6 in 2024 leaving mandatory prison work there at under 74 cents an hour. [cite:de35_ap_13th2022] [cite:de35_cm_prop6] The change in text has not always changed practice, and the federal exception stands. [cite:de35_ap_13th2022]

The ladder therefore has an uneven shape. The theorists are explicit about the top rungs that concern status, removal and confinement, and silent about the middle ones; the record supplies the middle rungs in full, for other purposes, and supplies the last rung through a constitutional exception that predates the theorists by a century and a half. The worst-case extension on that last rung would be compelled work attached to detention that follows no conviction. The $1 rate is the nearest existing thing, and the courts' answer to it was still pending in October 2026. [cite:de32_pogo_dollar] [cite:de32_scotus_25828]
