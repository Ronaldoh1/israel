The Jewish Agency Prepares: Partition as Policy, 1946

In the weeks after the King David Hotel bombing, the Jewish Agency was also at work on a map. The partition plan voted on in November 1947 is usually described as the product of the United Nations' own process: an eleven-member committee that investigated the problem from scratch and reached its recommendation independently. That committee was real, and its conclusions were its own. But one of the parties before it had arrived at partition long before the committee existed. The Jewish Agency had prepared its own partition proposal more than a year before the committee reported, and by then had made partition its declared aim.

Two separate diplomatic tracks lay ahead: recognition by the United States, and a decision at the United Nations. The Agency worked both, and the groundwork for the second was laid early. In August 1946 it submitted its own detailed partition proposal, with boundaries drawn for a Palestinian rump state [cite:jewishagency_1946plan]. Those boundaries bear a documented resemblance to the area that became the West Bank after 1948 [cite:jewishagency_1946plan]. The resemblance is real, and easy to overstate. The source records a likeness between two maps, not a line of descent: it does not show that the United Nations committee, or anyone after it, adopted the Agency's exact lines.

The proposal did not stand alone. Historical accounts of the United Nations process note that by 1946 partition had become the Jewish Agency's avowed, public policy [cite:unscop_testimony]. That is a different thing from a position adopted because an international body demanded one. The Agency did not arrive at partition in reaction to the United Nations. It came to the United Nations with partition already as its stated goal, and with a prepared case for it. The details of what it later presented were not necessarily identical to its 1946 position, and the sources here do not claim they were; what was fixed by 1946 was the commitment itself.

August 1946 was also a turning point in the Agency's other policy, the armed one. According to the heritage association of Lehi, one of the underground groups then allied with the Haganah, the Agency's meeting in Paris in early August 1946 decided to halt armed action until the Zionist Congress met, and the Congress, in Basel, confirmed the halt on 9 December 1946 [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance]. That account, written from Lehi's own perspective, is the only source here for the Paris decision. The sources do not say whether the partition proposal was discussed or adopted at the same Paris meeting, and this book does not join the two. What can be said is that in the same month the record shows the Agency submitting a partition plan, the Lehi account has it standing down from armed struggle until its Congress could meet. The story of that resistance movement is told in [scene:rd5_revolt|the Jewish Resistance Movement].

The timeline makes the central point plain. The Agency's proposal came roughly nine months before the United Nations created its committee, and more than a year before that committee's report. When the committee did hear the Agency in 1947, its head, David Ben-Gurion, told it that an independent Jewish state was the only acceptable outcome, and the Agency presented a prepared map and a large body of supporting material [cite:unscop_testimony]. That hearing, and the Arab Higher Committee's decision to boycott it, are the subject of [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair].

None of this makes the committee's recommendation a copy of the Agency's plan, and the evidence here does not support saying so. It does correct the impression that the United Nations faced a blank page. One party came to it with a position fixed and a proposal already drafted, the product of a diplomatic campaign that had begun before the international process did. Whether that preparation shaped the committee's final lines, and by how much, is not something the sources used here can measure. Nor do they say to whom, exactly, the August 1946 proposal was submitted, or set out its boundaries line by line. Those questions remain open; the date and the policy do not.
