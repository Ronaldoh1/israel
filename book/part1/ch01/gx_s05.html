"Bless Israel and Be Blessed": Genesis 12:3 and the Christian Dispute

The name Israel carries religious weight not only within Judaism but within Christianity, and for a large American constituency that weight bears directly on politics. One position holds that the Bible requires Christians to support the modern State of Israel. The opposite holds that the Bible is irrelevant to the question. The record shows something else: a single verse that Christians read in sharply different ways, with the disagreement running through Christianity itself.

The verse is Genesis 12:3. In it God tells Abram, later Abraham:

"I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse; and all peoples on earth will be blessed through you."

The promise of land follows in Genesis 12:7, 15:18 and 17:8. The words were spoken to Abraham. The question is whom they address today.

The Christian Zionist reading treats the verse as a standing mandate. Groups such as the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem describe it as the biblical basis for standing with Israel.[cite:icej_cz] [cite:rmc_cz] Many dispensationalist believers go further, viewing Israel's restoration as a precondition for the return of Christ. On this reading, the verse applies to the modern state, and support for that state is a religious obligation.

Other Christian traditions read the verse differently. Covenant theology and the theology known as amillennialism hold that the promise to Abraham is fulfilled in Christ and the church, citing Galatians 3:16 and 3:29, and that it does not transfer to a modern government.[cite:studylight_gen12] Some scholars also note that the passage places more emphasis on blessing to all nations than on land.[cite:pctii_gen12] On these readings, the Israel of Genesis and the state founded in 1948, a secular state, are not the same thing.

The dispute became public in June 2025 in an exchange between Senator Ted Cruz and Tucker Carlson. Cruz cited the verse. Carlson asked whether the political state led by Benjamin Netanyahu is the same "Israel" as the one in Scripture.[cite:think_gen12] The exchange settled nothing, but it placed a theological disagreement in open political view.

Judaism, too, contains differing views on the relationship between scripture and the modern state. Early Zionism was largely secular, and some Haredi Jews oppose the existence of a Jewish state before the coming of the Messiah. The belief that a religious text mandates support for a particular political state is therefore not shared uniformly by Christians or by Jews.

Palestinian Christians, about 1 percent of the population today, are also part of this story, and their history is treated separately in this book.

Whether Genesis 12:3 applies to a modern state is a theological question, and it is argued inside Christianity itself. This book does not resolve it. What it establishes is that the verse is cited for opposite conclusions, that the disagreement is ongoing, and that the claim "the Bible requires it" represents one tradition's reading rather than a settled fact.
