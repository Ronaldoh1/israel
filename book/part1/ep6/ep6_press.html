The Press: Profile, Silence, Exposure

For most of two decades the press was part of the machine, and for the last part of it the press was what broke it. Both are on the record.

The cut

In early 2003 Vicky Ward was writing a profile of Epstein for Vanity Fair. She had interviewed Maria and Annie Farmer, sisters who said Epstein and Maxwell had abused them, Annie at sixteen. In the winter of 2003 Epstein visited the magazine's editor, Graydon Carter. The profile, "The Talented Mr. Epstein", appeared in March without the sisters' accounts. [cite:ep6_npr_media2019] Why is disputed. Ward says the allegations were removed after Epstein's visit. Carter says she lacked the three sources the magazine's lawyers required, and later said she "simply didn't have the goods"; a 2022 New Yorker article argued that Ward's own account had changed over time, and Maria Farmer has criticised Ward too. [cite:ep6_mediaite_ward2022] What is not disputed is the result: the first national magazine to hear the Farmers' accounts did not publish them. Afterwards a bullet was left outside Carter's home and, in 2006, a severed cat's head in the yard of his house in Connecticut; there is no evidence that Epstein was involved. [cite:ep6_npr_media2019]

The silence

After the 2008 plea the coverage was thin, and the documents show why that suited the people involved. Records later published by the Miami Herald showed prosecutors working with Epstein's lawyers to limit press attention to the deal; the former Palm Beach police chief Michael Reiter said he had pointed reporters to the evidence and nothing came of it. [cite:ep6_guardian_media2019] In 2011 Ward herself wrote a blog post describing Epstein's "sexual peccadilloes", which she later regretted. In 2010 a dinner at Epstein's house for Prince Andrew included George Stephanopoulos, Katie Couric and Charlie Rose. [cite:ep6_npr_media2019]

Two episodes show the pressure at work. In 2015 ABC News paid for Giuffre to travel to New York and interviewed her for more than an hour; the interview never aired. Alan Dershowitz, one of Epstein's lawyers, called the network: "I did not want to see [Giuffre's] credibility enhanced by ABC." ABC said that "not all of our reporting met our standards to air." And Landon Thomas Jr., who wrote about Epstein for New York magazine in 2002 and later for the New York Times, solicited a $30,000 donation from Epstein for a Harlem children's charity, which a foundation formerly controlled by Epstein paid in 2017. The Times called it "a clear violation" of its policy, and Thomas had left by January 2019. [cite:ep6_npr_media2019] Lawsuits filed by survivors' lawyers in 2015 and 2017 drew little coverage; one of those lawyers, David Boies, said that before the 2019 arrest almost no one but the Miami Herald and the Daily Beast had reported substantively on the case. [cite:ep6_npr_media2019]

The exposure

In late November 2018 the Miami Herald published Julie K. Brown's series on the case. Brown had gone back to the police records, found the detectives, and identified eighty women who said Epstein had abused them, many of them as children. She set out how the federal deal had been made and how the victims had been kept in the dark. [cite:ep6_npr_brown2018] The series led directly to the 2019 federal charges and to the resignation of the man who had approved the deal, Alexander Acosta, by then Secretary of Labor. "He didn't do this alone," Brown wrote. "He had a whole ecosystem that he created that allowed this to happen." [cite:ep6_kpbs_brown2021] She included her own profession: "We need to look at ourselves, too." [cite:ep6_npr_media2019]

The lesson is not that the press failed or succeeded. A regional paper with limited resources did in one series what national outlets with far more had not done in fifteen years, and it did so by doing the slow thing: reading the records and finding the women.
