The Thesis and the Test

The thesis this part tests is that Hezbollah, the Houthis and the other armed groups aligned with Iran serve as a standing reason for continuing wars, for continuing military aid to Israel and for drawing the United States into the region's wars. The Hamas volume ran the same test on its own subject, setting the claims made in Hamas's name against what the record later showed. [scene:hm11_claims|The Hamas Architecture: claims made in Hamas's name] The method here is the same. Three kinds of statement are kept apart: documented facts, which rest on a document, an order, a law or an official's own words; patterns, which can be shown by setting what was claimed beside what followed; and inferences about motive, which are arguments, are labelled as arguments, and are given with the case against them.

The Bomb That Was About a Year Away

The largest threat named in this story is Iran itself, the state whose Revolutionary Guards trained Hezbollah's first fighters and which funds and arms most of the groups in this volume. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018] [cite:hz10_un_poe2024] In September 2012 Benjamin Netanyahu told the UN General Assembly, as the reports on the leak summarised his speech, that Iran was about one year from being able to build a nuclear weapon. A Mossad assessment dated the following month, shared with South Africa's intelligence service and published in the leaked "Spy Cables" by The Guardian and Al Jazeera in February 2015, said Iran "is not performing the activity necessary to produce weapons" and "does not appear to be ready to enrich" its uranium to higher levels. [cite:hz11_rferl_spycables] [cite:hz11_wiki_spycables] The two documents can be read in more than one way. The Times of Israel argued at the time that they were "hardly contradictory": the full Mossad passage described an Iran still closing gaps toward a weapon, and Netanyahu's deadline concerned enough enriched uranium for a bomb, not a finished bomb, so the disagreement was over speed rather than direction. [cite:hz11_toi_spycables]

The 2012 deadline was the latest of several. In 1992, as a member of the Knesset, Netanyahu is reported to have said Iran was three to five years from being able to produce a nuclear weapon and that the threat had to be "uprooted by an international front headed by the U.S."; the retrospectives that repeat the remark trace it to a newspaper timeline, and this book did not find the Knesset record itself. [cite:hz11b_week_1992] [cite:hz11b_intercept_wolf] [cite:hz11b_aje_rhetoric] His 1995 book Fighting Terrorism gave a similar short estimate, though accounts of its wording differ: one quotes it as placing Iran "between three and five years away from possessing the prerequisites required," another as "five to seven years at most." [cite:hz11b_mw_book] [cite:hz11b_week_1992] On 10 July 1996, as prime minister, he told a joint session of Congress that Iran was "the most dangerous" of the region's regimes, that its acquiring nuclear weapons "could presage catastrophic consequences," and that the deadline for stopping it was "getting extremely close." [cite:hz11b_jvl_1996] On 12 September 2002, out of office, he told a House committee that Saddam Hussein "is seeking and is working and is advancing toward the development of nuclear weapons," that Iraq and Iran were vying to be first to the bomb, and that removing Saddam "will have enormous positive reverberations on the region"; asked about Iran, he said, "I don't know that Iran has nuclear weapons." [cite:hz11b_gpo_2002] The Iraq Survey Group reported in October 2004 that Saddam had ended Iraq's nuclear programme after the 1991 Gulf War and held no stockpiles of banned weapons when the invasion began. [cite:hz11b_cnn_duelfer] A US embassy cable of February 2009, later published by WikiLeaks, recorded Netanyahu telling visiting members of Congress that Iran was "probably one or two years away." [cite:hz11b_intercept_wolf] [cite:hz11b_week_1992] At the UN on 27 September 2012 he asked for a "clear red line" before Iran finished the second stage of enrichment, which, by the UN's own summary of the speech, he said it could do by the following summer at the latest. [cite:hz11b_un_2012] None of those dates brought an Iranian bomb. Israel's stated case rested on two things the record does support: Iran's enrichment, which kept growing, and its concealment. On 30 April 2018 Netanyahu presented what he said were about 55,000 pages and 183 discs from Iran's secret "Project Amad" weapons programme, taken by Israeli intelligence from a Tehran warehouse, and said they proved Iran had lied. The documents appeared to date from the early 2000s, and he showed no direct evidence that Iran had broken the 2015 nuclear agreement; Iran, which denies ever seeking nuclear weapons, called the presentation a propaganda show, and its foreign minister called Netanyahu "the boy who can't stop crying wolf." [cite:hz11b_cbs_archive]

American intelligence assessments over the same years ran on a separate track. The National Intelligence Estimate of December 2007 judged "with high confidence" that Iran had halted its covert nuclear weapons programme in the autumn of 2003, and with moderate confidence that it had not restarted it by mid-2007; the White House, releasing the finding, said the risk of Iran acquiring a weapon "remains a very serious problem." [cite:hz11_wh_nie2007] [cite:hz11_npr_nie2007] A report compiled in November 2024 said Iran "is not building a nuclear weapon" but had "undertaken activities that better position it to produce one, if it so chooses." On 26 March 2025 the Director of National Intelligence, Tulsi Gabbard, told the House Intelligence Committee that the intelligence community "continues to assess that Iran is not building a nuclear weapon" and that Ali Khamenei "has not authorized the nuclear weapons program he suspended in 2003." In the same testimony she said Iran's enriched-uranium stockpile was at its highest level, "unprecedented for a state without nuclear weapons," and the head of US Central Command told Congress that Iran could produce enough material for ten weapons in three weeks. [cite:hz11_bg_gabbard] [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] [cite:hz11_pbs_gabbard]

In June 2025, as Israel and Iran fought what became the twelve-day war, President Trump was asked about Gabbard's assessment and said, "I don't care what she said," adding that Iran was "very close" to a bomb; Netanyahu had described a nuclear-armed Iran as an imminent threat. Iran maintained that its programme was peaceful. [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] [cite:hz11_pbs_gabbard] The United States fired between 100 and 150 of its THAAD interceptors defending Israel in that war, about a quarter of its supply. [cite:ow7_cnn_thaad2025] On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel struck Iran together, and Trump said the aim was to remove imminent threats. [cite:ow7_npr_epicfury]

The pattern the record shows has two halves. The specific claim, a weapon within about a year, was not borne out on its own timeline: more than twelve years after the UN speech, America's intelligence agencies still assessed that Iran was not building one. The broader claim, that Iran was building the capacity to make a weapon quickly if it chose to, was supported by the same agencies' own findings about its stockpile. Which of the two halves drove the decisions to go to war in 2025 and 2026 is a question of motive, and Part Twelve takes it up. [scene:hz12_wars|Dragging America in]

The Threat in the Budget

The threat posed by Iran and its allies is also written into the laws that fund Israel's military. In April 2024 Congress passed the Israel Security Supplemental Appropriations Act, which totalled $26.38 billion and provided, among other items, $3.5 billion in Foreign Military Financing, $4 billion for the Iron Dome and David's Sling missile-defence systems and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser system; [cite:hz12b_hac_onepager] the House approved it 366 to 58 on 20 April, the Senate 79 to 18 three days later, and it became law on 24 April. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] [cite:ow1_clerk_roll152] [cite:ow1_lobby_hr815] Brown University's Costs of War project counted at least $21.7 billion in US military aid to Israel in the two years after 7 October 2023, plus $9.65 billion to $12.07 billion spent on American operations in the region, including against the Houthis. [cite:hm_an_costsofwar] In February 2025 the administration used an emergency declaration to approve about $4 billion in arms sales to Israel without the usual congressional review period. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

The fire those sums answered was real, and it ran both ways. Between 7 October 2023 and 18 October 2024 the monitoring group ACLED counted 13,608 cross-border attacks between Israel and Lebanon: 2,370 by Hezbollah and allied groups, which killed at least 41 people in Israel, and 11,238 by Israel, which killed at least 2,318 people in Lebanon. [cite:hz7_aj_acled_year] The threat that the missile-defence money was voted to meet existed; it was also, by this count, less than a fifth of the cross-border fire.

Whether the Wars Ended the Threat

Israel's stated aim in its Lebanese campaigns was security for the north: in 1993 Rabin told the Knesset the goal was to "restore security to the lives of residents in communities of the north," and in September 2024 Yoav Gallant made the return of the north's displaced residents the task. [cite:hz4_jvl_rabin93] [cite:hz7_afp_gravity] Each campaign was followed, sooner or later, by the next. Hezbollah itself was founded during the 1982 invasion and the occupation that followed it, which Yitzhak Rabin, as defence minister, warned in February 1985 risked replacing PLO attacks with Shiite ones. [scene:hz2_founding|The founding] [cite:hz3_djn_rabin85] When the Taif Agreement of 1989 required Lebanon's militias to disarm, Hezbollah was allowed to keep its weapons as a "resistance force" against Israel's occupation of the south. [cite:hz4_wiki_taif] The 2006 war, set out in Part Five, did not end its arsenal; on 8 October 2023 it was able to open a front against Israel "in solidarity" with Gaza. [scene:hz5_war|The 2006 war] [cite:hz7_aj_8oct] In September 2024 Israel removed much of its chain of command in about ten days. [cite:hm_ns_hezbollah] Announcing the ceasefire that followed, President Biden said Hezbollah's infrastructure "will not be allowed to be rebuilt"; by October 2025 US Central Command said the Lebanese army had removed some 10,000 rockets and 400 missiles, and the Israeli army said it had killed more than 370 members of armed groups in the truce's first year. [cite:hz8_biden_dcpd] [cite:hz8_scr_nov25] [cite:hz8_idf_year] In January 2026 Netanyahu's office called the Lebanese army's progress "far from sufficient" and said "Hezbollah must be fully disarmed." [cite:hz8_cnn_phase1] On 2 March 2026, two days after Khamenei was killed, Hezbollah fired missiles and drones at Israel again; in the six weeks that followed, Israeli strikes killed 2,294 people in Lebanon by the health ministry's provisional count. [cite:hz8_am_mar2] [cite:hz8_olj_toll] Part Eight sets out where that war stood as of October 2026. [scene:hz8_war2026|The 2026 war]

The pattern is not that the threats were invented. Hezbollah's rockets fell on Israeli towns and Iran's stockpile grew. It is that the threat was repeatedly described as nearer, larger or closer to removal than the record later bore out, while each campaign launched to remove it was followed by another.
