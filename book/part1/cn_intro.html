Most people who own a Bible have never asked who decided what goes in it. The question sounds as if it has one answer: a council, a date, a list. The record gives a longer answer, and a more revealing one. Over about fourteen centuries, from a shipowner's break with the church of Rome in 144 to the Council of Trent's vote of 8 April 1546, a series of named men drew the line between scripture and everything else. They were bishops, emperors, popes and the sons and nephews of the families who bought, inherited or fought over the papacy. Then, for four more centuries, until 14 June 1966, the same institution kept a list of the books its members were forbidden to read. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] [cite:cn_va_index1966]

The companion volume, The Vatican Architecture, already sets out which books were kept and which were left out, document by document. [scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|How the Bible was made] This book asks a different question. At each point where the canon was fixed, enforced or defended, who held the pen, what did they gain by holding it, and who paid? The answer runs through money as often as through theology: an emperor's tax exemptions, a bishop's estates, a forged imperial grant, a banker's loan repaid by selling forgiveness, a pope's nephew strangled in a castle. It also runs through families, and the book is careful about what those families held and what they did not.

The documents come first throughout. Where a claim about the canon is commonly made and does not hold, the book says so. Symbolic and esoteric readings of the story appear only at the end, in a section marked as a reading.
