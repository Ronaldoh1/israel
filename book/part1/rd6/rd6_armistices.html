The Rhodes Armistices, 1949: Lines That Were Not Borders

The war of 1948 did not end with a peace. It ended with four separate agreements, signed between February and July 1949, each of which declared in its own text that it settled nothing political. The lines they drew lasted until 1967 and became known as the Green Line. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] Because the lines lasted so long, they are often remembered as the borders the war produced. The documents themselves say the opposite, and they say it repeatedly.

The man who brought the parties to the table was Ralph Bunche, who had succeeded the assassinated UN mediator, Count Bernadotte. In January 1949 he brought Israeli and Egyptian delegations to negotiate on the island of Rhodes. [cite:rs_rd6_nobel_bunche] The mediation he inherited had already cost lives: in July 1949 he reported to the Security Council that ten members of the UN mission, the Mediator among them, had died over fourteen months, and that about twice as many had been wounded. [cite:rs_rd6_bunche_letter_1949] The plans Bernadotte had put forward, and the killing that ended his mission, are told in [scene:rd6_bernadotte|the mediator's mission]. Bunche's task was narrower than Bernadotte's had been. He was not drawing a settlement. He was stopping a war.

The first agreement, between Egypt and Israel, was signed at Rhodes on 24 February 1949. Mahmed Seif El Dine and M. K. El Raliniany signed for Egypt; Walter Eytan, Yigael Yadin and Elias Sasson signed for Israel. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] Its operative terms dealt with three places. Egyptian forces encircled in the Al Faluja pocket were to withdraw beyond the Egypt–Palestine frontier under UN supervision, a movement that Annex I set to begin at 0500 GMT on 26 February 1949. The village of El Auja and its surroundings were demilitarized under Article VIII. In the Gaza–Rafah area, Article VI froze Egyptian forces at the positions they held. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] The Gaza Strip, which Egypt held under the armistice, became the territory whose administration is described in the section on Arab Palestine after the war. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map]

The Egyptian text is also the clearest statement of what an armistice was meant to be. Article IV(3) says that its provisions are "dictated exclusively by military considerations" and are valid only for the period of the armistice. Article V says that the demarcation line "is not to be construed in any sense as a political or territorial boundary," and that it is drawn without prejudice to the rights of either party in the ultimate settlement. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] Supervision was given to a seven-member Mixed Armistice Commission: three members appointed by each party, with the Chief of Staff of the UN Truce Supervision Organization in the chair, and its headquarters at El Auja. The agreement was to remain in force "until a peaceful settlement between the Parties is achieved" (Article XII). [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text]

The structure explains who could act and who could not. Neither side could change the line by itself without breaking a signed agreement; neither side gave up its claims by signing; and the body set up to supervise the agreement was a commission on which the two parties had equal numbers and a UN officer held the chair. The agreement froze the military facts. It left every political question exactly where the war had left it.

Lebanon had entered the war by its own government's decision. On 15 May 1948, the day after Israel declared independence, Lebanon joined Egypt, Syria, Jordan and Iraq in declaring war and invading the new state, fielding the smallest of the five Arab armies. Israeli forces repelled the invasion and temporarily occupied part of southern Lebanon. The armistice signed on 23 March 1949 restored the internationally recognized border between the two states. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] The decision to fight was not simply imposed on Lebanon from outside. According to Makram Rabah, a historian at the American University of Beirut, the country's founding leaders, chiefly Riad al-Solh and Bechara el-Khoury, who had led it to independence from France in 1943, had debated and rejected an alliance with the Zionist movement before 1948, concluding that they could not keep relations with the neighbouring Arab states if they made one. [cite:lebanon_1948_war]

The armistice returned the frontier to where it had been. It did not return the people who had crossed it. During the war an estimated 100,000–110,000 Palestinian Arabs, the large majority from northern Palestine, including the Acre area, fled or were displaced into Lebanon, a country of about 1.3 million people at the time. The International Committee of the Red Cross, and from 1950 UNRWA, ran the camps that received them. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy]

The agreement with the Hashemite Jordan Kingdom was signed at Rhodes on 3 April 1949. Colonel Ahmed Sudki El-Jundi and Lieutenant-Colonel Mohamed Maayte signed for Jordan; Reuven Shiloah and Lieutenant-Colonel Moshe Dayan signed for Israel. Article II(2) repeats the reservation of the Egyptian text: no provision shall prejudice the rights, claims and positions of either party in the final settlement. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text]

Two features set this agreement apart. The first was Jerusalem. Article VIII set up a Special Committee whose agreed agenda included free access to the Holy Places and cultural institutions, use of the cemetery on the Mount of Olives, the resumption of the institutions on Mount Scopus, and free movement on the Bethlehem road and the Latrun–Jerusalem road. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text] That agenda was written into the text. Whether it was carried out is another matter: according to Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education, Jews were denied access to their holy sites in the eastern part of the city that Jordan held after the war. [cite:rs_rd6_bpb_jerusalem] The fate of the city at the United Nations is taken up in the section on Israel's admission and Jerusalem.

The second feature was the Iraqi front. Iraq's army had fought in 1948 and held a sector of the line. Under Article VI, Jordanian forces replaced the Iraqi forces on that front, and the Wadi Ara area passed to Israel. The villagers there were to keep "full rights" of residence, property and freedom; those who chose to leave could take their movable property and were to be compensated for their land. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text] Here a transfer of inhabited territory came with written guarantees for the people living on it. How those guarantees fared under the regime later imposed on Arab citizens is a question for the section on military government.

The last agreement, with Syria, was signed on 20 July 1949 and completed the Rhodes armistice negotiations. [cite:rs_rd6_nobel_bunche] It was negotiated under a new regime: Syria's talks with Israel opened on 5 April 1949, days after Husni al-Za'im seized power in a coup, and the agreement turned the areas Syria evacuated into demilitarized zones that became the setting of two decades of clashes. [cite:tf_zaim] Both the negotiation and its aftermath are told in [scene:rd6_syria_1949|Syria in 1949].

The next day, 21 July 1949, Bunche wrote to the Security Council. He listed the four agreements by their UN document numbers: S/1264 (Egypt), S/1296 (Lebanon), S/1302 (Transjordan) and S/1353 (Syria). He called them "agreements voluntarily entered into by the parties," each of which incorporated "what amounts to a non-aggression pact." He noted that the Transjordan agreement also covered the Iraqi front, so that separate negotiations between Iraq and Israel were unnecessary. And he concluded that, with the armistices signed, "the mission of the Mediator has been fulfilled." [cite:rs_rd6_bunche_letter_1949] Bunche received the Nobel Peace Prize for 1950 and accepted it in Oslo on 10 December 1950. [cite:rs_rd6_nobel_bunche]

Set side by side, the four texts follow one pattern with local variations. Each froze a military position; each reserved the final settlement; each was built around the particular ground in dispute on that front.

Two Arab states that had taken part in the war signed nothing. Iraq never signed an armistice with Israel; its front was handed over to Jordan under the Jordanian agreement, which Bunche judged sufficient. [cite:rs_rd6_bunche_letter_1949] [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] Iraq's policies toward its own Jewish citizens in the same months, including the law of July 1948, are told in [scene:rd6_exodus|the section on the Jews of the Arab world].

Saudi Arabia never signed either. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] Its part in the fighting had been different in kind from its neighbours'. It did not launch a direct invasion in 1948; it sent a limited contingent of troops placed under Egyptian command, a modest commitment that the record attributes to its greater distance from Palestine. Saudi Arabia never established diplomatic relations with Israel, and for decades it barred entry to anyone holding an Israeli passport or a passport with an Israeli stamp. Instead of a military front, it took part in an economic one. The Arab League's boycott of Israel was formalized in 1948 and barred member states from any direct economic relationship with Israel. A secondary boycott, added in 1950, extended the ban to foreign companies doing business with Israel, and a tertiary boycott later targeted companies that did business with firms on the secondary list. Saudi Arabia was among the boycott's most consistent participants and maintained it in some form for over seven decades. The armistices ended the shooting on four fronts; the boycott carried the conflict into trade.

The new lines enclosed far more territory than the United Nations had proposed for the Jewish state. The 1947 partition plan had allotted that state 56% of the land, as the panel below shows. Israel's Foreign Ministry describes the territory inside the 1949 armistice demarcation lines as "about 78% of the Mandate area." [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map]

The same Foreign Ministry page states the limits of that territory as plainly as the treaties do. The agreements, it notes, were meant to be interim until replaced by peace treaties. The lines became known as the Green Line. Jordan held the West Bank and Egypt the Gaza Strip, and Iraq and Saudi Arabia never signed agreements with Israel. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] The difference between 56% and about 78% is the measure of what the fighting of 1948 changed on the ground. The texts of 1949 are the measure of what it did not change in law: by their own words, nothing in them settled where Israel's borders lay.

The year-by-year ledger of this period sets the armistices beside the other events of 1949: Israel's admission to the United Nations, the Lausanne conference and the creation of UNRWA. In the ledger's terms, 1949 was the year of consolidation, and the year the talks on return failed.

The common belief that the Green Line was Israel's agreed border in 1949 does not survive a reading of the texts. Article V of the Egyptian agreement and Article II(2) of the Jordanian one deny it in terms. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text] What the texts leave open is how much weight the line should carry afterwards, and here the two sides' readings diverge. At Lausanne a few months later, Israel held that the 1947 partition plan had been nullified by the Arab invasion; the Arab delegations, for their part, wanted the 1947 lines and demanded that Israel first accept the return of the refugees. The final frontiers remained, on both positions, open for the settlement the agreements anticipated. Bunche's own description is more modest than either: a set of voluntary agreements amounting to non-aggression pacts, nothing more and nothing less. [cite:rs_rd6_bunche_letter_1949]

What the record here does not settle is how the machinery the agreements created actually worked in the years that followed. The sources used for this section describe the Egyptian Mixed Armistice Commission in detail but not the commissions on the other fronts, and they do not give the commissions' rulings or the number of complaints they heard. Whether the Special Committee for Jerusalem ever met to any effect on its agenda is not documented here either; the only evidence on that point is the report that access to the holy sites in the eastern city was denied. The armistices were written as a bridge to peace treaties. None of those treaties came in the period this chapter covers, and the bridge became, for eighteen years, the only structure there was.
