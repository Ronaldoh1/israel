The Uganda Test: Could the Project Have Moved?

This section runs a thought experiment, and it should be read as one. Suppose the Sixth Zionist Congress had gone the other way in 1903, and a Jewish state had been built in British East Africa or in Argentina. What would it have been called, and would the structures later built on the name "Israel" still have worked? No document can answer a question about a history that never happened. What the documents can do is test one assumption that is often made without testing: that geography was incidental, and that the name and the theology later gathered around the state could have attached themselves to whatever territory the movement settled. Put to the record, that assumption does not hold up well. The record does not settle the counterfactual, but it shows which parts of the project depended on one particular place and which did not.

The section is therefore built from documented pieces: what Theodor Herzl wrote in 1896, what the Congress did in 1903 and 1905, what Genesis 15:18 says, and what Cyrus Scofield's prophetic notes require. The argument assembled from them is an argument, offered as such.

What Herzl wrote in 1896. In Der Judenstaat Herzl put the choice of territory as an open question: "Shall we choose Palestine or Argentine? We shall take what is given us, and what is selected by Jewish public opinion." He noted that Argentina's government would "gladly provide" Jews with land.[cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] His framework was, in the first instance, a political answer to European antisemitism, and Herzl himself was a secular Jew. One account of the text holds that he considered Argentina first and Palestine second, and that when he did prefer Palestine, it was because it would give "the Jewish problem" a solution "not only just, but brilliant," a practical and political reason (Palestine would mobilize Jewish public opinion) rather than a religious one. The Israeli peace activist and writer Uri Avnery went further, judging that Herzl "had obviously thought very little about Palestine as a concrete entity" and had inserted it into his original blueprint as an afterthought.[cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896]

The same pamphlet also sets the two territories side by side in different terms. Argentina offered practical advantages, fertile land and a sparse population. Palestine was "our ever-memorable historic home... a force of marvelous potency."[cite:herzl_judenstaat] Read this way, Herzl named, seven years before anyone offered him East Africa, the very difference the Uganda dispute would turn on: one territory had only practical merits, the other carried a weight no practical merit could replace. Both readings rest on the same 1896 text. They differ on emphasis: whether Palestine's pull, for Herzl, was mainly its power to rally Jewish opinion or its historic charge. Neither reading claims that every later leader or delegate shared Herzl's reasons. His words are evidence of his own stated view, not of a consensus.

What the Congress did, 1903–1905. At the Sixth Zionist Congress in August 1903 Herzl laid the British offer of land in British East Africa, known as the "Uganda Scheme" although the territory was not in Uganda, before the delegates as a temporary refuge for Jews fleeing persecution. One account gives the vote to send an exploratory commission as 295 to 177.[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The account followed elsewhere in this book gives 295 in favor and 178 against, with 98 abstentions, and records that members of the Russian delegation walked out arguing that the plan violated the Basel Program.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] The sources differ by one vote. The first account describes the minority as mainly Russian and Eastern European delegates who had lived through the worst of the pogroms, and reads their walkout as a statement that they were not looking for any land; they were returning to Zion.[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] Herzl died in 1904, and the Seventh Zionist Congress formally rejected the scheme in 1905.[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The vote, the walkout and the context of the Kishinev pogrom are told in full in [scene:s04e|the section on 1903].

Zionist historical scholarship describes two broad strands that the dispute brought into the open. "Territorial Zionism," sometimes called Ugandism, held that any safe territory would serve: the purpose was refuge from persecution, and location came second to safety. "Zion Zionism" held that only Palestine, the biblical Land of Israel, Eretz Yisrael, would serve, because the purpose was not refuge alone but national restoration to an ancestral homeland. The debate of 1903–1905 settled the question in favor of Zion.[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The conclusion this account draws is that even secular political Zionism could not override the religious and historical attachment of the Jewish masses to that land; for most supporters, location was not interchangeable.

What the scripture names. The land-promise on which the later theology rests is specific. Genesis 15:18, in one translation, reads:

"On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, 'To your offspring I give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates.'" [cite:genesis_15_18]

Another rendering has "I have given this land" and "the Euphrates river."[cite:genesis_1518_text] Other texts add detail. Deuteronomy 11:24 reads: "Every place on which the sole of your foot treads shall be yours; your territory shall be from the wilderness, from Lebanon, from the river, the Euphrates river, to the western sea." Numbers 34 describes specific borders. Together these passages describe a territory in the Levant bounded by the Euphrates to the northeast, the Nile (or Wadi el-Arish) to the southwest, Lebanon to the north and the Mediterranean to the west.[cite:genesis_1518_text] A promise naming a bounded river-to-river territory is a different kind of promise from a blessing on wherever a people comes to live, and that specificity is what makes the test possible at all. It does not settle how the verse ought to be read. Non-literal, allegorical reading of scripture is an ancient and formally recognized tradition, not a modern alternative to the literal one.[cite:genesis_15_18] The test concerns only the literal reading, because that is the reading later Christian Zionism adopted.

The Scofield test. Scofield's dispensationalism, set out in the notes of his annotated Bible (told in [scene:s05|the section on the Scofield Bible]), arranges prophecy into an ordered sequence. Every step after the second depends on the regathering happening at the place the earlier covenant named, Zion and Jerusalem.[cite:wiki_scofield_bible]

A state in East Africa calling itself Israel would not satisfy "Israel regathered" in the sense the sequence's own logic requires. It would not be a variant of the same prophecy but a different claim, one Scofield's notes never made. On this argument, the 30 to 40 million Christian Zionists who later grew out of his framework exist because the state that formed stood where their theology pointed; move it, and there is a serious case that this constituency, and the sustained American political support it supplied, would never have formed.[cite:wiki_scofield_bible] The argument concerns what the theology's own text requires. It is not a claim about what every believer has consciously reasoned through.

The Greater Israel argument. A second line of argument, made elsewhere in this library, pushes the same logic further. It holds that the idea of Greater Israel, Eretz Yisrael HaShlema, the "Complete Land of Israel," is a biblical concept that can exist only because the state was established in the biblical Land of Israel. In Argentina or East Africa, it reasons, five things would have been missing: the biblical land-promise framework, since Genesis 15:18 has no Argentine equivalent; the narrative of return to an ancestral homeland, since Jews had no historical claim to Argentina or East Africa; the concept of the Complete Land of Israel itself; the Revisionist Zionist claim to "both banks of the Jordan"; and the religious-nationalist settler ideology that draws explicitly on biblical texts about Levantine territory.[cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] [cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The argument's central step is that the same attachment to the land that made Argentina or Uganda unacceptable in 1903–1905 is the attachment that later generates territorial maximalism, so that the founding choice of Palestine already posed the question of the land's "completeness." As the argument itself puts it, "you cannot have the one without the other."[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] Those later claims belong to later periods and are followed in [scene:gia_src_s01|the Greater Israel question].

A symbolic reading, tested the same way. The opening of this library sets out one symbolic interpretation of the name, reading "Israel" as IS-RA-EL: a state of consciousness a person arrives at, rather than a bloodline or a border. That is an interpretation, not a documented finding, and it is labeled as such wherever it appears. Put to the same counterfactual, even this reading draws its resonance from places named in the text: Jerusalem, the Temple, and Peniel, where Jacob wrestled. A political entity in East Africa that borrowed the word would not carry that resonance by borrowing it.[cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] The literalist theology and the symbolic reading disagree about almost everything. On this one narrow point they converge: the place mattered, not only the word. The convergence goes no further, and it does not mean the symbolic reading endorses any of the literalist theology's other claims.

What would have travelled, and what would not. Fairness requires saying what a move would not have destroyed. The movement's financial and political institutions, among them the Jewish Colonial Trust and the Jewish National Fund, and later bodies such as AZEC, were built to fund and organize settlement, and nothing in their design tied them to one territory. Before Herzl, real settlement infrastructure had in fact been built in Argentina under the JCA. That machinery could have unloaded anywhere. What could not have travelled was the theological and symbolic layer.

If the test holds, it changes how the outcome of 1905 should be understood. Palestine was not merely the sentimental favorite among roughly equal choices. For at least one major source of the movement's eventual support, it was close to a structural requirement: the only site compatible with the theological and symbolic machinery that later supplied decades of American political and financial backing.

The other side. There is a strong case against reading too much into this. The familiar account holds that Zionism was first of all a response to European antisemitism, a movement for Jewish self-determination and safety; that the choice of Palestine was contested inside the movement, with some leaders open to other places; and that the eventual choice reflected the deep historical and religious attachment of most Jews to the biblical Land of Israel. Much of that account is borne out by the same record. The East Africa offer was a real offer of refuge, debated in good faith and voted on; those who walked out in 1903 were delegates from the communities that had suffered the worst pogroms, and what moved them was attachment to Zion, not a program of borders. The same source that builds the Greater Israel argument says plainly that the internal debate "was resolved by religious and historical attachment to the specific land, not by territorial maximalism."[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] That attachment to the land is a precondition of the later maximalist claims is well supported. That the attachment by itself produced those claims is the argument's own inference, and it needs the later history, not the events of 1903–1905, to bear it out. The Scofield test, likewise, bears on the support the state later received from Christians; it does not explain the choice the Zionist delegates themselves made.

What the record cannot settle. No one knows what the founders would actually have named a state in East Africa, or how a Christian constituency would have responded to it, because those things never happened and cannot be documented. The movement did seriously consider East Africa; that is on record. What is offered here is a structural test applied to documented facts, and its answer is narrow: the theological apparatus examined in this book depended on the place, not only on the name. Whether Herzl's own preference was chiefly political or chiefly historic remains a matter of reading the same pages two ways.
