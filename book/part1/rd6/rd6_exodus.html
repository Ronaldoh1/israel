The Jews of the Arab World After 1948

The war of 1948 produced refugees in two directions. About 700,000 Palestinians were displaced and not allowed to return. In the years that followed, the large Jewish communities of Iraq, Yemen, Libya, Egypt, Syria and North Africa shrank dramatically, through emigration, pressure, expulsion and state persecution in proportions that varied from country to country. [cite:w_jewish_exodus_arab] Estimates of the total who left Arab countries and Iran between 1948 and the 1970s commonly fall in the range of 800,000 to 900,000 people. [cite:w_jewish_exodus_arab] The historian Philip Mendes counts nearly one million Jews, Mizrahim, who left Arab countries in the decade or so after 1947–48, most of them for Israel. [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010] The two figures cover different spans and different sets of countries.

A familiar framing treats 1948 either as a moment after which nothing in the region changed, or as the source of every problem that followed. The record supports neither. The revolts of the 1920s and 1930s show that instability was already there; 1948 deepened and redirected it. What 1948 added was a permanent refugee question, a state of war, and a powerful political cause. The governments that lost the war were discredited, as armies and publics blamed their rulers: Syria's government fell to a military coup in March 1949, Egypt's monarchy in 1952, and Iraq's in 1958. [cite:w_1949_syria_coup] [cite:w_1952_egypt] [cite:w_1958_iraq] For the Jewish communities living under those governments, the years after 1948 were the years in which a long presence came to an end. What follows takes the countries one at a time, because the causes were not the same in each.

Iraq. Iraq's Jewish community was a community of some 2,800 years' standing. [cite:govil_aliyah] After the United Nations voted for partition in November 1947, anti-Jewish measures in Iraq escalated, and pogroms gave way to systematic confiscation of property and trumped-up prosecutions. On 15 May 1948, the day after Israel declared independence, Iraq joined the invasion. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] [cite:govil_aliyah] When the fighting ended, Iraq refused to sign an armistice with Israel; Israeli government sources describe it as the only Arab combatant to refuse, leaving a formal state of war that, by their account, has technically never ended. [cite:govil_aliyah] Saudi Arabia, whose limited contingent had fought under Egyptian command, signed no armistice either. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] The armistice talks themselves are told in [scene:rd6_armistices|the Rhodes armistices].

On 19 July 1948 Iraq made "Zionism" a crime punishable by up to seven years in prison. Since every Jew was presumed to be a Zionist, the law worked as a criminalization of the whole community. The testimony of two Muslim witnesses was enough to convict, and there was little avenue for appeal. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] Two months later the law found its most public victim. On 23 September 1948 Shafiq Ades, a prominent and well-connected Jewish businessman, was hanged in public in Basra on charges of selling weapons to Israel and to the Iraqi Communist Party. His body was left hanging for hours as a spectacle. The case is widely cited as the one that broke the community's remaining sense of security, convincing many that no one, however powerful, was safe. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] [cite:govil_aliyah]

In March 1950 Iraq passed a law allowing Jews to emigrate if they renounced their citizenship. It was framed as permission to leave. In practice it worked as a mechanism to strip Jewish citizens of nationality and then of property once they had registered: those who signed up were denaturalized and their assets taken. [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] Israel and the Near East Transport Company flew the community out, first by way of Cyprus and later on direct flights from Baghdad to Lod. The airlift, named Operation Ezra and Nehemiah after the biblical leaders of the return from Babylonian exile, was largely funded by the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, at a cost of about $4 million. [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:govil_aliyah] By 1952, according to Israeli government sources, a community of roughly 135,000 had been reduced to about 6,000. [cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] Other sources count differently: the Jewish Agency gives 121,512 of Iraq's 130,000 Jews as arriving in Israel, and a table drawn from the Israel State Archives gives 124,226 immigrants from Iraq between 1948 and the end of 1952. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852]

Between April 1950 and June 1951, during exactly the months when the denaturalization law was driving mass registration, at least five bombing incidents struck Jewish institutions and gathering points in Baghdad, including the Masuda Shemtov synagogue. At least four Jews were killed and others were injured. [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] Who set the bombs is a genuinely unresolved question, and it is presented here as one. The Iraqi government of the time, and later some historians, attributed them to Zionist underground agents trying to panic the community into leaving faster. Israeli officials have consistently and firmly denied any involvement, calling the accusation an antisemitic fabrication meant to shift the blame for a persecution the Iraqi state had itself created through pogroms, denaturalization and criminalization. [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:meforum_exodus] Iraq tried and executed men for the bombings: two, Basri and Shalom, were hanged in January 1952. The fairness of those trials, held amid intense anti-Zionist fervour, and the evidence on which they rested are themselves disputed. Independent historical work, drawing on newly released Israeli, British and American archives as reviewed by the Middle East Forum, finds insufficient evidence to confirm Israeli involvement, without fully closing the question. [cite:meforum_exodus]

The bombings were real, the deaths were real, and so were the prosecutions and executions. What the available record does not show is definitive proof of who was responsible. Both the Iraqi government's accusation and Israel's denial are documented positions, and the evidence does not decide between them. What is not in dispute is the legal sequence that preceded them: the 1948 law that made the community presumptively criminal, the hanging of Ades, and the 1950 law that turned emigration into the loss of citizenship and property.

Yemen. The Jews of Yemen had lived there for well over a thousand years, one of the oldest Jewish communities in the world. [cite:w_magic_carpet] Between June 1949 and September 1950, Operation Magic Carpet, also called "On Wings of Eagles", airlifted about 49,000 of them to Israel. It was organized with the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee and flown by Alaska Airlines and other carriers. Many reached the airlift only after walking for weeks to the British colony of Aden. [cite:w_magic_carpet] The counts differ here too. The Jewish Agency says that nearly all of Yemen's 45,000 Jews came, and the State Archives table gives 48,375 immigrants from Yemen and Aden together between 1948 and 1952. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] In Israel many of the newcomers were placed in transit camps, where hundreds of children were reported dead or vanished, the subject of [scene:mz_s02|the Yemenite Children Affair]. [cite:w_magic_carpet]

Morocco. Morocco's Jewish community was rooted over two millennia and numbered in the hundreds of thousands in the 1940s, one of the largest in the Arab world. [cite:w_yachin] On 7 and 8 June 1948, three weeks after Israel's founding, riots against Jews broke out in Oujda and Jérada, killing 5 Jews in Oujda and 38 people in Jérada, with more than a hundred wounded. According to the record compiled by JewishGen, the riots are linked to young Jews crossing the border near Oujda on their way to France and Israel. [cite:rs_rd6_jewishgen_oujda] Most of the community emigrated after 1948, mostly to Israel. The largest single movement came later and lies outside this chapter: between 1961 and 1964 the secret Operation Yachin brought about 97,000 Moroccan Jews to Israel with the Moroccan government's quiet consent, and historians record that Israel, together with American Jewish organizations, made payments to that government to allow the departures. [cite:w_yachin] Israelis of Moroccan descent became one of Israel's largest communities, and a human link between the two countries long before Morocco and Israel opened liaison offices in 1994, after Oslo, and closed them in 2000 during the second intifada. [cite:w_yachin] [cite:w_isr_morocco]

Egypt. Egypt's Jewish population was about 75,000 in 1948. Between June and November of that year, bombs set off in the Jewish quarter of Cairo killed more than 70 Jews and wounded nearly 200, according to the historian Howard Sachar as cited by the Jewish Virtual Library. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] The State Archives table counts 17,114 immigrants from Egypt to Israel between 1948 and 1952, a much smaller share of the community than left Iraq or Yemen in those years. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] Mendes notes that many Egyptian Jews appear to have left for economic reasons rather than because of specific persecution. [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010] The decisive rupture came after this chapter's period. After the Sinai campaign of 1956 the Egyptian government expelled almost 25,000 Jews and confiscated their property, and about 1,000 more were detained. A proclamation read in mosques on 23 November 1956 declared that "all Jews are Zionists and enemies of the state", according to contemporary reports by the Associated Press and the New York World-Telegram cited by the Jewish Virtual Library. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] That war is the subject of a later volume.

Libya. The sources here are thin on causes and give only numbers. The Jewish Agency records that 30,500 of Libya's 35,000 Jews came to Israel in the first years of the state; the State Archives table gives 32,129 immigrants from Libya between 1948 and 1952. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852]

Why did they leave? Three answers have been given, and Mendes describes the first two as partisan narratives. One, which he associates with Zionist and Israeli accounts, attributes the exodus almost solely to Arab violence or the threat of it. The other, which he associates with Arab and anti-Zionist accounts, attributes it to Zionist activity and agitation, in its strongest form "a malicious Zionist conspiracy". [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010] Each has evidence it can point to. The first can cite the Iraqi law of July 1948, the hanging of Ades, the denaturalization and confiscation of 1950, the dead of Oujda, Jérada and the Cairo Jewish quarter, and the Egyptian expulsions and mosque proclamation of 1956. The second can cite the Iraqi government's accusation over the Baghdad bombings, which independent historians have not been able to confirm but have not fully closed either. Mendes's own conclusion is the third answer: the exodus is best explained as "a complex combination of push and pull factors", with harassment, discrimination and, in Iraq, expulsion on one side, and the pull of Zionism and of life in Israel on the other. [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010] The country-by-country record bears out the variation. In Iraq the state criminalized and then denaturalized a community; in Yemen people walked for weeks to reach an airlift; in Egypt, before 1956, many appear to have left for economic reasons.

The antisemitism in this record is named here because it is documented: a law that presumed every Jew a criminal, a public hanging staged as a warning, bombs in a Jewish quarter, a state proclamation that all Jews were enemies. These were the acts of named governments, courts and rioters in particular places and years. They do not describe Arabs or Muslims as peoples, and the same record shows Jewish communities that had lived among their neighbors for one, two and nearly three thousand years.

Several things remain unsettled. The bombers of Baghdad have not been identified beyond dispute. The totals differ from source to source, for Iraq (about 135,000 reduced to about 6,000; 121,512 of 130,000; 124,226), for Yemen (about 49,000; nearly all of 45,000; 48,375 with Aden) and for the region as a whole (800,000 to 900,000 across three decades including Iran; nearly one million in about a decade), and no one figure can be preferred on the evidence given here. How the newcomers were received in Israel is the subject of [scene:rd6_ingathering|the mass immigration of 1948–1952].
