The Ecosystem Map: Six Layers and the Joins Between Them

This section puts the tools on one map, because the worst-case reading is about what they could do joined. The Sophia platform's framing for this book is that the mechanisms may not be connected now, but they exist, and in the worst case anything could be connected. The map below therefore does two things at once. It shows what exists, in six layers, and it grades every link between them on the same four-step scale: documented (it exists today, with a source), reported (named reporting, not yet confirmed by a primary record), possible (technically feasible, with no document showing it) and not supported (the record runs against it). "Possible" is a grade for a join between systems only, never for a fact about a person.

The six layers

The first layer is the records the government already holds because people must give them: tax returns, Medicaid enrolment, Social Security numbers, welfare files, visa and student records, travel manifests and the fingerprints, faces and irises in DHS's biometric systems. How the walls between them came down in 2025–26 is told in [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|Part Thirty-Two]. Documented joins to enforcement now include the April 2025 agreement under which ICE submits names to the IRS for address checks, the linking of Social Security data to the SAVE citizenship-check system in May 2025, and Medicaid data sent to ICE under a court order that limits it to five fields for people not lawfully present. [cite:de30_ap_krause] [cite:de24_npr_save] [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] On 28 September 2026 a federal judge declined to block HHS from sharing welfare (TANF) recipient data with DHS, calling the states' fears "vague, unsubstantiated predictions"; the disclosure notice took effect on 30 September. [cite:de28_bl_tanf] DHS's next-generation biometric system, HART, is designed to be shared across all DHS components, the Justice and Defense Departments, the intelligence community, state and local police and foreign partners, but was not yet operational when its privacy assessment was updated in August 2024. [cite:de28_hart_pia]

The second layer is surveillance the government runs: face matching in the field through ICE's Mobile Fortify app, phone extraction with Cellebrite devices, Paragon's spyware during the months ICE held it, and drones, on which Customs and Border Protection has committed more than $675 million to one manufacturer. [cite:de26_404_fortify] [cite:de28_fs_cellebrite] [cite:de28_npr_paragon] [cite:de28_brennan_tools]

The third layer is state and local systems that federal agents can reach. The clearest example is licence-plate cameras. In one month at a Texas school district's eight Flock cameras, more than 3,100 agencies ran more than 733,000 searches, and 620 of them cited immigration reasons, from 30 agencies; the district itself showed no immigration use, and Flock says its customers control sharing. [cite:de28_74_flock] DHS has also queried driver and vehicle records nationwide through information-sharing contracts. [cite:de28_brennan_tools] What school cameras and records mean for students is examined in [scene:de24b_schools|Part Twenty-Five].

The fourth layer is commercial surveillance vendors selling to ICE directly: Clearview's faces, Penlink's Tangles and Webloc, Zignal's and ShadowDragon's social-media tools, Babel Street's location software, all listed in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|the first section]. The fifth is data brokers whose products are people: LexisNexis's Accurint, Thomson Reuters's CLEAR and plate reads, Venntel's app-location data, and the employment and utility files held by credit bureaus. An Equifax-held utility file once reached ICE through CLEAR, until the utilities' exchange told Equifax to stop in 2021; Equifax's Work Number, built from employers' payroll data, supplies government agencies for welfare eligibility and child-support enforcement, and no record shows it reaching ICE. [cite:de24_globe_utility] [cite:de28_cfpb_twn]

The sixth layer is integration: the platforms that join the rest. At ICE that means Palantir's case-management system, ImmigrationOS, ELITE and the May 2026 enforcement-operations order, and a parallel system, RAVEn, which the Brennan Center describes as letting investigators "search and combine information from multiple databases to create detailed profiles," built with Booz Allen Hamilton, Acuity and Alethix, each paid tens of millions of dollars. [cite:de27_hg_ero] [cite:de28_brennan_tools] A 2020 solicitation described "FALCON/Raven Palantir systems," so RAVEn and Palantir may have been intertwined in an earlier period; the record does not settle it, and RAVEn should not be called a Palantir system without that caveat. [cite:de28_bu_lexis] The Brennan Center counted at least $2.9 billion committed by DHS to surveillance, data-collection and analytics tools since January 2021, a minimum. [cite:de28_brennan_tools]

Walking the map

From a person's point of view, the map is a chain. A face is matched in the street or a plate is read by a school camera; a name resolves to records in the people-search databases; those records, and the government's own, are joined on an integration platform; the platform produces a lead, an address and a confidence score; and an officer acts. The Brennan Center's July 2026 report described Palantir's case system as one that "structures and links data collected from these vendors and other commercial entities" and is being made Homeland Security Investigations' "authoritative data source," and ImmigrationOS as assembling "ICE's data streams into a central hub." [cite:de28_brennan_consol] Every adjacent pair in the chain has at least one documented instance. What the record does not show is one system carrying a person from the first link to the last in a single flow outside immigration enforcement, and the commercial vendors' data, apart from LexisNexis by design, has not been shown entering the integration layer at all.

Who has asked, and who has answered

Congress has asked about the joins repeatedly; the answers are mostly missing. Senators Mark Warner and Tim Kaine wrote to DHS's inspector general on 29 January 2026 about ICE's and CBP's growing use of biometric and surveillance data, and the inspector general opened an audit of the department's security of biometric data and personal information in February; no report had been located by late October 2026. [cite:de28_warner_audit] [cite:de28_bu_warner] Seventy members asked in March 2026 for an investigation of ICE's location-data purchases. [cite:de28_reg_wyden70] On 14 April 2026 thirty-three members, led by Representatives Dan Goldman and Nydia Velázquez and Senator Ron Wyden, asked DHS's secretary and ICE's acting director for every DHS contract with Palantir since 2020, the government and commercial datasets in Palantir-built systems, any American citizens' data in them, face-recognition errors, and any use against observers and protesters, naming ELITE, Clearview, Penlink, cell-site simulators and Paragon; the deadline was 24 April, and no reply had been found by October. [cite:de28_goldman_letter] DHS says its methods comply with the Constitution, including the Fourth Amendment. [cite:de28_reg_wyden70]

The worst-case reading, tested

The book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, reads immigration enforcement as the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination. This part bears on the occasion directly: ICE's data stack is the most complete set of surveillance tools any American civilian agency holds.

Documented: every layer of the map exists and ICE can reach each one, by contract, by agreement or through other agencies. The government buys data a warrant would otherwise gate, and no federal law stops it. [cite:de28_oig_23_61] [cite:de28_pogo_4th] DHS used administrative subpoenas, without a judge, to try to unmask people who criticised or tracked ICE, and in one case agents came to a critic's home. [cite:de28_aclu_doe] [cite:de28_int_meta] A federal court found that a policy of targeting non-citizen academics for their speech was unconstitutional. [cite:de28_ch_aaup] And Medicaid data reached Palantir outside a court's limits. [cite:de27_npr_medicaid]

Probable: that the integration layer will absorb more of the map, because work statements already require connections to it and DHS spending on Palantir rose roughly eightfold from 2024 to 2026. [cite:de28_bu_lexis] [cite:de28_brennan_tools]

Possible, as joins only: the commercial vendors' location, face and social-media data feeding the same platform; HART's biometrics and the newly opened welfare records joined to it. Each would need an API, an agreement or a rule change, not a new law.

Not supported: one standing system that carries a person from a camera to a cell outside immigration enforcement, or the stack's systematic use against citizens for political speech. The record runs against the worst case in three places. The stack was built across four administrations of both parties, so it is not the product of the present one's ideology. [cite:de28_epic_babel] [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] Its brakes have worked in part: a magistrate judge stopped one unmasking, DHS withdrew others when challenged, a court vacated the speech-targeting policy and another paused Medicaid sharing. [cite:de28_int_meta] [cite:de28_aclu_doe] [cite:de28_ch_aaup] [cite:de27_npr_medicaid] And they have also failed in part: a welfare-data suit lost, the subpoena scale is unknown, and the oversight letters of 2026 are unanswered. [cite:de28_bl_tanf] [cite:de28_eff_letter] [cite:de28_goldman_letter] The gap between "every link exists" and "one machine runs them" is now mostly a matter of data-sharing agreements and internal rules, which is the layer being loosened. How the same capabilities were used against Americans in an earlier era is the subject of [scene:de29_the_precedent|Part Thirty-One].
