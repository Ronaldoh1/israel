Lausanne, 1949: Where Return and Borders Were Negotiated, and Failed

A few months after the fighting, the refugee question came before a formal negotiation. Two opposite accounts of what happened are still repeated: that Israel never offered anything on refugees, or that the Arab states refused every offer. The United Nations' own report on the talks supports neither version. It shows two sets of conditions, each arranged in an order the other side would not accept.

The forum was the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine, created by Resolution 194 in December 1948. It convened talks at Lausanne from 27 April to 12 September 1949. The Arab delegations refused to negotiate directly with Israel, so the Commission shuttled between the parties. On 12 May they signed the Lausanne Protocol, which took the 1947 partition map as a basis for discussion. [cite:wiki_lausanne_1949] [cite:pij_lausanne]

The Arab states demanded that Israel first accept the return of refugees under Resolution 194, and they wanted the 1947 partition lines. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_israel_studies_peace] Israel held that the partition plan had been nullified by the Arab invasion and that refugees could be addressed only within a final peace. Israel first floated annexing Gaza together with its refugee population; Egypt refused.

On 3 August, according to the Commission's own progress report, Israel offered to accept the return of 100,000 refugees, which would have brought its Arab population to a maximum of 250,000. The offer was made "only as an integral part of a general and final peace settlement." [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_ccp_fourth_report] The Arab delegations called it contrary to Resolution 194 and to the Protocol. Israel later withdrew the offer. The Commission's report also records Israel blocking a committee on refugee-owned orange groves. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report]

The conference ended in September without agreement. The United States, which had already sent Ben-Gurion a secret warning in May, told Israel it might revise its relations over the impasse; the fate of that warning is traced in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's post-election policy].

The structure of the failure explains why it lasted. Israel would discuss return only after peace. The Arab states would discuss peace only after return. Each side's sequence was the reverse of the other's, and neither moved. The offer of 100,000 shows that Israel was prepared to put a number on the table; the conditions attached to it, and the Arab rejection of those conditions, show why the number never became a policy. With no agreement, roughly 700,000 refugees remained refugees.

That is the significance of Lausanne. It was not the last negotiation on the question, but it was the one at which the refugee problem passed from a consequence of the war into a permanent feature of the conflict. The question it failed to settle has outlived every negotiation since, and it turns, in the end, on the reading of a single paragraph of Resolution 194.
