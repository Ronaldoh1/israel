By the early 1950s the new state had secured its position on several fronts at once. In Washington it had won de jure recognition, full legal recognition, in January 1949 and a $100 million loan from the Export-Import Bank, and it had turned aside a presidential warning that was never enforced. At home, the Absentees' Property Law of 1950 had placed refugee property in the hands of a state Custodian. The law reached even citizens classed as "present absentees," estimated by 2001 at about 200,000, while military rule kept the displaced from going back. In the immigrant camps, the treatment of Jews from Arab and Muslim countries had opened a wound the state would acknowledge with "regret" only in 2021.

Abroad, the refugee question had hardened. Lausanne had failed over which should come first, peace or return. Resolution 194 had been adopted, reaffirmed, and read in two opposite ways. The new human-rights order counted the displacement of about 711,000 people, fed them and debated their fate. But it placed them outside the main refugee convention and gave them no court. In the West, the story that they had fled on orders would stand until the archives opened.

And the books on the years before the state could now be closed. The record showed mass private giving, documented budgets and defense channels, a connected American network, one documented case of money changing a decision, and an apparatus built over fifty years before the state was declared.
