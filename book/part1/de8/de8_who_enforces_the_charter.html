Who Removes a Bad Sovereign

Every system of government has to answer one question before any other: what happens when the person at the top goes wrong. A company answers it with a board, shareholders and, behind them, the courts of the state in which the company is registered. A constitutional republic answers it with elections, impeachment, courts, a legislature that controls money, and term limits; the American Constitution provides that a president "shall be removed from Office on Impeachment for, and Conviction of, Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors." [cite:de8_lii_art2s4] The Dark Enlightenment's state is designed to remove most of those answers, so it must supply its own. This section examines the one it gives, its alternative to the vote, and the answer it rejects.

The problem the texts set themselves

The difficulty begins inside the texts. The neocameral state, as Land's 2012 serial summarises it, rests on what Land calls a "Hobbesian" recognition: that "sovereignty cannot be eliminated, caged, or controlled," that "divided powers flow back together," and that constitutions have "exactly as much real authority as a sovereign interpretative power allows them to have." [cite:de8_land_de] That premise is the texts' case against checks and balances: a written limit on power, they argue, binds only as long as whoever holds power chooses to honour it.

The same premise cuts against the texts' own design. A neocameral state's chief executive is supposed to answer to a board, and the board to shareholders. [cite:de8_land_de] But a charter that says the board may dismiss the executive is itself a written limit on power. If constitutions bind only as far as the sovereign allows, so does a corporate charter, and an executive who controls the police and the army has no more reason to obey a board than a president has to obey a court. The texts therefore need a mechanism that does not depend on anyone's willingness to comply.

Yarvin's answer: the keys

Yarvin's answer is technical. According to Ava Kofman's 2025 account in the New Yorker, legitimacy, accountability and succession in each sovereign corporation would rest with "a secret board with the power to select and recall the otherwise all-powerful C.E.O." To stop a chief executive staging a military coup, "the board members would have access to cryptographic keys that would allow them to disarm all government weapons." [cite:de8_kofman_ny] A summary of his Patchwork writings by a sympathetic reviewer describes the same feature: shareholders holding encryption keys that give access to the national arsenal, and a design said to be stable only with modern cryptography. [cite:de8_kossina_patchwork] CNN, reporting on his views in 2025, summarised the design as a monarch "held accountable by some sort of corporate board, but not by the average citizen." [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin]

The record on this mechanism is short: it has never been built or tested anywhere. Land's own passage concedes that the full neocameral approach "has never been tried." [cite:de8_land_de] No state has locked its weapons to keys held by a board, and no evidence exists of how such a system would behave in a crisis. What can be assessed is its logic, and here the following points are reasoning about the design, not findings from a record.

First, the keys do not remove the problem of an unchecked sovereign; they move it. Whoever holds the keys to every weapon in the state is, on the texts' own Hobbesian premise, the real sovereign. If the board can disarm the executive, nothing disarms the board. Second, the board would be secret, by Kofman's account, which is the opposite of the formalism the texts promise: the public would not know who held final power. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] Third, shares in such a state would be transferable, so control could be bought. A critique published in Compact in November 2025 observed that under ordinary corporate law a dominant shareholder can install loyal directors and rewrite a company's constitution, and that the result would be oligarchy, "managerial democracy in corporate drag." [cite:de8_compact_ceo] Fourth, weapons are carried by people. A lock on a device governs the device, not the loyalty of the soldiers and police who carry out orders, and the texts do not explain why those people would obey a remote board rather than the executive in front of them.

Yarvin's one built system of cryptographic ownership and identity is Urbit, a computer network he created, launched in 2013 and developed by Tlon, the company he founded with seed money from investors led by Peter Thiel; he left Tlon in 2019. [cite:de8_wiki_urbit] It is the nearest thing to a test his ideas have had. What it shows, and what it does not, is the subject of [scene:de23_address_space|Part Twenty-Three] and [scene:de33_two_tests|Part Thirty-Three]; it governs computers, not people, and it does not test the keys to an arsenal.

Exit instead of voice

The texts' other answer to bad government is not removal but departure. In 1970 the economist Albert Hirschman distinguished two ways people respond when a firm or organisation declines. "Exit" is leaving: customers stop buying, members quit. "Voice" is speaking up: members "express their dissatisfaction directly to management." [cite:de8_hirschman] Land's serial adopts the distinction and chooses one side. Democracy is voice; the neocameral state offers its residents the other. If the state does not deliver value for its "sovereign rent," residents may complain to its customer service and, "if necessary take their custom elsewhere." Land's summary is four words: "No voice, free exit." [cite:de8_land_de]

Hirschman's own book argues against relying on exit alone. "The presence of the exit alternative," he wrote, "can therefore tend to atrophy the development of the art of voice." Those who care most about quality are the most likely to leave first, so the organisation loses its sharpest critics just when it most needs them; and where exit is unavailable, voice becomes the only way members can react. [cite:de8_hirschman] Leaving a country is not like changing brands. It requires another country willing to take you, money to move, and the abandonment of work, family and language, and the texts offer no guarantee that any other sovereign corporation would admit those who leave. The Compact critique made the same point: no one guarantees the right to exit, and a rival's immigration rules could make it meaningless. [cite:de8_compact_ceo] The platforms described in the previous part show what happens to customers who cannot easily leave. [cite:de7_ads_enshit] A state that removes voice and offers only exit removes the response Hirschman found most useful, and leaves the one least available to most people.

The designed answer: checks and balances

The answer the texts reject is the one the American founders wrote down. The Federalist No. 51, published in the New York Packet on 8 February 1788, started from the same distrust of rulers that the Dark Enlightenment claims as its own: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary." Its conclusion was the opposite. A government must first "control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself." The people were "the primary control," and the second was structural: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." [cite:de8_fed51] The design does not rely on a single good ruler or a single board. It divides power so that each holder has a motive to resist the others.

It is fair to say that the design has been tested and sometimes strained. The Supreme Court struck down part of Roosevelt's programme in 1935, the Senate shelved his plan to enlarge the Court in 1937, and the states limited presidents to two elected terms in 1951. [cite:de7_brit_schechter] [cite:de7_ncc_courtpack] [cite:de8_annenberg_22] In June 2026 the Supreme Court moved the balance the other way, overruling a 1935 precedent and holding that the President may remove commissioners of the Federal Trade Commission at will. [cite:de0_slaughter] Public trust in the courts that police the system is at a low. [cite:us2_axios_gallup2026] Checks and balances are not self-enforcing either; they depend, as Land's premise says, on people who choose to honour them. The difference is that the constitutional design spreads that choice across many hands, elected and appointed at different times by different people, so that no single defection settles the matter. The neocameral design concentrates it in one board.

The worst-case reading, tested: Book One

Book One asked what the texts say and whether their argument holds. On the worst-case reading, a sovereign executive without a veto is the destination; this book can grade only the first half of that claim, the destination as the texts describe it. Documented: the texts state that end state in their own words. Land's serial, partly in passages it quotes from Moldbug, describes a state owned as a business, run by a chief executive appointed by its owners, in which residents have "no voice" and political freedom is "unimportant by definition." [cite:de8_land_de] Yarvin said in 2025 that he did not "believe in actually voting at all," and that power should be concentrated "in a single individual." [cite:de7_yarvin_nyt2025] [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin] Documented: the texts deny that constitutions can bind a sovereign, and the only removal mechanism they offer, a secret board with keys to the arsenal, has never been built or tried. [cite:de8_land_de] [cite:de8_kofman_ny] Documented: the diagnosis behind the end state is partly real, and the models the texts point to are not what they claim. The hardest passages, on surplus residents and on slavery, are set out in Yarvin's own words in [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|Part Six].

Not supported in Book One: anything about adoption. Nothing in the texts or in this part shows that any official has taken up the program, that the 2025 executive order creating the Department of Government Efficiency or the 2026 ruling on removal power owes anything to these writings, or that any plan connects them. [cite:de0_eo14158] [cite:de0_slaughter] Resemblance between a later policy and a proposal in the texts is rhyme, not provenance. Probable or not is a question this book cannot yet grade, because it depends on documents about people and money that Book One does not examine. Where the record runs against the worst-case reading, Book One says so: Roosevelt's precedent was conditional and checked, the checks held, and the texts' own design for removing a ruler is untested.
