Fatah Takes Up Arms, January 1965–Spring 1967

In the first days of 1965, as Bonn's secret tank deal was coming apart, a Palestinian organization, Fatah, began an armed campaign against Israel through its military wing, al-Asifa. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron_f] Over the next two and a half years its raids, and Israel's answers to them, became one of the threads that drew the region toward the crisis of May 1967. The campaign was low in intensity and large in consequence. It drew Syria in as a sponsor, put Jordan and Lebanon in the position of hosts to raids they had not approved, and gave the United Nations Secretary-General one of his named causes of the tension on the eve of war. This section follows it from its first operation to the spring of 1967.

The first operation, in three versions. The opening act of the campaign is dated and described differently by the three sources consulted for it, and the differences are of substance, not only of date. They are set out here side by side, without a choice among them.

The encyclopedia of the Institute for Palestine Studies dates the first operation to dawn on 1 January 1965, against an Israeli water network, and records that Ahmad Muhammad Musa, known as "Salama," was killed. It notes that Palestinians mark that date as the start of their contemporary revolution. [cite:rs_cw6_ipsp_fatah] The historian Benny Morris, as summarized by the Center for Online Judaic Studies, dates the raid to the night of 2 January 1965: a squad planted a charge by the National Water Carrier in the Galilee; the charge was found and defused; on its way back the squad was intercepted by a Jordanian patrol, its leader was shot dead and the others were arrested. [cite:rs_cw6_cojs_morris] The water scholar Aaron Wolf records a failed attempt to sabotage the Carrier on 31 December 1964 and calls it "the PLO's first action." [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu_f]

Some of the differences may be differences of framing. A charge planted on the night of 31 December could be dated to the morning of 1 January by one source and to the following night by another, and a raid can fail in its object and still be the start of a movement. But two points cannot be reconciled from these sources. One is how the leader died: the Palestinian encyclopedia records a death without, in the claim consulted here, naming who killed him, while Morris says he was shot after a Jordanian patrol intercepted the squad. The other is whose operation it was: Wolf names the PLO, which a CIA memorandum describes as formed in 1964 with Arab League sanction, while the other two name Fatah. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_plo] The stakes of the first point are political as well as factual. If the first casualty of the Palestinian armed struggle fell to a Jordanian patrol, the episode shows from its first night that the Arab governments on whose territory the raids began did not control them. That theme recurs in the documents that follow.

The target is the one point on which all three agree. The first operation was aimed at Israel's water system, at the moment when Israel's National Water Carrier was entering operation and the Arab states had decided to divert the Jordan's headwaters, as told in [scene:cw6_water|the water war]. [cite:rs_cw6_ipsp_fatah] [cite:rs_cw6_cojs_morris] [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu_f]

Syria as sponsor. The campaign grew with a state behind it. The State Department historians record that Fatah incidents along Israel's borders, supported by Syria, increased from mid-1965, and that American officials opposed Israeli reprisals and urged action through the United Nations. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_f] A CIA memorandum of 2 December 1966 set out what the support consisted of: Damascus gave Fatah a base, training and a propaganda outlet. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] The same agency called Syria in that memorandum "the most bellicose Arab state," a judgment that belongs to the story of Syria's radical turn, told later in this stage. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s]

The money. According to the same CIA memorandum, Fatah's money came mainly from wealthy Palestinians in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] The memorandum describes private funding, not funding by the Kuwaiti or Saudi governments, and the distinction matters for the mechanism. Syria supplied the territory, the training and the voice; private Palestinian money from the Gulf paid for the organization. The cost of the raids fell on the Israelis who were their targets and, through Israel's reprisals, on targets in the West Bank. What the money bought in arms, and in what sums, the memorandum does not say.

Raids from territory whose governments had not agreed. The most consequential feature of the campaign was its geography. The CIA memorandum says that infiltrations were launched from Jordanian and Lebanese territory "without the approval of either the Jordanian or the Lebanese government." [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] The territory named is not Syria's: the governments whose borders were crossed were not the sponsors. GlobalSecurity's history of Jordan describes the same structure from Amman's side: Fatah and the PLO raided Israel without clearance from the Unified Arab Command or from Jordan, which had tried to avoid incidents that invited reprisals. In that account, Israel's reprisals on West Bank targets grew harsher and more frequent from May 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_f] That is a reported account, not a primary document, but it agrees with the CIA's on the central fact.

The mechanism created a chain in which no single government held all the links. Fatah chose the targets. Syria supplied the base and training. Jordan and Lebanon had the borders across which raids came but had not approved them. Israel answered with reprisals, and from May 1965, in GlobalSecurity's account, increasingly on the West Bank under Jordan. And Washington, which opposed the reprisals, could urge but not prevent. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_f] The objection to the reprisals was put most sharply in Washington after the raid on Samu in November 1966, when Johnson's adviser Rostow called it "aimed at the wrong target." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] That episode, in which a mine killed three Israeli soldiers and Israel struck the Jordanian-held village of Samu, is told in [scene:cw6_samu|Samu and Resolution 228]; the sources there differ on whether the mine was Fatah's (the Israeli army's archive) or, as the CIA reported Israel's attribution, the Palestine Liberation Front's. [cite:rs_cw6_idf_archive_samu] [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_samu]

Lebanon. The Lebanese border had its own story. "The Lebanese-Israeli border was quiet until around 1965 when the Fatah movement started launching low-intensity attacks on Israeli positions," writes Hilal Khashan, professor of political science at the American University of Beirut. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] The Lebanese army tried to counter these operations, but Lebanese opinion divided along sectarian lines: broad sympathy in Muslim and pan-Arabist communities, and reluctance among Maronite Christian parties that did not want Lebanon drawn into a conflict they felt did not concern it directly. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] It is sometimes said that armed conflict on the Israel–Lebanon border began only after Israel's conquests of 1967. Khashan's account dates it two years earlier, and shows the Lebanese state already caught between the raids launched from its soil and the division they opened within it.

The count by December 1966. By the end of 1966 the campaign had a number attached to it. The CIA memorandum of 2 December 1966 says Israel attributed 61 sabotage incidents to Fatah since it emerged in January 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] The figure is Israel's attribution as the CIA reported it, not an independent count, and the memorandum as consulted does not itemize the incidents, their targets or their casualties.

Toward the crisis. In Wolf's account, the water dispute and the raids fed what he calls a "prolonged chain reaction of border violence," with border air battles in July 1966 and April 1967. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] Israel's leaders placed the responsibility on Damascus: the Chief of Staff, Rabin, said its "authorities are behind the terrorists," according to Tal. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_jvl] Whether Israeli pressure or Syrian-sponsored raids did more to drive the Syrian front is a dispute taken up with the April air battle.

In May 1967, reporting to the Security Council on the situation in the Near East, U Thant named "El Fatah activities, consisting of terrorism and sabotage" as a major factor in the tension. [cite:rs_cw6_thant_s7896] It was a contemporary judgment from outside the parties.

The word "terrorism." The sources do not all use the same word, and the choice of word carries a judgment. U Thant's report characterized the activities themselves as "terrorism and sabotage." [cite:rs_cw6_thant_s7896] The CIA memorandum, in the claims consulted here, counts "sabotage incidents" and "infiltrations." [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] Rabin, as Tal reports him, spoke of "the terrorists" and laid them at Syria's door. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_jvl] Khashan writes of "low-intensity attacks on Israeli positions." [cite:lebanon_1948_war] The Institute for Palestine Studies describes the first operation as the start of a revolution. [cite:rs_cw6_ipsp_fatah] These are not interchangeable descriptions. U Thant was not a party to the conflict, and his word is the one in the UN's own record; but the sources consulted here describe the targets of the first raids as water installations and military positions, and they do not itemize the later incidents in a way that would show which were aimed at civilians and which at soldiers or infrastructure. A reader can see from the documents who used which word and in what setting. The record assembled here does not supply the incident-by-incident detail on which a fuller judgment of the campaign's character would rest.

The war of June 1967, its consequences for the Palestinians and for Lebanon, and Fatah's later place within the PLO, the broader body founded in 1964, belong to [scene:s12|the June war] and the volume that follows. [cite:lebanon_1948_war]

What the record does not settle. The documents used here do not settle the date, the outcome or the authorship of the first operation, and the three accounts are given as they stand. They do not itemize the 61 incidents Israel attributed to Fatah, so their targets and casualties cannot be counted from them. They name the sources of Fatah's money in general terms but give no sums. They establish that Syria provided a base, training and propaganda, but not what orders, if any, passed from Damascus to Fatah's operations.
