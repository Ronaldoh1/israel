Who Belongs: The Law of Return and the Nationality Law

Two statutes, passed two years apart, answered the question of who belonged to the new state. The Law of Return of 1950 said who had the right to come. The Nationality Law of 1952 said who became a national, and how. Read together, they set up two routes into citizenship, one for Jews from anywhere and one for the Arabs who had lived in Palestine and remained, and the conditions on the two routes were not the same. Both laws were short, and the clearest way to understand them is through their own texts.

The Law of Return opens with a single sentence. Section 1 states: "Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh", that is, as an immigrant who comes to settle. [cite:rs_rd6_mjl_lawofreturn] Section 2 makes the right operational. Every Jew who wishes to settle in Israel receives an oleh's visa, unless the minister finds that the applicant is acting against the Jewish people, or is likely to endanger public health or the security of the state. Section 4 closes the circle backward: Jews already in the country, and Jews born there, are deemed to have come as olim. [cite:rs_rd6_mjl_lawofreturn] The law was signed by Prime Minister David Ben-Gurion, by the Minister of Immigration, Moshe Shapira, and by Yosef Sprinzak as Acting President and Chairman of the Knesset. [cite:rs_rd6_mjl_lawofreturn] [cite:rs_rd6_adalah_lawofreturn]

Even its date is disputed in the sources. The law itself is dated 20 Tammuz 5710, which is 5 July 1950, and the Encyclopedia of the Modern Middle East gives the same day; the Palestinian jurist Anis Kassim says the bills were submitted on 3 July and the law adopted on 5 July. [cite:rs_rd6_mjl_lawofreturn] [cite:rs_rd6_adalah_lawofreturn] [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] [cite:rs_rd6_kassim_aardi] The Encyclopaedia Judaica gives 3 July 1950, the anniversary of Herzl's death. [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] The law's own dating is the firmer evidence for when it was enacted. Kassim's account, in which the bills were submitted on 3 July, would explain how the earlier date arose, but none of the sources says so directly, and the discrepancy stands.

Ben-Gurion's case for the law was stated in its own terms when he presented the bill. The right it recognized, he said, "is inherent in every Jew by virtue of his being a Jew", and "This right preceded the State of Israel". [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] On this reading the law granted nothing new. It declared a right that already existed. The Encyclopaedia Judaica places it in a line of documents running back half a century: the Basel Program of 1897, Article 6 of the Mandate of 1922, and the Declaration of Independence. [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] The law was passed in the middle of the mass immigration, in which 684,201 people arrived between May 1948 and the end of 1951, described in [scene:rd6_ingathering|the mass immigration of 1948–1952]. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption]

The exclusions were widened once in the period. An amendment of 23 August 1954 added a further ground for refusal: a person "with a criminal past, likely to endanger public welfare". [cite:rs_rd6_adalah_lawofreturn] A definition of who was a Jew entered the law only twenty years later, when an amendment in 1970 extended the law's rights to a Jew's child and grandchild and to their spouses, and defined a Jew as one "born of a Jewish mother or has become converted to Judaism and who is not a member of another religion". [cite:rs_rd6_adalah_lawofreturn] That amendment, and the long dispute over "who is a Jew" that produced it, belong to a later volume.

The Nationality Law took effect on 14 July 1952 and turned the right to come into the status of a national. It provided two main routes. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_nationality] The first was nationality "by return": every oleh under the Law of Return became an Israeli national by that fact alone. The second was nationality "by residence", for former Palestinian citizens. They could become nationals only if they met three conditions together: that they had been registered as inhabitants on 1 March 1952; that they were inhabitants on the day the law took effect; and that they had been in Israel continuously from the establishment of the state, or had entered it legally in the meantime. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_nationality] The same law repealed the Palestinian Citizenship Orders, the Mandate's citizenship legislation, retroactively to the day the state was established. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_nationality]

The mechanism matters because of what had happened in 1948. A Jew came into nationality by coming; the only bars were the minister's grounds for refusal. A former Palestinian citizen came into it only by passing a test of registration and continuous presence. Anyone who was not on the register of 1 March 1952, or who had been outside the state after its establishment without entering legally, failed the test. And because the Mandate's citizenship had been repealed retroactively, those who failed it were left, in Kassim's argument, without a nationality at all. How the Arabs who remained were governed in the same years is told in [scene:rd6_military_government|the military government].

The strongest case against the law was made by Kassim. In his reading, the Law of Return amounts to "legally-authorized racial discrimination": it grants Jews from anywhere what it denies to Palestinians who lived on the land, so that "an indigenous Palestinian has no such inherent right even if he used to live on the land". He argues that the Palestinians who stayed were left stateless for about thirty years. [cite:rs_rd6_kassim_aardi] The two positions face each other directly. To Ben-Gurion and the Encyclopaedia Judaica, the law was one of return, recognizing a right older than the state. To Kassim it was one of discrimination, granting to Jews from anywhere what it denied to Palestinians who had lived there. [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] [cite:rs_rd6_kassim_aardi] The word "return" itself carries the dispute, because the same word stands at the centre of the United Nations' resolution on the Palestinian refugees, read in [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194 and the refugee count].

The debate was not only between Jews and Palestinians. Within Jewish life the law drew argument from several directions. According to the Encyclopedia of the Modern Middle East, some Orthodox leaders wanted the definition of "Jew" narrowed; the Reform and Conservative movements opposed that; and some non-Zionists called for the law's repeal as discriminatory. [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn] Those arguments, about the boundaries of the people the law was written for, ran alongside the argument about the people it left out.

Several things the record here does not settle. It does not give the number of Arab inhabitants who met the residence test in 1952, or the number who failed it. Kassim's figure of about thirty years of statelessness is his own, and the sources cited here do not show how many people it describes or how their status was later resolved. And the date on which the Knesset passed the Law of Return, 3 or 5 July 1950, remains in conflict between the law's own text and the Encyclopaedia Judaica.
