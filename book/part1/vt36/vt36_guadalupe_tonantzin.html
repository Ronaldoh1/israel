Guadalupe and Tonantzin: The Mother on the Hill of Tepeyac

On the northern edge of Mexico City stands the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe, the most visited Catholic shrine in the world; on 11 and 12 December 2009 alone, a record 6.1 million pilgrims passed through it. The hill behind it, Tepeyac, was a place of pilgrimage before the Spanish arrived, and a Franciscan friar who knew the Nahua world better than almost any European of his century wrote that the pilgrims were still coming to the same mother under a new name. Whether that friar was right, and what exactly happened on that hill, is one of the oldest open arguments in the history of the Americas. This section sets out the documents in the order they appeared, the debate about them, and the competing readings of the image itself, keeping the devotees' account and the critics' account side by side. [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The friar's complaint

The earliest and most direct statement linking Tepeyac to a pre-conquest mother goddess comes from Bernardino de Sahagún, the Franciscan who spent decades compiling, with Nahua collaborators, the encyclopedic work now known as the Florentine Codex. In an appendix to Book 11, written in 1576, he listed places where he believed the old worship continued under Christian cover. Of Tepeyac he wrote that the Nahua had there "a temple dedicated to the mother of the gods, whom they called Tonantzin, which means Our Mother," and that people came to its sacrifices from more than twenty leagues away. Now that a church of Our Lady of Guadalupe stood there, he complained, preachers were calling the Virgin "Tonantzin" too, when the proper Nahuatl title for the Mother of God was "Dios Inantzin." He called this "a satanic device to conceal idolatry," and found the devotion "suspicious" because pilgrims walked past many other churches of Our Lady to reach this one. [cite:vt36_sahagun1576]

Two features of the complaint matter. First, it is the testimony of a hostile witness inside the Church: Sahagún wanted the pilgrimage corrected, not celebrated, and his account of the old temple is not corroborated by an excavated building on the hill. Second, his objection is about a word. "Tonantzin" is built from to- ("our"), nān ("mother") and the honorific -tzin, and it was a title, used for several goddesses connected with earth, sustenance and maize, much as "Our Lady" is a title in Spanish. The theologian Rosemary Radford Ruether doubted that a single goddess called Tonantzin had a temple at Tepeyac at all, reading the word as the maternal title of any of several deities, which Nahua Christians could then carry over to Mary. Sahagún himself, by one account, used the word in sermons into the 1560s before turning against it. [cite:vt36_wiki_tonantzin] [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The story and its paper trail

The apparition story, as the shrine and the Catholic Church tell it, runs as follows. Between 9 and 12 December 1531, by the Julian calendar then in use, a young woman appeared four times on Tepeyac to a Nahua man named Juan Diego and asked for a church to be built there. The bishop-elect of Mexico, Juan de Zumárraga, asked for a sign. Juan Diego gathered Castilian roses on the barren hill in his cloak, the tilma; when he opened it before the bishop the roses fell, and the image of the Virgin was found printed on the cloth. In the Nahuatl account she asks him whether she, his mother, is not there with him. [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The difficulty for historians is the order in which the documents appear. Zumárraga's surviving writings do not mention the event. The first clear record of a controversy comes in 1556. Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar, a Dominican, promoted the devotion at Tepeyac; on 8 September 1556, the Franciscan provincial Francisco de Bustamante preached against it, attacking the archbishop for encouraging a devotion to an image he said had been painted by an Indian named Marcos. The record of Montúfar's inquiry into the sermon was published only in 1888. The art historian Jeanette Favrot Peterson has argued that the painter was Marcos Cipac de Aquino; Stafford Poole and others have doubted that identification. Montúfar ended Franciscan custody of the shrine and authorised a larger church. [cite:vt36_wiki_olg] [cite:vt36_wiki_marcos]

The full narrative appears in print more than a century after the date it describes. In 1648 the priest Miguel Sánchez published Imagen de la Virgen María, the first printed account, which identified the Guadalupe image with the woman "clothed with the sun" of Revelation 12. In 1649 Luis Laso de la Vega, vicar of the Tepeyac chapel from 1647 to 1657, published the Huei tlamahuiçoltica, a 36-page tract mostly in Nahuatl. Its centrepiece is the Nican Mopohua ("Here is told"), the account of the apparitions, praised as a jewel of Nahuatl literature. [cite:vt36_wiki_huei]

Who wrote the Nican Mopohua is disputed. Laso claimed authorship in his preface, and Lisa Sousa, Stafford Poole and James Lockhart, in their 1998 edition, accepted him as its author, with possible collaborators, drawing on Sánchez's book. Mexican Nahuatl scholars, among them Miguel León-Portilla and Edmundo O'Gorman, lean towards Antonio Valeriano, a Nahua scholar trained by the Franciscans, writing around 1556; the seventeenth-century savant Carlos de Sigüenza y Góngora named Valeriano in a sworn statement, and the New York Public Library holds a partial copy that some date to about 1556. On this question the evidence does not close. [cite:vt36_wiki_huei]

Critics, defenders and a saint

Poole's 1995 history, Our Lady of Guadalupe: The Origins and Sources of a Mexican National Symbol, 1531–1797, read the silences in the sixteenth-century record as decisive; one reviewer summarised his conclusion as finding "no objective historical basis" for the apparition story. The book was widely praised by historians, while one reviewer doubted it would shake popular faith. The dispute reached the shrine itself: Guillermo Schulenburg, abbot of the basilica, resigned in 1996 after saying Juan Diego was a symbol rather than a historical person. Defenders pointed to other evidence, including the Codex Escalada, announced in 1995, a document dated 1548 said to bear the signatures of Valeriano and Sahagún; sceptics did not accept it as genuine. [cite:vt36_enc_poole] [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The institution's answer was given in its own register. John Paul II beatified Juan Diego on 6 May 1990 and canonized him at the basilica on 31 July 2002 as Juan Diego Cuauhtlatoatzin, "the first indigenous saint of the American continent." His homily spoke of the Virgin's mestizo face and presented Guadalupe as a model of evangelization that entered Indigenous culture rather than erasing it. Earlier popes had built the devotion into the institution: Pius X named her patroness of Mexico by the decree Gratia quae of 16 June 1910, and the Congregation of Rites approved 12 December as her feast in 1754. [cite:vt36_va_jd2002] [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

Reading the image

The image on the tilma shows a young woman with dark hair, eyes lowered and hands joined, in a rose-coloured dress with a dark sash and a blue-green mantle scattered with gold stars. She stands on a crescent moon held up by an angel, against a golden sunburst. Two families of readings exist, and both are serious. [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The first reads it as a European Marian image. The sunburst, the crescent underfoot, the stars and the joined hands are the elements of the woman of Revelation 12, "clothed with the sun," the type that Spanish and later Catholic art used for the Immaculate Conception; Sánchez made exactly this identification in 1648. [cite:vt36_ci_immac] [cite:vt36_wiki_olg]

The second reads it as a Nahua text in images. In this reading the blue-green of the mantle was the colour reserved for the divine couple Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl; the dark sash marks pregnancy; a small cross-shaped figure beneath the sash is the nahui ollin sign, the Nahua emblem of the cosmos; and the rays recall the spines of the maguey plant. Some writers go further: the historian María de los Ángeles López de Canseco reads the four-petalled flower as the four cardinal points and the four past ages awaiting a fifth, and says the stars on the mantle match the sky of 12 December 1531, a claim resting on planetarium software rather than a sixteenth-century source. Devotees and Church writers often hold that the Nahua reading was intended, a message addressed to Juan Diego's people in their own sign language; historians who trace the image to European models treat the Nahua reading as a later interpretation. [cite:vt36_wiki_olg] [cite:vt36_exp_signos]

What the record shows

Three findings stand. A Franciscan of the 1570s believed Tepeyac had been a shrine to a mother deity and that the Guadalupe pilgrimage continued that worship; he wanted it suppressed, and the archbishop's party, not the friars, won. The apparition narrative is first attested in print in 1648–1649, and whether an older Nahuatl original lies behind it is unsettled. And the institution did not erase the mother of Tepeyac; it took the devotion up, built it a basilica, made its visionary a saint and its image the patroness of a nation, while its own friar's complaint, that a goddess's name was being carried into the Virgin's, remains part of the record. Whether that is suppression, survival or both is the question the rest of this part follows, from the Andes and the Caribbean [scene:vt36_mothers_of_the_world|to the other mothers of the world] and back to the Mediterranean.
