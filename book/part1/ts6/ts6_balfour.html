Balfour, Lloyd George and Wingate: Faith Among the Motives

On 2 November 1917 the Foreign Secretary, Arthur Balfour, signed a letter to Lord Rothschild stating that the British government viewed "with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." [cite:wb_bl_addms41178] [cite:wb_ra_balfour] How the letter was drafted, why it went to Walter Rothschild, and what it promised the Arab majority are told in the Road to 1948 and Divine 3+1 volumes. [scene:s06|The Balfour Declaration] [scene:dv2_british_state|What the British state did] This section takes up a narrower question that belongs to the history of Christian Zionism: how much the religious beliefs of the two men most responsible, Balfour and the Prime Minister, David Lloyd George, weighed in the decision, and what the record allows a reader to say about it.

The case for a Christian formation

The religious reading has serious advocates. The scholar Philip Alexander has written that "a crucial ingredient in Balfour's Zionism" may have been "his Christian belief," and he names the historian Donald Lewis, author of The Origins of Christian Zionism (2010), as the most persuasive advocate of the thesis that British Christian formation prepared the ground for the Declaration. [cite:ts6_wiki_cz] On this view both Balfour and Lloyd George "were influenced by Christian Zionism," in the sense that they had grown up inside the restorationist reading of the Bible that Shaftesbury had carried into British politics in 1839–40. [cite:ts6_wiki_cz] [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|Shaftesbury and the British restorationists]

For Lloyd George there is specific testimony. Oren Kessler, writing on the Prime Minister's evidence to the Palestine Royal Commission, notes that his Baptist upbringing predisposed him toward Chaim Weizmann's vision and that he felt more at home with the place names of Palestine than with those of the Western Front. [cite:ts6_fathom_kessler] A sentence often quoted from a speech Lloyd George gave to the Jewish Historical Society, about being taught more Jewish history than the history of his own land, could not be traced to a dated primary text for this volume and is not used here.

The reasons the government recorded

The reasons the British government gave itself at the time were of another kind. The War Cabinet approved the Declaration for reasons ministers could name: the security of the approaches to the Suez Canal and the hope of keeping Russia in the war and drawing in American support [cite:cohen_palestine_great_powers]; the standing Weizmann had earned through his wartime work on acetone [cite:wb_ra_balfour]; and a belief in a unified and powerful "world Jewry" that could be won to the Allied side, which Schneer treats as stereotyped thinking about Jews. [cite:ts6_wiki_balfourdecl] The cabinet minutes of 31 October 1917 record Balfour's own argument that a declaration would allow "extremely useful propaganda both in Russia and America." [cite:ts6_wiki_balfourdecl] Lord Robert Cecil, a Foreign Office minister, wrote: "I do not think it is possible to exaggerate the international power of the Jews." [cite:ts6_wiki_balfourdecl] The historians Jonathan Schneer and James Renton both describe a foreign-policy elite that believed Jews held real power they could bring to bear on the war. [cite:ts6_wiki_balfourdecl]

Lloyd George's own later account points the same way. In secret evidence to the Peel Commission in 1937, released in 2017 and brought to light by Kessler, he explained the Declaration as a wartime measure: he said "the friendliness or hostility of the Jewish race might make a considerable difference," and called Jews "a dangerous people to quarrel with, but they are a very helpful people if you can get them on your side." [cite:ts6_jn_lloydgeorge] [cite:ts6_fathom_kessler] Kessler sums up the man as "Judeophobe and philo-Semite, militarist and appeaser, Zionist champion and Hitler enthusiast." [cite:ts6_fathom_kessler] The testimony was given twenty years after the event, when the Mandate was in crisis, and it is one man's justification; it is also the only account in his own words of why he acted.

The one Jewish member of the government, Edwin Montagu, opposed the policy in a memorandum of 23 August 1917 headed "Anti-Semitism in the Present Government," calling Zionism "a mischievous political creed." [cite:wb_tna_cab21_58_218] He was overruled.

Weighing the two

The two readings are not exclusive, and the strongest position the record allows combines them. Schneer calls the Declaration "the highly contingent product of a tortuous process," and his book concentrates so heavily on politics that one reviewer, Colin Chapman, faulted it for leaving out "the religious beliefs of key players like Lord Balfour and David Lloyd George." [cite:ts6_chapman_schneer] That criticism is fair as far as it goes: belief can explain why two men were receptive to Weizmann. It does not show that belief decided the matter. The documents that record the decision speak of empire, propaganda and an exaggerated notion of Jewish influence; the evidence for faith is about formation, not about the cabinet room. Accounts that present the Declaration as an act of Christian Zionism, or as a purely strategic act with no religious colour, both claim more than the record supports. [cite:ts6_wiki_balfourdecl] [cite:ts6_wiki_cz]

Orde Wingate: faith in the field

Where Christian Zionism can be seen directly shaping conduct in this period is in the career of one British officer. Orde Wingate's father, Colonel George Wingate, became a committed member of the Plymouth Brethren early in his army career, and the children memorised Scripture daily. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] The Brethren were the movement in which John Nelson Darby had developed his system, as Part Three describes, and Wingate grew into an ardent Christian Zionist who regarded a Jewish state as a religious duty. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] [scene:ts3_darby|Darby and the Brethren]

As an intelligence captain in Palestine during the Arab Revolt, he proposed joint units of British soldiers and Jewish supernumerary police drawn from the Haganah. General Haining approved the Special Night Squads in June 1938, and they operated from Kibbutz Ein Harod until October, with about seventy-five Haganah volunteers, among them Moshe Dayan and Yigal Allon. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] [cite:wiki_special_night_squads] [cite:nli_wingate_hebrew] [scene:br_s01|Wingate's Night Squads and the Palmach] Wingate returned often to Ein Harod, according to his biographers because of its association with the biblical judge Gideon. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] After rebels killed Ein Harod's leader, Chaim Sturman, with a mine in September 1938, Wingate led a reprisal on the Arab quarter of Beisan. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] The historian Tom Segev called him "quite mad, and perhaps a sadist, too"; Michael Oren disputed Segev's account. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] The Israeli writer Uri Avnery traced a line of retaliation-raid doctrine from Wingate to the Israel Defense Forces, a critic's argument rather than a settled finding. [cite:avnery_dayan_wingate]

His faith did not make him an agent of British policy; it put him at odds with it. He was transferred to Britain in May 1939: after he spoke publicly in favour of a Jewish state while on leave in Britain, his superiors in Palestine removed him from command, judging that he was "promoting his own agenda rather than that of the army or the government." [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate] The same administration that had sponsored the squads imprisoned Dayan in 1939 for training with arms outside British command. [cite:archiveshub_wingate] Wingate died on 24 March 1944 when the American B-25 carrying him crashed in the hills of Manipur. Israel's national sports institute, the Wingate Institute, the youth village of Yemin Orde and a square in Jerusalem bear his name. [cite:ts6_wiki_wingate]

The British record thus shows Christian Zionism at two levels. At the top, it was a predisposition in men whose recorded reasons were strategic, and whose strategic reasoning included a belief about Jewish power that historians such as Schneer describe as stereotyped. In the field, in Wingate, it was a personal conviction that shaped a military method and the careers of future Israeli commanders, and that the British army itself treated as a problem. Neither level fits the claim that the British state acted on prophecy.
