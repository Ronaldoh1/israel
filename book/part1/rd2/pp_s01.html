The Palestinian National Movement: Seven Congresses and a Demand for Elections

It is sometimes said that the Palestinians had no leaders, no national identity and no political demands before Israel existed. The record of the early mandate years shows otherwise. From 1919, Palestinian Muslims and Christians organized together, met in national congresses, chose an executive, sent delegations to London and put a clear demand to Britain: an elected government.

The first Palestine Arab Congress met from 27 January to 10 February 1919. Its 27 delegates came from Muslim–Christian Associations across the country, organizations that joined the two communities. Between 1919 and 1928, seven congresses met, in Jerusalem, Jaffa, Haifa and Nablus. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jvl_palestine_arab_congress]

Their program was consistent. The congresses called for Palestine to be part of the independent Arab state Britain had promised during the war, and for "a national government responsible to a representative assembly" chosen by the country's Arabic-speaking inhabitants. They rejected the Balfour Declaration and mass Jewish immigration. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive] In other words, the Palestinian leadership was asking for the principle that the League's own Covenant described, that the wishes of the inhabitants should weigh in the choice of who governed them.

The third congress, held in Haifa in December 1920, elected a nine-member Arab Executive Committee. Its chairman was Musa Kazim al-Husseini. Earlier that year, after the riots of April 1920, the British had dismissed him as mayor of Jerusalem and appointed in his place Raghib al-Nashashibi, head of a rival leading family. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] Rivalry between factions aligned with the Husseini and Nashashibi families divided Palestinian politics for years afterward. Only in 1928 did a congress briefly form a united front. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim]

Musa Kazim led delegations to Winston Churchill, in Cairo and in Jerusalem, and to London in 1921 and 1922. They were rebuffed. In a telegram he concluded that the cause would not find justice "at the hands of the British government which is under Zionist influence." [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] That was his judgment, written after his delegations had been turned away, and it records how the Palestinian leadership came to see the mandatory power.

Britain never recognized the Arab Executive. The contrast with its treatment of the Zionist movement was marked: the mandate gave formal standing to the Zionist Organization's Jewish Agency, while the Palestinians' own elected executive remained without official status throughout its existence. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]

By the early 1930s the leadership was under pressure from within as well. In 1932 the Executive launched a fund known as the Piaster Project to keep land in Arab hands. The same year, the more radical Istiqlal, or Independence, party began attacking the established leadership for its inaction, a challenge that continued into 1933. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

In October 1933 Musa Kazim joined a protest against immigration in Jaffa and was injured by British forces. He died in March 1934. The Arab Executive dissolved that year. In 1936 a new body, the Arab Higher Committee, was formed. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

These events reach beyond the years covered by this stage, but they belong here because they began here. The revolt that broke out in 1936 was preceded by some fifteen years of congresses, petitions and delegations asking for representative government, all of them addressed to a mandatory power that recognized one national movement in Palestine and declined to recognize the other.
