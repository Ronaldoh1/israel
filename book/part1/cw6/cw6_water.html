The Water War, 1964–1966

Stage Two left the Syrian frontier with its disputed demilitarized zones and the American attempt, through the Johnston Plan, to divide the Jordan's waters by agreement; this section picks up [scene:sy_s02|that thread] in 1964, when Israel began drawing water from the Jordan basin on its own and the Arab states answered by trying to divert the river's headwaters before they reached it. Each scheme was an engineering project; each was also a claim about who had a right to the river; and the fight over them ran straight into the raids and air battles that led toward 1967.

The plan that was never ratified. The background is the American mediation of 1953–55. Under American mediation by Eric Johnston, from 1953 to 1955, the parties negotiated, and by 1955 the negotiators had reached a technical plan for sharing the Jordan system. Israel accepted it. The Arab League declined to ratify it, according to the scholar Ines Dombrowsky, because a treaty with Israel would have meant recognizing Israel. [cite:rs_cw6_dombrowsky_bpb] On one reading, the plan's fate shaped what followed. The engineers had agreed on numbers, but there was no signed agreement that either side was bound by, and each could later claim to be acting within the plan or to be free of it.

The Carrier. Israel built on the plan's numbers without the treaty. Aaron Wolf, in his study of the hydropolitics of the Jordan for the United Nations University, writes that Israel's National Water Carrier was supported "within the Johnston allocations," and that by 1964 enough of it was complete for diversions from the Jordan basin to the coastal plain and the Negev to be imminent. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] The Carrier entered operation in 1964. It drew from Lake Tiberias, which meant lifting the water from 212 metres below sea level. [cite:rs_cw6_dombrowsky_bpb] [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] Wolf gives Israel's withdrawal as 320 million cubic metres a year from 1964. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu]

The exact date of the opening is less certain than the year. The Center for Israel Education, an educational organization, dates the Carrier's start to 10 June 1964 and gives its two trunk lines a capacity of 1.7 million cubic metres a day. [cite:rs_cw6_cie_carrier] That date rests on a secondary timeline; no primary record of the opening was fetched for this book, and the date should be read as that source's. What the scholarly sources agree on is the year and the scale.

The phrase "within the Johnston allocations" is Israel's case in brief. The Johnston Plan had been negotiated under American sponsorship, Israel had accepted it, and the quantity Israel drew could be presented as no more than the share the plan had assigned. To the Arab states, the same project was an Israeli diversion of a shared river, carried out without any agreement they had signed; the Cairo summit's communiqué, discussed in [scene:cw6_summits_plo|the summits and the PLO], condemned it as a threat to Arab riparian rights. The two cases did not meet, because one rested on a plan and the other on the refusal to ratify it.

Washington's position. The United States took a side on the water, and said so in private. Before the Carrier opened, Bundy warned Israeli diplomats that 1964 would be a difficult year because of the pro-Israeli stance Washington planned to take on the Jordan waters. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7_w] In June 1964 Johnson told Eshkol: "We back Israel fully on the Jordan water." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d65_w] Support for the Carrier did not extend to force in its defence. The State Department's editors record that Washington asked Israel to pursue peaceful means on the Jordan waters rather than preemptive action. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_w] The American position, then, had two halves: Israel's withdrawal was legitimate, and Israel should not strike first to protect it.

The Arab choice, 1964. At the Cairo summit of January 1964, according to Wolf, the Arab states weighed three options: complaint to the United Nations, diversion of the Jordan's upper tributaries, or war. A military assessment found the Arab states unprepared to defend diversion works. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] The finding mattered: it meant that any diversion would be built within reach of Israeli forces by states that did not expect to be able to protect it. The September 1964 summit chose diversion nonetheless. It agreed to finance works in Lebanon and Syria and to help Jordan build a dam on the Yarmuk. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] The sources fetched here do not give the sums or say which governments paid what.

The diversion scheme. Work began in 1965. The plan would have diverted two of the Jordan's sources before they reached Israel: the Hasbani into the Litani, inside Lebanon, and the Banias into the Yarmuk, where the water would be impounded by a dam at Mukheiba. Together the works could divert up to 125 million cubic metres a year. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] Lebanon stopped its part of the work in July 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] That left the Syrian works on the Banias as the part of the scheme still being built.

The strikes of 1965. Israel attacked the diversion works in Syria in March, May and August 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] How it did so is disputed between the two scholarly sources fetched here. Wolf writes that Israel used long-range tank fire, not artillery or aircraft, and that it chose this method partly because of American warnings against preemptive action. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] Dombrowsky writes that Israel "bombed" the construction site several times shortly after work began. [cite:rs_cw6_dombrowsky_bpb] The difference is not a quibble. Wolf's account describes a calibrated use of force, shaped by Washington's request and kept below the threshold of air attack; Dombrowsky's word, if taken literally, describes air power. Wolf gives the months and the weapon and ties the choice to the American request for restraint; Dombrowsky gives no further detail. Neither source gives casualty figures or an order of battle, and the sources here cannot settle the point beyond that.

Dombrowsky records that the Banias diversion project was finally abandoned in 1966. [cite:rs_cw6_dombrowsky_bpb] The sources fetched here do not set out the decision to abandon it, but the outcome matched what the Arab states' own military assessment of January 1964 had foreseen: works that could not be defended were not completed. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] [cite:rs_cw6_dombrowsky_bpb] The broader account of the Syrian frontier drawn on here, which describes the strikes as hitting the works in "1965–67," gives a longer span than either scholarly source, which place the attacks in 1965 and the abandonment in 1966.

The first Palestinian attack on the Carrier. The Carrier was also a target for the new Palestinian armed groups, and the sources disagree on the first attempt against it. Wolf calls an attempt to sabotage the Carrier on 31 December 1964 "the PLO's first action." [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] The Institute for Palestine Studies encyclopedia and Benny Morris, as cited by the Center for Online Judaic Studies, attribute the first operation to Fatah, not the PLO, and date it 1 January 1965 (the Institute) or the night of 2 January 1965 (Morris). [cite:rs_cw6_ipsp_fatah_w] [cite:rs_cw6_cojs_morris_w] The two disagreements, over the date and over the organization, are separate, and both are open on these sources. The three versions of the operation are set side by side in [scene:cw6_fatah|Fatah's armed campaign]. What all of them share is the target: the first Palestinian armed operation of this period, whoever carried it out, was aimed at the water.

From water to the border war. The water war did not end when the diversion was given up; it changed form. Wolf records border air battles in July 1966 and April 1967 as part of what he calls a "prolonged chain reaction of border violence." [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] The Syrian frontier, where the zones had been disputed since 1949, became the place where the engineering conflict turned into a military one.

The map of the Golan line places this period in its long sequence. Its second stage, "DMZ & water war," covers 1949 to 1967 and is labelled "both sides": it runs from the armistice of 1949, which left the demilitarized zones as small strips without agreed sovereignty, to the escalation that ended in June 1967. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz] [cite:w_dayan] The label reflects the record. Syria fired on Israeli farmers and fishermen from the heights; Israel sent armoured tractors to cultivate disputed land and carried out reprisal raids. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz] [cite:w_dayan] The familiar account holds that the clashes on this frontier were caused entirely by Syrian shelling from the Golan. The documented conduct of both sides does not support so simple a version, and on this reading the water war is part of the reason: the Carrier, the diversion and the strikes on the diversion were each a deliberate act by a government, and each was answered.

One later statement sharpens the dispute without settling it. In an interview given in 1976 and published in 1997, Moshe Dayan said that at least 80 percent of the clashes had been provoked by Israel, which sent tractors into the zones until the Syrians fired. [cite:w_dayan] It is his own account, given long after the events, and it has been contested by others. It concerns the zones in general rather than the water works in particular, and it is evidence of how one senior participant later judged Israeli conduct, not a count of incidents. The dispute over who bore more responsibility for the Syrian frontier continues.

What the record does not settle. On the sources fetched here, several points remain open: the exact date the Carrier opened, which rests on one secondary timeline; whether Israel's strikes on the diversion works were made by tank fire alone or also from the air; the date of the first sabotage attempt against the Carrier and which organization made it; and the sums the Arab summit committed to the diversion, and who paid them. What is not open is the sequence. Israel drew from the Jordan in 1964, within the Johnston allocations as Wolf puts it, under a plan it had accepted and its neighbours had not ratified; the Arab states chose in September 1964 to divert the headwaters, knowing they could not defend the works; Israel struck the works three times in 1965 while Washington backed its water rights and urged it not to strike first; and by 1966 the Arab scheme was abandoned and the conflict over the river had become part of a wider war of raids and air battles on the Syrian line.
