Testing a Hypothesis: Arms, Aid and Standing at the United Nations

A common explanation of pro-Israel advocacy abroad, and one this volume was asked to test, is that it serves two practical ends: keeping military aid and arms flowing to Israel, and protecting Israel's standing at the United Nations. The explanation can be checked against the record assembled in this part. The result is mixed. One half of it fits the evidence in the two countries that matter most; the other half needs to be restated before it fits at all; and in much of the world the benefits run in a different direction from the one the hypothesis assumes.

Arms and aid. Israel buys most of its imported weapons from two countries. According to estimates by the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute for 2019 to 2023, the United States supplied 69 percent of Israel's arms imports and Germany about 30 percent; American military aid alone exceeds $3 billion a year. [cite:pm_wapo_suppliers] The two countries with the largest and best-funded pro-Israel advocacy, the United States with AIPAC and Germany, where ELNET is registered, are therefore the two on which Israel's arms supply depends. That fits the hypothesis. But elsewhere the arms trade runs the other way. Israel was the world's ninth-largest arms exporter in the same period, and its largest customers were India, which took 37 percent of its exports, and the Philippines, which took 12 percent; Israel supplied 28 percent of the Philippines' arms imports and 11 percent of Morocco's. [cite:pm_globes_exports] Germany's purchase of Arrow 3, which ELNET counts among its successes, was also a sale by Israel, not aid to it. [cite:pm_intercept_elnet] In those countries the interest that advocacy may serve is commercial and strategic, and the buyer, not Israel, is the one receiving arms.

Standing at the United Nations. Israel's membership of the United Nations is not in question. It was admitted by General Assembly Resolution 273 on 11 May 1949, and no mechanism of the advocacy described here concerns it. [cite:rs_rd6_unga273_text] What advocacy abroad has sought is something narrower: votes. Netanyahu told Israel's envoys to Africa that his first interest there was to "dramatically change the situation regarding African votes at the UN," and to change their voting patterns. [cite:pm_alg_netanyahu_un] Status in regional bodies has been contested in the same way. The chair of the African Union Commission granted Israel observer status in 2021; South Africa protested, and after an Israeli diplomat was removed from the union's summit in February 2023 the status was suspended pending review by a committee, with Israel blaming "extremist states like Algeria and South Africa." [cite:pm_aljaz_au] Restated as a hypothesis about votes and observer seats rather than recognition, this half fits the record: it is a stated goal of Israeli diplomacy and of the caucus network, whose aims include embassy moves to Jerusalem. [cite:pm_wiki_iaf]

What the other countries gain. The hypothesis also leaves out why governments abroad respond. The best-documented case is Guatemala, which moved its embassy to Jerusalem in 2018. Israel had been Guatemala's largest supplier of weapons, training and surveillance technology in the 1980s, after the United States cut off military aid in 1977, and analysts tied the embassy decision to that history, to the president's evangelical political base, and to Guatemala's dependence on American aid and goodwill. [cite:pm_pitt_guatemala] In that account the aid at stake is American aid to Guatemala, and the road to it runs through Washington, where the United States had opened its own embassy in Jerusalem on 14 May 2018. [cite:wiki_unsc478] A Central American government aligning with Israel was aligning with the United States as well. The caucus network's appeal to Christian voters, "faith-based diplomacy" in its own phrase, points to the same mixture of motives: religious conviction among parliamentarians and their voters, as well as interest. [cite:pm_ih_iaf50]

The amounts complete the picture. Against the American military aid of more than $3 billion a year, the international advocacy described in this part is small: an Israeli budget for public diplomacy of about $730 million in 2026, the largest single item; at least $11 million from American funders to ELNET since 2022; a little over £1 million to British MPs in a five-year Parliament; less than $700,000 on Canadian MPs' trips over sixteen years; and a caucus network run on about $1.4 million a year. [cite:pm_jta_730m] [cite:pm_intercept_elnet] [cite:pm_declassified_quarter] [cite:pm_maple_cija] [cite:pm_wiki_iaf] By the measure of money, the pro-Israel effort is overwhelmingly an American effort, in American politics. Abroad it is cheaper, harder to see and, on the evidence here, aimed less at keeping Israel's place in the world than at turning that place into votes, purchases and embassies.
