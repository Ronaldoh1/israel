At the end of 1922 the promise of 1917 had become an instrument of the League of Nations, but not yet a settled one. The League Council had approved the Mandate for Palestine on 24 July 1922 on a condition that still had to be met, and the Ottoman Empire's successor had not yet given up its claim to the land. [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force] [cite:w_lausanne] Britain's first census that year counted 757,182 people, 83,794 of them Jews, about 11 percent. [cite:jvl_census_1931] The institutions of the Jewish community were small. The Histadrut, the labour federation founded in Haifa in December 1920 and open only to Jews, had about 8,400 members in 1922; the Haganah, placed under its control, counted "a few hundred" volunteers. [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] The Palestinian Arab congresses had an Executive under Musa Kazim al-Husseini, which Britain never recognized. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]

This stage asks what was built in the fourteen years that followed, by whom, on what terms, and what was left unbuilt. The answer lies less in declarations than in institutions: a labour federation with its own bank and building firm, a land fund that never resold, a Jewish Agency widened to take in non-Zionist money, an electricity monopoly, a currency board in London. It asks with equal weight what Palestinian Arabs built in the same years, what Britain tried to change after the violence of 1929, and why it reversed itself within four months. [cite:w_passfield]

The cast is wide. On the British side stood the High Commissioners in Jerusalem, down to Sir Arthur Wauchope; Lord Passfield at the Colonial Office; Ramsay MacDonald in Downing Street; and the inquiries of the Shaw Commission, Sir John Hope Simpson and the Murison Commission. [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d] [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal] [cite:rs_rd3_murison_palquest] The Zionist movement worked through Chaim Weizmann; through David Ben-Gurion, the Histadrut's secretary from the end of 1921; through Pinhas Rutenberg's power company and Yehoshua Hankin's land purchases; and, from 1929, through the American non-Zionists Louis Marshall and Felix Warburg. [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:wiki_israel_electric] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14] Ze'ev Jabotinsky's Revisionists contested the leadership from within, and Jewish dissent reached further: Jacob de Haan spoke for the Orthodox old Yishuv until he was shot in 1924, and Brit Shalom argued for a binational country. [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists] [cite:w_dehaan] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom] On the Palestinian side were the Mufti, Hajj Amin al-Husseini; his rival Raghib al-Nashashibi; Jamal al-Husseini; the founders of the Istiqlal party; the women of the 1929 congress; and, outside every party, Shaykh 'Izz al-Din al-Qassam. [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi] [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_jamal] [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_hajibrahim] [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_womens] [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam] Beyond Palestine, Turkey signed at Lausanne, and in Berlin a democracy fell to a regime that soon signed a transfer agreement with Zionist bodies. [cite:rs_rd3_lausanne_fcdo] [cite:history_weimar_republic] [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]

The sections follow that building roughly in time. They begin with the Histadrut and "Hebrew labour", Rutenberg's concession and the grid it produced, and the country as the census counted it. Then come the legal steps of 1923–1925, from Lausanne to the Citizenship Order. They follow the Revisionist line from Betar and the "Iron Wall" to secession, the killing of de Haan, the immigration of the Fourth and Fifth Aliyot, the enlarged Jewish Agency, the Hebrew University and Brit Shalom, and the money and currency of the Mandate economy. August 1929 at the Wall, in Hebron and in Safed is the hinge. After it come Palestinian politics from the last congress to the parties of 1935; Weimar's collapse; the inquiries, the Passfield White Paper and MacDonald's letter; land and labour; and the armed line from the Haganah to the Irgun. The American Palestine Committee and the Jews of Baghdad and Constantine widen the view. So do [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement], the murder of Chaim Arlosoroff and the Nuremberg Laws. The stage ends with al-Qassam's death in the hills near Ya'bad and with [scene:rd3_council_1935|Wauchope's offer of a legislative council], rejected by the Zionist leadership and dividing Arab leaders as the country moved toward [scene:s07a5|the Arab Revolt].
