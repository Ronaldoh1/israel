Laws Applied Differently: Leahy, the Veto and the Bomb

The last three shields are not laws written for Israel. They are general American laws and powers whose application to Israel has differed from their application elsewhere: a human-rights law applied through a special procedure, a veto used more often on Israel-related drafts than on any other subject, and nuclear non-proliferation laws that have never been invoked because the United States does not officially acknowledge what Israel is widely assessed to possess. For each, this section separates what the law says, how it has been applied, and what the government says in its defence.

The Leahy Law. Two statutes named after Senator Patrick Leahy bar American assistance to any unit of a foreign security force where there is credible information that it has committed a gross violation of human rights: Section 620M of the Foreign Assistance Act for State Department programmes and 10 U.S.C. 362 for the Pentagon. In practice vetting focuses on torture, extrajudicial killing, enforced disappearance and rape under colour of law. The State Department may resume aid if it determines that the government is "taking effective steps to bring the responsible members of the security forces unit to justice." [cite:ow11_crs_leahy] The law applies to Israel as to every recipient. The question is how it has been applied.

For Israel, the State Department created a separate procedure, the Israel Leahy Vetting Forum. Charles Blaha, a retired State Department official, wrote in June 2024 that under it a finding that an Israeli unit is ineligible rests with the Deputy Secretary of State, which he said was "true for no other country in the world"; that cases advance only after formal written requests to Israel, which may take "as long as three months" to answer; and that in over four years the forum had not identified a single ineligible Israeli unit. [cite:ow11_js_blaha] On 29 April 2024 the department announced its first such finding: five Israeli units had committed gross violations, all before October 2023 and outside Gaza. Four, it said, had "effectively remediated" them; the fifth, reported to be the Netzah Yehuda battalion, remained under review. An Israeli official said Israel "categorically rejects any attempts to harm the IDF." [cite:ow11_toi_leahy2024] On 9 August 2024 Secretary Blinken concluded that Netzah Yehuda's violations had also been remediated, after Israel removed two soldiers from combat duty, changed the unit's vetting and required human-rights training, and announced that it "can continue receiving security assistance." [cite:ow11_axios_ny2024] In October 2025 the Washington Post reported that a classified report by the State Department's Inspector General described "many hundreds" of potential violations by Israeli units in Gaza awaiting review, a backlog it said would take "multiple years" to clear; the department did not comment. [cite:ow11_wapo_oig]

The government's position is that the law has been applied: units were found responsible, Israel took corrective steps, and the statute's remediation clause allows aid to continue in those circumstances. [cite:ow11_crs_leahy] [cite:ow11_axios_ny2024] The critics' position, Blaha's among them, is that a procedure found nowhere else, with decisions reserved to the political level, has produced no barred unit at all. [cite:ow11_js_blaha] Both describe the same documents. A claim sometimes made, that Israel is exempt from the Leahy Law, is wrong: it is subject to the law, and the finding is how the law has been applied.

The veto. The United States first used its Security Council veto on an Israel-related draft on Sunday 10 September 1972, against a Somali resolution on Israeli air strikes in Syria and Lebanon after the killing of Israeli athletes at Munich; the vote was 13 to 1, with one abstention. Ambassador George H. W. Bush said the Middle East violence was "inextricably interwoven with the disaster in Munich" and criticized a "double standard" that ignored terrorism. [cite:ow11_djn_veto1972] [cite:ow11_un_s10790] Counts of the vetoes since then differ by method. Al Jazeera, using UN data, counted at least 53 American vetoes of drafts critical of Israel by May 2021; Agence France-Presse counted 46 Security Council vetoes, by all members, on the Palestinian territories since 1970, as of 18 September 2025. [cite:ow11_aj_vetoes2021] [cite:ow11_afp_veto2025] On that day the United States cast its sixth veto of a Gaza ceasefire demand since the war began, against the other fourteen members; its envoy, Morgan Ortagus, said the draft "fails to condemn Hamas or recognise Israel's right to defend itself." [cite:ow11_aj_veto2025] Successive American ambassadors have given the same kind of reason across decades: that the drafts were one-sided, or that the Council should not settle questions reserved for negotiation. In 2011 Susan Rice vetoed a draft on the illegality of settlements while saying Washington did not dispute its view of settlements but considered it "unwise" for the Council to try to resolve core issues. Earlier, John Negroponte said a 2001 draft aimed to "isolate politically one of the parties," and in 2018 Nikki Haley called a Gaza draft "a grossly one-sided view" of events. [cite:ow11_aj_vetoes2021]

The bomb that is not acknowledged. Israel has never signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] Two American laws of the 1970s address proliferation by cutting off aid, and both are often misdescribed. The Symington Amendment of 1976, now Section 101 of the Arms Export Control Act, bars most assistance to a country that trafficks in uranium-enrichment equipment or technology outside international safeguards. The Glenn Amendment of 1977, now Section 102, bars assistance to a non-nuclear-weapon state that, among other things, detonates a nuclear explosive device; it was applied to India and Pakistan after their tests in 1998. [cite:ow11_aca_glenn] Neither law cuts off aid to a country simply because it is outside the NPT or is believed to hold nuclear weapons; statements that Symington "requires cutting off aid to non-signatories" are wrong. Neither has been applied to Israel. Glenn's trigger is a test, and Israel is not publicly known to have conducted one; Symington requires an executive determination about enrichment transfers that, as one 2026 analysis noted, no administration has made. [cite:ow11_mil_symington]

The reason no determination is made lies in a policy, not a statute. At a White House meeting on 26 September 1969, according to the scholarship of Avner Cohen as reviewed in Arms Control Today, President Nixon and Prime Minister Golda Meir reached an understanding: the United States would end its inspections of the Dimona reactor and stop pressing Israel to sign the NPT, so long as Israel did not declare, publicize or test its arsenal. The written record of that understanding has not been released. [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] Israel's formula, that it will not be the first to introduce nuclear weapons into the region, dates to the 1960s and appears in Rabin's letter on the Phantom sale described in [scene:ow1_founding|Part One]. [cite:ow1_jvl_phantomconds] The American side of the bargain is silence: by not acknowledging an Israeli arsenal, successive administrations avoid the question of whether the non-proliferation laws apply. Cohen describes the arrangement as giving "enough credible evidence to deter enemies but sufficient ambiguity" to avoid the consequences of declaration, and argues it has become "anachronistic and awkward because Israel's nuclear status is no longer ambiguous." [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] Whatever view is taken of it, the documented fact is that a policy of non-acknowledgement, not any statutory exemption, keeps the question closed.
