The Order of 21 November 1962: The Justice Department and the American Zionist Council

Volume II traced the line from the wartime American Zionist Emergency Council, founded in 1943, to the American Zionist Council and to Isaiah L. Kenen's lobbying committee, and it recorded the bare fact of the Justice Department's foreign-agent order of 21 November 1962, in [scene:s07g|From AZEC to AIPAC]. This section does not retell that lineage. It takes up the order itself: what the law it invoked required, what money it rested on, how the Council fought it, which organization was which on the day it was issued, and what the record does and does not show about how the episode ended.

The claim to be tested is a common one. It holds that AIPAC has always been a purely domestic American lobby, independent of the Israeli government, and that the foreign-agent question is a modern invention of its critics. The record does not support the second half of that claim. In 1962 the Justice Department of the United States, under an Attorney General who was the President's brother, formally ordered the umbrella body over the lobbying committee to register as the agent of a foreign principal. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] [cite:acjna_fara] But the record also does not support the opposite claim, that the order proved the lobby was a foreign agent. It was an order, not a judgment. It was contested by the Council, according to the main account of the episode, it was never tested in a court, and the organization it named and the organization known today as AIPAC were, by most of the evidence, not the same body.

What the law required. The Foreign Agents Registration Act was passed in 1938. It required any person or organization acting "at the order, request, or under the direction or control" of a foreign principal to register publicly and to disclose the relationship, its funding and its activities. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] It was a disclosure law, not a prohibition. Registration did not make an organization illegal; it made its relationship with a foreign principal a matter of public record. According to Kobby Barda's summary of the Act as it then stood, an agent had to register within ten days of becoming one, and failure carried penalties of up to five years in prison, fines, or both; Barda puts the ceiling at up to ten years where the principal was a foreign government. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] In practice, violations were usually treated as civil or administrative matters to be resolved by registering, not by prosecution. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] The law was not new to this circle of organizations. The same question had been put a decade earlier in Feinberg's case, as Volume II recounts, and Isaiah Kenen himself had earlier been a registered foreign-agent lobbyist and an employee of Israel's information office at the United Nations, a status that ended when he moved to the American committee. [cite:wrmea_aipac] The Jewish Agency's own American Section, moreover, was already a registrant under the Act in 1963, as the agent of the World Zionist Organization and the Jewish Agency in Jerusalem. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] The question in 1962 was therefore not whether the Agency was a foreign agent; it had registered as one. The question was whether an American body that received the Agency's money had become one too.

The order. On 21 November 1962 the Internal Security Section of the Justice Department ordered the American Zionist Council to register as an agent of a foreign principal under the Act. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] Grant F. Smith of IRmep, an advocate of the lobby's registration whose work is the main account of the episode reached for this book, frames it as an order to register as an Israeli foreign agent. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] The issuing authority was Robert F. Kennedy's department; he served as Attorney General throughout his brother's presidency, from 1961 to 1963, and the Internal Security Section acted under his authority. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] The order did not come from nowhere. The Eisenhower administration had pressed the Council on the same question through the 1950s. [cite:mercury_aipac_fara] What changed in November 1962 was form: informal pressure became a written order with a date and a sum attached. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] In the Senate, J. William Fulbright was pressing the same question through the Foreign Relations Committee in the same period. [cite:wrmea_aipac]

No surviving document reached for this book records either Kennedy brother explaining why the department acted on this matter at this time. The sources document the institutional actions, the order, the hearings and a later FBI offer, but not the private reasoning behind them, and this book does not supply one. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order]

The money. The order's basis was money from the Jewish Agency for Israel. The department alleged that the Council had received more than $5 million from the Agency over the preceding eight years, and one account describes the allegation as funding by the Israeli government through the Agency's New York office. [cite:azc_fara_doj] [cite:acjna_fara] Smith states that the money was routed to the Council for lobbying and propaganda. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:rs_cw5_smith_antiwar_2013] A more exact figure appears in the record of the Senate inquiry that followed: $5,100,001.02, paid by the Jewish Agency to the American Zionist Council between 1 January 1955 and 31 December 1962, which Fulbright entered into the Foreign Relations Committee's record in 1963. [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing] The two figures are close but not the same thing. The order spoke of "more than $5 million" over eight years; the Senate figure runs to the last day of 1962, more than five weeks after the order was signed, so it cannot be the sum the order itself recited. The order's own text is not among the sources reached for this volume.

A third figure stands against both. Barda, writing in Modern Judaism in 2026, gives $1.5 million for the same years, presenting it as a claim raised in the matter without stating its source and without mentioning the $5.1 million figure. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The two totals have not been reconciled, and this book does not choose between them. What the Agency's money actually bought, and how the two sides at the Senate hearing described it, is the subject of [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the committee].

How to characterize the payer was itself disputed. Fulbright treated the Agency's payments to the Council as functionally equivalent to direct funding of an American lobby by the Israeli government; the Agency's counsel rejected that on the record, in an exchange told with the hearing. [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing] The Agency, at work since 1929, served as a bridge between Israel and Jewish communities abroad, including on fundraising and immigration. Both characterizations are documented, and the dispute was never settled in court, because the matter never reached one. [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing]

The Council's fight. What followed the order is known chiefly through Smith's account, and it should be read as his. According to Smith, the Council fought the order. It retained Simon H. Rifkind, whom Smith describes as a friend of President Kennedy. Rifkind argued that registration would "choke the very life" out of the Zionist movement, and said that the Council would stop routing Jewish Agency money into the United States for lobbying and propaganda. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] Said Arikat, writing in Al-Quds in May 2026 and citing period Justice Department correspondence without naming the documents, adds that the Council sought a 120-day delay and mobilized lawyers, public-relations firms and sympathetic members of Congress. [cite:rs_cw5_arikat_2026]

The order was argued among American Jewish organizations as well as between the Council and the government. Smith reports that the American Council for Judaism, whose dissent from Zionism is told later in this stage, welcomed the order, and that the New York Times sought inside information on it. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] Inside the government, too, the pressure did not run one way. Smith reports that Justice Department lawyer Nathan Lenvin told the Council's representatives that the Fulbright hearings showed the Council had not been distributing "educational" material but receiving large Agency transfers for publicity and lobbying. He also reports a Wall Street Journal story that Justice officials were weighing the risk of offending Jewish opinion, a story that, in his account, prompted letters to Congress and to Robert Kennedy urging enforcement. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] And Smith reports that in October 1963, as evidence of foreign control accumulated, the FBI offered to help enforce the order. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order]

Meanwhile, according to some accounts, the Council temporarily stopped distributing certain publications funded from abroad, the Near East Report among them, to meet the department's interim demands, while its core domestic work continued with little interruption. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] Kenen's own affidavit to the Senate committee put the end of the Council's purchases of that newsletter in 1962. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026]

Did the Council register? Almost every account says it did not. Smith and Arikat both write that the order was never enforced to completion and the Council never registered. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:rs_cw5_arikat_2026] [cite:acjna_fara] One source says otherwise. A version of the Wikipedia article on the Council, as it stood on 7 March 2016, said that President Kennedy and the Attorney General "forced the AZC to register as a foreign agent". [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc_2016] No primary document reached for this volume supports that statement, and it conflicts with every other source. It is recorded here so the reader who meets it knows it is the outlier. The Justice Department's own report on the Act for 1964 does not mention the American Zionist Council at all. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964]

Which body was which. The most consequential confusion in this story concerns names. Three bodies were involved in 1962, and the sources do not agree on how the third of them stood.

The first was the American Zionist Council itself. Founded in 1949 as a tax-exempt umbrella body representing nine nationwide Zionist organizations, it was led by figures including Louis Lipsky. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc] [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] It is the body the order named. It did not end in 1963: according to Powerbase, it was renamed the American Zionist Federation in May 1970 and the American Zionist Movement in 1993. [cite:rs_cw5_powerbase_azc] Smith's statement that the Council fought the order before it eventually shut down should be read against that continuity. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast]

The second was the Jewish Agency's American Section, the registered foreign agent that paid the money.

The third was Kenen's lobbying committee, and here the accounts divide. One account holds that AIPAC began in 1951 as a subcommittee-level body under the Council's umbrella, that it was still inside the Council when the order was issued, and that in early 1963, in direct response to the order, the Council's lobbying function was separated into an independent American Israel Public Affairs Committee, presented as funded solely by private American donations and so not a foreign agent. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] A second account puts it more sharply: the Council's functions moved, weeks after the order, into a newly incorporated AIPAC, legally distinguishable from the Council. [cite:factually_aipac_fara] The 2016 Wikipedia snapshot says AIPAC incorporated separately six weeks after the order, and Smith writes that the lobbying division "split off" six weeks after it. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc_2016] [cite:rs_cw5_smith_antiwar_2013]

The other account dates the committee much earlier and treats it as separate long before 1962. The current Wikipedia article, citing Nancy Jo Nelson in the Journal of Palestine Studies (1980), dates Kenen's unincorporated American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs (AZCPA) to 1951, records that Kenen was the Council's Washington representative from 1951 to 1953, and says the Council divested itself of the committee in 1954 because its leaders did not want tax-exempt funds used for lobbying. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc] Barda, working from the Council's own records, dates the decision to create the new lobbying arm to March 1954, after Council elections on 28 February 1954, with a letter to its executive committee dated 15 March 1954. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] Kenen himself, speaking off the record to a United Jewish Appeal audience in New York on 13 September 1963, described his American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs as founded in 1953 and later renamed the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] The renaming is dated to 1959 by Wikipedia, citing Doug Rossinow in Modern American History (2018), and to 1958 by Barda. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc] [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] On either date, AIPAC bore its name three or four years before the order. And by Kenen's affidavit to the Senate committee, he had stopped his own work for the Council in July 1960. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026]

One reading reconciles them: each account dates something real. On that reading, the earlier dates mark the creation of a lobbying committee distinct from the tax-exempt Council and the adoption of its present name; Kenen, by September 1963, spoke of AIPAC as a going concern with a past. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] The 1963 date, where the sources give it, marks a further separation of whatever lobbying function the Council still held, in the weeks after the order. The two can both be true only if the Council retained some lobbying role after 1954, and the sources reached here do not establish what that role was. What they do not support is the version in which AIPAC was invented in 1963 to escape the order: the name and the committee existed before it. Nor do they support the version in which the order had nothing to do with the lobby: the Council it named had paid for Kenen's newsletter, and had employed Kenen, within the eight years the order covered. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] On the account that dates a restructuring to early 1963, the record shows a documented organizational change, on a documented timeline, in response to a documented federal order: order, then restructuring, in that order, within months. It does not show whether the change was purely cosmetic or purely substantive. That depends on whether the post-1963 funding arrangement was a genuine and complete break from foreign money or chiefly a legal repositioning, an open question that the public record reached here does not settle. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order]

How it ended. The documented sequence has an order but no recorded conclusion. Smith states that the Justice Department kept the order secret until 2008 and has never tried to enforce it since; he calls the effort "serious, but fizzled out". [cite:rs_cw5_smith_ipa_2022] The sources divide on why. Smith says the effort "gradually falls apart" after November 1963, and Arikat describes a loss of momentum under President Johnson; neither cites a document recording a decision to stop. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:rs_cw5_arikat_2026] Barda argues the opposite case: the lobby had separated from the Agency's money, whose purchases ended in 1962, and operated lawfully as a domestic lobby, so there was nothing left to enforce. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] Some writers go further and tie the end of the effort to President Kennedy's death; Philip Giraldi, writing in 2017, says only that the Council "was pressured" by the Justice Department in 1962 and 1963 and that "Kennedy's death stopped that effort". [cite:rs_cw5_giraldi_2017] [cite:crustnews_aipac_history] That is a statement of sequence, not evidence of cause, and it is examined, with the other claims of a link, in [scene:s11c|22 November 1963].

Two later attempts to compel registration, a petition in 1988 and a bill in 2024, belong to later chapters. Neither succeeded. [cite:crustnews_aipac_history]

The record leaves several things open. The text and signatory of the 21 November 1962 order are not in the sources reached for this volume, so the order's own wording of its sum and its grounds cannot be quoted. The size of the Agency's payments stands at three figures, more than $5 million in the order, $5,100,001.02 in the Senate record and $1.5 million in Barda, and they have not been reconciled. Almost everything known about the Council's fight, Rifkind's argument, Lenvin's warning, the Wall Street Journal report and the FBI's offer, rests on Smith's account, the work of an advocate for registration, and has not been independently confirmed from the documents themselves. No document shows why enforcement stopped, or that anyone decided it should. What the record does establish is narrow and solid: a federal order, on a stated date, naming a specific organization, on the ground of a specific stream of money from a body already registered as a foreign agent. That is more than the claim that the question was invented by modern critics allows, and less than the claim that the order proved what it alleged.
