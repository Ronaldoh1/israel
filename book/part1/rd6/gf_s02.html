Charity for an Army: Budgets, Defense, and Britain's Bill

In 1939 the American consul in Jerusalem filed a report on a budget. It offers one of the first clear views of how the movement paid for its defense. The familiar account says the pre-state army was funded in secret, by unknown means, and that Britain ran Palestine on the cheap. The record is more specific on both counts. The operating budgets were reported at the time. Defense money ran through named channels. And Britain itself put a figure on what holding the country cost.

The budgets can be followed from that year. After the Zionist Congress, the consul reported that the budget of the Jewish Agency, the movement's executive body in Palestine, had been set at £720,000, about $74 million today. Of that, £100,000 was for settling refugees and £80,000 for "the Jewish security service (defense)." [cite:frus_1939_ja_budget] Defense, in other words, was a budget line from at least that date.

The American share grew fast. In 1944 the Jewish National Fund, the movement's land-buying arm, spent $10.8 million raised in the United States, and the Jewish Agency and Keren Hayesod spent $14.9 million, a combined $25.7 million. The United Palestine Appeal's budget for 1945 was $35.3 million. [cite:djn_1945_upa] In 1946 the Agency said it had received $12 million from the United States the year before. It sought a budget of $56 million, about $0.93 billion today. [cite:djn_1946_budget]

Defense money had its own channels. In 1938 the Jewish National Council, the representative body of the Jewish community in Palestine, created Kofer HaYishuv. This was a levy on cigarettes, restaurant bills and luxuries, meant to fund central security and expand the Haganah, the community's underground militia. [cite:w_kofer] Ten years later the link between charity and defense was stated openly in the United States. In June 1948 a community campaign advertisement in Detroit told donors that "the United Jewish Appeal is the official American agency for aid to Haganah," and that the only channel for such aid was the local Allied Jewish Campaign. It also warned that collecting money for dissident militias was fraud. [cite:djn_1948_haganah] Charity and defense ran through the same pipe, and the campaign said so.

The other side of the ledger was Britain's. Under the Mandate, Britain's administration of Palestine, the civil budget rose from £P1.9 million in 1922–23 to £P3.2 million in 1934–35. [cite:horowitz_1937] The garrison cost £1.5 million in 1923–24. [cite:league_report_garrison] By the British government's own end-of-Mandate statement, the military forces in Palestine cost the British taxpayer about £100 million in 1945–47, about $6.7 billion today. Parliament debated keeping some 80,000 to 100,000 soldiers there. [cite:hmg_termination_1948] [cite:hansard_1947_08] About 750 British soldiers and police were killed over the period. [cite:nam_palestine]

The resulting picture is substantial but not complete. The operating budgets, the defense levy, the charity channel and Britain's cost are all documented. No audited total of the Haganah's budget is publicly available, and this book does not supply one. What can be said is that the defense effort was not financed by unknown means. It was paid for partly through a published budget line, partly through a local levy, and partly through American campaigns that called themselves the official channel of aid. Britain, for its part, was paying heavily, by its own account, to hold the territory in the Mandate's final years.
