Jacob Becomes Israel: What the Text Says

Before the word "Israel" named a movement, a territory or a state, it named a man. It is commonly assumed that the word has always simply meant the Jewish nation or the Promised Land. The text in which it first appears says something narrower and more personal. Its first appearance in the Hebrew Bible is a renaming, given to one individual after a single night.

The setting is Genesis 32. Jacob is alone at the ford of the Jabbok river on the night before he is to meet his brother Esau, whom he had deceived years earlier. Through the night he wrestles with a figure the text calls a man, an angel and, in Jacob's own words afterward, God. At the end of the struggle he is given a new name:

"Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed."

This verse, Genesis 32:28, is the word's first documented occurrence anywhere in the Hebrew Bible.[cite:genesis_32_biblegateway] The word appears 2,362 times across the Hebrew and Christian scriptures, 2,284 times in the Hebrew Bible and 78 in the New Testament, but every later use traces back to this verse. Jacob names the place of the struggle Peniel, "face of God," because, he says, "I saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared." Centuries later the prophet Hosea retold the same story (Hosea 12:3–4), which indicates that later biblical writers understood this episode as the origin of the name.

The name's construction is well established. Yisra'el combines El, the ancient Semitic word for God, with a root related to sarah, meaning to strive, contend or prevail. The standard scholarly reading is "he who struggles with God," or, if El is read as the subject, "God strives." The grammar allows both, and the text does not settle which was intended.[cite:wiki_israel_name] El was not exclusive to Hebrew; it was shared across Canaanite, Ugaritic and other Northwest Semitic languages, a point developed in [scene:s00b|the discussion of the word "Semitic"]. This derivation is not contested. It is the consistent position of Hebrew lexicons, Brown-Driver-Briggs among them, of rabbinic commentary and of Christian biblical scholarship.

The text also builds in a contrast. Jacob's original name, Ya'akov, relates to akev, "heel": he was born grasping the heel of his twin, Esau, and the root carries connotations of crookedness and of following from behind. The letters of Yisra'el can also evoke yashar, "straight" or "upright." Several Hebrew-language sources read this as deliberate: the grasping, deceptive Jacob and the upright, prevailing Israel are the same person, transformed.[cite:wiki_jacob] Before the name belongs to a nation, it describes a change in one man's character, earned through struggle.

From that point the name accumulates layers. At the moment it is given, it belongs to one person. Generations later it becomes the name of his twelve sons' descendants. Centuries after that it becomes attached to a specific territory. Only in 1948 does it become the name of a modern nation-state. Each of these is a separate, later development, and each deserves to be tracked on its own rather than read backward into the verse as though they had always been the same thing.

Reading the verse in its original context makes no claim about how later biblical authors, ancient Israelite communities or modern political movements would use the word. Those uses are real and documented, and they are taken up elsewhere in this book. What the primary text establishes is the starting point: a personal name, given after a night of wrestling, meaning one who has struggled with God and with men and prevailed.
