The Allon Plan and the Bloc of the Faithful

Between 1967 and 1977 Israel was governed by the Labor movement, and the government's answer to the whole-land demand was a plan to keep part of the land. It carries the name of Yigal Allon, who was then minister of labour and later foreign minister. It was never formally adopted, but it guided where the government settled for a decade. From 1974 a religious movement, Gush Emunim, set out to break those lines, and it inherited the cause of the movement described in the [scene:gi4_movement|previous section]. The West Bank volume gives the settlement story itself in [scene:wb3_first|its first decade] and [scene:wb4_movement|after 1977]; this section reads both programmes for what they said about borders.

The plan, 1967–1968

Allon put his first version to the cabinet on 27 July 1967. It proposed Palestinian autonomy in two large enclaves of the West Bank, and on 30 July most ministers rejected it. In early 1968 he revised it, dropping the Palestinian option in favour of a Jordanian one. Israel would keep the Jordan Valley, from the river up to the eastern slopes of the hill ridge, together with East Jerusalem, the Etzion bloc and, in the 1968 version, part of the Hebron hills. The densely populated hill country would go back to Jordan, joined to it by a corridor through Jericho. [cite:gi4_wiki_allon] [cite:wb_wiki_allon] Sinai was mostly to return to Egypt. For the Golan, Allon floated the idea of a Druze state on the Syrian side. Gaza he first wanted annexed and later placed in a Jordanian-Palestinian state. [cite:gi4_wiki_allon]

The plan's logic was the reverse of the manifesto's. It was built, in one summary of it, "to include as few Arabs as possible in the areas claimed for Israel": territory for depth and security where few Palestinians lived, and none of the towns where most did. Israeli leaders of the period, by the same account, ruled out absorbing the West Bank's population into a greater Israel because doing so would change the state's Jewish majority. [cite:gi4_wiki_allon] It did not start from a renunciation, though. The Israeli journalist Reuven Pedatzur reported that in June 1967, before drafting it, Allon had said that "the last thing we must do is to return one inch of the West Bank." [cite:gi4_wiki_allon] Both readings belong in the record. The plan kept roughly a third of the West Bank, and it was also the first official Israeli design to give most of it back.

The plan put to Hussein

The plan was put to King Hussein of Jordan, in secret. The United States followed the talks from May to November 1968. According to the State Department's own documentary history, "In November the Israelis proposed an agreement based upon the 'Allon plan,' which posited Israeli control over the West Bank, while returning political control of some two-thirds of a demilitarized West Bank to Jordan." Hussein grew sceptical that Israel would offer terms he could accept, and he eventually broke off the talks. [cite:gi4_frus_d186] Jordan's stated position was Resolution 242's principle that territory cannot be acquired by war. Under the plan Jordan would have recovered the towns while Israel kept a 10–15 kilometre strip along the river, most of the Judean Desert and East Jerusalem, which was never open for discussion. [cite:gi4_wiki_allon]

No Israeli cabinet ever formally adopted the plan, but in the first decade of occupation settlement largely followed its map. Settlements went into the Jordan Valley as lookouts on the strip to be kept, and around Jerusalem, away from the Palestinian towns; Kiryat Arba, approved in 1970 beside Hebron, marked its western edge. [cite:gi4_wiki_allon] [cite:wb_wiki_allon] Allon held to the plan in office. As foreign minister he told President Ford on 11 October 1976 that "we need some changes in the Green Line," and that within Israel "the debate is about giving less, not more." [cite:gi4_ford_allon1976] In Foreign Affairs that October he set out the strategic zone he wanted, which the former Israeli ambassador Dore Gold later put at about 700 of the West Bank's 2,100 square miles. Gold notes that Allon's thinking shaped later plans of both Labor and Likud, and that in 1997 Netanyahu called his own map "Allon-Plus." [cite:gi4_jcfa_gold]

Gush Emunim, 1974

The challenge to Allon's lines came after the October 1973 war, and from a different quarter. Gush Emunim, the "Bloc of the Faithful," was founded in the spring of 1974; the name was coined in February at the home of Rabbi Haim Druckman. Its founders were rabbis, religious veterans of the war and activists of the National Religious Party's Young Guard, most of them influenced by Rabbi Zvi Yehuda Kook, the same rabbi who had signed the 1967 manifesto. Hanan Porat and Moshe Levinger led it with Druckman, and it called itself "nonpartisan, extra-parliamentary." [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:wb_ej_gush] Its stated aim, in Lustick's account, was the permanent incorporation of the whole Land of Israel into the state, together with a transformation of Israeli society. It opposed Palestinian autonomy and saw settlement as a way to block it. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] The religious teaching behind it is the subject of [scene:gi11_religious|Part Eleven].

Its first campaign was against withdrawal. When the government negotiated the 1974 disengagement with Syria, the Gush backed a hunger strike against a withdrawal from Quneitra. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] It then went to the hills the Allon Plan had left for Jordan. It made eight attempts to settle near Nablus in 1974 and 1975, and the army removed seven of them. At Hanukkah 1975 about 2,000 supporters gathered at the old railway station at Sebastia. After a long confrontation with Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin's government and the defence minister, Shimon Peres, the settlers were allowed to stay in the nearby army camp at Kadum. A march of some 20,000 people in May 1976 showed the size of its following. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:wb_ej_gush] A Labor government had set the Allon lines; a Labor government also let the first settlement outside them stand.

The handover, 1977

By the time the Likud won the election of 1977, Gush Emunim had absorbed the activists of the older movement, which Lustick says had "virtually ceased to exist." [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:gi4_biu_goldstein] The new government gave official status to several Gush settlements. In September 1977 Ariel Sharon, the agriculture minister, put forward a plan for more than a million Jews in the West Bank within twenty years. Lustick counts about $400 million spent between 1977 and 1981 and the settler population rising from roughly 3,500 to 18,500, though Peace Now's series puts the 1977 starting point at 4,400. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:wb_ej_gush] [cite:wb_peacenow_pop] The alliance had limits. In 1979 the Gush attacked Begin's government for giving up Sinai and for its autonomy plan. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush]

The decade sorts into three kinds of material. The documented facts are a manifesto that stated a whole-land programme; a government plan, never adopted, that set out to keep about a third of the West Bank and hand back the populated rest; and a religious movement whose stated aim was the whole land. The pattern that can be shown over time is that unauthorized settlements, starting with the Hebron hotel in 1968 and Sebastia in 1975, ended in compromises that left the settlers in place. [cite:wb_wiki_hebron] [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] Whether that pattern reflected a hidden consent inside the Labor governments, or a weak government's failure to enforce its own map, is an argument that the documents cited here do not settle. The record above shows that the government's stated policy, through 1977, was partition along the Allon lines and not the whole land.
