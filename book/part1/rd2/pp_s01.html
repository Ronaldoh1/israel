A National Movement: Congresses, Delegations and a Demand for Elections

The objections that Britain set aside in Paris and London came from somewhere. A persistent claim holds that before Israel existed the Palestinians had no leaders, no national identity and no political demands. The record of the first years of British rule says otherwise. From the end of the war Palestinian Muslims and Christians organized together, held a series of national congresses, elected an executive, sent delegations to London and put a clear demand in writing: a government elected by the people of the country. Britain answered in writing, and refused. This section follows that movement from its first demonstrations in 1918 to the dissolution of its executive in 1934, with its rivalries as well as its demands.

The movement's first organizations were the Muslim-Christian Associations, or Muslim-Christian Societies, local bodies that joined members of the two communities. They were organized in the country's leading towns between 1917 and 1920; one account places their formation at the end of 1918, and they grew into a body with branches in many towns. [cite:rs_mca_ips_christianity] [cite:rs_mca_ifpo] When and where the first association was founded has not been established in the sources gathered here. Their aims were plain from the start: they pressed the Peace Conference and Britain to repeal the Balfour Declaration, and they called for a united Syria. [cite:rs_mca_ips_christianity]

How the associations began is itself disputed. One account, from a 2019 conference on a century of Palestinian nationalism, describes them as a Palestinian initiative, formed to counter British "divide and rule" and to present a united Palestinian front. [cite:rs_mca_ifpo] Another, from the Institute for Palestine Studies, records that the British military administration encouraged their establishment, and that Muslim leaders welcomed Christian participation as a sign of a united Arab opposition. [cite:rs_mca_ips_christianity] The two accounts need not exclude each other, and the sources do not resolve them.

The first public test came on the first anniversary of the Balfour Declaration. In November 1918 the associations organized counter-demonstrations against the anniversary celebrations. On 3 November the mayor of Jerusalem, Musa Kazim al-Husseini, led a demonstration that ended at government house with a written protest. [cite:rs_mca_naser_2015] Musa Kazim would chair the movement's executive from 1920 until his death in 1934.

The First Palestine Arab Congress met in Jerusalem from 27 January to 10 February 1919, with 27 delegates from Muslim-Christian Associations across the country. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jvl_palestine_arab_congress] It was timed to coincide with the Paris Peace Conference, so that Palestinians could submit counter-claims to the Zionist claims being made there. [cite:rs_mca_ifpo] According to the Jewish Virtual Library's account, the congress resolved that Palestine was part of Arab Syria and cabled the Peace Conference opposing the Balfour Declaration. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] What happened to Palestine's case in Paris is told in [scene:pc_s01|the section on the peace conference].

Between 1919 and 1928 seven congresses met, in Jerusalem, Jaffa, Haifa and Nablus. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jvl_palestine_arab_congress] They called for Palestine to be part of the promised independent Arab state, and for "a national government responsible to a representative assembly" chosen by the country's Arabic-speaking inhabitants. They rejected the Balfour Declaration and mass Jewish immigration. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive]

By the time the third congress met, the British had removed Musa Kazim from office. After the April 1920 clashes in Jerusalem, the authorities accused him of inciting the violence, dismissed him as mayor and appointed Raghib al-Nashashibi, head of a rival leading family, in his place. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] The clashes themselves are the subject of [scene:ri_s01|the section on the riots of 1920 and 1921].

The Third Palestine Arab Congress met in Haifa in December 1920. It declared the Balfour Declaration null and void, demanded an end to Jewish immigration, and created a nine-member Arab Executive Committee, with Musa Kazim al-Husseini as its chairman, a post he held until his death in 1934. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] At the same congress the Muslim-Christian Association framework became the main organisational structure of the national movement. Musa Kazim was its president, and its vice-presidents were two Christians: Ya'qub Farraj of Jerusalem and Tawfiq Abdallah of Acre. [cite:rs_mca_ips_christianity] The leadership itself joined the two communities.

In 1921 the executive began to deal directly with the British government. Musa Kazim led delegations to Winston Churchill, then Colonial Secretary, in Cairo and in Jerusalem. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] According to the Jewish Virtual Library, Churchill met the Arab Executive in Jerusalem on 28 March 1921. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] The Fourth Congress, meeting in Jerusalem in 1921, then sent a delegation led by Musa Kazim to London, to persuade Britain to abandon the Balfour Declaration policy. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] The sources give three different dates for that congress: 29 May to 4 June 1921 in the PalQuest encyclopedia; simply May 1921 in a 2015 study of Musa Kazim by Naser and Abu Hilal; and 25 June 1921 in a Jewish Virtual Library article that credits Wikipedia. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:rs_mca_naser_2015] [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress]

A photograph in the Palestinian Museum Digital Archive identifies the members of the London delegation: Musa Kazim al-Husseini, Tawfiq Hammad, Ibrahim Shammas, Shibli al-Jamal, Amin al-Tamimi and Mu'in al-Madi. The archive dates the photograph 1920, earlier than the delegation's journey as other sources describe it. [cite:rs_mca_palarchive_photo]

The delegation's exchange with the Colonial Office was later published by the British government, and it shows both sides' positions in their own words. On 21 February 1922 the Palestine Arab Delegation, in a letter signed by Musa Kazim al-Husseini as President and Shibly Jamal as Secretary, rejected the draft Order in Council. It demanded a "national independent Government," and it stated that the Balfour Declaration could not be a basis for discussion. [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] It invoked Article 22 of the League of Nations Covenant in making that demand. [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700] The legal question this raised is examined in [scene:blg_s01|the section on Article 22].

The Colonial Office's answers were direct. On 1 March 1922 it replied that "the creation at this stage of a national Government would preclude the fulfilment of the pledge" made to the Jewish people. On 11 April 1922 it wrote that there could be "no question of rescinding the Balfour Declaration." [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] In the Colonial Office's own words, then, a national government was refused because of the pledge.

The delegation came home without a concession. Musa Kazim concluded, in a telegram, that the cause would not find justice "at the hands of the British government which is under Zionist influence." [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] That was his judgment. The documents record what Britain refused; they do not by themselves establish why, beyond the reason the Colonial Office gave in its own letters. Britain's upper house had in fact voted against the Mandate in its present form in June 1922, on 20 or 21 June by the differing sources, but the House of Commons upheld the government's policy on 4 July; that parliamentary episode is told in the section on Article 22. [cite:rs_mca_spectator_1922] [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:rs_mca_langworth]

The movement's next step was a boycott. According to the Jewish Virtual Library and to Naser and Abu Hilal, the Fifth Congress opened in Nablus on 22 August 1922 and resolved to reject the new constitution and to boycott the elections to the Legislative Council that Britain had proposed. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] [cite:rs_mca_naser_2015] By the same accounts, the Sixth Congress met in Jaffa in June 1923. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] [cite:rs_mca_naser_2015] The council itself, the terms on which it was offered and the boycott's result belong to the section on the Legislative Council.

Britain recognized the Zionist Organization's Jewish Agency. The Palestinians' own executive was never recognized by the British authorities at all. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] The movement could petition, protest, travel to London and boycott, and Britain declined to create the elected government it asked for.

Its later history, which runs past this chapter, shows both the movement's persistence and its divisions. Rivalry between Husseini-aligned and Nashashibi-aligned factions split Palestinian politics; the seventh congress, in 1928, briefly formed a united front. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] In 1932 the Arab Executive launched a "Piaster Project," a fund to keep land in Arab hands. In 1932–33 the more radical Istiqlal, or Independence, party attacked the leadership's inaction. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] In October 1933 Musa Kazim was injured by British forces at a protest against immigration in Jaffa. He died in March 1934, and the Executive dissolved that year. The Arab Higher Committee formed in 1936. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

The record therefore answers the claim it began with. Before the revolt of 1936 there were some fifteen years of congresses, petitions and delegations, led by named men and organized across religious lines, asking for an elected government. What the record leaves less clear is the movement's inner life. The sources here say little about how congress delegates were chosen, how widely the associations reached beyond the towns, or how far the Husseini–Nashashibi rivalry was a contest of families and how far a contest of policy. They establish what the movement asked for and what Britain answered. They do not yet tell the full story of how Palestinians argued among themselves about what to do when the answer was no.
