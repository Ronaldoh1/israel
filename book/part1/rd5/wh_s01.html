Inside the White House: Niles, Clifford and the Yom Kippur Statement

On 4 October 1946, the eve of Yom Kippur, Harry Truman issued a statement on Palestine that would echo through the next two years. A common account holds that his Palestine policy was made on its merits, by the State Department. The record tells a different story: a policy shaped in significant part inside the White House, by named advisers, with timing that critics at the time tied to the electoral calendar.

The first of those advisers was David K. Niles. He served in the White House from 1942 to 1951, under Roosevelt and then Truman, and he was one of only two Roosevelt aides Truman kept on. Niles was entrusted with minority affairs. He was also a committed Zionist and an important channel through which American Zionists reached the White House. [cite:wiki_david_niles] [cite:hnn_clark_truman] A second adviser, Max Lowenthal, counseled the special counsel Clark Clifford on Palestine. Truman's old friend and former business partner Eddie Jacobson offered a third, separate channel, described in [scene:s08|the previous chapter].

The clearest test of how these influences worked came with that October statement. Its date was the eve of Yom Kippur, and it fell a month before the midterm elections. In it Truman regretted the adjournment of the London conference on Palestine and urged immediate large-scale immigration. He also said that the United States could support a solution along the lines of a viable Jewish state "in an adequate area of Palestine." [cite:avalon_yomkippur_1946] [cite:nuremberg_yomkippur] It was a public signal that an American president could support a Jewish state, given the year before the question went to the United Nations.

The British reaction was sharp. Prime Minister Attlee was furious. Truman wrote to him that he "very much" regretted any embarrassment. [cite:frus_truman_attlee_1946]

Observers at the time drew their own conclusions about the timing. On 7 October, James Reston wrote in the New York Times that the President "went against his advisers' foreign policy and chose to follow the promptings of those who were primarily interested in retaining Democratic majorities in Congress." [cite:palestinestudies_christison_truman] Truman rejected that reading. In a statement in February 1947 he called the impression that partisan and local politics drove American policy "most unfortunate and misleading," and said his position had been consistent since 1945. [cite:jvl_truman_feb1947]

The two accounts can be weighed, but these sources cannot fully settle them. What is on the record is who had access to the President and when he chose to speak: a Zionist aide in the White House, an adviser counseling Clifford, a personal friend with an open door, and a statement issued on the eve of a religious holiday a month before an election. What remains contested is motive. Named sources argue both sides, Reston on one and Truman's own statement on the other. The honest answer may be that conviction and electoral interest worked together rather than against each other.

The statement also had a precedent of its own. Earlier in 1946, an official Anglo-American inquiry had heard Zionist, Arab and binationalist cases and produced recommendations that Washington used only in part. That inquiry, and what the White House chose to keep from it, is the subject of the next chapter.
