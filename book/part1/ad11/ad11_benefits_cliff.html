The Benefits Cliff

The modern safety net was rebuilt around one promise: that work should always pay. On 17 February 1993, asking Congress to expand the Earned Income Tax Credit, President Bill Clinton put it plainly: "if you work 40 hours a week and you've got a child in the house, you will no longer be in poverty." [cite:ad1_clinton_1993] For a family with low earnings, the promise largely holds; the tax credit adds money to every dollar earned. The trouble comes a little higher up, where a family that has climbed out of the poorest bracket starts to lose help from several programs at once. This section explains how that happens, measures how often it bites, and sets out the evidence on the other side.

The central measure is the effective marginal tax rate: the share of an extra dollar of earnings that a family loses, counting both the taxes it pays on that dollar and the benefits it gives up because its income rose. A rate of 30 per cent means the family keeps 70 cents of the dollar. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, which builds the most widely used tools for modelling this, distinguishes two cases. On a benefits plateau, lost benefits erase most of a raise. On a benefits cliff, a raise or promotion leaves the family financially worse off, because benefits fall by more than earnings rise: the effective rate on that step is above 100 per cent. [cite:ad11_atl_what] [cite:ad11_atl_bridge]

How the phase-outs stack

Each benefit program was written by a different Congress, for a different purpose, with its own income test. Some taper smoothly; some stop at a line. Take a single parent with two children, earning enough to be on the downward slope of the Earned Income Tax Credit in 2026. That credit pays 40 cents for each dollar earned up to $18,290, reaching a maximum of $7,316, then shrinks once income passes $23,890 until it disappears at $58,629. [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] The arithmetic of the shrinking part: $7,316 spread over the $34,739 between $23,890 and $58,629 is a loss of 21.06 cents of credit for each extra dollar. The payroll tax takes another 7.65 cents. [cite:ad11_ssa_2026] So before any other program is counted, this parent keeps about 71 cents of each extra dollar.

Food assistance comes next. The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) assumes that a household can spend 30 per cent of its net income, income after the program's deductions, on food, and reduces the benefit by that amount; most households must also have gross income below 130 per cent of the federal poverty line to qualify at all. [cite:ad11_irp_snap] For a household of three in the 48 contiguous states and the District of Columbia, that line is $2,960 a month for the year that began on 1 October 2026, and the maximum benefit is $808 a month. [cite:ad11_fns_fy27] If every extra dollar counted fully toward net income, SNAP would take up to 30 cents more, bringing the combined rate to as much as 58.7 cents in the dollar before any state income tax; deductions make the SNAP share somewhat smaller in practice. These are smooth slopes. The cliffs come from programs that end at a line.

The lines

Childcare subsidies are the steepest. Under the federal Child Care and Development Fund (CCDF), eligibility ends at no more than 85 per cent of a state's median income (states may set the line lower), and in many states that point is a sheer drop: the family goes from a subsidised copayment to the full price of care. [cite:ad11_atl_bridge] The Department of Health and Human Services' policy office (ASPE) measured what that means. Using 2014 data and a standard simulation model, it found that families receiving childcare subsidies with incomes between 100 and 149 per cent of the poverty line faced a median effective rate of 67 per cent on a $2,000 raise, 73 per cent on a $5,000 raise and 79 per cent on a $10,000 raise. On the $5,000 raise, the median family kept $1,350. Among subsidised families between 100 and 199 per cent of poverty, a $10,000 raise cost 37 per cent of them their entire subsidy, a median loss of $4,400 a year. [cite:ad11_aspe_ccdf] A 2024 federal rule had capped family copayments at 7 per cent of income; a new rule published on 12 May 2026 and effective 13 July 2026 repealed the cap. [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2026]

Health coverage has its own lines. Adults in states that expanded Medicaid qualify up to 138 per cent of the poverty line. [cite:ad8_kff_obbba_prov] Above that, marketplace coverage under the Affordable Care Act is subsidised, and the enhanced premium credits in place from 2021 removed the old upper limit. Those enhanced credits expired at the end of 2025; the House passed a three-year extension on 8 January 2026, 230 to 196, but as of October 2026 no extension has become law. [cite:ad8_astho_ptc] [cite:us16_ib_subsidy] The cliff at 400 per cent of the poverty line therefore returned in 2026: $84,600 for a household of two. For a 63-year-old couple in Charleston, West Virginia, earning $84,500, the benchmark silver plan costs a little over $700 a month after the subsidy. At $85,000 they receive nothing, and the cheapest bronze plan costs $3,648 a month. The arithmetic: $500 more income moves the cost of the cheapest coverage to $43,776 a year, against about $8,400 a year for a better plan just below the line. [cite:ad11_hi_cliff]

Other limits work on savings rather than income. Supplemental Security Income, the federal benefit for poor disabled and older people, is denied to anyone with more than $2,000 in countable assets, or $3,000 for a couple, limits unchanged since 1989, and it reduces the benefit by a dollar for every two dollars earned above $65 a month. [cite:ad5_cbpp_ssi] A bill sponsored by senators and representatives of both parties, the SSI Savings Penalty Elimination Act, would raise the asset limits to $10,000 and $20,000; as of October 2026 it has not passed. [cite:ad5_ffyf_ssi2026]

A worked example

The Atlanta Fed's Career Ladder Identifier and Financial Forecaster (CLIFF) follows hypothetical families through real program rules. In one published case, a single mother of two in Clarke County, Georgia, works near the minimum wage as a concessions worker and trains toward nursing. Moving from concession work to certified nursing assistant leaves her more than $11,000 worse off in net resources over the first three years; moving from nursing assistant to licensed practical nurse costs more than $35,000 over the first three years, because hours fall while she studies and benefits fall away as pay rises. Over a lifetime, the Atlanta Fed finds that both steps pay off. [cite:ad11_atl_what] Families respond to these sums: in a 2021 survey of Florida businesses cited by the Atlanta Fed, about one in three reported employees or candidates turning down jobs, hours or promotions for fear of losing benefits. [cite:ad11_atl_action]

Where it works as promised

Cliffs are real, but they are not the typical experience of a low-wage worker. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that workers below 450 per cent of the poverty guidelines faced an average effective marginal rate of 31 per cent in 2016, counting income and payroll taxes, the SNAP phase-out and health-insurance subsidies, with wide variation at the lowest earnings. [cite:ad11_cbo_emtr] Even among families receiving childcare subsidies, ASPE found that a $2,000 raise pushed 10 per cent over a cliff, while 15 per cent faced a negative rate, gaining more than the raise because a credit was still phasing in. [cite:ad11_aspe_ccdf] At the bottom of the scale, the Earned Income Tax Credit works exactly as Clinton described: on the way up it adds 40 cents to each dollar earned. [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] Whether it draws more people into work is disputed. A long-standing consensus held that it raised employment among single mothers substantially; a 2024 reappraisal by Henrik Kleven found that apart from the 1993 expansion, later expansions show no clear, robust effect on employment, with about 500 estimates centred on zero. [cite:ad11_kleven_eitc] States have begun to file down the edges, through calculators that show families their options, income disregards in Indiana and Maine, the removal of welfare asset limits in Colorado and Massachusetts, gradual childcare phase-outs in Florida, Maine and Vermont, and transition payments in Ohio and Tennessee. [cite:ad11_atl_bridge]

What changed in 2025–2026

The 2025 reconciliation law, signed on 4 July 2025, extended SNAP's work requirement to adults aged 18 to 64 and narrowed the exemption for parents to those with children under 14; from 1 October 2026 states pay 75 per cent of SNAP's administrative costs, and from fiscal 2028 they pay up to 15 per cent of benefits depending on their error rates. As of October 2026 the work-requirement changes are in force and the administrative cost shift has just begun. [cite:ad11_mn_snap] [cite:ad11_ncsl_snap] From 1 January 2027, adults covered through the Medicaid expansion must show 80 hours a month of work, community service, training or half-time study, or income of at least $580 a month, which is 80 hours at the federal minimum wage of $7.25. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] Those rules add a cliff at the bottom as well as the top: a worker whose hours fall below the threshold can lose coverage, just as a worker whose pay rises above 138 per cent of the poverty line already loses Medicaid and must move to marketplace coverage with a premium.

Interpretation, not finding: the cliff as a ceiling

The Sophia platform reads the benefits cliff as the clearest case of the Dream's promise working in reverse: a set of rules that holds families near the bottom of the wage scale by charging the highest marginal rates in the country on the act of climbing, while the people at the top face lower rates on income from assets. Read this way, the cliff is part of the same structure as the regressive state and local taxes and the fines described earlier in this part.

The strongest counter-readings are these. First, the cliffs come from a trade-off rather than a plan: aid aimed at the lowest incomes must stop somewhere, and a slower taper reaches families further up the income scale and costs more, so every design chooses between steep rates, high cost and narrower help. Second, the programs were written separately, by Congresses of both parties over six decades, and no document shows them designed to interact; the stacking is what happens when they are not coordinated. Third, average rates are around 31 per cent, not above 100, and the long-run returns to advancement are positive even in the Atlanta Fed's hardest cases. [cite:ad11_cbo_emtr] [cite:ad11_atl_what] The record supports the narrower claim: at specific income lines, chiefly childcare, Medicaid and marketplace eligibility, a family that earns more can end the year with less, and the 2025–2026 changes added new lines without removing old ones.
