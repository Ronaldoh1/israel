Eviction and the Traps: When Housing Extracts

For most owners, the house is the asset the Dream promised: a store of savings that grows while the family lives in it. For a large minority of households, housing works the other way. Rent takes a growing share of income, a court filing follows the tenant from one application to the next, and some buyers who cannot get a mortgage sign contracts under which they can pay for years and own nothing. This section sets out four of these mechanisms in turn: the eviction filing and the record it leaves, tenant screening, the contract for deed, and shared pricing software. Each is scoped to what the documents show, and each is set beside the evidence that cuts against the strongest reading of it.

The scale of eviction

An eviction filing is a landlord's lawsuit asking a court to remove a tenant; it is not the same as an eviction, because many cases are settled, dismissed or end with the tenant paying. A study of court records by Ashley Gromis and colleagues, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2022, estimated that landlords filed about 3.6 million eviction cases a year on average between 2000 and 2018, against about 2.7 million households; the difference reflects landlords filing repeatedly against the same tenants. The share of renter households threatened with eviction peaked at about 7.5 per cent in 2005 to 2008 and fell to about 6 per cent in recent years. [cite:ad6_nlihc_pnas] The lab's tracking system, which covers 48 cities and states holding about a third of renter households but is not a national sample, counted 1.23 million filings in 2025, down from 1.26 million in 2024 and 3.2 per cent below the 2023–24 average. The filing rate across those sites was 8.0 per cent, about one filing for every 13 renter households. [cite:ad6_el_2025]

The sociologist Matthew Desmond made eviction a national subject with Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City (2016), which followed eight families in Milwaukee in 2008 and 2009. [cite:ad6_wiki_evicted] It won the 2017 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction; the board's citation called evictions "less a consequence than a cause of poverty". [cite:ad6_pulitzer] That causal claim is the contested part. The columnist Christian Schneider, writing in the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, argued that eviction "simply exacerbates the existing dysfunctions" in people's lives rather than causing their poverty. [cite:ad6_wiki_evicted] From the other side, a group of housing researchers and organisers argued in Shelterforce in 2018 that Princeton's Eviction Lab undercounts evictions, a gap they tied to its not working with local housing-justice organisations. [cite:ad6_shelterforce] The count is therefore disputed in both directions, and the direction of cause is disputed too; the existence of millions of filings a year is not.

The record that follows the tenant

A filing does not end when the case does. Court records are bought in bulk by tenant-screening companies, which sell landlords reports on applicants. Adam Porton, Ashley Gromis and Matthew Desmond examined more than 3.6 million eviction records from 12 states and found that, on average, about 22 per cent contained ambiguous information on how the case was resolved or misstated the tenant's history. [cite:ad6_porton] A figure often attributed to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), that it found "22 per cent of eviction records are false", misstates the source: the CFPB's November 2022 report on the screening market cites that 22 per cent from the Porton study, and itself says there is "no definitive analysis of the frequency of matching errors" in the industry. [cite:ad6_cfpb_tbc]

What the CFPB report does document is how errors arise. Some screeners search court records using only partial names, a practice it calls "wildcard" matching; some include sealed records; a single case moved between courthouses can appear as two evictions. Automated pulls of court records rarely cost the screener more than $1, against about $7 for a manual check, and only five of the 17 firms the bureau examined advertised manual verification. [cite:ad6_cfpb_tbc] The report also notes that serial filings exceed 20 per cent of cases in at least four states and cites a Federal Reserve study finding that corporate landlords file at "substantially higher" rates. [cite:ad6_cfpb_tbc] In October 2023 the Federal Trade Commission and the CFPB charged TransUnion's rental-screening unit with reporting single cases as several evictions, omitting dismissals and labelling landlords' claims as court judgments; the companies agreed to pay $15 million, $11 million of it to consumers, the largest tenant-screening recovery in the FTC's history. [cite:ad6_ftc_turss]

The contract for deed: paying without owning

A contract for deed, also called a land contract, is an arrangement in which the seller keeps legal title to the house while the buyer makes a down payment and monthly instalments; the buyer receives the deed only after the last payment, pays the taxes, insurance and repairs meanwhile, and builds no equity along the way. One missed payment can mean eviction and the loss of everything paid. [cite:ad6_duke_plunder] It flourished where mortgages were refused. A 2019 study by Duke University's Samuel DuBois Cook Center on Social Equity and Chicago partners, built on the case files of the Contract Buyers League, a group of West Side homeowners who sued in the late 1960s, estimated that 75 to 95 per cent of homes sold to Black families in Chicago in the 1950s and 1960s were sold on contract. Sellers marked the homes up 84 per cent over what they had paid, and buyers paid about $587 a month more, in 2019 dollars, than they would have under a fairly priced FHA or conventional loan. [cite:ad6_duke_plunder] [cite:ad6_wbez_plunder]

The device returned after the 2008 crash. Harbour Portfolio Advisors bought foreclosed homes in bulk, mostly from Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, and between 2012 and 2016 resold several thousand of them on contracts for deed to buyers unable to obtain conventional financing. [cite:ad6_cfpb_harbour] In Atlanta, homes bought for as little as $10,000 were resold for $40,000 to $50,000 at about 10 per cent interest over 30 years, and Atlanta Legal Aid found the sales concentrated in census tracts that were 86 per cent African American; its suit on behalf of 17 buyers was settled with restructured loans and damages. [cite:ad6_atlegal_harbour] The CFPB's 2020 settlement with Harbour concerned credit-reporting practices, not the contracts themselves, and carried penalties of $25,000 and $10,000. [cite:ad6_cfpb_harbour] A Pew Charitable Trusts survey published in 2022 estimated that about 36 million Americans had used some form of alternative home financing, including land contracts, and about 7 million were in such arrangements at the time; most such deals are not recorded with any public office and are not regulated as mortgages are. [cite:ad6_npr_pew] This is the clearest case in the housing record of captured friction: an arrangement that exists to serve buyers outside the mortgage system and, where it is unregulated, transfers their payments to the seller.

Pricing by software: the RealPage case

Rents can also be raised by coordination. On 23 August 2024 the Justice Department and eight states sued RealPage, a Texas company whose software recommends rents to landlords. The complaint alleged that competing landlords fed the software nonpublic data on their own rents, that RealPage's "auto accept" feature and pricing advisers encouraged them to follow its recommendations, and that the software tended to push rents up and discourage concessions; it alleged RealPage held about 80 per cent of the market for such software for apartment buildings. [cite:ad6_doj_realpage24] These were allegations, and the company's later settlement with the government contains no admission of wrongdoing. [cite:ad6_wsoc_realpage]

Under the proposed terms filed on 24 November 2025, RealPage agreed to stop using competitors' nonpublic data in its live pricing, to train its models only on such data at least 12 months old, to stop modelling at any level narrower than a state, to remove features that limited rent cuts, and to accept a court-appointed monitor. [cite:ad6_doj_realpage25] A federal judge in North Carolina approved the judgment in May 2026. [cite:ad6_wsoc_realpage] Separately, in the private class action brought by renters in Tennessee, 41 landlords agreed to pay $359,925,000, and the court gave final approval in October 2026; no court has ruled on how much the software raised rents. [cite:ad6_mlex_class] New York's state ban on algorithmic rent-setting was blocked by a federal preliminary injunction in late September 2026 while RealPage's free-speech challenge proceeds. [cite:ad6_dive_ny]

The end of the line

Homelessness is the point at which these mechanisms end for some households. HUD's count on a single night in January 2025 found 745,652 people homeless, 266,320 of them unsheltered, down 3 per cent from the record 771,480 of 2024; chronically homeless individuals reached a record 155,750, while homeless veterans fell to 32,495, the lowest since 2009. [cite:ad6_crs_homeless26] [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] The count is a one-night snapshot and excludes people at imminent risk of losing their housing. HUD's current leadership attributed the 2025 decline to falls in "Sanctuary Cities" and said the "housing first" approach had failed; that is the department's stated position, and Part Fourteen weighs the evidence on it. [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] [scene:ad14_homelessness|Part Fourteen: homelessness]

Interpretation, not finding: the eviction machine

The Sophia platform reads these mechanisms together as an "eviction machine": a system in which rent extracts income, filings and screening records lock tenants out of better housing, and the loss falls on the same households that federal policy once excluded from ownership. Read through this lens, the contract for deed and algorithmic pricing are two forms of the same transfer, from people without access to credit to those who hold property. The strongest counter-readings are these: the mechanisms have different owners and different legal status, and no document shows them operating as one design; eviction filings fell in 2025 and rents fell where supply grew; and each mechanism above has drawn a legal answer, from the Fair Housing Act to the TransUnion and RealPage settlements, which is the system correcting itself, if slowly. The record supports the narrower claim that each mechanism, where unregulated, moves money from tenants and excluded buyers to landlords, sellers and data firms.
