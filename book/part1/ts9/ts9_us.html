The American Side: Votes, Pulpits, Sales and Donors

On the American side the return is spread across several hands: a party that won a reliable bloc of voters, preachers and publishers whose ministries and books found a mass market, and donors who funded the organisations in between. This section takes each in turn, keeping three kinds of statement apart: what the polls and filings count, what named people said their reasons were, and what critics and defenders argue those reasons really are. [scene:ts9_israel|Israel's side]

The vote

White evangelical Protestants have been the most solidly Republican religious group in recent presidential elections, by every measure available. The national exit poll conducted for CNN found 80 per cent of white born-again or evangelical voters backing Donald Trump in 2016, 76 per cent in 2020 and 81 per cent in 2024. [cite:ts9_rns_exit2024] The Pew Research Center's panel, which re-interviews the same people, put the figure at 77 per cent in 2016 and 84 per cent in 2020, while the share of white adults calling themselves evangelical rose from 25 to 29 per cent. [cite:ts9_bng_pew2021] Pew's study of about 7,100 validated voters found white evangelical support in 2024 "no different" from 2020, and the political scientist Ryan Burge, using the Cooperative Election Study, put it at 83 per cent, the highest on record. [cite:ts9_rns_pew2025] [cite:ts9_ru_burge2025] The surveys define the group differently, and Burge warns that exit polls have structural flaws; the figures agree on the order of magnitude, roughly four in five, not on the decimal.

What the numbers do not show is why these voters chose as they did. None of the polls cited asks whether Israel decided a vote. The firmest evidence on belief is LifeWay's 2017 survey of Americans with evangelical beliefs, in which 80 per cent said Israel's founding in 1948 fulfilled prophecy but only 23 per cent backed a treaty for a Palestinian state, 31 per cent opposed it, and 46 per cent were unsure. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] What is documented is that at least one candidate drew the connection himself. At a rally in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, on 17 August 2020, Trump said of moving the US embassy to Jerusalem: "That's for the evangelicals," adding that "the evangelicals are more excited about that than Jewish people." [cite:ts9_almon_trump2020] That is a documented statement of his stated reason in a campaign setting. It is not proof that evangelical pressure caused the 2017 decision, a causal claim taken up in Part Twelve. [scene:ts12_embassy|The embassy move]

Pulpits and publishers

For preachers and publishers the return was a market. Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth sold an estimated 35 million copies by 1999 and has been described, in secondary sources, as the best-selling nonfiction book of the 1970s; Tim LaHaye and Jerry B. Jenkins's Left Behind novels, published by Tyndale House between 1995 and 2007, sold some 80 million copies by one count. [cite:ts7_wrap_lindsey] [cite:ts7_sa_lahaye] [scene:ts7_media|Spreading the system: books and broadcasting] Sales on that scale are revenue for authors and publishers and a sign of demand; they do not by themselves show that buyers adopted the books' politics.

The ministries that raise money for Israel also form an economy of their own. MinistryWatch found that the International Fellowship of Christians and Jews spent more than $19 million on fundraising in 2019, paid one direct-response agency, The Bigham Agency, nearly $8 million in 2018, and paid its founder more than $700,000 a year. [cite:ts9_mw_ifcj] [cite:ts9a_mw_ifcj] For the larger pastor-led ministries this volume found no audited, named-source revenue figure. John Hagee's ministries are reported to have given $100 million to Israeli charities, but their own income was not found in a filing or a named report consulted here, and CUFI's budget is treated in Part Eight. [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021] [scene:ts8_orgs|The organisations] Claims about pastors' personal fortunes that circulate on net-worth websites are not evidence and are not used.

Donors

The documented donor link runs mainly through one family. According to +972 Magazine's review of the Adelson Family Foundation's tax filings, Sheldon Adelson gave CUFI $4.6 million from 2012 and about $22.1 million to the Maccabee Task Force, a campus anti-boycott group he funded from its creation in 2015 and which is led by David Brog, CUFI's former executive director. [cite:ts9_972_adelson] Sheldon and Miriam Adelson spent $123 million on the 2018 midterm elections, nearly all of it benefiting Republicans, and in 2024 Miriam Adelson gave $5 million to the Republican Jewish Coalition's super PAC and was the largest individual donor to outside groups. [cite:pm_sludge_2019] [cite:pm_israelite_2024] These are funding relationships in the record. The figures for CUFI and Maccabee rest on one outlet's reading of the filings and were not re-checked against the returns for this volume. The +972 writer, Alex Kane, argued that Adelson's legacy was to fuse evangelical and Jewish right-wing politics; that is his interpretation, not a finding of the filings. [cite:ts9_972_adelson] [scene:pm3_donors_scale|Follow the Money: donors]

Motive: two arguments

Why American Christian Zionists back Israel is argued, not settled, and both sides are named here. The critical argument is put most sharply by the journalist Sarah Posner, in writing quoted to her on Democracy Now in November 2023: "At the heart of Christian Zionism is not a love for Israel but rather Christian nationalism." In her account Christian Zionists have merged support for Israel with being a good American Christian; Hagee's movement, she said, gives Israel's defenders a constituency with "the ear of the Republican Party," and treats Israel as "just a pawn" in a prophecy that ends with Jesus ruling the world, and he has "hoodwinked many Jews" into believing he supports Israel. [cite:ts9_dn_posner] [cite:ts9a_dn_posner] CUFI's answer is on the record too. Its spokesman Ari Morgenstern wrote in 2010 that most Christian Zionists back Israel because of the promise in Genesis that God will bless those who bless the Jewish people, and argued that end-times belief cannot be the motive because Hagee and other premillennialists read Matthew 24:36 as teaching that no human can hasten the end. [cite:ts9_jta_morgenstern2010] Its executive director David Brog told a San Francisco synagogue in 2017 that "Christians support Israel for the most noble of motives," that the support "starts with the Bible, but it does not end with the Bible," and that evangelicals rejected replacement theology and CUFI's members, knowing Jews better than other Christians did, were more sensitive to Jewish aversion to proselytising. [cite:ts9_j_brog2017] [cite:ts9a_j_brog2017]

Neither argument can be proved from the documents gathered here, and the volume does not adjudicate between them. Posner's describes an organisation and its leader, not the beliefs of every member; CUFI's describes the stated theology of its leaders, not the private reasons of every person it counts. What the record does establish is narrower. A large, reliably Republican voting group exists; one president said in public that a major Israel decision was made "for the evangelicals"; prophecy books sold in the tens of millions; and a small number of named donors and charities moved documented sums in both directions.
