Two Economies, and the Aid Behind Them

By the 2020s the economies of Lebanon and Israel were not in the same class. The gap widened in two steps: Lebanon's own financial collapse from 2019, which began four years before the war with Israel, and then the damage of the wars of 2023–24 and 2026. This section sets out both, then sets the armies' budgets and their foreign aid side by side.

Lebanon's collapse. Lebanon's economy was worth about $55 billion in 2018. In 2019 its banking system and currency collapsed, and by 2023 its output, measured in dollars, was just over $20 billion: it had lost almost two-thirds of its value in five years, before the war with Israel began. [cite:hz9_ifl_rdna] Measured in real terms, the World Bank estimated that the economy shrank by about 40 percent between 2019 and 2024. [cite:hz9_wb_rdna] The crisis reached the state's own army. By early 2022 the pay of a junior Lebanese soldier had fallen to about $100 a month, one-eighth of its pre-crisis value, and in 2020 the army stopped serving meat to its troops. [cite:hz9_fdd_laf] Part Six covers the crisis, the protests of 2019 and Hezbollah's place in the political system that presided over it. [scene:hz6_state|Hezbollah and the state]

What the 2023–24 war cost Lebanon. The World Bank's Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment, published on 7 March 2025 and covering 8 October 2023 to 20 December 2024, put the war's cost to Lebanon at $14 billion: $6.8 billion in physical damage and $7.2 billion in lost economic activity. Housing took the heaviest damage, $4.6 billion; commerce, industry and tourism lost $3.4 billion. Real output fell 7.1 percent in 2024, against 0.9 percent growth the Bank estimated without the war. It put recovery and reconstruction needs at $11 billion, of which $3 billion to $5 billion would have to come from public money. [cite:hz9_wb_rdna] The Lebanese finance ministry's institute, summarising the same assessment, found that the Nabatiyeh governorate in the south bore 47 percent of the damage, and that $8.4 billion of the needs were immediate, for 2025 to 2027. [cite:hz9_ifl_rdna] [cite:hz9_lol_rdna] The war's $14 billion cost equals about two-thirds of Lebanon's entire output in 2023. A comparable World Bank assessment for the March–April 2026 war had not been located for this book as of October 2026; its human toll is set out in Part Eight. [scene:hz8_war2026|The 2026 war]

Israel's economy. Israel's economy, by World Bank figures, produced $513 billion in 2023, $542 billion in 2024 and $611 billion in 2025: it grew through two years of war on several fronts. [cite:hm_fred_ilgdp] In 2023 it was about 25 times the size of Lebanon's. Israel's arms industry alone exported a record $14.8 billion in 2024, nearly three-quarters of Lebanon's whole 2023 output; Part Eleven examines who gains from the wars. [cite:hm_globes_exports] [scene:hz11_gains|Who gains]

The aid to the two armies. The United States funds both armies, on very different scales. Israel receives $3.8 billion a year under the 2016 memorandum, at least $21.7 billion more in military aid in the two years after 7 October 2023, and a cumulative total since 1946 that the Congressional Research Service puts at about $174 billion in current dollars. [cite:hm_dn_mou] [cite:hm_an_costsofwar] [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] The Lebanese Armed Forces have received, by the State Department's figure in May 2021, more than $2.5 billion in American security assistance since 2006, an average of roughly $200 million a year in military and economic aid together; in 2021 the United States added packages of $105 million, $15 million and $67 million as the army's pay collapsed, and the administration requested $160 million for 2022. [cite:hz9_fdd_laf] The figures this book could verify for Lebanese army aid stop in 2022; they are drawn from a 2022 study by the Foundation for Defense of Democracies, a Washington think tank that argued for reassessing the aid, which quotes the State Department's own totals. [cite:hz9_fdd_laf]

The two programmes rest on different legal footing. Since 2008, US law has defined Israel's "qualitative military edge", requires it to be assessed every four years, and bars arms sales to other states in the region that would adversely affect it. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] No comparable statutory guarantee applies to Lebanon; its aid is set year by year, and the FDD study argued that it should be reassessed altogether. [cite:hz9_fdd_laf] Part Eight sets out the Lebanese army's 2025 plan to disarm Hezbollah and how far it went. [scene:hz8_disarm|Disarmament]

What the gap does not mean. Lebanon's poverty is not only a product of Israel's wars: by the World Bank's figures, most of the loss in its economy's dollar value came between 2019 and 2023, before the war began. [cite:hz9_ifl_rdna] Part Six covers the domestic causes. [scene:hz6_state|Hezbollah and the state] Nor does the gap settle the question of who threatens whom: a weaker force can still do great harm, as Part Seven records for northern Israel. [scene:hz7_front|The support front] Israel's and Hezbollah's stated reasons for their arsenals, and how those claims compare with outcomes, are tested in Part Eleven. [scene:hz11_pattern|The pattern] What the numbers do show is that the contest is between a state with one of the world's largest military budgets, supplied by the United States, and an armed movement inside a state whose whole economy is smaller than that budget.
