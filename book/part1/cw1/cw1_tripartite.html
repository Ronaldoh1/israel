The Tripartite Declaration, 25 May 1950

The last of Truman's decisions on the region traced in Volume II was a joint one. In May 1950 the United States, Britain and France issued a declaration on the Arab states and Israel, closing the sequence of choices told in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's 1948–50 decisions]. This section reads that declaration from its text, sets beside it the American policy paper it was written to carry out, and then follows how the governments of the region received it. Each step turns out to be more qualified than the declaration's reputation as a Western guarantee suggests.

The three governments issued the declaration on 25 May 1950, after their foreign ministers had met in London; the tripartite communiqué of that meeting was dated 13 May 1950. The text was printed in the Department of State Bulletin of 5 June 1950, at page 886. [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_tripartite] [cite:rs_cw1_avalon_tripartite] It was a statement by three governments, not a treaty: no state of the region was a party to it, and its commitments ran from the three powers outward to the states it named.

The declaration had three operative paragraphs, and each did something different.

The first paragraph dealt with arms. It accepted that the Arab states and Israel needed armed forces for internal security and self-defence. It reaffirmed the three governments' opposition, already stated at the Security Council on 4 August 1949, to an arms race between them. And it set a rule for the suppliers themselves: "All applications for arms or war material for these countries will be considered in the light of these principles." [cite:rs_cw1_avalon_tripartite] [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_tripartite] The paragraph did not ban arms to the region. It made each sale a matter for the three governments' judgment, measured against two tests, internal security and self-defence, and against a third aim, the avoidance of an arms race. The lever was supply: so far as these three governments were the sellers, the decision on each request was theirs.

The second paragraph attached a condition to the sales. It recorded that recipient states had given assurances that the purchasing state "does not intend to undertake any act of aggression against any other state", and said that similar assurances would be sought from future recipients. [cite:rs_cw1_avalon_tripartite] The obligation here fell on the buyer. A state that wanted arms from the three powers had to promise in return not to use them in aggression.

The third paragraph was the one that gave the declaration its weight. In it the three governments declared their "unalterable opposition to the use of force or threat of force" between states in the area. They pledged that if any state were preparing to violate frontiers or armistice lines, they would act immediately, within and outside the United Nations, to prevent it. [cite:rs_cw1_avalon_tripartite] [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_tripartite] Two features of the wording mattered. The pledge covered "frontiers or armistice lines" together, so that it protected the armistice lines of 1949 as well as recognized frontiers. And the promise to act "outside the United Nations" as well as within it meant the three powers did not make their response depend on a vote in the Security Council.

Behind the public text stood an American policy paper. NSC 65/3, "United States Policy toward Arms Shipments to the Near East", was approved as policy on 19 May 1950, six days before the declaration. The Under Secretary of State's first progress report on it, dated 7 August 1950, says that the declaration was drafted at the London meeting in line with that policy. It states the rule plainly: American policy allowed only equipment needed "to help the states there to maintain internal order and provide for legitimate defense," while seeking to "avoid being drawn into any arms race in the area." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1950_nsc65] The declaration's first paragraph is that rule, made joint with Britain and France and stated to the world. Read together, the two documents show the mechanism. The American decision came first, in a classified paper; the London meeting turned it into a common position of the three Western suppliers; and the public declaration announced the terms on which the region would be armed.

The same progress report records how the governments of the region took it, and its account is mixed on both sides. Voices in the Arab press criticized the declaration on the ground that it "froze" the territorial status quo. Yet the Council of the Arab League issued a joint commentary on 12 June 1950 that the report calls generally favourable. As for Israel, the report presents it as pleased: Israel, it says, welcomed the declaration as recognizing Israel as "a permanent element in the Near East." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1950_nsc65]

The Arab criticism and the Israeli welcome, as the American report recorded them, were two readings of the same paragraph. A pledge to act against any violation of the armistice lines protected whoever held the ground behind them. To critics in the Arab press, that meant accepting as permanent the outcome of the war of 1948. To Israel, on this account, it meant that the three Western powers now treated the state as a fixture of the region rather than a passing fact.

A second account of Israel's reaction contradicts the first. Avi Shlaim writes that Israel rejected the declaration as unfavourable. He cites David Ben-Gurion's diary entry of 30 May 1950, five days after the declaration, which accused it of permitting arms to the Arab states while denying them to Israel. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Each account has real strength. The American report is a contemporaneous official document, written for the National Security Council within three months of the event. Ben-Gurion's diary is the private record, written within the week, of the man who led the Israeli government. It is possible that both are accurate, that a government welcomed in public what its leader resented in private. But neither source says so, and the material here does not allow the two to be reconciled. On the evidence available, Israel's reception of the declaration is disputed.

Ben-Gurion's complaint went to the heart of the first paragraph. The declaration did not divide arms between the two sides by any formula. It left each application to the judgment of the three suppliers. A government that believed the suppliers' judgment would favour its neighbours had reason to read the declaration as a restraint on itself, whatever its third paragraph promised about the lines.

One Arab government asked the Americans directly what the declaration meant. In June 1950 Charles Malik, Lebanon's minister, asked the State Department for clarification. Raymond Hare answered: "There was, of course, no intent to recognize spheres of influence." He added that the declaration's wording "should not be given a legalistic interpretation." Malik, for his part, called the declaration potentially the three powers' most important contribution to peace, if it was implemented with "honest and sustained impartiality." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1950_malik] The exchange shows two concerns on Lebanon's part, one about Western dominance and one about even-handedness, and it shows the American answer: the text was a statement of policy, not a legal instrument to be read clause by clause. That answer cut both ways. It reassured Malik that the three powers claimed no sphere in the region. It also told him that the pledge in the third paragraph was not to be read as a binding legal obligation.

The declaration came only weeks before the outbreak of war in Korea. Its premise, that the three Western powers could manage the region's arms and lines among themselves, sat beside an Israeli policy that still claimed friendship with Moscow as well as Washington. Within weeks Israel would take the Western side at the United Nations, as told in [scene:cw1_korea|the Korean decision]. The declaration itself outlived the administration that made it: in 1953 Secretary of State John Foster Dulles said, as reported at the time, that the United States would stand by it. [cite:rs_cw1_djn_dulles_1953]

What the record here does not settle is as important as what it does. It does not show whether the third paragraph's pledge was ever invoked in these years, or what "immediate" action the three governments had in mind. It does not show how the arms rule was applied to particular requests from Israel or from the Arab states, which is the question on which Ben-Gurion's complaint turned. And it does not resolve the conflict between the American report and Ben-Gurion's diary over how Israel received the declaration. The text is clear; its working, in these first years, is not documented in the sources available to this book.
