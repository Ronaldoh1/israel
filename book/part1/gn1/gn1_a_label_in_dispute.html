A Label in Dispute: Was There Ever "Gnosticism"?

Before going further, a reader should know that the word in this book's title is contested by the scholars who know the subject best. The noun "Gnosticism" is modern. It was coined in the seventeenth century by the English philosopher Henry More, and for most of the time since it has been used as if it named a single religion with a fixed set of beliefs. [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] The ancient evidence does not support that picture, and in the last thirty years two major studies have argued that the category should be taken apart.

The first was Michael A. Williams's Rethinking "Gnosticism": An Argument for Dismantling a Dubious Category, published by Princeton University Press in 1996. Williams argued that the term gives "the false impression of a monolithic religious phenomenon", and that "the modern construct 'gnosticism' is not justified by any ancient self-definition". [cite:gn_pup_williams] The groups grouped under the label never agreed on a shared set of beliefs, he showed, and the features said to define them, such as hatred of the body, either asceticism or libertinism, do not fit the texts found at Nag Hammadi. Their opponents tended to name them after their leaders, Valentinians, Basilideans, Carpocratians, and the general label was largely a heresy-hunter's tool. [cite:gn_wiki_rethinking] Williams proposed a more exact name for what many of the groups shared: "biblical demiurgical traditions", that is, readings of the Bible that distinguish the creator of the material world from the highest God. [cite:gn_wiki_rethinking]

The second was Karen L. King's What Is Gnosticism? of 2003, which likewise questioned whether the category described anything the ancient groups would have recognised. [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] Other scholars keep the term but narrow it. David Brakke and Simone Pétrement, among others, treat the Gnostics as a movement inside early Christianity rather than an older religion that Christianity absorbed, and James M. Robinson, the editor of the Nag Hammadi library in English, judged that a pre-Christian Gnosticism is "hardly attested". [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] [cite:vt24_brit_gnosticism]

Some of the groups did use the word of themselves. Irenaeus reports that the Carpocratians "style themselves Gnostics", and scholars today use the modern category "Sethian" for a family of texts, including the Apocryphon of John, Zostrianos and Allogenes, that share a myth: an unknown God, a first female emanation called Barbelo, and Sophia's act that produces the flawed creator Yaldabaoth. [cite:vt24_ah1_25] [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism] The Valentinians, the other great family, developed a more moderate version, in which the creator is ignorant rather than evil. [cite:vt24_ect_flora]

This book keeps the word "Gnostic" because readers know it and because some of the groups claimed it, but it uses it in the narrower sense the evidence allows: for teachers and texts that taught salvation through knowledge and distinguished the true God from the maker or ruler of this world. Where a claim applies only to one school, the book names the school.

The dispute over the label is not a scholarly footnote. It is part of the story this book tells. The single, sinister "Gnosticism" of the textbooks was largely the creation of the men who wanted it suppressed, and later of scholars who inherited their categories along with their quotations. Recovering the actual teachers, as the next part does, means looking past the name their enemies gave them.
