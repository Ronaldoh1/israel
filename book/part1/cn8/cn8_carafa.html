The Carafa Index: A Pope, His Nephews and the List of Banned Books

A canon defines what must be read as scripture. The Index of Prohibited Books defined what must not be read at all. The first Roman Index, issued in 1559, was the work of a pope whose reign shows, in a single household, every element of the system this book has traced: zeal for orthodoxy, the use of the office to enrich and empower relatives, and a violent end that the institution later admitted was unjust.

Gian Pietro Carafa was elected pope as Paul IV on 23 May 1555, through the influence, according to his Wikipedia biography, of Cardinal Alessandro Farnese, the grandson Paul III had made a cardinal at fourteen. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] [cite:cn_wiki_paul3] Carafa had long been the hardest man in the Curia. As early as 1542 he had ordered the confiscation of the works of many authors, Erasmus among them. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] Within two months of his election, on 17 July 1555, he issued the bull Cum nimis absurdum, which confined the Jews of Rome to a walled quarter with a single gate locked at sundown, later known as the Roman Ghetto, required them to wear yellow hats and limited them to one synagogue per city. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4]

His Index followed. Under his authority all books written by Protestants were banned, together with Italian and German translations of the Latin Bible; a first draft was finished in 1557 and the list was promulgated by the Inquisition in 1559. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] [cite:vt14_brit_index] The ban on vernacular Bibles is the point at which this book's two threads meet. Toulouse in 1229 had kept the scriptures from the laity of one region. The Roman Index of 1559 kept translations of the Bible itself, the Bible whose list Trent had just fixed, out of the hands of Catholics in Italy and the German lands, and put them on the same list as the works of the reformers.

While Paul IV policed the books of Christendom, his own family ran his government. On 7 June 1555 he raised his nephew Carlo Carafa to the cardinalate, and Carlo became Cardinal Nephew, directing papal policy. [cite:cn_wiki_ccarafa] Carlo's brother Giovanni was made Duke of Paliano. [cite:cn_wiki_ccarafa] In January 1559 the pope, persuaded by accusations against them, publicly disgraced his nephews and banished them from Rome. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] [cite:cn_wiki_ccarafa]

The pope died on 18 August 1559. The people of Rome had already begun to riot. They put a yellow hat on his statue on the Capitol, decapitated it after a mock trial and threw it into the Tiber. They freed more than four hundred prisoners from the city's jails, then attacked the palace of the Inquisition, killed the inquisitor Tommaso Scotti, freed seventy-two prisoners and set the building on fire, destroying its records. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] Carlo Carafa reached Rome on 19 August and had his uncle buried that night at ten o'clock for fear that the crowd would desecrate the body. [cite:cn_wiki_paul4] The hat the rioters placed on the statue was the yellow hat the pope had forced on the Jews of Rome four years earlier.

The next pope, Pius IV, turned on the family. In June 1560 he had Carlo, Giovanni and their nephew arrested and their papers seized. On the night of 6 March 1561 Carlo and Giovanni were strangled in Castel Sant'Angelo; contemporaries largely regarded the execution as political. [cite:cn_wiki_ccarafa] Six years later, on 26 September 1567, Pius V declared the sentence unjust, and "the memory of the victims was vindicated and their estates restored". [cite:cn_wiki_ccarafa] Within twelve years a papal family had governed the Church, been disgraced by its own head, been executed by the next pope and been rehabilitated by the one after that, and its estates had been taken and given back.

This was the household that gave the Catholic world its first list of forbidden books. The Index outlived the Carafa by four centuries, and its later history, told in the next section, was more bureaucratic than violent. Its beginning belongs in this book because it shows, as clearly as any episode since Damasus, that the people who decided what Christians could read were not a body apart from the struggles for wealth and position around them. They were at the centre of those struggles.
