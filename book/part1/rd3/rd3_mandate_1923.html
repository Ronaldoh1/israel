How the Mandate Became Law, 1923–1925

By the summer of 1922 Britain had governed Palestine for nearly five years. Its forces had taken Jerusalem in December 1917, and the text of the Mandate had been approved by the Council of the League of Nations. Yet two legal steps were still missing. The state that had ruled the country for four centuries had not yet given it up, and the Mandate had not yet formally entered into force. Between 1923 and 1925 both gaps were closed, or so the standard account holds, and the Mandate government then defined, for the first time, who was a citizen of Palestine. Each step has a date, a document and, in at least one case, a serious scholarly challenge.

The first step was taken at Lausanne. The peace treaty between the Allies and Turkey was signed there on 24 July 1923 [cite:rs_rd3_lausanne_fcdo]. In it the new Republic of Turkey, founded that year, renounced all rights over the former Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire [cite:w_lausanne]. The treaty made modern Turkey the empire's successor state, not a claimant to Palestine [cite:w_lausanne]. Professor John Quigley stresses a further point about the wording: the renunciation was not made in favour of Britain or of the Allies [cite:rs_rd3_quigley_2026]. Turkey gave up its rights; it did not hand them to anyone. The treaty did not take effect at once. It entered into force on 6 August 1924 [cite:rs_rd3_abualia_khalil] [cite:rs_rd3_quigley_2026].

The map above follows Turkey's long relationship with the land and, later, the state, from four centuries of Ottoman rule through Lausanne to the present. For this chapter only its second step matters: the renunciation that closed the Ottoman claim.

The second step was the Mandate's entry into force, and here the record holds two accounts. The League Council had approved the mandates for Palestine and Syria on 24 July 1922, on a condition: they would take effect once France and Italy reached a final agreement over Syria [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force]. One scholarly source gives a different date for that approval, 12 August 1922 [cite:rs_rd3_abualia_khalil]; the UN's document gives 24 July [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force]. At the Council's meeting of 29 September 1923, according to the minutes as reproduced by the United Nations, the mandates for Syria and Palestine "had thereby automatically and simultaneously entered into force," and the British representative, Sir Rennell Rodd, said the question was "finally settled" [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force]. On this account, 29 September 1923 is the date the Mandate became law. Britain, the League Secretariat and, in 1947, the United Nations all treated it as such [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force].

Quigley disputes it. He argues that the Council minutes used future-tense wording and that the Council took no resolution. In his reading, the past-tense formula, that the mandates had entered into force, was supplied afterwards by the League's Secretary-General, Eric Drummond, in his Circular Letter 101. If that is right, the 29 September 1923 date has no legal force, and since Lausanne did not take effect until 6 August 1924 and gave Britain no title in any case, Britain held Palestine without title [cite:rs_rd3_quigley_2026]. Quigley's argument was published in July 2026; it is a challenge to the standard date, not yet a settled finding.

Each side has a real case. The standard date rests on a primary document, the minutes, and on the conduct of the bodies involved: Britain administered the Mandate, the League supervised it, and the UN in 1947 started from that date. Quigley's case rests on the same kind of evidence read more closely: the tense of a verb, the absence of a resolution, and the question of who changed the wording. The practical history of the Mandate did not wait on the answer; the legal question remains open in the sense that the argument has been made and not, in the sources used here, answered.

The third step was to decide who belonged. The Palestinian Citizenship Order was issued on 24 July 1925 and took effect on 1 August 1925. It made Palestinian citizens of Ottoman subjects habitually resident in Palestine on that date [cite:rs_rd3_abualia_khalil]. Two groups stood outside that rule. The first were Arabs who had emigrated abroad: the Order, Lauren Banko notes, denied them citizenship [cite:rs_rd3_banko_podcast]. The second were immigrants, who could acquire citizenship by naturalization.

How naturalization worked in practice is known mainly through one study. According to Nadia Abu Alia and Asem Khalil, the Government of Palestine reportedly accepted about 100 of some 9,000 naturalization applications in one period, using an "absolute discretion" clause. By 1946, on their figures, about 130,000 foreigners had acquired Palestinian citizenship by naturalization, 99 percent of them Jews [cite:rs_rd3_abualia_khalil]. The two figures describe different things: the first a single period of tight refusal, the second the cumulative total over two decades. The sources do not say which period the first figure covers or whose applications were refused.

The historian Lauren Banko reads the Order as a first step and as a contrast at once. It was, she notes, the first Mandate legislation to treat the Arab population as citizens rather than as "ex-enemy Ottoman subjects" [cite:rs_rd3_banko_podcast]. But it treated Jewish citizenship and the naturalization of immigrants in sharp contrast to Arab citizenship, in Banko's account, and Palestinian Arabs answered it by invoking Ottoman-era principles of descent and birthplace [cite:rs_rd3_banko_podcast]. Descent and birthplace would have reached the emigrants abroad, whom the Order excluded. Read beside Banko's argument, the naturalization figures reported by Abu Alia and Khalil, in which 99 percent of those naturalized by 1946 were Jews, suggest which way the contrast ran in practice; that is a reading of the two sources together, not a finding stated in either [cite:rs_rd3_banko_podcast] [cite:rs_rd3_abualia_khalil].

The Mandate text's own provisions on nationality, passports and currency are set out in [scene:s07|the Mandate text] and are not retold here. Some things remain unsettled. Whether the Mandate ever validly entered into force is argued, not decided. The sources do not give the number of Arab emigrants who lost a claim to citizenship under the 1925 Order, nor the year-by-year breakdown of naturalizations, nor, for the reported period when about 100 of 9,000 applications were accepted, who the applicants were. What is documented is the sequence: a treaty that ended the Ottoman claim without transferring it, a League record whose wording is contested, and a citizenship law that, by 1946, had naturalized about 130,000 people, almost all of them Jews.
