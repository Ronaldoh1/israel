The Observer Effect, Read Correctly

In quantum mechanics a particle such as an electron or a photon is described by a wave of possibilities. Left alone, the possibilities can interfere with one another, which is what produces the famous stripes of the double-slit experiment. Once the particle is measured, the interference disappears and a single definite result appears. That disappearance is the observer effect, and the popular version of it says that a conscious watcher, by looking, makes reality choose.

The physics does not say that. What destroys the interference is interaction: the particle becoming entangled with something else, a detector, a stray molecule of air, a photon of heat, in a way that leaves a record of which path it took. The process is called decoherence. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes the environment as interacting with a system "in such a way that it could in principle be used as a measuring apparatus". [cite:ob_sep_decoherence] Nothing in that definition requires an eye or a mind. A dust grain or a Geiger counter does the job, and in a laboratory it does it constantly; keeping a quantum state undisturbed for any length of time is one of the hardest problems in building a quantum computer.

Decoherence explains why the world we see looks solid and definite. It does not settle everything. The same encyclopedia entry is careful to say that "decoherence as such does not provide a solution to the measurement problem": it explains why the possibilities stop interfering, but not why one particular outcome is the one we find. [cite:ob_sep_decoherence] That remaining question is where the interpretations of quantum mechanics divide, and it is the opening through which consciousness was first brought into physics.

The correct sentence is therefore narrower than the popular one, and stranger. Observation changes a quantum system because observation is a physical interaction, and the universe is full of such interactions with nobody watching. What remains open is not whether a mind is needed to make particles behave, but why a world of spreading possibilities yields the single world that minds then find.
