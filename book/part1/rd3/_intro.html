Between 1922 and 1936 the Zionist project in Palestine stopped being chiefly a matter of declarations and diplomacy and became a matter of building. The British Mandate now governed the country, and inside it the movement assembled the working parts of a state that did not yet exist: a labor federation with its own bank, construction firm and health fund; a Jewish Agency widened to take in wealthy American non-Zionists; a banking channel chartered in London; an electricity monopoly whose grid followed Jewish settlement. Over the same years Britain counted the country's people, investigated its violence, and once tried to slow the pace of immigration and land transfer, only to reverse itself within months.

The period was not one of unity. Jewish opposition to Zionism existed inside Palestine and was argued to the British; one of its spokesmen was shot dead in Jerusalem in 1924. The movement itself split, and the Revisionist break of 1925 produced both the paramilitary organizations of the 1930s and 1940s and the institutional line that later became Likud. In 1929 violence at Hebron removed a Jewish community present there for centuries; organized Jewish life in the city did not resume until 1968. And from 1933 everything was shadowed by Germany, where a democracy was voted out of existence, a transfer agreement with the new regime divided the Jewish world, and the Nuremberg Laws gave persecution the form of statute.

This stage asks a single question: what was actually built, by whom, and on what terms, in the years when the state-in-waiting took shape?
