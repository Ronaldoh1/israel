The Pius XII Files: What Six Years of Research Have Shown

No pope's record has been argued over more than that of Eugenio Pacelli, Pius XII, who reigned from March 1939 to October 1958. The question is what he knew about the murder of Europe's Jews, when he knew it, and why he did not condemn it by name. For decades the debate rested on the Holy See's own selection of wartime documents and on the archives of other governments. On 2 March 2020 the papers of his whole pontificate were opened. This section reports what has been found since, who found it, how the Holy See has answered, and what remains in dispute. The wartime narrative itself, and its bearing on the Holy See's attitude to Zionism, is told in Part Sixteen. [scene:vt16_war_and_pius_xii|Part Sixteen: the war and Pius XII]

What was opened

Before 2020 the main published source was the Holy See's eleven-volume edition Actes et documents du Saint Siège relatifs à la Seconde Guerre mondiale, prepared by Jesuit historians, whose first volume appeared in 1965. [cite:vt14_vn_napolitano2022] [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] Critics had long pointed out that it was a selection made by the institution under examination.

Pope Francis announced on 4 March 2019 that the full papers would open a year later. He said he trusted serious research to show that the pope's decisions, in "moments of serious difficulties, of tormented decisions", reflected "human and Christian prudence". [cite:vt1_va_archive2019] The opening on 2 March 2020 covered the Apostolic Archive and the archives of the Secretariat of State and other curial offices: about 120 series and some 20,000 archival units, much of it digitised, with room for about 120 researchers at a time. [cite:vt14_vn_open2020] [cite:vt14_cchq_brownfleming] The reading rooms closed again soon afterwards because of the coronavirus pandemic, so the first findings came from the few researchers who had got in during those first days. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] [cite:vt14_brown_kertzer2022]

The first finding: a report the Vatican said it could not confirm

A team from the University of Münster led by the church historian Hubert Wolf reported in 2020 on the Holy See's answer to an American inquiry of September 1942. A US diplomat had passed the Vatican a Jewish Agency report on the mass killing of about 100,000 Jews in the Warsaw ghetto and about 50,000 in Lviv, and asked whether the Vatican could confirm it from its own sources. The Vatican replied that it could not. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020]

Wolf's team found that a note confirms Pius XII read the American report, and that the Secretariat of State already held two corroborating accounts: a letter from the Greek Catholic archbishop of Lviv, Andrey Sheptytsky, describing 200,000 Jews massacred in Ukraine, and the report of an Italian businessman who had described the "incredible butchery" of Jews in Warsaw to Monsignor Giovanni Battista Montini, the future Paul VI. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] They also found a memo by Angelo Dell'Acqua, a Secretariat of State official, casting doubt on the Jewish report on the ground that Jews tended to exaggerate. Wolf said the memo was missing from the eleven-volume edition. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] The memo is one official's view on file; it rests on a stereotype about Jews held in the office that drafted the reply, and it is not a policy statement of the pope.

Kertzer's The Pope at War and the replies

David Kertzer of Brown University was among the first scholars in the reading room. His book The Pope at War, published by Random House in June 2022, drew on more than 7,000 pages of Vatican and state documents. [cite:vt14_brown_kertzer2022] He argued that Pius XII's overriding aim was to protect the institutional Church in a Europe he expected Hitler and Mussolini to dominate, not admiration for Hitler, and that memos from his aides show antisemitism among his trusted advisers. [cite:vt14_brown_kertzer2022] [cite:vt14_cchq_cucchiara] He described secret contacts with Hitler in 1939 through Prince Philipp von Hessen, who met the pope on 11 May 1939, and the Vatican's response to the SS roundup of Rome's Jews in October 1943, when officials intervened for those who were baptised or married to Catholics and made no wider protest. [cite:vt14_lrb_evans]

The British historian Richard J. Evans, reviewing the book, concluded that the pope's "main concern was always to preserve the interests of the Church as an institution". [cite:vt14_lrb_evans] Martina Cucchiara called it "an unsparing and detailed narrative" but faulted it for thin engagement with research on the rescue of Jews in Roman convents and for overstating how far the Vatican's wartime role had been forgotten. [cite:vt14_cchq_cucchiara]

The Holy See's newspaper answered as the book appeared. Writing in L'Osservatore Romano on 21 June 2022, the historian Matteo Luigi Napolitano argued that the 1939 talks were already known from the first volume of the Actes et documents, that Hitler rather than the pope had sought a new arrangement, and that Pius XII set five conditions Hitler would not accept. He cited the warning Cardinal Maglione gave the German ambassador on 16 October 1943, disputed the date of an American audience on which part of Kertzer's case rests, and called the view that Pius cared only for baptised Jews "worn-out". [cite:vt14_vn_napolitano2022]

The König–Leiber letter

In September 2023 the Italian daily Corriere della Sera published a letter found by Giovanni Coco, an archivist at the Apostolic Archive. Dated 14 December 1942, it was written by the German Jesuit Lothar König, who was linked to the Catholic resistance to Hitler, to Robert Leiber, a fellow Jesuit who was Pius XII's secretary and long-time adviser. König reported that up to 6,000 people a day, especially Poles and Jews, were being killed in the "blast furnace" at Bełżec, with transports from Rava Ruska. [cite:vt14_euronews_coco2023] [cite:vt14_cns_coco2026] The letter had been registered in the archive in 2019 and the rest of the correspondence has not been found. [cite:vt14_cns_coco2026]

The letter shows that detailed information on an extermination camp reached the pope's closest aide. It does not prove that Pius read it, and the reports are careful to say so. [cite:vt14_euronews_coco2023] Ten days later, in his Christmas radio message of 24 December 1942, Pius XII spoke of people "marked down for death or gradual extinction" because of their nationality or race, without naming the Jews or Germany. [cite:vt14_cns_coco2026] The same weeks brought British and Polish diplomatic notes reporting up to a million Jews killed in Poland. [cite:vt14_euronews_coco2023]

The "Ebrei" series

The Holy See's own contribution has been to publish one archival series in full. In June 2022, at Francis's request, Archbishop Paul Richard Gallagher announced the online release of the Secretariat of State's "Ebrei" (Jews) series: 170 volumes, nearly 40,000 files and more than 2,700 cases of Jews, baptised and not, who asked the pope for help with visas, asylum, release from detention or news of deported relatives. [cite:vt14_vn_ebrei2022] The last part went online in February 2023 with an inventory of more than 2,500 people who made requests. [cite:vt14_aleteia_ebrei2023] For most requests, the outcome is not recorded in the file. [cite:vt14_vn_ebrei2022]

Johan Ickx, head of the historical archive that holds the series, presented it in his book Pius XII and the Jews (2021) as "the list of Pius XII", evidence of an effort aimed at "saving every single human being, regardless of colour or creed". [cite:vt14_vn_ickx2021] That is the Holy See archivist's reading; the series itself documents requests and the office's handling of them, and its incomplete record of outcomes limits what it can prove about how many were saved.

What is settled and what is not

Six years of research have narrowed the old argument without ending it. On knowledge, the files point one way: by the second half of 1942 the Secretariat of State held detailed reports of mass killing from several independent sources, Pius XII read at least one of them, and his office told the United States it could not confirm what it had on file. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] [cite:vt14_euronews_coco2023] Wolf and Evans both conclude from the new material that the pope was well informed, which leaves little room for the older defence that reliable information was lacking. [cite:vt14_toi_wolf2020] [cite:vt14_lrb_evans]

On motive and on action the record is still read in two ways. Kertzer and Evans read the silence as institutional caution, the protection of the Church as an organisation. [cite:vt14_brown_kertzer2022] [cite:vt14_lrb_evans] The Holy See's stated position, given by Francis in 2019 and developed by Napolitano and Ickx, is that the pope's decisions were "tormented" acts of prudence meant to keep humanitarian help alive, and the "Ebrei" series documents that such requests were handled. [cite:vt1_va_archive2019] [cite:vt14_vn_napolitano2022] [cite:vt14_vn_ickx2021] Reviewers such as Cucchiara note that the scale of rescue in Rome's religious houses still needs to be integrated with the critical findings. [cite:vt14_cchq_cucchiara] Whether particular letters reached the pope's desk, and how far rescue was directed from the top, remain open questions that the files have not yet answered.

For this volume's central question, one gap matters most. The same opening made available the Secretariat of State's papers for 1945 to 1958, the years of partition, the founding of Israel and Pius XII's encyclicals on Jerusalem. Studies drawn from those papers are the private record against which the Holy See's public refusal of Zionism can be tested; they are taken up, so far as they have been published, in Part Seventeen.
