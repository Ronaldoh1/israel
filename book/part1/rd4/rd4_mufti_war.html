Al-Husseini's War: Exile, Iraq and Berlin, 1937–1945

The Arab Revolt left its leader outside Palestine. Hajj Amin al-Husseini had held the office of Mufti of Jerusalem since 1921 and in April 1936 became president of the Arab Higher Committee, the body that united the leaders of the rival Palestinian parties [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai]. By 1941 he was in Berlin. The path between those two points is one man's documented choices, and this section follows them in order: flight in 1937, Iraq and the collapse of the pro-Axis coup there in 1941, and the war years in Germany. It is told precisely because it is so often told loosely, in two opposite directions. Some accounts use his record to indict Palestinians as a people; others minimize it. Both misstate the record, and this section holds to what the documents show about him and only him.

From sanctuary to exile, 1937. One account has al-Husseini taking sanctuary in the Dome of the Rock on 17 July 1937 to avoid arrest by the British [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. After gunmen killed Lewis Andrews, the District Commissioner for Galilee, in Nazareth on 26 September, Britain outlawed the Arab Higher Committee, stripped al-Husseini of his official positions and deported several of the committee's members to the Seychelles [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_andrews] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_wiki_ahc]. He escaped in the autumn of 1937, to Lebanon according to Unpacked and to Syria according to the Jewish Virtual Library, a disagreement set out with the rest of the revolt in [scene:s07a5|the section on the Arab Revolt] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_andrews]. From there he moved on to Iraq [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini].

Losing his offices did not end his role. Historians document that he continued to direct the revolt from exile [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. By Unpacked's account he authorized attacks from abroad on Britons, on Jews, and on Palestinians considered collaborators [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked]. The sources gathered here establish that this direction from exile took place; they do not itemize his communications or the orders he gave. By the summer of 1939 the revolt had been suppressed.

His rivalry with the Nashashibi family followed him abroad. Fakhri Nashashibi, who had organized the British-paid "peace bands" against the rebels, was killed in Iraq in 1941 by gunmen backed by al-Husseini, according to Matthew Hughes's study of the bands. Fakhri 'Abd al-Hadi, a former rebel commander who had turned to the bands, was murdered in 1943; Hughes's account as summarized here does not name his killers [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hughes_peacebands_full].

Iraq, 1941: the coup and the Farhud. In Iraq, Rashid Ali al-Gailani led a pro-Axis coup. Its government collapsed when British forces defeated it, days before the beginning of June 1941. The sources here place al-Husseini in Iraq in this period but do not document his part in the coup itself, and this section does not supply one [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] [cite:wiki_farhud].

The collapse produced a pogrom. On 1–2 June 1941, during the Shavuot holiday, mobs in Baghdad turned on the city's Jewish community. Iraqi soldiers and police who had backed the coup, together with the pro-Axis Futuwwa youth militias, incited and led two days of killing, rape and looting, joined by civilian mobs and by Bedouin from the outskirts of the city. British forces, victorious just outside Baghdad, did not intervene [cite:ushmm_farhud] [cite:wiki_farhud]. The power vacuum left by Rashid Ali's fall, and the nationalist and pro-Axis feeling it had inflamed, produced the violence [cite:wiki_farhud]. The word sometimes used for it, "riots," understates the record: this was a targeted, organized pogrom with members of the state's own security forces among the perpetrators.

The numbers differ by source. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, the most rigorous single source used here, counts between 150 and 180 Jews killed, 600 injured, an undetermined number of women raped, and roughly 1,500 stores and homes looted [cite:ushmm_farhud]. Some other sources give higher death tolls, of 500 or more [cite:wiki_farhud]. Volume I of this library, in [scene:s00e2|its account of the Farhud], gives at least 128 and up to 180 killed and more than 1,000 injured. The counts do not agree, and the table below sets them side by side. On the consequence the Museum's account is plain: the Farhud marked the turning point after which Iraq's ancient Jewish community began its decline toward near-total emigration within a decade [cite:ushmm_farhud]. The sources drawn on here do not document a direct role for al-Husseini in the Farhud.

Berlin, 1941–1945. After his period in Iraq, al-Husseini relocated to Berlin in 1941 [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. There he met Hitler in person. He asked for German support for Arab independence and for German opposition to a Jewish national home in Palestine [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. Throughout the war he broadcast Nazi propaganda to the Arab world over German radio. He personally recruited and inspected Muslim volunteer units for the Waffen-SS, primarily in the Balkans; the scale given is thousands of volunteers for Waffen-SS divisions [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. These facts rest on German diplomatic records, wartime broadcast archives and postwar historical research, and they are not contested [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. They are his own documented, individual wartime record.

After the war. Al-Husseini escaped prosecution. He was in French custody, escaped in 1946, avoided the Nuremberg proceedings, and eventually settled in Egypt. Yugoslavia sought his extradition for war crimes connected with the SS recruitment, and it was never carried out. He went on to play a diminished but real political role among Palestinian factions into the 1960s [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini].

One man, not a people. The common claim about this record runs in one of two directions. One version treats al-Husseini's alliance with Nazi Germany as the wartime stance of Palestinian Arabs. The other treats the revolt he led as leaderless and his later choices as beside the point. The record supports neither. He was a named individual holding a specific religious and political office, who made specific, dated personal choices [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. Those choices matter to the history of the period on their own terms. They do not stand in for a population. The overwhelming majority of Palestinian Arabs were not involved in his wartime activities, had no vote in his exile-era decisions, and had no means of removing him from a religious appointment he had held since 1921 [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. The killing of Fakhri Nashashibi in Iraq shows, too, that Palestinian politics was itself divided over his leadership. The rule this book applies everywhere applies here with full force: the conduct of one person, however senior, is not the record of a people.

This section covers only the documented core of al-Husseini's war. Several parts of the fuller record have not yet been researched for this volume, and nothing about them is asserted here. They include the details of his meeting with Hitler, the particular Waffen-SS formations he recruited for, his wartime correspondence about the emigration of Jewish children, and the historians' dispute over his role in the Holocaust. The sources gathered here also do not document what he did in Iraq during the coup, whether he had any part in the Farhud, or where exactly he went when he left Palestine in 1937, on which they disagree.
