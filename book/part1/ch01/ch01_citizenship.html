Who May Become a Citizen: Return, Residence and the Marriage Ban

Arguments about Israel's character often come down to a question that can be answered from statute: who may become a citizen, and on what terms? Two laws passed in the state's first four years set the answer. One opened the door to every Jew in the world. The other decided which of the Arabs who had remained inside the new state in 1948 would be counted as its citizens. Half a century later a third measure, presented as temporary, closed a route by which Palestinians from the occupied territories could join spouses who held Israeli citizenship. Each of the three is read in two ways. Defenders call the first a law of refuge and the third a security measure; critics call the first discriminatory and the third demographic. The texts themselves, and the numbers that came with them, show what each law did and where the dispute begins.

The Law of Return, 1950. The Knesset passed the Law of Return on 5 July 1950. Its first section reads: "Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh." The second section obliges the state to grant the immigrant's visa, with only three exceptions: the minister may refuse an applicant found to be acting against the Jewish people, or likely to endanger public health, or likely to endanger the security of the state.[cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] The structure matters. The law does not create a scheme of selection under which the state chooses whom to admit. It states a right held by the applicant, and it lists the narrow grounds on which an official may refuse.

Two amendments widened and then defined that right. On 23 August 1954 the Knesset added a fourth ground for refusal, a criminal past likely to endanger the public welfare, and moved the power to decide from the Minister of Immigration to the Minister of the Interior.[cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] The larger change came on 10 March 1970. Amendment No. 2 extended the law's rights to the child and the grandchild of a Jew, to the spouse of a Jew, and to the spouses of that child and grandchild. It excluded a person who had been a Jew and had voluntarily changed religion (Section 4A). For the first time the law also said who counted as a Jew: a person born of a Jewish mother, or converted to Judaism, and not a member of another religion (Section 4B).[cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] The two clauses pulled in different directions. The definition in 4B followed the religious line of descent through the mother. The grandchild clause in 4A reached well beyond it, to people with a single Jewish grandparent who were not Jews under that definition at all.

The effect of the grandchild clause showed up decades later. According to Christian Joppke and Zeev Rosenhek, people who were not Jews under the religious definition made up nearly 20% of immigrants in 1990–94, 45% in 1997 and 53% in 1998.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek] On these figures, by 1998 a majority of the law's immigrants were not Jews under the religious definition. The same study reports that 64% of Jewish Israelis surveyed at the time supported the Law of Return.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek]

The Nationality Law, 1952. A right to immigrate is not yet citizenship. That was settled by the Nationality Law, which came into force on 14 July 1952. It created two main routes. Citizenship by return went automatically to every oleh under the Law of Return. Citizenship by residence went to a former citizen of Mandate Palestine only if three conditions were all met: he had been registered as an inhabitant on 1 March 1952; he was an inhabitant on the day the law took effect; and he had been in Israel continuously from the establishment of the state, or had entered legally.[cite:rs_ch01_nationality_1952] A third route, naturalization, was open to others on conditions that included three years' residence out of the previous five, a right to permanent residence, some knowledge of Hebrew and renunciation of any prior nationality.[cite:rs_ch01_nationality_1952]

The residence route was the one that decided the citizenship of Arabs who remained. Its conditions were written around dates and registers. A person who had stayed put throughout, and whose name was on the register by March 1952, qualified. A person who was off the register, or who had left during the fighting and come back without legal entry, did not qualify by that route, whatever his family's history in the place. The scale of what was at stake appears in Joppke and Rosenhek's figures: of the 700,000–900,000 Palestinians they count, about 170,000 remained after 1948–49.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek] Those who were outside the state when the law took effect had no path through the residence clause. The property they left behind was governed by a separate statute, told in the next volume: [scene:ap_s01|the Absentees' Property Law].

The contrast between the two routes can be stated plainly from the texts.

The demographic setting explains why the state's founders gave immigration such weight. At the 1948 census, 65% of Israel's Jewish population had been born abroad, and from 1948 to 1995 net migration supplied nearly half of Jewish population growth.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek] Immigration was not a side policy of the new state. It was one of the means by which the state's population was built.

Refuge law or discriminatory law? The dispute over the Law of Return turns on whom it admits and whom it does not. The strongest form of the defense was put by Alex Stein in 2013: the law restores Jewish access to a homeland lost since 70 CE, and it provides a refuge, because states with non-Jewish majorities did not reliably protect their Jews.[cite:rs_ch01_dailybeast_lor] On that reading the law is a response to a documented history of persecution, and its preference for Jews is its purpose, not a flaw.

The strongest form of the criticism starts from the same text and asks who is left out. Palestinian refugees and their descendants cannot return, while any Jew, anywhere, may immigrate. Critics, including Palestinian advocates, make that point, as Stein's article itself records.[cite:rs_ch01_dailybeast_lor] Adalah, the Arab-Israeli legal center, lists the Law of Return in its database of discriminatory laws; the English text used in this section comes from that database.[cite:rs_ch01_lor_text]

A third reading treats the law as neither unique nor simply explained by Jewish nationhood. Joppke and Rosenhek, writing in 2001, set Israel beside West Germany, which admitted ethnic Germans as "resettlers." About 1.4 million resettlers arrived in 1950–87 and 2.2 million in 1988–97. Germany then changed course: a 1993 law ended the status for anyone born after 1 January 1993 and capped arrivals.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek] Israel's equivalent law survived. The authors explain that survival not by ethnic nationhood alone but by two particular pressures: the demographic rivalry with the Palestinians, and the memory of the Holocaust.[cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek] Their comparison answers one common claim and complicates another. Ethnic-priority immigration was not unique to Israel. But the one comparable democracy they study wound its scheme down, and Israel did not.

The family-unification ban, 2003–2022. The third measure concerned marriage. The Citizenship and Entry into Israel Law was passed in 2003, during the Second Intifada, as a one-year temporary measure. It generally barred residence permits for Palestinians from the occupied territories who were married to Israelis, and was later extended to citizens of Iran, Lebanon, Syria and Iraq. The temporary law was extended at least 17 times.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] Its critics argue that it discriminates against Arab citizens of Israel, who are more likely than other Israelis to marry Palestinians from the territories.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship]

The courts came close to striking it down. In 2006 Israel's Supreme Court upheld the law by six votes to five in HCJ 7052/03, even though a majority of the justices found that it harmed family life and equality disproportionately. A second challenge, HCJ 466/07, failed by a single vote in 2012.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] The law then ran on for nearly another decade. In July 2021 the Knesset failed to extend it and it lapsed. On 10 March 2022, the Times of Israel reported, it was re-enacted by 45 votes to 15, passing with votes from the right-wing opposition. The new version states that its aim includes protecting Israel's Jewish majority.[cite:rs_ch01_toi_citizenship2022] [cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] After the vote, according to the Times of Israel, Interior Minister Ayelet Shaked called the result a victory for a "Jewish and democratic state" over "a state of all its citizens." Meretz and Ra'am voted against.[cite:rs_ch01_toi_citizenship2022]

Security or demography? The government's case was security. Its explanatory notes, and the State Attorney in court, argued that status gained through marriage could be exploited for terror.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] Shin Bet testimony cited about 48 such cases in 2001–2021.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] [cite:rs_ch01_toi_citizenship2022] Interior Ministry data for 2020, as summarized by the Israel Democracy Institute, give the scale around those cases: about 800–1,000 family-unification applications a year, and about 13,000 Palestinians living in Israel on permits or temporary status. Of those who had received status through family unification, 45 were linked to terror offenses between 2001 and 2020, and none in 2018–2020.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship]

The case for reading the law as demographic rests on what its supporters and its text said. Some supporters told the Knesset that the aim was to prevent large-scale Palestinian settlement in Israel; the 2022 text names the Jewish majority as part of its purpose; and, as noted above, critics argue that it discriminates against Arab citizens. Some of the justices themselves doubted the security case, given how small the numbers were.[cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] [cite:rs_ch01_toi_citizenship2022] The two readings are not exclusive. A law can answer a real, documented security risk and also serve a demographic aim, and the 2022 version says in its own words that it does the second. What the record shows is the size of the risk the government cited, a few dozen cases over two decades against some thousands of applications, and the scale of the applications and permits around them.

What the record leaves open. Several questions a reader will ask are not settled by the material behind this section. The two counts of terror cases, 45 for 2001–2020 from Interior Ministry data and about 48 for 2001–2021 from Shin Bet testimony, cover different periods and come from different bodies; this book has not reconciled them case by case. The terms of the 2022 law beyond its vote and its stated aim, including any quota and how long it runs, have not been verified here, so this section does not say whether an Arab citizen's spouse from the West Bank can obtain residence today, or on what terms. Nor does the record here document any route by which a Palestinian refugee from 1948 who was outside the state in 1952 could become a citizen; the residence route was closed by its own dates, and naturalization required a right to permanent residence, which the record here does not show such a refugee could obtain. Whether the Law of Return is best understood as refuge or as preference is a judgment the texts cannot make for the reader. They show only that it extends a right to every Jew, that the critics' objection rests on the refugees of 1948 having no equivalent right, and that the people who defend it and the people who oppose it are describing the same law.
