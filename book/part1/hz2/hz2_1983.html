The Bombings of 1983

The multinational force that came back to Beirut in late September 1982 was meant to be neutral, and many Lebanese did not see it that way. Lebanese Muslims came to believe that the force, and the Americans in particular, were "unfairly siding with the Maronite Christians". The Amal leader Nabih Berri asked the United States and France to leave. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] In 1983 the American role moved from oversight to fighting. On 8 September 1983 US destroyers offshore fired in defence of the Marines during battles around Souk el-Gharb in the mountains above Beirut. The Defense Department's commission of inquiry later recorded a "prevalent view" among American commanders that this fire was linked to the attack that followed. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] That spring Lebanon and Israel had also signed an agreement, described below. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks]

The embassy, 18 April 1983. A suicide bomber drove into the US embassy in Ain al-Mraiseh, on the Beirut seafront. The blast killed 63 people, 17 of them Americans. [cite:hz2_an_1983] [cite:hz2_rf_pence] Arab News, reviewing the attacks in 2025, gives two different figures for the Lebanese dead, 35 in its text and 46 in a photo caption, without noting the difference. [cite:hz2a_an_1983]

The barracks, 23 October 1983. At about 06:22 a truck bomb destroyed the building near Beirut airport where the US Marines were quartered. Minutes later a second bomb hit the French paratroopers' building, known as the Drakkar. The attacks killed 241 American service members (220 Marines, 18 sailors and 3 soldiers), 58 French paratroopers and 6 civilians. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] [cite:hz2_an_1983] An FBI explosives expert later testified that the blast was equal to between 15,000 and 21,000 pounds of TNT. [cite:hz2_anderson]

Who claimed it, and who denied it. Two telephone callers claimed the bombing in the name of the Islamic Jihad Organization. [cite:hz2_d1_levitt] A statement sent to the Beirut daily As-Safir claimed it for an "Islamic Revolution Movement". Syria, Iran and Amal denied involvement, and the United States first blamed Amal and its offshoot, Islamic Amal. [cite:hz2_an_1983] Hezbollah, which had not yet announced itself, later officially denied involvement while praising the "two martyr mujahideen" who carried it out. Hezbollah, Iran and Syria have continued to deny any part in the bombings. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] The US government had warning it did not use. The National Security Agency intercepted a message of 26 September 1983 from Iran's intelligence ministry to its ambassador in Damascus. It was not passed to the Marines until 26 October, three days after the bombing. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks]

The 17 May agreement. A month after the embassy bombing, on 17 May 1983, Lebanon and Israel signed an agreement negotiated with the United States. Its text declared that the state of war between them "has been terminated and no longer exists" and that the border was inviolable. Israel was to withdraw all its forces within 8 to 12 weeks of the agreement entering into force. Lebanon was to enforce security arrangements in a "Security Region" in the south and to prevent "irregular forces, armed bands" from operating against Israel, and a joint liaison committee with American participation would oversee it. [cite:hz2b_jvl_may17] It set up liaison offices rather than embassies. [cite:hz2b_majalla_may17] Israel made its withdrawal conditional on Syria withdrawing too. [cite:hz2b_wiki_may17] Lebanon's parliament approved it in June 1983, with two votes against and three abstentions among 72 members present. Syria's president, Hafez al-Assad, rejected it, and with Syrian troops still in Lebanon it was never carried out. [cite:hz2b_majalla_may17] [cite:hz2b_wiki_may17] The two governments said in it that they recognised their "right and obligation to live in peace with each other"; many Lebanese Muslims and much of the Arab world opposed it as a surrender imposed on Lebanon. [cite:hz2b_majalla_may17] [cite:hz2b_wiki_may17]

What US courts found. On 30 May 2003 Judge Royce Lamberth of the federal court in Washington ruled for the families of the servicemen in Peterson v. Islamic Republic of Iran. [cite:hz2_wt_2003] His findings, later relied on in further suits, were these. Iran's Ministry of Information and Security told the Iranian ambassador to Syria to have his group attack the multinational force and to "take a spectacular action against the United States Marines". At a meeting in Baalbek, held at the ambassador's direction, Hezbollah and Revolutionary Guard members planned simultaneous attacks on the American and French barracks. The truck was "driven by an Iranian member of Hezbollah". [cite:hz2_anderson] [cite:hz2_fain]

Iran did not appear to contest the case. The court decided it by default, on evidence it found "satisfactory to the court". [cite:hz2_anderson] On 7 September 2007 Lamberth awarded the families about $2.66 billion. His opinion, setting out the families' case, described Hezbollah as "the terrorist organization that orchestrated and carried out the bombing". [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] [cite:hz2a_peterson07] Iran called that ruling "baseless" and "politically motivated", saying a court that heard only one side had no legal value. [cite:hz2_jurist_2007] In April 2016 the US Supreme Court, by six votes to two, upheld a 2012 law directing about $2 billion in frozen Iranian central bank assets to more than 1,300 relatives of victims of attacks linked to Iran. [cite:hz2_pbs_markazi] Iran's foreign ministry called the ruling "a theft", and Iran continued to deny any link to the bombing. [cite:hz2_aj_theft] Iran then took the United States to the International Court of Justice. On 30 March 2023 the court ruled it had no jurisdiction over the central bank funds, but found that other US measures against Iranian companies breached a 1955 treaty between the two countries. [cite:hz2_iranprimer_icj]

The American response. Vice-President George H. W. Bush, visiting Beirut the day after the barracks bombing, said: "We will not allow terrorism to dictate or change our foreign policy." [cite:hz2_an_1983] The Pentagon's commission of inquiry, under retired Admiral Robert Long, put the blame for the security failures on the military chain of command. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] The fighting went on. Israel pulled its forces out of the Aley and Shouf districts on 4 September 1983. The battleship USS New Jersey shelled Druze and Syrian positions on 14 December 1983, and again on 8 February 1984. France struck positions in the Bekaa that it said belonged to the Revolutionary Guards. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] On 7 February 1984 President Reagan ordered the Marines to begin leaving, largely because support in Congress was fading. The last Marines left Beirut airport on 26 February. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] Days later, on 5 March 1984, the Lebanese government formally repudiated the 17 May agreement; parliament revoked it in May 1987. [cite:hz2b_majalla_may17] [cite:hz2b_wiki_may17] Thirty-four years later, Vice-President Mike Pence called the bombing "the opening salvo in a war that we have waged ever since".
