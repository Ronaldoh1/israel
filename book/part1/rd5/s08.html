FDR Dies, Truman Chooses

The American decision on Israel was made by a president whom almost no one, three years earlier, had expected to be making it. The story of that decision therefore begins not in 1948, and not even in 1945, but at the Democratic convention in Chicago in 1944.

Henry Wallace had served as Franklin Roosevelt's Vice President since 1941. He was a progressive in the full sense of the word in his time: pro-labor, anti-imperialist and anti-colonial, an early and forceful advocate of civil rights well ahead of the American political mainstream, and a supporter of accommodation rather than confrontation with the Soviet Union. To the Democratic Party's conservative and machine wings he was unacceptable. At the 1944 convention, with an ailing Roosevelt unlikely to survive another term, the party bosses maneuvered Wallace off the ticket and replaced him with a compromise choice, the little-known senator from Missouri, Harry Truman. [cite:wiki_choosing_truman] [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] Wallace went on to run for president on the Progressive Party ticket in 1948, lost badly, and died in 1965. [cite:wiki_choosing_truman] The fuller account of his removal, including the reasons behind it and Wallace's own Zionist sympathies, belongs to an earlier part of this book; what matters here is the single substitution and its timing.

Roosevelt himself had one more Middle Eastern act left. In February 1945, aboard the USS Quincy in the Suez Canal, he met King Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia and gave assurances that no action would be taken hostile to the Arab people without consultation. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] A letter of 5 April put the assurance in writing. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] One week later, on 12 April 1945, Roosevelt, born in 1882, died at Warm Springs, Georgia, less than three months into his new term and before any of the postwar decisions on Palestine had to be made. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] That meeting and that letter are told in full in [scene:s07ca|FDR and Ibn Saud]. Their place in this chapter is only their timing: the president who had given the assurance did not live to be tested by it.

What Roosevelt would have done had he lived cannot be known, and this book does not pretend to know it. The record shows the promise and the date of the death, side by side, and nothing more. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud]

One interpretive reading, and it is a reading rather than a documented finding, draws a larger lesson from the 1944 convention. On this reading, but for the convention maneuver, Henry Wallace and not Harry Truman would have become president in April 1945, and the 1948 recognition of Israel, along with the opening of the Cold War, might have unfolded very differently. The reading uses Wallace to mark the contingency of the whole story: an American commitment to Israel that is often narrated as destiny turned, at its origin, on a party back-room and a single substitution, so that "FDR dies, Truman chooses" became the operative sequence only because the machine had already chosen Truman over Wallace four years before recognition. The reading's own limit should be stated as plainly as its claim. Wallace's dated removal is documented; what Wallace would have decided about recognition had he been president is not, and no record can supply it. The counterfactual is untestable, and it is offered here as a way of seeing the period, not as a conclusion about it. [cite:wiki_choosing_truman]

Truman, born in 1884, a Missouri Democrat and a former haberdasher turned senator, had been Vice President for eighty-two days when Roosevelt died. He arrived in the presidency with no prior foreign-policy experience and minimal briefing on the major foreign questions then pending. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] Over the next three years he would make the single decision on which this chapter's American story turns, and he would make it with a recurring cast around him. That cast is set out here, because its members reappear, in one combination or another, at each American turning point between 1945 and 1948.

The first is Clark Clifford (1906–1998), White House Special Counsel. In November 1947 Clifford sent Truman a memo arguing that the Jewish-American vote was likely decisive in several key swing states, New York, Pennsylvania and Illinois, for Truman's uncertain reelection prospects in 1948, and framing early and unambiguous recognition of a Jewish state as electorally advantageous. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] This is not a reconstruction by later historians from indirect evidence. Clifford's own memoir, Counsel to the President, published in 1991, openly describes this domestic-political framework as part of the advice he actually gave Truman, and acknowledges both Marshall's opposition and the argument between the two men. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] It is a rare case of a key adviser putting his own strategic reasoning on the record, after the fact, in his own words, and it is the strongest single document behind the reading that electoral politics drove the timing of recognition. A related document appears in another source: a November 1947 memo to Truman titled "The Politics of 1948 and the Jews," which analyzed the Jewish vote for the coming election. [cite:jvl_truman_index] The sources used here do not say whether that title and Clifford's memo are one document or two.

The second figure is the man who objected to Clifford most strongly. George Marshall, Secretary of State, architect of the strategy that won the war in Europe and of the Marshall Plan, was one of the most prestigious figures in postwar American public life. He told Truman directly: "If you follow Clifford's advice and if I were to vote in the election, I would vote against you." [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] He threatened to resign. Because his standing was so uniquely high, his opposition was politically dangerous to the President. Truman recognized Israel anyway, and Marshall did not resign. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Marshall's decision not to carry out the threat is itself part of the documented record; it does not show that his broader relationship with Truman was permanently damaged.

Behind Marshall stood the third element of the cast, the professional State Department. Loy Henderson, director of Near Eastern Affairs, Under Secretary Robert Lovett and Marshall himself opposed early recognition. Their argument was cold strategic realism: recognizing a Jewish state would damage American relations with the Arab world, jeopardize access to Middle Eastern oil at the opening of the Cold War, and possibly require American troops to enforce partition. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] The written warnings they and others filed in the autumn of 1947 belong to a later section of this chapter. The pattern they form is the point here, and it is a pattern this book meets again: the permanent foreign-service establishment judged support for the Zionist program to be against American strategic interest, and the elected political leadership overruled it. Not every career diplomat held an identical view; what is on record is the opposition of these named senior officials.

The fourth figure entered by a door that no organization chart shows. Eddie Jacobson, an American Jew from Kansas City, was Truman's old army comrade and his former partner in a men's clothing store, Truman & Jacobson, which went bankrupt in the early 1920s; Jacobson had been best man at Truman's wedding and remained one of his most trusted friends. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] By March 1948 Truman, worn down by Zionist lobbying, had refused to see Chaim Weizmann through official channels. Jacobson went to the Oval Office and, invoking their friendship and Truman's admiration for the aging Zionist leader, persuaded the President to receive Weizmann privately. That meeting, on 18 March 1948, is documented in multiple biographies and in Truman's own recollections, and historians widely credit it with securing Truman's commitment to recognition. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] Jacobson's documented contribution is that one meeting; the record does not give him a broader coordinating role in the recognition decision.

The meeting with Weizmann had an immediate and awkward sequel. The very next day the American delegation at the United Nations announced a retreat from partition toward a temporary trusteeship, and the collision between what the President had told Weizmann in private and what his diplomats said in public is the subject of [scene:rd5_trusteeship|Washington's retreat in March 1948].

The end of this American arc can be stated briefly here and told in full later. On 14 May 1948, at 6:11 p.m. Washington time, eleven minutes after David Ben-Gurion declared the State of Israel in Tel Aviv, the United States extended de facto recognition. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The speed caught the State Department off guard. It also caught the American delegation at the United Nations, which was at that moment still working the floor for a trusteeship proposal, an alternative to statehood, and learned from the news that its own government had recognized the new state. By the account in the sources used here, Clifford orchestrated the timing, and Marshall's professionals were overruled without being forewarned. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] De jure recognition followed only in January 1949, after Truman's election victory. The Soviet Union, pursuing its own aim of pushing Britain out of the Middle East, extended full de jure recognition first, in May 1948. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The constitutional basis of the President's power to act alone, and the comparison with other governments, are taken up in [scene:rec_s01|who recognizes a nation].

Why did Truman do it? The scene this section draws on gives a pointed answer. On its reading, the recognition was primarily a domestic political calculation for the 1948 election, orchestrated by Clifford: Marshall threatened to resign, the State Department opposed, and the President's personal channel to Zionist leadership ran through his former business partner. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] Eleven minutes, on this reading, is not the interval of a deliberative foreign-policy consensus; it is the interval of a decision executed for maximum effect by a president facing a hard election. Truman's own memoir later presented the recognition as principled, and Clifford's memoir and Marshall's documented opposition tell a different story. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] The case is strengthened by Clifford's own admission, which is first-person evidence and not inference.

The familiar account holds the opposite: that Truman recognized Israel out of moral conviction. That account has evidence too, and the strongest form of it rests on Truman's own words. As a senator in 1943, years before any recognition question or any campaign money, he spoke at a mass rally for Europe's Jews, as the next section shows. In February 1947 he publicly called the impression that partisan and local politics motivated American policy on Palestine "most unfortunate and misleading," and said his position had been consistent since 1945. [cite:jvl_truman_feb1947] The same documents that show Clifford's arithmetic also show that Truman was pressed by Zionist lobbying to the point of refusing to see Weizmann, and that it took a friend's personal appeal, not a political operative's, to change his mind about that meeting. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel]

The evidence on both sides does not resolve into a single cause, and the sources this section draws on say as much themselves. Clifford's memo documents one real and significant thread in the decision; it does not show that electoral calculation was the only factor, or that humanitarian and strategic considerations played no part. Multiple motives existing together is the more accurate picture than any single-cause explanation. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] What the record does show, specifically, is a president with little foreign-policy grounding moving faster than his own government's formal machinery, guided partly through a personal relationship outside normal channels, against the explicit, documented objection of his Secretary of State. That was not a uniquely corrupt way for foreign policy to be made; personal relationships and domestic pressure shape presidential decisions in many administrations and on many issues. It was, however, plainly not the product of careful interagency deliberation reaching a clean consensus. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition]

The cast also answers a different and more corrosive question, one the record settles more firmly than the question of motive. Accounts of 1948 as a hidden-hand operation, in which an organized interest secretly controlled American policy, cannot accommodate the documents. A plot that controls a government does not have that government's Secretary of State, its Near Eastern division and its oil-strategic establishment fighting it openly in signed memoranda, as Marshall and Henderson did. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Nor was Jacobson a secret agent; he was the opposite. His role shows the actual texture of the decision: friendship, fatigue, sentiment and access, one old friend reaching a tired president while the professional foreign service advised the other way. To readers inclined to conspiracy he looks like proof of hidden influence; on the record he is proof of something more ordinary, that a haberdasher's personal loyalty could move a president when the State Department could not, and that the channel was a friendship documented in biographies, not a secret. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] What 1948 shows, in this reading of the evidence, is convergence and contingency: a president's political judgment prevailing over his own experts, not coordinated control.

What remains open is the weight of each motive in Truman's own mind. The record establishes the sequence: the 1944 substitution, the April 1945 death, the November 1947 memo, Marshall's threat, the 18 March meeting, the eleven minutes. It establishes who argued what. It does not establish which argument decided the President, and no document yet found does. Nor can it say what Roosevelt, or Wallace, would have done in Truman's place; those questions are counterfactuals, and they stay open by their nature.
