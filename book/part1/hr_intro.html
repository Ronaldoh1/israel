A heretic, in the Church's language, was not simply someone who was wrong. It was someone who chose, and kept choosing, a belief the Church had condemned. This book follows the men and women the medieval and early modern Church named heretics because they sought God by a road it did not control: dualists in Bulgaria and Languedoc, laywomen in the towns of northern Europe, mystics in Spain and Rome who taught that the soul could rest in God without the Church's mediation.

The record of what happened to them is precise. A Bogomil leader was burned in Constantinople around 1100. A crusade against the Cathars began in 1209, and its first great massacre, at Béziers, was reported to the pope as the killing of almost twenty thousand people "irrespective of rank, sex, or age". [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] [cite:hr_wiki_amalric] About two hundred and ten people were burned below the fortress of Montségur in 1244. A woman was burned in Paris in 1310 for a book she would not withdraw. A priest whose book on prayer "caused a sensation" died in a papal prison. [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur] [cite:hr_wiki_porete] [cite:hr_brit_molinos]

The record is also partial in a way this book must say plainly: most of what is known about these movements comes from the people who prosecuted them. Part Six sets out a live dispute among historians over how far the "Cathar church" was real and how far it was a picture drawn by its enemies. [cite:hr_ihr_moore] The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads what the heretics themselves wrote, where their words survive.
