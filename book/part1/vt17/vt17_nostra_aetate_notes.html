Two Tracks: Nostra Aetate, the Notes and the State Left Unnamed, 1958–1990

Between the death of Pius XII in 1958 and the opening of negotiations with Israel in 1992, the Holy See changed its teaching on the Jewish people more than in any comparable period of its history, while its diplomatic position on the State of Israel moved only by small, dated steps. The two changes ran on separate tracks, and the Holy See said so in writing in 1985. Keeping them apart is the only way to read this period accurately. A reader who treats the end of the deicide charge as recognition of Israel, or the refusal of recognition as a judgment on Jews, mixes two subjects the documents themselves distinguish.

Paul VI in the Holy Land, January 1964

Paul VI was the first pope in modern times to travel to the Holy Land. He flew to Amman on 4 January 1964, where King Hussein received him, and spent most of the three days in the Jordanian-held Old City of Jerusalem and in Bethlehem; there he met the Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras. [cite:vt17_ebsco_paul1964] On 5 January he crossed into Israel at Megiddo, where President Zalman Shazar and Prime Minister Levi Eshkol greeted him, and visited sites in Galilee before returning through the Mandelbaum Gate. [cite:vt17_ebsco_paul1964] [cite:vt17_jta_nissim1963]

The visit to Israel lasted about eleven hours. According to Evrony, the pope did not use the name "Israel" during it, and his telegram of thanks afterwards was addressed to "President Shazar, Tel Aviv", naming neither Israel nor Jerusalem. [cite:vt17_evrony_menorah] Official Vatican statements spoke of "the Holy Land." [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] Israel's foreign ministry later wrote that the visit made "patently, and painfully, clear that the Holy See did not recognise Israel de jure." [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] Israel's Chief Rabbi, Yitzhak Nissim, had declined to join the welcome at Megiddo, saying he could take part only "on the basis of reciprocity", meaning a papal call on the Chief Rabbinate. [cite:vt17_jta_nissim1963] The pope had come as a pilgrim; no Holy See document gives a reason for the wording, and the visit was not a diplomatic act.

Nostra Aetate, 28 October 1965

The road to the Second Vatican Council's declaration on the Jews began with a private audience. On 13 June 1960 John XXIII received the French Jewish historian Jules Isaac, whose work on the Christian "teaching of contempt" had moved him, and the task of drafting a text was given to Cardinal Augustin Bea's Secretariat for Christian Unity. [cite:vt17_va_fumagalli] [cite:vt17_rutishauser_scjr] The draft was a statement of Catholic theology written by the Council's own commission; the documented consultations with Jewish scholars and organisations are part of the ordinary history of the text, and the claim that the declaration was "written under Jewish influence" belongs to an antisemitic narrative of infiltration, not to the record.

The text was delayed by politics from outside. In 1962 the World Jewish Congress announced that Dr Chaim Wardi, an official of Israel's Ministry of Religious Affairs, would represent it at the Council. Arab governments protested, Wardi never attended, and the draft on the Jews was taken off the agenda of the first session. [cite:vt17_rutishauser_scjr] [cite:vt17_va_fumagalli] Representatives of Arab states described the draft as "a Zionist conspiracy" and asked that the Council also address Islam. [cite:vt17_rutishauser_scjr] The final text became a declaration on all non-Christian religions, with Judaism treated in its fourth paragraph. It was adopted on 28 October 1965 by 2,221 votes to 88. [cite:vt17_va_fumagalli] [cite:vt17_rutishauser_scjr]

Paragraph 4 said that "God holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers." [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] It addressed the oldest charge in the Christian repertoire against Jews, the claim that "the Jews" as a people were guilty of killing Christ, known as the deicide charge, and rejected it: what happened in the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today." [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] Jews were not to be presented "as rejected or accursed by God", and the Church decried "displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone." [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] The declaration repudiated the charge of collective and permanent responsibility for the death of Christ. [cite:vt17_va_fumagalli] It said nothing about the land, Zionism or the State of Israel. Pius X's reply to Herzl in 1904 had joined the two subjects ("The Jews have not recognized our Lord, therefore we cannot recognize the Jewish people"); Nostra Aetate changed the teaching on the people and left the state untouched. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] [scene:vt16_herzl_1904|Part Sixteen: Herzl and Pius X]

The diplomatic track moved after the Six-Day War. Once Israel held the whole of Jerusalem, Paul VI in December 1967 replaced the call for internationalisation with a call for a "special statute, internationally guaranteed" for the city and its holy places. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] [cite:vt1_wiki_corpus] Contacts continued without recognition: Paul VI received Foreign Minister Abba Eban in 1969, Prime Minister Golda Meir on 15 January 1973, the first such audience for an Israeli prime minister, and Moshe Dayan on 12 January 1978. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael]

The Commission and the 1974 Guidelines

On 22 October 1974 Paul VI set up the Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews, and placed it beside the Holy See's office for Christian unity (today the Dicastery for Promoting Christian Unity, which still publishes its documents), not in the Secretariat of State that conducts diplomacy. [cite:vt17_va_guidelines1974] The placement matched the subject: relations with Jews were treated as a matter of religion, not of foreign policy. Its first document, the Guidelines and Suggestions for Implementing the Conciliar Declaration Nostra Aetate, dated 1 December 1974 and signed by Cardinal Johannes Willebrands, asked Christians to learn "by what essential traits the Jews define themselves in the light of their own religious experience" and condemned antisemitism as contrary to the spirit of Christianity. [cite:vt17_va_guidelines1974] The Guidelines did not discuss the land or the State of Israel. [cite:vt17_va_guidelines1974]

John Paul II: naming the state, keeping the line

John Paul II's first decade sent signals both ways. On 15 September 1982 he received the PLO chairman Yasser Arafat. Prime Minister Menachem Begin denounced the audience and accused the Vatican of inaction during the Holocaust; an Israeli official said the government was "very disturbed"; the Vatican "angrily rejected Israel's charges." [cite:vt17_csm_arafat1982]

Two years later the pope named the state for the first time in a signed papal document. In the apostolic letter Redemptionis anno of 20 April 1984 he wrote: "For the Jewish people who live in the State of Israel ... we must ask for the desired security and the due tranquility." [cite:vt17_ccjr_redemptionis] [cite:vt17_evrony_menorah] The same letter said the Palestinian people "have the natural right in justice to find once more a homeland", and asked for Jerusalem "a special statute internationally guaranteed so that no party could jeopardize it." [cite:vt17_ccjr_redemptionis] Israel's security and a Palestinian homeland were named in the same letter, and neither naming was a diplomatic act.

The decisive text came from the Commission. Its Notes on the Correct Way to Present the Jews and Judaism in Preaching and Catechesis, with a preface dated 24 June 1985, asked Christians to understand Jews' "religious attachment" to the land, "which finds its roots in biblical tradition", "without however making their own any particular religious interpretation of this relationship." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] It then drew the line in one sentence: "The existence of the State of Israel and its political options should be envisaged not in a perspective which is in itself religious, but in their reference to the common principles of international law." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] The Holy See had stated, in an official document, that its view of Israel was a matter of law and politics. That closed the theological route to a hostile reading of the state, and equally closed the route by which a Catholic might read the state as the fulfilment of prophecy, the reading Protestant Christian Zionism adopts. [scene:ts11_churches|The Theology Shield: the churches]

On 13 April 1986 John Paul II became the first pope on record to visit a synagogue, Rome's Great Synagogue, where Chief Rabbi Elio Toaff received him and he called the Jews "our dearly beloved brothers" and "our elder brothers." [cite:vt17_vn_synagogue1986] The next year showed the limits of the new climate. In June 1987 the pope received Austria's president, Kurt Waldheim, whom Jewish organisations accused of participation in Nazi war crimes during his service in the German army. [cite:vt17_csm_castel1987] Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir called it an "unfriendly act" and said that, without relations, Israel "can do nothing but express its unhappiness." [cite:vt17_djn_waldheim] The Vatican replied on 20 June that it received democratically elected heads of state at their request and that the reaction caused it "surprise and profound pain." [cite:vt17_criterion_waldheim] After a meeting with Jewish leaders on 1 September 1987, the Vatican agreed to prepare a document on the Holocaust and to give further consideration to relations with Israel. [cite:vt17_csm_castel1987] In Miami that month the pope spoke of the Jewish people's right to a homeland "as does any civil nation, according to international law." [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] Recognition did not follow for six years.

What the timing shows

The theological changes of 1965, 1974 and 1985 came from the Council and a commission with a religious mandate; none was tied to a diplomatic concession, and each left the state aside. The diplomatic steps followed events on the ground: the 1967 change adjusted the Jerusalem demand to Israel's capture of the eastern city, and the 1984 naming of the state came with a Palestinian right to a homeland. Public Israeli pressure, over Arafat in 1982 and Waldheim in 1987, did not change the Holy See's course. Arab-state pressure appears in the sources used here once, over the Wardi affair, and it delayed the conciliar text without changing its theology. Rabbi David Rosen, who later served on the bilateral commission, wrote that the Holy See had long said there was no theological barrier to relations and that its reluctance was "the consequence of secular political considerations." [cite:vt17_rosen_1994] What those considerations were, and how they were resolved in 1993, is the subject of the next section. [scene:vt17_fundamental_agreement|The Fundamental Agreement]
