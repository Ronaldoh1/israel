Peel's "Exchange of Population": Transfer Enters the Record

The Peel report of July 1937 is remembered for its partition map. Beside the map it made a second recommendation. It proposed an "exchange of population" between the two states it would create. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

The model was explicit: the exchange of populations between Greece and Turkey in 1923. Approximately 225,000 Arabs would be moved out of the territory allotted to the Jewish state, and about 1,250 Jews out of the Arab state. The report stated that in the last resort the exchange should be compulsory. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] It was the first official British document to put population transfer in writing. The sources round the figures differently. Ori Yehudai gives about 200,000 Arabs and about 1,000 Jews. Middle East Eye gives about 225,000 Palestinians against 1,250 Jews, matching the figures above. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_mee_strike] Either way, the scale was lopsided. The movement the report envisaged was overwhelmingly of Arabs out of the proposed Jewish state.

A widely held view is that the idea of removing the Arab population from a future Jewish state was invented by Israeli extremists after 1948. The documents do not support that chronology. Compulsory transfer entered the official record in a British royal commission report in 1937. It was then discussed, and not rejected, at the top of the Zionist movement more than a decade before the displacement of 1948. That leaves open what any individual leader privately intended. The concept itself was on the table well before the Nakba.

The mechanism matters. The recommendation was Britain's, made by a royal commission of the Mandatory power, and it would have been Britain's to adopt or drop. The Zionist movement and the Arab Higher Committee could only respond to it. The Arab response was a plain rejection. The Zionist response is the disputed part of the record.

The most quoted evidence is a private letter. In October 1937 David Ben-Gurion wrote to his son Amos. The letter welcomes partition as a stage toward a larger state and discusses the Arab population. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] His diary and letters of the period discuss compulsory transfer as a possibility. One sentence in the Amos letter has become the centre of a dispute over its wording. It has been quoted as support for expelling Arabs. In a different reading of the handwritten Hebrew, it says the opposite. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] Historians have not only disagreed about the sentence; some have changed their own positions on it, Benny Morris among them. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

Each side of this dispute has a serious case. Those who read the sentence as support for expulsion can point to its context: a letter welcoming partition as a stage, written in the same months in which Ben-Gurion's diary and letters weighed compulsory transfer as a possibility. Those who read it the other way rest on the handwritten original itself, arguing that the text does not say what the familiar quotation makes it say. This book does not resolve the sentence. The original wording is not reproduced in the sources available here, and without it no reader can judge between the readings. The dispute should be presented as a dispute.

What is not disputed is the movement's formal response. The Twentieth Zionist Congress met in Zurich in August 1937. It accepted partition in principle as a basis for negotiation, and it did not reject the transfer clause. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] The Congress's wider position on Peel, with its rejection of the proposed boundaries, is described in [scene:s07a6|the previous section]. Transfer was therefore a live subject at the highest level of the movement eleven years before 1948. The Arab Higher Committee rejected the Peel report outright, the transfer recommendation with the rest. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

The word did not leave British politics either. In December 1944 the Labour Party's conference adopted a resolution on Palestine that again spoke of Arabs moving out as Jews moved in. That resolution, and what Labour did with it once in office, is told in [scene:rd4_labour|Labour and the end of the war].

The record leaves several things unsettled. The exact wording, and so the meaning, of Ben-Gurion's sentence to Amos remains disputed between historians. The sources here do not show how the Congress debated the transfer clause, only that it did not reject it. And the figures themselves differ slightly by source: about 225,000 and 1,250 in the report as cited here, about 200,000 and 1,000 in Yehudai. What is firm is that in 1937 a British royal commission proposed moving some 225,000 Arabs and about 1,250 Jews, by compulsion in the last resort, and that the Zionist Congress that met a month later did not reject the proposal.
