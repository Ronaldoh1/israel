The Ladder Abroad

The American Dream has long been taught as American in a particular sense: that the United States, more than other countries, lets children rise above the station of their parents. That is a comparative claim, and it can be checked against comparable data from other rich democracies. This section uses the comparison for one purpose only, to show which parts of the American mobility record are shared with peer countries and which are particular to the United States, and so to locate the American mechanism more exactly. It draws on the economist Miles Corak's international comparisons, the OECD's 2018 report A Broken Social Elevator?, and newer estimates of absolute mobility in eight countries, with the strongest critics of the comparison beside them.

The measure and the curve

Most international comparisons use the intergenerational earnings elasticity, usually shortened to IGE. It asks, for fathers and sons, how much a given percentage difference in fathers' earnings carries over into sons' earnings. An IGE of 0.5 means that if one father earned twice as much as another, the first father's son would on average earn about half again as much as the second's: roughly half of the father's advantage or disadvantage is passed on. An IGE near zero means sons' earnings have little to do with fathers'. [cite:ad3_corak_iza] Unlike the rank-rank slope in the previous section, the IGE is sensitive to how unequal incomes are, and to which years of income are used; estimates for the same country can differ by a wide margin. [cite:ad3_winship_archbridge]

In a widely cited 2013 paper in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, Corak plotted the IGE for sons born in the early to mid-1960s against each country's income inequality around 1985, measured by the Gini coefficient, a standard 0-to-1 index of how unequally income is spread. The more unequal countries had the higher elasticities. Alan Krueger, chairman of President Obama's Council of Economic Advisers, named the pattern the "Great Gatsby Curve" in a speech on 12 January 2012. [cite:ad3_corak_iza] On Corak's estimates, the United States, with an IGE of 0.47, sits beside the United Kingdom and Italy at about 0.5, against 0.19 for Canada and 0.15 to 0.18 for Denmark, Norway and Finland. [cite:ad3_epi_corak] Corak wrote that the curve "is not a causal relationship", while arguing it was "too glib to dismiss" as mere correlation. [cite:ad3_corak_iza]

Canada: a neighbour with a similar middle

Canada is the closest comparison: a similar economy, similar immigration and a shared continent. Corak found that the two countries differ mainly at the two ends of the ladder. About half of American sons of fathers in the bottom tenth of earnings rose no further than the third tenth, while a much larger share of Canadian sons from the bottom tenth reached the upper half. At the top, more than half of American sons of top-tenth fathers fell no further than the eighth tenth, a stickiness Canada showed less of. In the middle of the distribution, the two countries were similar. [cite:ad3_corak_iza] The American difference, in other words, is concentrated at the floor and the ceiling.

The OECD's generations

The OECD, the Paris-based organisation of mostly rich democracies, published A Broken Social Elevator? How to Promote Social Mobility on 15 June 2018. Its most quoted figure translates earnings persistence into time: with the average elasticity across member countries of about 0.38, and with the bottom tenth earning about a third of the mean, a child born into the bottom tenth of families would need four to five generations for the family to reach the average income. The report described mobility as weakest at the extremes, with "sticky floors" at the bottom and "sticky ceilings" at the top, and noted that in the United States and Germany almost half of the sons of high-earning fathers were themselves in the top quarter of earners. [cite:ad3_oecd_elevator] [cite:ad3_oecd_elevator_pdf] As reported from the same study, the estimate was five generations for the United States and two for Denmark, the shortest of any member. [cite:ad3_piie_denmark] [cite:ad3_statista_oecd] The report is not internally consistent on the Nordic figure: its executive summary gives about two generations, while its overview chapter says the Nordic countries would need at least four. [cite:ad3_oecd_elevator_pdf] The figure is a projection that assumes today's persistence holds unchanged for a century or more; it illustrates the size of the elasticity rather than forecasting any family's future.

Absolute mobility abroad: the decline is shared, the cause is not

A newer line of work compares the measure closest to the Dream, the share of children who out-earn their parents. Robert Manduca, one of the authors of the American study, and thirteen co-authors estimated it for children born from 1960 to 1987 in eight countries. For recent cohorts, the United States and Canada were both near 50 per cent, while Norway held steady at about 75 per cent and Finland above 70. [cite:ad3_manduca_iza] Denmark complicates the picture: its share was about 50 per cent for children born in 1982 when measured, as in the American study, on income before taxes, but above 65 per cent on income after taxes and benefits. [cite:ad3_manduca_iza] In Canada, measured on disposable income, the share was 57.5 per cent for the 1977 cohort and 57.1 per cent for 1984. [cite:ad3_manduca_iza]

The study's explanation for the American position is specific. American and Norwegian economies grew at almost the same rate per person over the period, 1.80 against 1.86 per cent a year, so slow growth does not explain the gap. What differed was how much of that growth reached households in the study's samples: American incomes rose only 71 per cent as fast as GDP, against 95 per cent in Norway. Replacing the American pattern of relative mobility with Norway's barely changed the American result. [cite:ad3_manduca_iza] A separate estimate by the economist Yonatan Berman found that absolute mobility fell in every country he studied, but that the United States had the largest decline since 1940, 39.2 percentage points, and that rising inequality accounted for 24.6 of those points against 14.5 for slower growth; in most other countries, slower growth was the larger factor. [cite:ad3_berman]

The critics of the comparison

The strongest critique of the Nordic comparison comes from Rasmus Landersø and James Heckman, a Nobel laureate, in a 2017 paper titled "The Scandinavian Fantasy". They found that Denmark is more mobile than the United States when mobility is measured by income but not when it is measured by education, and attributed Denmark's income advantage largely to taxes, transfers and a compressed wage scale rather than to greater equality of opportunity in childhood. Danish programmes for disadvantaged children raised test scores without improving educational attainment, which the authors linked in part to weaker financial rewards for more schooling in a highly redistributive system. [cite:ad3_landerso_heckman] Jacob Funk Kirkegaard of the Peterson Institute made a related point: Denmark's mobility rests on taxes and public spending well above American levels, and "there is no free lunch". [cite:ad3_piie_denmark] The measurement objections run in both directions. Scott Winship argues that, with better long-run data, American IGEs for family income are about 0.65 to 0.75, which would make the United States look less mobile than in Corak's figures, not more, while he also argues that absolute mobility is higher than the headline estimates. [cite:ad3_winship_archbridge]

Where it works as promised

In the middle of the earnings distribution, American sons moved about as freely relative to their fathers as Canadian sons did; the gap between the two countries lies at the extremes. [cite:ad3_corak_iza] The fall in absolute mobility is not an American peculiarity: it happened in every country Berman studied. [cite:ad3_berman] And before taxes and benefits, Denmark's most recent measured cohort out-earned its parents at about the American rate. [cite:ad3_manduca_iza] What does set the United States apart is narrower and measurable: higher persistence at the bottom and top of the ladder, and a smaller share of growth reaching the households whose children are being compared with their parents.
