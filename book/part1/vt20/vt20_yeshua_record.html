Yeshua in the Record: What the Earliest Sources Show

Book Two begins three centuries before an emperor convened a council and nineteen centuries before a Vatican City existed, with a Jewish teacher from Galilee who was executed by a Roman governor. This section sets out what can be said about him from the sources historians actually use, how early those sources are, and what the earliest layer of his teaching said about the "kingdom of God". It also explains a choice of names that runs through the rest of the book.

Two names for two subjects

The man's name in his own languages was Yeshua, a shortened form of Yehoshua (Joshua), meaning roughly "the Lord saves". It was an ordinary name in his time. The archaeologist Amos Kloner called it "one of the common names in the time of the Second Temple"; it appears on 71 burial finds from the period, and Tal Ilan's lexicon of Jewish names counts 85 bearers of the form Yeshua. Greek-speaking Jews wrote it Iesous, which passed through Latin into English as "Jesus". [cite:vt20_wiki_yeshua] This book uses "Yeshua" when it discusses the historical figure, a first-century Jew reconstructed by the ordinary methods of history, and "Jesus Christ" when it describes the figure defined in the creeds of the councils, beginning with Nicaea in 325 ([scene:vt22_what_it_decided|Part Twenty-Two]). The two names do not mark two different people. They mark two different kinds of claim: one about what the evidence of a life can show, the other about what churches came to teach about that life. This book keeps the two claims apart throughout.

Dates

No source gives an exact birth year. The gospels of Matthew and Luke both place the birth in the reign of Herod the Great, and most scholars, following Emil Schürer's calculation that Herod died in 4 BCE, place it between about 6 and 4 BCE. The crucifixion fell under Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea from 26 to 36 or 37 CE; scholars generally place it between 30 and 36, and astronomical work on the Passover calendar has narrowed the candidates to two Fridays, 7 April 30 and 3 April 33. [cite:vt20_wiki_chronology]

The non-Christian witnesses

Two writers outside the Christian movement mention Yeshua within a century of his death. The Jewish historian Josephus finished his Antiquities of the Jews around 93 to 94 CE. In Book Twenty he describes how the high priest Ananus, in the interval between two Roman governors, brought before the Sanhedrin "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James" and had him stoned; citizens complained to King Agrippa and to the incoming governor Albinus, and Ananus was removed after three months. [cite:vt20_josephus_ant20] Scholars accept this passage almost without exception; Louis Feldman described its authenticity as "almost universally acknowledged". [cite:vt20_wiki_josephus]

Book Eighteen of the same work contains a longer paragraph about Jesus, known as the Testimonium Flavianum. In the form transmitted by Christian copyists it calls him the Christ, hedges whether it is "lawful to call him a man", and reports his resurrection, which no Jewish author who stayed outside the movement would plausibly have written. Most scholars now hold that Josephus wrote a shorter, neutral notice that Christian copyists later expanded; a tenth-century Arabic version preserved by the bishop Agapius, published by Shlomo Pines in 1971, reads that "Pilate condemned him to be crucified" without the confessional phrases. A minority, following Ken Olson, argue that the whole paragraph was composed by the fourth-century church historian Eusebius; Feldman adopted that view in 2012, while James Carleton Paget and Alice Whealey rejected it. [cite:vt20_wiki_josephus] The claim found in some Observe The System material that "Eusebius forged it" is therefore one scholarly position, not the consensus.

The Roman historian Tacitus, describing the fire of Rome in July 64 [cite:vt20_audit_wiki_fire64], says Nero blamed it on a group "whom the crowd styled Christians", and explains the name: "Christus, the founder of the name, had undergone the death penalty in the reign of Tiberius, by sentence of the procurator Pontius Pilatus." [cite:vt20_tacitus_ann15] Tacitus wrote with contempt for the movement, which makes his notice the more useful as independent confirmation of the execution and of who ordered it. Crucifixion was a Roman penalty, imposed by a Roman official. The later Christian charge that "the Jews" as a people bore guilt for the death was rejected by the Catholic Church at the Second Vatican Council: Nostra Aetate, proclaimed on 28 October 1965, states that what happened in the passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today". [cite:vt20_va_nostra]

The earliest layer: the kingdom sayings

The four gospels were written decades after the events by authors who do not name themselves. Mark, the earliest, is dated around 70 CE, either just before or just after the Roman destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem; it was therefore written after Paul's letters, not before them. [cite:vt20_wiki_mark] Mark summarises Yeshua's message in one line: "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." [cite:vt20_esv_mark1] The "kingdom of God" in this sense is God's rule arriving in the world, and much of the sayings tradition is about its timing and its nature.

One saying in Luke has become central to the reading this platform brings to the subject. Asked by Pharisees when the kingdom would come, Yeshua answers that it does not come with signs to be observed, because "the kingdom of God is entos hymōn". [cite:vt20_audit_esv_luke17] The King James Version (1611), Luther's Bible and the Latin Vulgate (intra vos) render the phrase "within you"; the Revised Standard and New Revised Standard versions and most modern Catholic and Protestant translations render it "among you" or "in the midst of you". Ilaria Ramelli, in a 2009 study of the ancient Syriac versions, argues that "inside" is the correct translation: the Greek for "among" would normally be en mesō, which Luke uses elsewhere; the sense "among" is, she writes, "practically unattested" for entos; and Greek church writers such as Origen and Athanasius read the phrase as "inside you". She notes that most modern commentators follow "among" without explaining why. [cite:vt20_ramelli_luke17] Her reading is a scholarly argument with evidence behind it, not a consensus; the translation question is genuinely open, and the book records it as contested.

The Gospel of Thomas, a collection of sayings known complete only from a Coptic manuscript copied shortly before 350 and found near Nag Hammadi in Egypt, carries the same theme in plainer form. Its third saying says that "the kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you"; its last-but-one says that "the kingdom of the father is spread out upon the earth, and men do not see it". [cite:vt20_thomas_lambdin] When Thomas was composed is disputed. Greek fragments found at Oxyrhynchus date from shortly after 200 onward; scholars who think it independent of the canonical gospels, such as Stephen Patterson and Ron Cameron, place it in the late first century, while others place it in the second, and the overall range proposed runs from about 50 to 140 CE. [cite:vt20_ecw_thomas] If the early date holds, Thomas preserves sayings as old as the gospels; if the late date holds, it is an early reading of them. Part Twenty-Five returns to Thomas in full ([scene:vt25_sayings_gospels|The Sayings Gospels]).

The face: what is documented about the image

No gospel describes Yeshua's appearance. The historian Joan Taylor, in What Did Jesus Look Like? (2018), concludes from skeletal studies of Second Temple Judaean populations and from the texts that he was most likely of average height for his time, about 165 centimetres, with "olive-brown skin, brown-black hair and brown eyes", and probably short hair. She finds that the earliest images show him as a philosopher figure, and that the long-haired, bearded type became standard between the fourth and seventh centuries, when artists borrowed the hair and beard of gods such as Serapis and Zeus to signal divinity. [cite:vt20_toi_taylor] The specifically white, often fair-haired Jesus is later still. The historians Edward Blum and Paul Harvey, in The Color of Christ (2012), trace how a white Jesus became widespread in the United States in the early nineteenth century and grew more Nordic in look between the 1880s and 1910s, and note that the Native American writer William Apess was already calling Jesus "a man of color" in the 1830s. [cite:vt20_unc_colorofchrist] Warner Sallman's Head of Christ, painted in 1940, was reproduced more than 500 million times by the end of the twentieth century. [cite:vt20_wiki_headofchrist] The image most people carry is therefore a documented artistic and cultural construction, centuries after the man; that much is record, not interpretation.
