"Impartial": The Dulles Line, May–July 1953

Moscow's break with Israel was not the only change of course in 1953. In the spring the new Eisenhower administration's Secretary of State travelled through the Near East, and when John Foster Dulles came home he told the American public what he had found. A familiar account treats American policy toward Israel as a straight line rising from 1948. The documents of 1953 show a bend in it. Dulles's report spoke of Arab resentment of the United States "that has resulted from the creation of Israel", of Arab fear that America would back Israel "in aggressive expansion", and of a policy toward Israel and the Arab states that must be "impartial". Within two months Washington publicly regretted an Israeli decision about Jerusalem. Neither step cut the relationship, and both need to be read with care about what each source actually is.

The address itself is a documented fact. On 1 June 1953 Dulles reported on his Near East trip over radio and television, and the text is printed in the Department of State Bulletin of 15 June 1953, pages 831 to 835. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1953_dulles_report] The words quoted in this section, however, do not come from the Bulletin text. They come from a contemporary newspaper account of the address and of Dulles's testimony to Congress, published in the Detroit Jewish News on 5 June 1953. [cite:rs_cw1_djn_dulles_1953] They are reported quotations, close to the event, and they should be checked against the Bulletin's pages before they are treated as the official wording.

As that account reported it, Dulles said the United States "should seek to allay the deep resentment against it that has resulted from the creation of Israel." He said: "Today the Arab peoples are afraid that the United States will back the new state of Israel in aggressive expansion." He said that Israel "should become a part of the Near East community", and that the United States would stand by the Tripartite Declaration. Before the House Foreign Affairs Committee he said that policy toward Israel and the Arab states must be "impartial." [cite:rs_cw1_djn_dulles_1953] On the two most contested questions of the period he went further. He said that "the world religious community has claims in Jerusalem which take precedence over the political claims of any particular nation." And on the refugees he said that some "could be settled in the area presently controlled by Israel", while most could more readily be integrated into neighbouring Arab countries, depending on irrigation. [cite:rs_cw1_djn_dulles_1953]

Each of those sentences touched a specific instrument of American policy. The Tripartite Declaration of 25 May 1950, in which the United States, Britain and France declared their "unalterable opposition to the use of force or threat of force" between states in the area, was the existing guarantee of the armistice lines, and Dulles kept it. [cite:rs_cw1_avalon_tripartite] On refugees, his formula left a door open to some return to Israeli-held territory, while placing most of the solution in Arab countries and tying it to water; the stalled diplomacy behind that question is told in this stage's account of the refugees after Lausanne. On Jerusalem, his words gave priority to the claims of "the world religious community" over those of "any particular nation", a formula that on its face covered Israel's claims as well.

The Jerusalem sentence was not abstract. The city's status in 1949–50 is told in [scene:rd6_un_admission|Israel's admission and the Jerusalem question]. In 1952 Israel's Foreign Ministry, through which foreign governments dealt with Israel, was still in Tel Aviv, and Israel planned to move it. The United States had objected to the plan in July 1952, under Truman, and again in March 1953, under the new administration. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_fm_jerusalem_1953] Israel went ahead. On 28 July 1953 the United States publicly regretted the move of Israel's Foreign Ministry from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem. The statement said the move would embarrass the United Nations, which "has a primary responsibility for determining the future status of Jerusalem"; that it "will add to rather than to relax" tensions in the area; and that the United States would not move its embassy. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_fm_jerusalem_1953]

The mechanism was simple and limited. Two American objections had not stopped the move. What Washington controlled was where its own embassy stood, and therefore where American diplomats would do their business with the Israeli state. The statement used exactly that lever, and only that one: the embassy stayed in Tel Aviv. It also placed the question back where American policy held that it belonged, with the United Nations. The objections of July 1952 and March 1953 show that this was a continuing position across two administrations, not a new departure by Dulles; what was new in 1953 was the public statement after the fact.

Set side by side, the statements show both continuity and change. The guarantee of the armistice lines and the objection to the Foreign Ministry's move carried straight over from the Truman years. So did the logic of the Middle East Command proposal of 1951, in which Egypt was invited to be a founding member and Israel received a separate approach asking it to "give most serious consideration" to a question that "involves vitally" its security. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_note] [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_circular] That proposal had faded by 1953, and with it, in Avi Shlaim's account, Israel's hope of military integration with the West. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] What changed in 1953 was the public diagnosis. In the address as reported, a Secretary of State told an American audience that the creation of Israel had produced resentment against the United States, and that Arab peoples feared Israeli expansion with American backing. The prescription followed from the diagnosis: Israel was to become "a part of the Near East community", and policy toward Israel and the Arab states must be "impartial".

How each side read the word "impartial" can be told only as far as the sources go, and here they go only a short way. The word had an Arab history in this chapter. When Lebanon's minister Charles Malik asked the State Department about the Tripartite Declaration in June 1950, he called it potentially the three powers' most important contribution to peace if it was carried out with "honest and sustained impartiality." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1950_malik] For one Arab diplomat, on that evidence, impartiality was the test American policy would have to meet; the source does not record what he took it to require. Whether Arab governments heard Dulles's 1953 word as the answer to that request, and whether Israel's government heard it as a retreat from the relationship of the Truman years, these sources do not record. They contain no Israeli or Arab government response to the address. The Detroit Jewish News account reports what Dulles said; it is not, on the evidence here, a statement of how Israel received it.

Two episodes followed in the next months, outside this stage. In September 1953 Washington suspended aid to Israel, an episode told in [scene:cw2_banat_yaacov|the 1953 aid suspension]. Then came Qibya, told in [scene:cw2_unit101_qibya|Unit 101 and Qibya]. Whether Dulles's June address foreshadowed those decisions, or whether they arose from their own events, is a question for Stage Two.

The gaps are plain. The Bulletin text of the address is cited but not quoted here, so the reported wording cannot be confirmed against it in this book. The sources do not record why Dulles chose the word "impartial", what instructions, if any, followed from it inside the State Department, or what Israel and the Arab states said in reply. Nor do they record Israel's own stated reasons for moving the Foreign Ministry in July 1953 despite two American objections. What is documented is the sequence: an address that named Arab resentment as a problem for American policy, and a public statement, eight weeks later, that drew the line on Jerusalem at the American embassy's door.
