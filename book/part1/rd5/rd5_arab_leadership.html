Who Spoke for the Palestinian Arabs, 1945–1948

In the years when the Jewish Agency was building a diplomatic case, an arms network and a government in waiting, the Arab side of Palestine's politics was being reorganized from outside the country. On one reading of the record, set out below, the central fact of the period is institutional: the decisions that shaped the Palestinian Arab response to partition were taken, for the most part, not in Jerusalem or Jaffa but in the council chambers of the Arab League, in Cairo, Bludan, Aley and Alexandria. The Palestinian leadership had a voice in those rooms, and a record of its own choices. What it lacked, the sessions below show, was a government of its own and the League's backing for one.

The League's line was set early and held for more than two years. From its founding in March 1945 until Britain's decision to withdraw in the autumn of 1947, the Arab League consistently supported, in the historian Avi Shlaim's words, a "unitary and independent Palestinian state." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] It was the position the Arab case to the Anglo-American Committee had set out in 1946, when it promised that Jews "will be full citizens" of a Palestinian state. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002]

The League also rebuilt the Palestinians' own leadership body. The Arab Higher Committee had first been formed in 1936, by the Palestinian parties themselves. After a nine-year hiatus it was re-established in 1946, and this time, Shlaim notes, not by the Palestinian parties but "by a decision of the Arab League." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] The difference in origin would define the committee's position for the rest of the period: it spoke for the Palestinian Arabs, but it held its mandate from the League.

Its chairman was Hajj Amin al-Husayni, the Mufti of Jerusalem. Al-Husayni had spent the Second World War allied with the Axis powers, a record told in [scene:s07a5|the Arab Revolt and Berlin]. After the war he was refused asylum in Switzerland and held by France, which declined a British proposal to deport him to the Seychelles. In May 1946 he escaped from his residence near Paris to Cairo, where King Faruq of Egypt gave him asylum, and the Arab League made him chairman of the new Arab Higher Committee. [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] The man who led the Palestinian Arabs through 1947 and 1948 therefore did so from exile in Egypt, under the protection of an Arab king, at the head of a body the League had created.

The League's own decisions on Palestine can be followed session by session. The list compiled by the Jewish Virtual Library from League records gives the following. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_arableague] In December 1945 the Council set up a committee to enforce a boycott of Zionist goods (Resolution 16) and provided for up to three Palestinian representatives to sit on the Council (Resolution 17). In March 1946 it approved a company with £E1 million to buy Arab land in Palestine. In June 1946, at Bludan, it made the boycott part of the "creed of the Arab nations," called for land sales to Zionists to be made a crime (Resolution 72), and created a Higher Arab Executive for Palestine (Resolutions 80 and 82). In December 1946 it rejected partition (Resolution 142). In October 1947, at Aley, it adopted a resolution titled "Defending Palestine" (the League's own Resolution 181, not to be confused with the UN resolution of the same number a month later), calling for military preparations along the Arab borders. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_arableague]

The money in these decisions tells its own story. The League's chosen instruments in 1945 and 1946 were economic: a boycott of Zionist goods, a land fund of £E1 million to keep Arab land from being sold, and a call to criminalize sales to Zionists. They were aimed at the mechanism by which the Jewish community had been acquiring land. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_arableague] When, in February 1948, the Arab Higher Committee asked the League for a loan to meet its administrative costs, the League refused. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990]

The year 1947 brought the decisive tests. The Arab Higher Committee chose to boycott the United Nations special committee that visited Palestine that summer, a decision told in [scene:s07i|the UNSCOP section]. The UN partition vote of 29 November then moved the question from argument to war. Two League sessions in the months that followed show how far the League was prepared to go, and how far it was not. For the Cairo session of December 1947, the list records no public announcement, and a decision against formal military intervention unless the United States and Britain intervened militarily. In February 1948 the Council approved political, military and economic measures, including the withholding of petroleum concessions and sanctions on states aiding the Zionists. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_arableague] The League would arm volunteers and threaten oil concessions; it would not, as of December 1947, commit its member states' armies.

The Mufti wanted something the League would not give. At the Council sessions in Aley in October 1947 and Cairo in December 1947, Shlaim writes, al-Husayni "pleaded passionately for the establishment of a shadow government" under the Arab Higher Committee. In February 1948 the League rejected his demand for a government-in-exile, and refused the loan for the committee's administrative costs. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] In the months when the Jewish Agency was preparing the institutions of a state, the Palestinian leadership was refused both a government and the money to run its own committee.

The fighting forces reflected the same division. The Arab Liberation Army was created by the Arab League and led by Fawzi al-Qawuqji; its forces were volunteers. The Army of the Holy War was made up of Palestinian volunteers and was subordinate to the Husayni family. The two acted without cooperating. [cite:rs_rd5_lodz_2019] And one League member had plans of its own. The Dictionary of the Israeli-Palestinian Conflict describes the League as divided: King Abdullah of Transjordan "opposed every move toward Palestinian independence" and refused to deal with al-Husayni. [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] Abdullah's separate course, and his contacts with the Jewish Agency, are told in [scene:rd5_abdullah|Abdullah between the Agency, Britain and the League].

The League changed course only after the state of Israel had been proclaimed and the Arab armies had entered the war. The story runs past this chapter's end, but its first steps belong here. On 8 July 1948 the League's Political Committee agreed on a "temporary civil administration" for Palestine, responsible to the League. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] On 20 September 1948, at Alexandria, the Political Committee announced an All-Palestine Government with its seat in Gaza, headed by Ahmad Hilmi Abdul Baqi. On 22 September the Arab Higher Committee proclaimed Palestine independent, and on 30 September a Palestinian National Council met in Gaza under the Mufti's chairmanship. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] Egypt recognized the new government first, in mid-October 1948, followed by Iraq, and then Syria, Lebanon and Saudi Arabia. Britain refused. Abdullah opposed it and said it would not operate in the areas held by his Arab Legion. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990]

Abdullah answered with an assembly of his own. On 1 December 1948, according to Shlaim, a congress of about 3,000 delegates at Jericho proclaimed the union of Palestine and Transjordan and acknowledged Abdullah as king. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] Abdullah afterwards stripped al-Husayni of the title of Mufti of Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] By the end of 1948, then, two rival claims to speak for the Palestinian Arabs existed: a government in Gaza recognized by five Arab states and chaired in council by the Mufti, and a congress at Jericho that had placed the Arab parts of Palestine under the Transjordanian crown.

Historians weigh this record differently, and the difference is a question about Palestinian agency itself. Shlaim reads it as a story of control: the League reconstituted the Arab Higher Committee, refused it a government-in-exile until September 1948, and armed the Arab Liberation Army under its own command. On this reading the Palestinian leadership's instruments were in other hands, and decisions about Palestine were made by states with their own interests. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] The Dictionary of the Israeli-Palestinian Conflict gives more weight to Palestinian choices, and is harder on them. The Mufti and the Arab Higher Committee pursued their own line, boycotting the UN special committee, pressing for a shadow government and forming the Army of the Holy War; but the 1946 committee was ineffective, its paramilitary forces were uncoordinated, and the League around it was divided. [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] The two readings are not exclusive. One explains what the Palestinian leadership could not do; the other records what it chose to do, and how weakly it was able to do it.

The record leaves several questions open. The League's session list comes from a compilation of League records rather than from the full minutes, and for the Cairo session of December 1947 it records a decision without any public announcement; what was said in that room is not in the material here. The date and attendance of the Jericho Congress rest on Shlaim's account alone. And the counterfactual that hangs over the period, whether a Palestinian government formed in early 1948, when the Mufti asked for one, would have changed the course of the war, is one the documents cannot answer. What they do establish is the sequence: a League committed to a single Palestinian state, a Palestinian leadership reconstituted by the League and refused a loan, a refusal of government in February 1948, and a government granted in September 1948, four months after the Mandate had ended.
