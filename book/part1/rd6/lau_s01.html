Lausanne, 1949: Two Sequences That Never Met

Za'im's offer was made in the same months as the conference that was meant to settle the refugee question for all the parties at once. Two opposite accounts of that conference, at Lausanne, are still repeated. One says that Israel never offered anything on the refugees. The other says that the Arab states refused every offer. The United Nations' own report on the talks supports neither. It records an Israeli offer, a number, and the conditions attached to it; it records an Arab position with conditions of its own; and it shows that each side's conditions came in an order the other would not accept. That is why the conference failed, and why the refugee question it was meant to settle outlived it.

The forum was the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine, created by Resolution 194 of December 1948. That resolution named France, Turkey and the United States to the Commission [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194]; its full text, and the competing readings of its refugee paragraph, are set out in [scene:rr_s01|the section on Resolution 194]. The Commission convened talks at Lausanne from 27 April to 12 September 1949. The Arab delegations refused to negotiate directly with Israel, so the Commission shuttled between the parties. [cite:wiki_lausanne_1949] [cite:pij_lausanne]

The Commission had begun to press on the refugee question before the conference opened. In April 1949 it asked Israel to suspend its absentee regulations until the final peace settlement, and Israel declined. [cite:un_ccp_absentee_1949] That request, and the law of 1950 that made the regime permanent, are told in [scene:ap_s01|the section on absentee property].

On 12 May 1949 the parties signed the Lausanne Protocol, which took the 1947 partition map as a basis for discussion. [cite:wiki_lausanne_1949] [cite:pij_lausanne] The two sides read it differently from the start. The Arab states wanted the 1947 partition lines, and demanded that Israel first accept the return of the refugees under Resolution 194. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_israel_studies_peace] Israel held that the partition plan had been nullified by the Arab invasion, and that the refugees could be dealt with only within a final peace. Israel first floated a proposal to annex the Gaza Strip together with its refugee population. Egypt refused.

Washington intervened while the talks were under way. On 28 May 1949 Truman's secret note to Ben-Gurion warned that if Israel kept rejecting the December 1948 resolution, "a revision of its attitude toward Israel has become unavoidable." Israel rejected the demands in June, and the revision never came. [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] [cite:djn_1977_truman_note] The note is set in the sequence of Truman's decisions in [scene:tr_s02|the section on Truman after recognition].

Israel's offer came on 3 August. According to the Commission's own progress report, Israel offered to accept the return of 100,000 refugees, which would have brought its Arab population to a maximum of 250,000. The offer was made "only as an integral part of a general and final peace settlement." [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_ccp_fourth_report] The Arab delegations called it contrary to Resolution 194 and to the Protocol. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_israel_studies_peace] Israel later withdrew it. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_ccp_fourth_report] The same report records that Israel blocked a committee on orange groves owned by refugees. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] The conference ended on 12 September without agreement, and the United States warned Israel that it might revise its relations over the impasse.

The positions on Jerusalem at Lausanne are not recorded in the sources used for this section, which is why the comparison leaves them out.

Each side's case had force in its own terms. Israel's rested on three points: the partition plan the Arab delegations now invoked had, in its view, been nullified by the Arab invasion; Resolution 194 itself spoke of refugees who wished to return "and live at peace with their neighbours," which Ben-Gurion read as making return contingent on peace with the Arab states [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:unwatch_194]; and an offer of 100,000 within a final peace was a concrete figure, not a refusal. The Arab case rested on three points as well: paragraph 11 of the resolution said that refugees wishing to return "should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date" [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:wiki_res_194], which on their reading made return something owed rather than something to be traded for peace; the Protocol Israel had signed took the 1947 map as its basis; and an offer conditional on a final peace, later withdrawn altogether, returned no one. Both cases rest on documents.

The shape of the failure explains why it lasted. Israel would discuss return only after peace. The Arab states would discuss peace only after return. With neither moving, some 700,000 refugees remained refugees, and the question has outlived every negotiation since. The offer of 100,000 shows that Israel was prepared to put a number on the table; the conditions attached to it, and the Arab rejection of those conditions, show why the number never became policy. In the same months Israel was declining Husni al-Za'im's offer to resettle 250,000–300,000 refugees in Syria as part of a peace, an episode told in the section on Syria in 1949. [cite:tf_zaim]

What the record does not settle is whether either sequence could have been moved. The Commission's report shows the positions as stated; it does not show what either side would have accepted under sustained pressure, and the one external power that threatened such pressure, the United States, did not apply it. Whether a larger Israeli offer, or Arab acceptance of the 100,000 as a first step, would have led to a settlement is a matter of judgment, not of documents. What the documents do show is the moment at which the refugee problem stopped being a consequence of the war and became one of the conflict's permanent features.
