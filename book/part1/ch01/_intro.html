This volume comes before the history, and its purpose is to clear the ground. Most readers arrive at the story of Israel and the Palestinians already carrying a map, a vocabulary and a set of claims, picked up in classrooms and places of worship, from news coverage, and in the comment threads where much of the argument now takes place. Some of those claims are accurate. Many attach a true fact to a conclusion it does not support. A few are false. The Baseline sorts them before the narrative begins, so that the volumes that follow can tell the history without stopping on every page to define a word or correct a premise.

It does three things. First, it sets out the facts that every later chapter assumes: where the lines run and who governs on each side of them, who the Palestinians are in law, who may become a citizen of Israel, and who lived on the land before either national movement existed. The map of the West Bank drawn by the Oslo II agreement of 1995 belongs here, and so does the Ottoman district of Jerusalem that answered directly to Constantinople from 1872.[cite:wiki_oslo_areas] [cite:wiki_mutasarrifate_jerusalem]

Second, it fixes the definitions. "Occupation," "apartheid" and "genocide" each have a meaning in international law, and different bodies, of very different standing, have applied them. "Semitic" is a linguist's term of 1781 for a family of languages; "antisemitism" names a hatred of Jews far older than the word.[cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_bloodlibel] Zionism is a political movement founded at Basel in 1897; Judaism is a religion and a set of lineages thousands of years old.[cite:basel_program] The volume keeps these apart because so much public argument depends on running them together.

Third, it examines the ways the story is told: in American textbooks, in historians' arguments over 1948, through state-funded messaging, and on the platforms that decide what stays posted. These channels are part of the record too, and they are held to the same standard as everything else, in both directions.

The volume has six chapters. Getting Your Bearings states the method and sets out the map, the population, the citizenship laws and the three legal words. The Land Before the State covers three thousand years in brief, Ottoman Palestine, the Jewish community before Zionism and the two peoples' histories on the land. Words and Names examines the name "Israel," the words "Semitic" and "antisemitism," the hatred itself, the flag, and the definitions now in contest. Peoples and Traditions describes the Jewish lineages, a thousand years of Jewish life in the Muslim world, the land's other communities, the line between Judaism and Zionism, Jewish dissent and the Christian argument over one verse of Genesis. Two Displacements tells the exodus of the Jews of the Arab world and tests its comparison with the Palestinian displacement of 1948. How the Story Is Told closes the volume.

The method is stated at the start and applied throughout. Each claim is presented as documented, reported, contested or unresolved; both sides' official records are used; advocacy is named as advocacy; and interpretive readings are labeled as readings. What the Baseline does not do is narrate. The sequence of events that led to the founding of the state, and the war of 1948, belong to the next volume. One question runs through every section here, and it is simple to ask and harder to answer: what does the record actually show, as distinct from the version most readers were handed?
