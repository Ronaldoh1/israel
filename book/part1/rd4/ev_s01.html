"No One Wants Them": Évian, Bermuda and the Closing Doors

After Germany annexed Austria in 1938, President Roosevelt convened an international conference on the refugees fleeing Nazi rule. It met at Évian from 6 to 15 July 1938. Delegates from 32 countries attended. Nearly all of them expressed sympathy, and nearly all of them declined to take more people. [cite:wiki_evian]

The reasons they gave were specific. Britain's delegate, Lord Winterton, said the United Kingdom "is not a country of immigration." France claimed it had reached saturation. Brazil asked for certificates of Christian baptism. Canada wanted only experienced farmers. The United States, whose president had called the meeting, offered only to fill its existing German–Austrian quota, which had previously gone unfilled. [cite:wiki_evian] [cite:newhumanitarian_evian] [cite:facinghistory_evian]

It is sometimes said that the world did what it could for Jewish refugees before the Holocaust. The record of Évian shows governments doing what they chose to do, which in most cases was very little. Only one country offered to take a large number: the Dominican Republic, ruled by the dictator Rafael Trujillo, whose soldiers had massacred thousands of Haitians the year before. The Nazi party's newspaper in Germany drew its own conclusion from the proceedings: "No one wants them." Chaim Weizmann told the Manchester Guardian that the world was now divided into places where Jews could not live and places they could not enter.

One possible destination was kept out of the discussion altogether. At Britain's insistence, any mention of Palestine as a destination was excluded from the agenda. Golda Meir attended as the Jewish observer from Palestine, but she was not permitted to participate. [cite:holocaust_au_evian] [cite:wiki_evian]

Five years later, the question returned in far worse circumstances. In April 1943, with the extermination of Europe's Jews under way, the United States and Britain met again, this time in Bermuda. A program proposing that Britain open Palestine to refugees was not considered, and the conference produced little; it was widely judged ineffective. [cite:usf_bermuda_evian]

The two conferences framed the problem that the rest of this period would turn on. When nearly every country refused to take Jewish refugees, the argument that Jews needed a place of their own gained force. At the same time Britain, which had kept Palestine off the agenda at Évian, held the one door that the Zionist movement was pushing. Both facts belong in any account of how the state of 1948 came about: the refusal of the many, and the contest over the one. The people who could not go anywhere else would soon try to reach Palestine by other means, a story told in the section on [scene:ab_s01|Aliyah Bet].
