Demolished for Lack of a Permit

The count. In 2025 Israeli authorities demolished 1,657 Palestinian structures in the West Bank, according to the UN's humanitarian office. Of these, 1,538, or 93 percent, were demolished because they had been built without an Israeli permit; 87 were punitive demolitions, mostly the family homes of attackers; 32 were destroyed in military operations. [cite:wb_ocha_snap2025] Part Five showed why permits are scarce: in Area C, where Israel controls planning, less than one percent of the land has been planned for Palestinian building, and few applications are approved. [scene:wb5_areac|Area C and the permit regime]

Over the longer run, the UN counted nearly 12,000 people displaced by permit demolitions in Area C between 2009 and 2025, about two-thirds of them from Bedouin and herding communities, and 2025 was the second-worst year in that series. Punitive demolitions since 7 October 2023 displaced 420 more people, 166 of them children. [cite:wb_ocha_upd348] Israel's position, set out in Part Five, is that building in Area C requires approval under the interim agreements, and that unauthorized building is enforced against. [cite:wb_cogat_2015] Most of 2025's record displacement came from a different source, the army's operations in the northern refugee camps, which Part Nine covers. [cite:wb_ocha_snap2025]

Masafer Yatta

In the hills south of Hebron, about 7,400 acres were declared Firing Zone 918, a military training area, in the early 1980s. More than a thousand Palestinians live there, in eight villages, many of them in caves and herding hamlets. After two decades of litigation, Israel's Supreme Court ruled on 4 May 2022 against the residents. It accepted the state's case that they had not proven they lived there permanently before the zone was declared, and it treated the prohibition on forcibly transferring an occupied population as a treaty rule that Israeli courts could not enforce. The Norwegian Refugee Council said the ruling opened the door for the army to uproot entire Palestinian communities, and UN human rights experts urged Israel to stop the forcible transfer of the residents. [cite:wb_sabrang_masafer]

Khan al-Ahmar

Khan al-Ahmar is a hamlet of Bedouin, fewer than 200 in 2021 and about 300 by 2026's count, of the Jahalin tribe beside the main road from Jerusalem to Jericho. Its school was built, with Italian money, from mud and old tyres. In 2018 Israel's Supreme Court approved its demolition. The state's case was that the shacks and tents had been built without permits and stood dangerously close to a highway; the residents answered that Israel almost never grants Palestinians permits in that area. The state offered a site near Abu Dis with water, electricity, sewage and a new school. Successive governments then asked the court for delays; critics said they wanted to avoid an international incident over a village that the European Union and rights groups had taken up, and the pro-settlement group Regavim went to court to force the demolition. [cite:wb_toi_khan] In May 2026 Smotrich ordered preparations to evacuate the village; as of that month the order still needed the cabinet's approval and had not been signed. [cite:wb_newarab_khan2026]

Sheikh Jarrah

In East Jerusalem the eviction cases run through civil courts. A law of 1970 allows Jews to reclaim property in East Jerusalem lost in 1948. The Absentees' Property Law of 1950 prevents Palestinians from reclaiming property they lost inside Israel. In 2021 the Norwegian Refugee Council counted about 970 Palestinians in East Jerusalem at risk of eviction, in 218 households with cases filed against them. [cite:wb_nrc_sj] In Sheikh Jarrah the claim is held by Nahalat Shimon, a company owned by a firm registered in Delaware, which bought it from two Jewish associations that had acquired the land in the late nineteenth century. In March 2022 the Supreme Court let four families stay at least until the land is formally registered; in April 2024 a lower court ordered the Diab family, 23 people, out. [cite:wb_pn_diab] The District Court rejected their appeal in February 2025; in November 2025 the Supreme Court agreed to hear a further appeal and froze the eviction, where the case stood at the end of 2025. [cite:wb_amnesty_diab2025]

The claimants' case, as the courts have heard it, rests on documents of purchase from the nineteenth century; the families and rights groups point to the asymmetry in the table. [cite:wb_pn_diab] [cite:wb_nrc_sj] Both describe the same two laws.
