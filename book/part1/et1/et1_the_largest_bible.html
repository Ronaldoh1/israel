Eighty-One Books, Counted Two Ways

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church states its canon plainly on its own website: "46 books of the Old Testament and 35 books of the New Testament that will bring the total of canonized books of the Bible to 81". [cite:vt27_eotc_books] Its New Testament adds to the familiar twenty-seven a set of church-order books: the Sinodos, the Book of the Covenant, Clement and the Didascalia. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] The number eighty-one is fixed. What it counts is not, and that is the first thing a reader of this Bible needs to know.

The scholar R. W. Cowley, who studied the question in 1974, found that the church uses two reckonings that both arrive at eighty-one. A broader canon, from the traditional commentary on the law code called the Fetha Nagast, has 46 Old Testament and 35 New Testament books. A narrower canon, from the church's prayers and the Bibles issued under the emperor, has 54 Old Testament books, counting some as separate that others combine, and the usual 27 of the New. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The law code itself says "81" at the head of its list, Cowley noted, but the list as given does not add up to eighty-one. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] In practice, he concluded, the narrower canon is undisputed, while the additional books of the broader canon are treated more as authoritative commentary. [cite:vt27_cowley1974]

A canon counted two ways is a canon that was never closed by a single act of authority, and that is the most important fact about it. There was no Ethiopian Trent. The eighty-one books are a tradition that grew, recorded in law codes, prayers and imperial Bibles, and the church has lived with the looseness for centuries. The Western reader trained by The Canon to look for the council and the vote will not find one here.

What the Ethiopian church did control, as tightly as Rome ever did, was the language. Its Old Testament was translated into Ge'ez, the classical language of the kingdom of Aksum, from the Greek Septuagint. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] Ge'ez is attested in inscriptions from the third and fourth centuries; it ceased to be spoken as an everyday language somewhere between about 900 and 1200, and it remains the liturgical language of the church. [cite:sm_eb_geez] For most of the last millennium, then, Ethiopian Christians heard their scriptures in a language the clergy had learned and the people had not, exactly as Western Christians heard theirs in Latin. The texts were different. The structure of access was the same.

The age of the Ethiopian Bible is not in doubt. The Garima Gospels have been radiocarbon dated: one volume to between about 390 and 570, the other to between about 530 and 660, and the scholar Curt Niccum has argued that most of the New Testament was translated into Ge'ez before the end of the fourth century. [cite:vt27_met_aksum] The scriptures arrived early, and with them came a church, a throne and the bond between them that the rest of this book follows.
