Part 9: The Jewish Agency Before the Senate Committee, May–August 1963

Of the thirteen parts of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee's inquiry into nondiplomatic agents of foreign principals, Part 9 concerned the Jewish Agency for Israel and the lobbying organizations connected with it. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] It was the part that touched the body the Justice Department had ordered, the previous November, to register as a foreign agent, as told in [scene:s11a|the order of 21 November 1962]. It was also the part conducted most fully behind closed doors, and the part whose meaning has been argued over most since. This section follows the hearing through the summer of 1963: who appeared, what the money bought, what the committee is recorded as concluding, and how the two sides have read the result.

The closed sessions. On 28 June 1963 the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported that the committee had "continued a closed inquiry on the activities of the Jewish Agency", with Senator J. William Fulbright, Democrat of Arkansas, presiding. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] The witness named in that report was Gottlieb Hammer of New York, a former officer of the New York section of the Jewish Agency for Israel and, since April 1960, executive vice chairman of the Jewish Agency, Inc., a body the report described as not itself a subject of the inquiry. He was accompanied by the Agency's attorney, Maurice Boukstein. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] The report added that the committee had for "several months" been studying how the Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938 was administered, the frame within which the Agency was being examined. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] The Agency's American Section was already registered under that Act; the question was what its registration disclosed, and what it did not. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628]

Boukstein's role was to contest the premise. Fulbright treated the Agency's payments to the American Zionist Council as functionally equivalent to direct funding of an American lobby by the Israeli government. Boukstein challenged that characterization directly and sharply on the record, and the Agency's side described it as "a completely independent body composed of representatives of Zionist organizations from all over the free world." [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing] (The account this library first drew on calls Boukstein the Council's counsel; the contemporary report of 28 June calls him the Agency's attorney.)

The funding inquiry. Kobby Barda, in a 2026 article in Modern Judaism, dates the opening of the committee's investigation of the Agency's American funding of the Council's publications to 2 August 1963, a date he takes from Isaiah L. Kenen's affidavit, and he places the hearings in August. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The central witness was Isidor (also spelled Isadore) Hamlin, director of the Agency's American branch. Barda quotes his answer on the core question of where the money went: "we did not pay Mr. Kenen directly, we paid for the subscriptions of the American Zionist Council." [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] From the exchange with Hamlin, Barda reports Agency payments of $38,000 for subscriptions to Kenen's newsletter, the Near East Report. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026]

Hamlin's distinction is the hinge of the whole dispute. A payment for subscriptions is a purchase from a publisher; a payment to Kenen would have been a payment to the head of a lobbying committee. On Hamlin's account the Agency had bought copies of a newsletter through the Council, not paid the lobbyist who wrote it. On the opposing account the distinction was one of form: money from a registered foreign agent reached the lobbyist's publication by way of an unregistered American body.

Kenen's affidavit. Kenen was not called as a witness. He submitted a written affidavit instead. In it he stated that the Council's purchases had never exceeded 23 per cent of the newsletter's distributed copies, even at their peak; that he had stopped his work for the Council in July 1960; and that the subscription purchases had ended in 1962. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] He gave his salary while working for the Council as $100 a week. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The affidavit's argument was proportion and time: the Council was one buyer among many, its purchases were a minority of the run, its link with Kenen had ended three years earlier, and the money had stopped before the hearing began.

The newsletter's origins. The Near East Report had begun as a joint venture with the Council. According to the Council's records as Barda reads them, its board decided on 25 January 1955 to launch the newsletter that became the Near East Report, with an initial run of 6,500 copies for the public-relations committee and a further 5,500 taken by the Council at 15 cents a copy. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] At that rate the Council's share of a single issue came to $825; the records reached here do not say how many issues it bought. By October 1957 the newsletter was building a paying readership. On 21 October 1957 Kenen reported to Rabbi Philip Bernstein that 1,100 subscriptions had been added that week and that 7,000 copies were being distributed, and he proposed cutting the price from $10 to $5. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] Kenen's own later account gives a different starting point. Speaking off the record on 13 September 1963, he said the Near East Report began in 1957 as a biweekly newsletter. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] The sources do not reconcile the two dates, and the difference is left as they give it.

The money in dispute. How much Agency money reached the Council is the first of the two disputes the hearing left behind. On one side stands a figure of about $5.1 million. The Justice Department's order had alleged "more than $5 million" over eight years, and the Senate record carries a figure of $5,100,001.02 for the period from 1 January 1955 to 31 December 1962, which Fulbright entered into the committee's record. [cite:azc_fara_doj] [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing] On the other side stands Barda, who writes that the Council "received $1.5 million from the Jewish Agency" between 1955 and 1962. He presents that as a claim raised in the matter, gives no source for it, and does not mention the $5.1 million figure. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] He frames the payments he does document, the $38,000, as subscription purchases for a commercial newsletter. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] These figures do not measure the same thing. The $5.1 million is a total of Agency payments to the Council for all purposes; the $38,000 is the part of them that Barda reports, from Hamlin's testimony, as spent on the Near East Report. The sources reached here do not say what the rest of the Council's receipts paid for, and they do not reconcile the $5.1 million with the $1.5 million.

What the hearing established. The second dispute concerns what Part 9 showed, and here the evidence on each side is of a different kind.

The first reading comes from the Agency itself and from Barda. In August 1963, Dr Emanuel Neumann, chairman of the Jewish Agency–American Section, told the Jewish Telegraphic Agency that Fulbright had praised the Agency's achievements and had said repeatedly that there was "no wrong doing". The only open question, Neumann said, was a technical one: whether the Agency's periodic reports were detailed enough. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630816] He called the result a vindication: "a prolonged and meticulous examination has turned up absolutely nothing damaging to the status of the Jewish Agency." [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630816] Barda, working from the hearing record and the Council's papers, concludes that the separation between the Agency and Kenen was "absolute". [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The strongest form of this case is that the Agency was a registered agent, its payments were for copies of a newsletter sold to the public, the purchases were a minority of the run and had ended, Kenen had left the Council's employ in 1960, and the committee's own chairman is reported to have found nothing wrong.

The second reading comes from Grant F. Smith of IRmep, an advocate of registration, and from the American Council for Judaism, whose dissent from Zionism is told later in this stage. Both read Part 9 as showing Agency money funding American lobbying and public relations, and both treat the closed sessions, and what they describe as White House involvement, as evidence that the full story was withheld. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:rs_cw5_acj_1997] Barda records that Smith obtained the 1963 hearing records under the JFK Records Act and cites his books America's Defense Line (2008) and Foreign Agents (2009). [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The strongest form of this case is that the Justice Department's own lawyer, as Smith reports him, read the hearings as showing that the Council was receiving large transfers for publicity and lobbying, not distributing educational material, and that the sums on the order and in the Senate record run to millions, not thousands. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast]

Some of the conduit reading's specific claims are unconfirmed and should be read as such. An unsigned article published by the American Council for Judaism on 1 September 1997 states that the Agency's executive director and his deputy testified in executive session "because the White House intervened"; that some witnesses asked to appear without oath, transcript or publicity and were refused; and that the closed transcript was withheld. These are the Council's account; the contemporary reports found for this book do not confirm them. [cite:rs_cw5_acj_1997] The same article attributes to Fulbright's biographer, Randall Bennett Woods, a claim that Agency representatives admitted paying expenses of Lyndon Johnson's entourage at the 1960 Democratic convention. That claim could not be checked against Woods's book or the hearing transcript, and it stands unverified. [cite:rs_cw5_acj_1997]

The two readings are not wholly incompatible. Neumann's report concerns the Agency's status as a registrant, which the committee is reported to have left intact. The conduit reading concerns the Council's status, which was the subject of the Justice Department's order, not of the Senate's finding. Both can be accurate about their own question. What divides them is whether a newsletter subscription bought by a registered agent through an American umbrella body was a sale or a subsidy, and that the record reached here does not decide.

Kenen's own words. One month after the hearings, Kenen spoke to the Third Annual Young Leadership Conference of the United Jewish Appeal in New York, off the record, on 13 September 1963. He described his American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs as founded in 1953 and later renamed the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] Of the inquiry he said: "We seem to have anticipated the issues raised in the Fulbright investigation by a dozen years", and that neither AIPAC nor the UJA "was seriously involved in the Senate Hearings." [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] The remark states his position precisely. On his account, the committee he ran had been built from the start to stand outside the arrangement the senators were examining. It is the account of an interested party, given in private to a sympathetic audience, and it sits beside an affidavit in which he acknowledged that the Council had bought copies of his newsletter until 1962 and had once paid his salary. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026]

The legal position that emerged. By the end of 1963 the lobby's legal footing rested on a structural argument that has defined it since. AIPAC's defence is that it is funded by American donors, not directly by the Israeli government, and that its activity is domestic political advocacy rather than action at a foreign principal's direction. [cite:factually_aipac_fara] That was a real distinction from the arrangement the 1962 order had named, in which an American body received money directly from the Agency, whatever one concludes about its persuasiveness. [cite:factually_aipac_fara] The question was raised again in a 1988 petition by the former Senator Fulbright and Victor Marchetti, a former senior CIA official, which the Justice Department rejected, and in a 2024 bill by Representative Thomas Massie; those episodes, and the Act's later shift toward civil enforcement, are told in [scene:fis_s07|why AIPAC is not registered]. [cite:crustnews_aipac_history] How the same distinction looks beside a European organization that has taken direct reimbursement from the Israeli government is examined in [scene:s19g|the lobby beyond America].

The gaps in the record are larger than the findings. The full transcript of the closed sessions is not among the sources reached for this volume, and neither are the dates of any public sessions on the Agency. The committee's own conclusions are known here only through Neumann's account of what Fulbright said, which is the account of a party to the hearing. The total of the Agency's payments to the Council stands at two unreconciled figures, $5,100,001.02 in the Senate record and $1.5 million in Barda, and what most of that money paid for is not documented in the material reached here. What is documented is narrower: a registered foreign agent bought $38,000 of subscriptions to a lobbyist's newsletter through an American umbrella body; the lobbyist said those purchases never exceeded 23 per cent of his run and ended in 1962; and the committee's chairman, by the Agency's account, found no wrongdoing, while the Justice Department's order against the umbrella body had still not been complied with.
