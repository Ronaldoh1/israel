The Idea Appears, 1862–1882

The familiar account holds that Zionism is millennia old and that Jews always yearned for Israel. As a written political idea, however, Zionism appears in the 1860s through the 1880s, as scattered proposals that answered a modern problem: modern European antisemitism. It was contested from the start, and the migration data of the period point in a different direction.

The earliest of these proposals came from Moses Hess (1812–1875), a German-Jewish philosopher and early socialist who had collaborated and corresponded with Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels in the 1840s. His 1862 book, Rome and Jerusalem: The Last National Question, argued that assimilation had failed to end antisemitism and that Jews needed national self-determination. It proposed a socialist Jewish commonwealth in Palestine. [cite:wiki_moses_hess] The book sold poorly. Mainstream European Jewish thought in those decades was moving toward emancipation and integration, and Hess's socialist nationalism fit neither camp. He died in 1875 largely unread. Only after the Zionist Congress of 1897 was he rediscovered and placed in the movement's origin story as a founding intellectual figure. The record does not show a continuous line of influence from Hess to Herzl, who developed his own position chiefly in response to the Dreyfus Affair. Hess is best read as an early, isolated voice, not the hidden start of a plan.

The pressure that produced a wider response came from Russia. After Tsar Alexander II was assassinated in March 1881, waves of pogroms broke out across the Pale of Settlement, where roughly five million Jews, the largest Jewish population in the world, were confined by law. The violence was organized, in many cases with local official complicity. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] Under Alexander III, the May Laws of 1882 then formally restricted where Jews could live, what they could study and how they could earn a living. [cite:wiki_may_laws] This was persecution written into law with a date attached, not a vague climate of hostility. The violence did not stop in the 1880s. A second wave came in 1903–1906 and included the Kishinev pogrom of April 1903, in which 49 people were killed. [cite:wiki_kishinev_pogrom] The third, during the Russian Civil War of 1917–1921, was by far the deadliest, with an estimated 100,000–250,000 Jews killed. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire]

The first territorial answer came from Leon Pinsker (1821–1891), an Odessa physician who had earlier supported Jewish integration into Russian society. The pogroms of 1881 and the May Laws convinced him that integration offered no safety. His pamphlet Auto-Emancipation, published in 1882, argued that Jews needed a territory of their own rather than relying on the goodwill of host nations. [cite:wiki_auto_emancipation] The argument was political and secular, framed around physical safety, not scriptural return. Pinsker did not insist on Palestine. He considered any suitable territory and is documented as open to alternatives including Argentina. [cite:wiki_leon_pinsker] At its written origin, political Zionism was territorially agnostic; the fixed focus on Palestine came later.

The people fleeing the pogroms made their own choice. Roughly two million Jews left Imperial Russia in this era. Documented estimates put 90–95 percent of them in the United States and about 3 percent in Palestine. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] Jewish communities in New York, Chicago and Philadelphia grew from small pre-1881 populations into a diaspora of millions. Given a choice under real persecution, the great majority chose the country that offered civic equality and economic opportunity. That pattern is hard to square with any account in which Palestine was the obvious destination. It does not make the smaller group who went to Palestine unimportant; their settlements would shape much of what followed.

Opposition also had a formal voice. In 1885 American Reform Judaism adopted the Pittsburgh Platform, which stated:

"We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine..."

This was the founding-generation position of the largest Jewish religious denomination in the United States, adopted three years after Pinsker's pamphlet. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] Reform Judaism's embrace of Zionism came only in the mid-twentieth century.

Two further facts belong to this moment. The word "Semitic" had been coined in 1781 by the German scholar August Ludwig von Schlözer, from Shem, son of Noah, to name a language family that includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic and Amharic. It had no political charge until Wilhelm Marr coined "antisemitism" in 1879, three years before Pinsker wrote. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] The overlap is one of timing; no causal link is claimed. Finally, the land under discussion was already inhabited. Palestinian Arabs, Semitic-language speakers descended from populations present in the region since antiquity according to peer-reviewed population genetics, were the majority population of Ottoman Palestine in this period. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_nature] They are absent from the earliest proto-Zionist writing. That absence was noticed within the movement: Ahad Ha'am raised the concern in writing in 1891.
