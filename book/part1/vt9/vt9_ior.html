The Institute for the Works of Religion

Three different bodies hold and move the money that is usually called "the Vatican's". The Institute for the Works of Religion (Istituto per le Opere di Religione, IOR), often called the Vatican Bank, takes deposits and manages assets for clients. The Administration of the Patrimony of the Apostolic See (APSA) owns and manages the Holy See's own property and investments. The Governorate of Vatican City State runs the territory and its museums. They publish separate reports, and the sums in one cannot simply be added to the sums in another. This section deals with the IOR. The next deals with APSA, the Governorate and the Holy See's consolidated accounts. [cite:vt9_vn_apsa_mandate] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024]

From a charitable commission to an institute

The IOR's own statutes trace its origin to 1887, when Leo XIII created a Commission ad pias causas (for pious causes), which became the Administration for the Works of Religion. Pius XII approved that Administration's statutes on 17 March 1941. By a chirograph (a papal letter with the force of law) dated 27 June 1942, he founded the Institute for the Works of Religion, gave it legal personality and folded the Administration into it. A second chirograph of 24 January 1944 issued new rules, and John Paul II gave the Institute a new framework on 1 March 1990. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2019] Summaries that credit Pius XI with the founding are wrong: the 1942 chirograph is Pius XII's. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2019]

The present statutes come from Francis. He renewed them on a two-year trial basis by chirograph of 8 August 2019 and issued them in final form on 30 January 2023, saying that the aim was to define clearly what each organ is responsible for. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2019] [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2023] Article 1 sets the purpose that both texts repeat: the IOR exists to provide for the custody and administration of movable and immovable goods that natural or legal persons transfer or entrust to it, intended for works of religion or charity. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2023] It is not a bank licensed in Italy or the European Union. It describes itself as "the only institution authorized to provide financial services in the Vatican City State". [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] Its supervision under Vatican anti-money-laundering law, and the Council of Europe's MONEYVAL evaluations of it, are covered in [scene:vt5_moneyval_euro|Part Five].

Who may hold an account

The IOR is not open to the public. After the scandals that [scene:vt10_ambrosiano_calvi|Part Ten] covers, it reviewed its client files and closed accounts that did not fit its purpose. When it published its 2014 results in May 2015, the Associated Press reported that it had closed about 4,600 accounts by the end of 2014, some dormant and some held by clients outside the categories it now accepted: religious communities, Catholic bodies, clergy, current and former Vatican employees, and embassies and diplomats accredited to the Holy See. It had 15,181 account holders left. By October 2015, when the review was completed, about 4,800 accounts had been closed in all ([scene:vt10_reform_aml|Part Ten]). [cite:vt9_ap_ior2015] [cite:vt10_ansa_aif2016] Its 2024 and 2025 reports describe "over 12,000 clients" in more than 110 countries, all belonging to or serving the Catholic Church, and the 2025 report singles out religious congregations as the group it worked hardest to serve. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] [cite:vt9_vn_ior2025]

Who runs it

The statutes set up five organs, each above the next. A Commission of Cardinals, five cardinals named by the pope for five years and renewable once, checks that the Institute keeps to its statutes and approves the dividend. A Prelate, appointed by the Commission, acts as its secretary and attends the board. The Board of Superintendence, seven members appointed by the Commission for five years, manages the Institute and approves its accounts; its President is the IOR's legal representative. A Director General, appointed by the Board with the Commission's approval, runs daily operations. An external auditor, appointed by the Commission on the Board's proposal, serves three years and can be renewed once. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2019] [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2023] The 2024 accounts were audited by Mazars Italia and the 2025 accounts by Deloitte & Touche; both gave clean opinions. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025]

Jean-Baptiste de Franssu finished his term when the 2025 accounts were approved on 28 April 2026, and François Pauly took office as President. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] Pauly's earlier career at Edmond de Rothschild and the claims made about it are examined in [scene:vt11_pauly_2026|Part Eleven]. No wrongdoing by him is documented in any source used in this volume.

What the 2024 and 2025 reports show

The 2025 report was the IOR's fourteenth annual report. Its press release on the 2024 report, dated 11 June 2025, gave a net profit of 32.8 million euros, up 7 per cent; net equity of 731.9 million euros; and a Tier 1 capital ratio of 69.43 per cent, which the release said placed the IOR among the most solidly capitalised institutions in the world. Client funds were 5.7 billion euros, up from 5.4 billion, covering "deposits, current accounts, asset management and securities under custody". [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] The 2025 report, published on 11 May 2026, gave net income of 51 million euros, up 55.5 per cent and the highest in ten years. Net assets were 815.3 million euros, the Tier 1 ratio was 71.9 per cent, client assets were 5.9 billion euros, and the Institute had 115 employees. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] [cite:vt9_vn_ior2025]

Two of these figures are often confused. Client assets of 5.9 billion euros are money the IOR holds or manages for its clients: congregations, dioceses, Holy See offices, clergy and staff. The IOR does not own that money. Net assets of 815.3 million euros are the Institute's own capital. Reports that call the 5.7 or 5.9 billion the IOR's "total assets" or its "wealth", as an earlier Observe The System scene did, mistake the clients' money for the bank's. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025]

The dividend and what it pays for

The IOR has no shareholders. Each year the Commission of Cardinals decides how much of the profit to pass to the pope and how much to keep as capital. For 2024 it voted a dividend of 13.8 million euros. For 2025 it voted 24.3 million euros, up 76.1 per cent, having considered "the Institute's capitalization needs". [cite:vt9_ior_ar2024] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] The releases say only that the dividend serves the Institute's "mission to support works of religion and charity"; they do not list what it is spent on. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] The Holy See's own accounts show where the money goes. In 2015, the first year of consolidated accounts under the new rules, the Secretariat for the Economy listed a 50 million euro contribution from the IOR as one of the Holy See's main sources of income beside its investments, together with 24 million euros from dioceses. [cite:vt9_hs_2015] The IOR's dividend therefore helps cover the running costs of the Roman Curia. It is not a separate charity fund.

One bank for the Holy See, and then not only one

Before 2022, offices of the Holy See could keep money in foreign banks as well as at the IOR. On 23 August 2022 Francis issued a rescript (a papal ruling on how a law is to be read) interpreting Article 219 of his new constitution for the Curia, Praedicate Evangelium. It made the IOR's role as "asset manager and custodian of the movable patrimony of the Holy See" its "exclusive responsibility" and ordered funds held elsewhere moved to the IOR within thirty days of 1 September 2022. [cite:vt9_ewtn_rescript2022] Leo XIV reversed this in part. His rescript Coniuncta Cura, made public on 6 October 2025, repealed the exclusivity. APSA is to use the IOR as a rule but may use banks abroad if the competent bodies "deem it more efficient or convenient", provided it follows the policies of the investment committee chaired by Cardinal Kevin Farrell. [cite:vt9_cna_coniuncta] As of October 2026 that is the rule in force.

Interpretation, not finding: "IOR" read backwards

A reading held on the Sophia platform notes that "IOR" read backwards gives "ROI", the business abbreviation for return on investment. It calls this either a coincidence or an accidentally honest name for an institution that produces a return for the papacy every year. Read through this lens, the dividend figures above give the joke a point: in 2025 the Institute that exists for "works of religion" returned 24.3 million euros to the pope.

The reading proves nothing. The acronym comes from an Italian name chosen in 1942, Istituto per le Opere di Religione, and no document suggests that anyone at the time intended a second meaning. The figures above would be the same under any name. In this volume the wordplay is a way to remember the dividend, not evidence of what the IOR is for. What the IOR is for, by its own account, is stated in Article 1 of its statutes. [cite:vt9_ior_statutes2023]
