Myths Tested: What Nicaea Did Not Do, and What It Did

A common summary of the founding of institutional Christianity runs like this: Constantine, an emperor; Paul, who never met Yeshua; and a council that decided what Christianity was going to be. Each of the three parts points at something real. Each also carries a popular overstatement that the record does not support. This section tests the summary piece by piece, and then tests the most widely repeated claims about Nicaea itself: that it chose the Bible, that it voted Jesus divine by a narrow margin, that it burned rival gospels, that it removed reincarnation from Christian teaching, that it shut out Ethiopian or Gnostic bishops, and that the creed said in churches today is the one written in 325. The aim is not to defend the council. The previous section showed that its central act, a definition of Christ's nature adopted under an emperor's eye and enforced by exile and book-burning, is a documented finding. [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided] The aim is to keep that finding from being buried under claims that fail.

Constantine: supported, with one correction

The emperor's part is the strongest element of the summary. Constantine summoned the bishops, moved them by the imperial post, opened the council in person, and, on the testimony of Eusebius of Caesarea, urged the insertion of the word homoousios into the creed. He then exiled the bishops who would not sign and ordered Arius's books burned. [cite:vt22_vc3] [cite:vt22_socrates1] The correction concerns motive and consistency. His own letter of about 324 called the dispute "this truly idle question", which suggests he wanted agreement more than any particular answer, and within a few years he recalled the exiles. The bishop who baptised him in 337 was Eusebius of Nicomedia, one of the men exiled after Nicaea. [cite:vt22_vc2] [cite:vt22_wiki_eusnic] Constantine shaped the outcome of 325, but he did not hold to it as a fixed doctrine of state; that came later, under Theodosius I. Part Twenty-One covers the emperor's conversion and titles. [scene:vt21_conversion|Constantine's conversion]

Paul: real, but not at Nicaea

Paul's own letters claim a revelation of the risen Christ rather than acquaintance with Yeshua in life, and they also record that he met Peter and James in Jerusalem; Part Twenty sets out that record. [scene:vt20_paul|Paul] Paul had no part in Nicaea. He wrote in the first century, and the council he describes attending was the meeting in Jerusalem of Galatians 2: "after fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas". [cite:vt22_gal2] By 325 his letters had been read in churches for more than two centuries, and both Arius and his opponents argued from them. The point in the summary that holds is that Paul's letters, written by a man who knew the risen Christ only by his own account of revelation, were already the shared foundation of the debate at Nicaea. The council did not add them; it inherited them.

"The council decided what Christianity would be": partly supported

Nicaea decided one central question, the relation of the Son to the Father, for the churches in communion with the emperor, and set the method by which later councils would decide others. That is a large finding. It did not decide the scriptures, it did not create belief in Christ's divinity, and it did not settle even its own question: the Nicene party lost imperial favour within a generation and regained it only in 381. [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] [cite:vt22_wiki_const381] Nor did it decide for all Christians. Communities outside the empire and groups already separated, such as the Novatians, kept their own life; the Novatian bishop Acesius accepted the creed and still refused to rejoin. [cite:vt22_sozomen122]

The canon and the altar

The most widespread claim is that Nicaea chose the books of the Bible. Its creed and twenty canons name no book of scripture, and no ancient account of its sessions mentions a list. [cite:vt22_canons] The first list matching the 27 books of the New Testament is Athanasius's letter of 367, more than forty years later; Part Fourteen sets out the steps from the second century to Trent in 1546, and Part Twenty-Three goes deeper. [scene:vt14_canon|Who chose the Bible] [scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|Lists and letters]

The claim has a traceable origin. The Synodicon Vetus, a Greek summary of church councils written between about 887 and 920, says the books were "placed down by the holy altar" and that the council prayed for the inspired works to be found on top and the spurious beneath. It is the earliest source to link the canon to Nicaea, and historians describe it as unreliable and full of errors. [cite:vt22_wiki_synodicon] It was first printed in 1601 by the theologian Johannes Pappus, from a copy supplied by Andreas Darmarios, a manuscript dealer with a poor reputation. [cite:vt22_wiki_synodicon] In the eighteenth century Voltaire retold the story in his Philosophical Dictionary, with the books to be rejected falling to the floor, and added drily: "It is a pity that this eloquent procedure has not survived". [cite:vt22_voltaire] Voltaire is the main channel through which the altar story, and the wider idea that Nicaea made the canon, reached modern readers. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] [cite:vt22_wiki_synodicon]

"They voted Jesus divine"

Dan Brown's novel The Da Vinci Code (2003) told readers that Christ's divinity was passed at Nicaea by "a relatively close vote". [cite:vt22_america_hagen] Two parts of that are wrong. First, there was no close vote: all but two bishops signed. [cite:vt22_socrates1] Second, Christians called Christ divine long before 325. Around 111 to 113 the Roman governor Pliny reported to the emperor Trajan that Christians in Asia Minor met before dawn to sing "a hymn to Christ as to a god". [cite:vt22_pliny] Ignatius of Antioch, writing early in the second century, called him "our God, Jesus Christ". [cite:vt22_ignatius] Arius himself called the Son God. [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] What Nicaea voted on, if subscription under the emperor's eye can be called a vote, was a definition of how the Son is divine: eternal and of the Father's own substance, against Arius's created Son. A fairer statement of the platform's point is that the council chose one disputed account of Christ's divinity and made it binding. The record supports that statement.

"They burned the other gospels"

The one documented burning order of the council's aftermath is Constantine's letter commanding that Arius's treatises be "consigned to the flames". [cite:vt22_socrates1] No order from Nicaea, or from Constantine in connection with it, concerns the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Mary or any other gospel. The Nag Hammadi codices were buried in Egypt some decades later for reasons that are not documented; Part Twenty-Five sets out the hypotheses. [scene:vt25_the_jar|The jar] Constantine did act against a Gnostic group by name, in a separate edict that Eusebius preserves without a date. Addressed to "Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites, Paulians" and Montanists, it forbade them to "assemble yourselves together" in public or in "any private house", and ordered their houses of prayer "made over without delay to the catholic Church". [cite:vt22_vc3_64] [cite:vt22_vc3_65] Eusebius adds that the law ordered a search for their books, but he does not say the books were burned, and no surviving text records what was done with them. [cite:vt22_audit_vc3_66] That edict suppressed meetings, confiscated buildings and sought out books; it is not an order to burn gospels. Part Twenty-One places it in Constantine's wider church policy. [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Church and empire] The documented suppression of books at Nicaea is real but narrow: it was aimed at the losing party in the council's own dispute.

"They removed reincarnation"

Nothing in the creed or canons of 325 concerns reincarnation or the fate of souls. [cite:vt22_canons] The claim usually confuses Nicaea with a council held 228 years later. In 553 the Second Council of Constantinople met under the emperor Justinian, chiefly to condemn writings known as the Three Chapters; a set of fifteen anathemas against the teachings of Origen and Evagrius, which most scholars think the bishops signed before the council formally opened, condemned "the fabulous pre-existence of souls" and the "restoration" of all things. [cite:vt22_wiki_const553] [cite:vt22_origen_anath] Pre-existence of the soul is not the same as reincarnation, and no book of the Bible was edited as a result. The documented act is a sixth-century condemnation of a speculative teaching associated with Origen, not a removal of reincarnation from scripture at Nicaea.

"Ethiopian and Gnostic bishops were shut out"

Ethiopia had no bishop until Athanasius consecrated Frumentius, traditionally in 328 and possibly in the 340s. [cite:vt22_wiki_frumentius] No record says that teachers of gnosis were invited or barred. The one rival bishop known to have been present, the Novatian Acesius, was called by the emperor himself. [cite:vt22_sozomen122] The council's canons also set terms for receiving Novatian and Paulianist clergy into communion, which shows the council dealing with other Christian groups by rules of admission rather than by exclusion from the room. [cite:vt22_canons]

"The creed said on Sunday is the creed of 325"

The creed recited in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and many Protestant services is the longer Niceno-Constantinopolitan form. Compared with 325, it adds clauses on the Holy Spirit, the Church, baptism and the resurrection, adds "whose kingdom shall have no end", and drops the anathemas. No surviving document quotes it before the Council of Chalcedon in 451, which accepted it as an authentic interpretation of Nicaea. [cite:vt22_wiki_creed] Latin churches later added that the Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son" (Filioque); Rome took the addition into its liturgy in 1014, and the Eastern Orthodox churches still reject it. [cite:vt22_wiki_creed] The words most Christians say are therefore the product of at least three councils and a later western addition, not of Nicaea alone.

Tested this way, the summary loses its two weakest parts and keeps its strongest. Paul was not in the room and the Bible was not on the table. An emperor was, and the council he convened established that the content of Christian belief could be defined in a written formula and enforced with exile and fire. Which books Christians would read was settled by a different and slower process, in letters and regional councils over the following two centuries, and Part Twenty-Three follows it.
