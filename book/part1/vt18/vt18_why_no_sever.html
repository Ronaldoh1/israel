Why the Holy See Speaks but Does Not Sever

Through three years of war in Gaza the Holy See said things about Israel's conduct that Israel's government called regrettable, disappointing and a double standard, and it did nothing institutional that Israel would have felt as a sanction. As of October 2026 no recall of the Holy See's nuncio, no downgrade or suspension of the relations opened in 1994 and no economic measure has been reported; on the contrary, Leo XIV appointed a new nuncio to Israel, Archbishop Giorgio Lingua, on 22 January 2026. [cite:vt1_aleteia_lingua] [cite:vt18_wiki_hsisrael] For readers of this volume the gap between the words and the acts is a live question, because the front hypothesis predicts that public opposition never costs the other side anything. Before that gap can be read as evidence of anything, the documented reasons for it have to be set out: what the Holy See's treaties say, how it has behaved towards other states in comparable conflicts, what its critics argue, and what it says itself.

What the treaties say

Two treaty clauses state the Holy See's posture in its own words. Article 24 of the Lateran Treaty of 11 February 1929 reads, in the official Italian text, that the Holy See "vuole rimanere e rimarrà estranea alle competizioni temporali fra gli altri Stati": it wishes to remain, and will remain, extraneous to the temporal competitions between other states and to international congresses called for that purpose, "a meno che le parti contendenti facciano concorde appello alla sua missione di pace", unless the contending parties together appeal to its mission of peace. It reserves "in ogni caso" the right "di far valere la sua potestà morale e spirituale", to assert its moral and spiritual power, and in consequence Vatican City is to be "sempre ed in ogni caso" neutral and inviolable territory. [cite:vt1_va_lateran] [cite:vt2_lateran_text]

The Fundamental Agreement with Israel repeats the principle and applies it to the Holy Land. In Article 11(1) both parties commit themselves to the peaceful resolution of conflicts, "excluding violence and terror from international life". In Article 11(2) the Holy See, "while maintaining in every case the right to exercise its moral and spiritual teaching-office", recalls that it is "solemnly committed to remaining a stranger to all merely temporal conflicts", a principle that "applies specifically to disputed territories and unsettled borders". [cite:vt1_va_fa1993]

Read together, the two clauses match the pattern seen in 2023–2026: a right to judge in moral terms, which both texts reserve explicitly, and a commitment not to become a party to the territorial conflict. Neither clause forbids the Holy See to recall a nuncio or to suspend relations; diplomatic relations are not a "temporal conflict". But a measure taken to press one side in a war over territory would sit uneasily with a treaty in which the Holy See promised Israel, by name, to stay out of "disputed territories and unsettled borders". That is a documented constraint, written in 1929 and 1993, not an inference about motive.

How the Holy See has behaved elsewhere

The Holy See's practice elsewhere points the same way. It kept its nuncio, Cesare Orsenigo, in Berlin from 1930 until the nunciature lost its official status after Germany's defeat in May 1945; he left the bombed nunciature for Eichstätt only in February 1945. [cite:vt18_wiki_orsenigo] Through the Syrian civil war "the Apostolic Nunciature in Damascus always remained open", and the nuncio, Mario Zenari, kept his residence in the capital; many other embassies reopened in Damascus only from 2019. [cite:vt18_fides_zenari2019] When the breach came in Nicaragua it came from the other side: the government expelled the nuncio, Archbishop Waldemar Sommertag, on 12 March 2022, and suspended relations in March 2023 after a bishop, Rolando Álvarez, had been sentenced to 26 years in prison. The Holy See answered with "great surprise and regret", and Francis said the Holy See always tries to save diplomatic relations through patience and dialogue. [cite:vt18_vn_nicaragua2023]

The exception in this volume's record shows where the line is. In July 2011, after Ireland's Taoiseach accused the Holy See in the Dáil of trying to frustrate an inquiry into clerical abuse, the Holy See recalled its nuncio to Rome for consultation; Ireland closed its embassy to the Holy See in November 2011, citing cost, and announced its reopening in January 2014. [cite:vt12_tj_nuncio2011] [cite:vt12_tablet_embassy2014] The one documented recall in this volume concerned an accusation against the Holy See itself, not a war between other parties; see [scene:vt12_accountability|Part Twelve]. On this record, "speak, do not sever" is the Holy See's normal practice, not a policy made for Israel.

What the critics argue

Critics have attacked the gap from two opposite sides, and both are named here as individuals and groups, not as "the Church" or "the Jews". Those who want more than words include an Association of Priests Against Genocide, about 3,000 clergy, most of them Italian parish priests, whose open letter asked Italy's bishops for "a word calling for an immediate and permanent ceasefire" and asked the Italian state to end its "military, economic, and diplomatic complicity" with Israeli policy. [cite:vt18_972_caridi2026] The journalist Paola Caridi, writing in +972 Magazine on 10 July 2026, argued that Leo XIV has called for peace and aid but has rarely named Israeli responsibility, and that pressure from clergy and Palestinian Christian leaders will decide whether he changes course. [cite:vt18_972_caridi2026] The Palestinian Christian ecumenical initiative Kairos Palestine, in its second document of November 2025, called the war a "genocide, ethnic cleansing and forced displacement" and asked churches worldwide for pressure, sanctions and boycotts, including support for the BDS movement. [cite:vt18_maryknoll_kairos2026]

From the other side, Israel's government and several Jewish organisations argued that the Holy See's words already went too far. Israel's foreign ministry spoke of "double standards and the singling out of the Jewish state"; its ambassador to the Holy See said calling the war by another name than self-defence was "singling out the Jewish state"; the Assembly of Italian Rabbis said Francis had accused "both sides of terrorism". [cite:vt18_almon_cruelty2024] [cite:vt18_ncreg_genocide2024] [cite:vt18_jta_clarify2023] For these critics the problem was not the absence of sanctions but the content of the speech.

What the Holy See says

The Holy See's own account is consistent with its treaties. Parolin, in October 2025: "The Holy See—sometimes misunderstood—continues to call for peace, to invite dialogue, to use the words 'negotiation' and 'discussion.'" [cite:vt18_vn_parolin2025] Leo XIV, on 30 November 2025: "We are also friends with Israel, and we are trying to act as a mediating voice for both parties." [cite:vt18_ewtn_flight2025] On 3 April 2026, in the middle of a regional war, the Holy See described the pope's call to Israel's president as a reminder of "the need to reopen all possible channels of diplomatic dialogue". [cite:vt18_crux_herzog2026] There is also a documented practical stake: the Church's institutions in Israel rest on the 1993 and 1997 treaties, as the Latin Patriarchate showed on 31 December 2025 when it relied on them to keep Caritas Jerusalem working in Gaza, the West Bank and Jerusalem. [cite:vt18_caritasjr_2025] That stake is a fact; whether it, the treaty clauses or a theory of mediation weighs most in any given decision is not stated in any document found, and this volume does not assign a motive.

Interpretation, not finding: the "loud silence" reading

The simulation that preceded this volume offered a reading it called the "loud silence": a pattern in which the Holy See speaks forcefully but declines institutional action, set beside Pius XII's wartime reticence. Read through this lens, 2023–2026 repeats a habit of moral speech without cost. The simulation itself cautioned that the pattern does not prove any single motive, and the treaty clauses and the Nicaraguan, Syrian and German cases above offer a documented account of the same behaviour that requires no hidden alignment. The lens is reported here as a reading held by that earlier work, not as a finding.
