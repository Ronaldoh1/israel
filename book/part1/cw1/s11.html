The Coup of August 1953: TPAJAX and Boot

By the autumn of 1952 the Anglo-Iranian dispute had run through a court case, two missions, an embargo and a diplomatic break, as told in the previous section. In 1953 it was settled by other means. On 19 August 1953 Mohammad Mossadeq, the prime minister the Majlis had brought to office in 1951, was overthrown in a coup that the American Central Intelligence Agency and Britain's Secret Intelligence Service had planned under the names TPAJAX and Boot. The fact of the operation is no longer in dispute; the CIA's own history of it has been released. What remains argued is narrower and harder: exactly when it was approved, how much it cost and on what, how much of the result was the work of foreigners and how much of Iranians, and whether oil or fear of the Soviet Union drove it.

A common explanation of the long hostility between Iran and the United States holds that it springs from religious fanaticism and nothing else. The record of 1953 shows a documented foreign operation against a constitutional, nationalist government, a quarter-century before the Islamic Republic existed. It does not, on its own, prove that later hostility began there, or that the operation was undertaken only to protect oil. Both of those are arguments about the record, and historians make them in more than one direction. This section sets out what the documents establish and where the arguments begin.

The planning began in mid-May 1953. That is the date given by a CIA Directorate of Plans memorandum of March 1954, titled "Campaign to Install Pro-Western Government in Iran," which the State Department later printed in its documentary series. The memorandum sets out two objectives: to bring about the fall of the Mossadeq government "through legal or quasi-legal methods," and to replace it with a pro-Western government under the Shah, with General Fazlollah Zahedi as prime minister. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d363] The plan built on earlier British planning. [cite:greydynamics_tpajax_overview] [cite:globalsecurity_operation_ajax] For Britain the operation was Boot; for the CIA it was TPAJAX, also called Operation Ajax. [cite:greydynamics_tpajax_overview]

The British motive was the one the oil crisis had made plain. Mossadeq's government had nationalized the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, later BP, and Britain wanted the concession restored. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] According to the account this book draws on, Britain first tried to bring Mossadeq down through blockade and boycott, and when that failed, its intelligence service planned a coup and brought it to the Americans; the British supplied the original networks and much of the apparatus on the ground, and the CIA the scale, the money and the political will once Washington had been persuaded to treat an oil dispute as a front of the Cold War. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] That is a summary reading, not a quotation from the British or American files; the files themselves are taken up below.

On the British side, the approval is dated. According to the historian Malcolm Byrne's reading of the State Department's 2017 volume, Donald Wilber's internal CIA history confirms that Winston Churchill approved the overthrow on 1 July 1953. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] On the American side, the date is disputed, and the dispute itself tells something about how such decisions were recorded. Kermit Roosevelt, the CIA officer who ran the operation in Tehran, wrote in his memoir that high-level approval was given at a State Department meeting on 25 June 1953, but no official record of that meeting has been found. The Library of Congress country study likewise dates the Eisenhower administration's approval to June 1953. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d225] [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] The CIA's March 1954 memorandum gives a different date. It records that the project was approved by the President, the Secretary of State and the Director of Central Intelligence on 11 July 1953, under National Security Council paper NSC 136/1. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d363] A CIA chronology dates State Department and Foreign Office authorization to the same day, and a handwritten note says the Director obtained presidential approval that day. The State Department's editors add a sentence that has to stand beside every account of the decision: "No written authorization from the President has been found." [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d225] The men behind the 11 July approval were President Eisenhower, Secretary of State John Foster Dulles and CIA Director Allen Dulles. [cite:greydynamics_tpajax_overview] [cite:globalsecurity_operation_ajax]

The two dates need not exclude each other. A meeting in late June may have settled the matter in principle and the formal sign-off may have followed on 11 July; the sources do not say so, and that reconciliation is no more than a possibility. What the record does establish is the form the approval took: on the American side, a decision attested by the CIA's own memorandum, a chronology and a handwritten note, without a presidential signature on any document yet found.

The approval came with conditions. According to the same memorandum, before approving, the President and the Secretary of State asked for assurances that the British would be flexible on oil and that adequate American interim economic aid would go to the successor government. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d363] The conditions show where the two governments' interests met and where they differed. Washington's price for joining was a British promise not to insist on the old terms for oil, and an American promise to pay for the government that would follow.

The money is the part of the story most often reduced to a single round number. A widely repeated summary puts the cost of the operation at about $5 million and calls it one of the cheapest covert operations in the CIA's history. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] [cite:globalsecurity_operation_ajax] The documents give a more exact and more interesting picture, because they show several different sums for several different purposes. Wilber's 1954 CIA history records that the Director approved, on 4 April 1953, a budget of $1,000,000 that the Tehran Station could use. [cite:rs_cw1_maryferrell_tpajax] The approval date is itself telling: the money was authorized more than a month before the inception date that the March 1954 memorandum gives for the project. The memorandum's own figure is a grand total of $5,330,000, spread across three phases: the operations up to Zahedi's installation, a follow-up phase to solidify the Shah and Zahedi, and emergency funds for the new government. The amount of each phase is still not declassified. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d363] So the "$5 million" figure, read against the documents, is not the price of the coup alone; most of the documented total may have gone to propping up the government that followed, but because the phases have not been released, no one can say from the public record how the total divides.

Two further figures come from reporting and scholarship rather than from released documents. James Risen, writing in the New York Times in 2000, reported that the CIA covertly funnelled $5 million to Zahedi's government two days after the coup. The historian Mark Gasiorowski found that Zahedi and his colleagues received at least $50,000 beforehand. [cite:rs_cw1_maryferrell_tpajax] Risen's $5 million and the memorandum's $5,330,000 may overlap, since the memorandum's third phase was emergency money for the new government; the sources do not say whether they are the same funds.

How the money was used is described in general terms. The operation used bribed newspaper editors to fill the press with material against Mossadeq, paid crowds to stage demonstrations, and coordinated with royalist officers in the Iranian army. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] [cite:globalsecurity_operation_ajax] Kermit Roosevelt Jr., a grandson of Theodore Roosevelt, ran it on the ground. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] He arrived in Tehran on 19 July 1953 as its field commander. [cite:roosevelt_nl_operation_ajax] [cite:npr_declassified_1953_coup] Not every American in Tehran shared the purpose with the same conviction. In oral histories recorded long afterwards, Lewis Hoffacker, third secretary at the embassy in 1951–53, said that "our goal was to support the Shah and to try to contain Mossadegh"; John Stutesman recalled Ambassador Loy Henderson's "deep reluctance to have a covert operation displace a chief of state," a recollection at second hand. [cite:rs_cw1_adst_iran]

The decisive weeks were August's. On 3 August 1953 Mossadeq held a plebiscite on dissolving the Majlis and then dissolved it. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] His opponents called the plebiscite rigged. [cite:loc_mossadeq] This is part of the record and part of the case his critics made: a prime minister who had come to office through parliament ended that parliament by a popular vote whose fairness was disputed. On 13 August the Shah appointed Zahedi prime minister. Mossadeq refused to step down and arrested the Shah's emissary, and the Shah fled the country. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] This was the first attempt, of 14–15 August, and it failed. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017]

What happened next turned on one man's decision to stay. According to Byrne's reading of the 2017 volume, CIA headquarters advised Roosevelt to evacuate his team after the failure, but he stayed and, with Iranian collaborators, revived the operation. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] Another account describes headquarters as directing that the operation stop, and Roosevelt as continuing regardless, organizing staged demonstrations and securing military backing. [cite:roosevelt_nl_operation_ajax] [cite:npr_declassified_1953_coup] The two descriptions differ in force, advice to evacuate in one and an order to stop in the other, and the difference matters for how much the outcome depended on Roosevelt personally; both agree that he did not leave. On 19 August pro-Shah army units and street crowds defeated Mossadeq's forces. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] "Some 300 people died during fighting in Tehrān," in Britannica's words; Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty gives at least 300 dead in gun battles in the capital. [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_1953_coup] [cite:rs_cw1_rferl_1953]

The record of those last four days is thinner than for any other part of the operation, and for a specific reason. According to Byrne, most of the operational cables for 16 to 19 August no longer exist; CIA officers destroyed many records in the early 1960s. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] The days on which the question of agency is most acute, who in the streets of Tehran was paid, organized or simply moved by events, are the days for which the American paper record is weakest.

The aftermath was a political purge. Mossadeq was sentenced to three years' imprisonment and then kept under house arrest until his death in 1967. His foreign minister, Hosain Fatemi, was executed. Hundreds of activists of the National Front, the Tudeh party and other groups were arrested. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] Another account compresses this into a sentence of house arrest; the Library of Congress study separates the prison term from the house arrest that followed it. [cite:kathryn_palmer_1953_coup] [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] Mohammad Reza Pahlavi (1919–1980), the Shah, had his authority restored and centralized, and became the last Shah of Iran. [cite:kathryn_palmer_1953_coup] [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] The founding bargain, as one account puts it, was a client arrangement: the Shah's rule in exchange for oil access and an anticommunist alignment on the Soviet Union's southern border. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] The commercial settlement that followed in 1954, and the secret police, SAVAK, created in 1957, belong to the next stage, in [scene:ira_s00b|the 1954 consortium and SAVAK]. One claim often attached to this story, that Israel's Mossad trained SAVAK from 1957, is not established for that year by the sources used here; the question of Israel's part, before and after the coup, is examined in the closing section of this chapter.

The man at the centre of these events is sometimes presented in terms the record does not support in either direction. Mossadeq, born in 1882, was a nationalist and constitutionalist, and in the account summarized here a liberal nationalist rather than a Communist. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] He came to office as the Majlis's choice, named by the Shah under parliamentary and popular pressure, not by a direct popular election. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] Time named him its Man of the Year for 1951, but in words that were openly hostile, as the previous section shows. [cite:rs_cw1_time_1952] He was the prime minister of a constitutional government that had come to rule by full powers and ended by dissolving its parliament through a disputed plebiscite; he was also removed by force, with foreign money and foreign planning, by men acting for two other governments. Both statements are documented.

The dispute over who carried out the coup, and why, runs through every serious account. On one side stands the CIA's own internal history, which, in the National Security Archive's description, "specifically states the agency planned and helped implement the coup." [cite:rs_cw1_nsarchive_2013] The strongest form of this case is that the operation was conceived in London and Washington, approved by named officials on both sides, paid for from a documented budget, directed by a CIA field commander who revived it when it failed, and carried out with bribed editors, paid crowds and officers coordinated from outside. On the other side, Byrne notes that whether Americans and Britons or Iranians ultimately carried the coup out remains heavily disputed. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] The strongest form of that case is that the forces which prevailed on 19 August were Iranian: army units loyal to the Shah and street crowds, in a country already divided over the plebiscite, and that foreign money could exploit these divisions but did not create them. The released record cannot fully settle it, because the cables for the decisive days are gone.

The question of motive divides the same way. One reading, put strongly in the account this book draws on, holds that the coup was at bottom an Anglo-American oil operation dressed in anticommunist language, the anticommunist case serving as the wrapper that made the repossession of an oil concession acceptable in 1953. [cite:wiki_1953_iranian_coup] The British motive, the restoration of the concession, is documented, and the American conditions for approval, British flexibility on oil, show that oil was part of the American calculation too. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_d363] But the 2017 documents complicate the oil-only reading. Byrne writes that the Soviet threat dominates most of the volume's reports, and that American analysts saw Ayatollah Kashani as a source of trouble and feared an "unholy alliance" between him and Mossadeq. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] The documents are read both ways, and historians continue to weigh British oil interests against Cold War fears in explaining why the United States joined. [cite:nsarchive_cia_confirms_role] [cite:explaininghistory_1953_coup] The convergence is itself the finding: a British company's lost concession and an American fear of Soviet advance were distinct interests that met in one operation, and the condition Washington set, that Britain be flexible on oil, is where they can be seen meeting on paper.

The record of the coup was itself withheld for sixty years, and the history of its concealment is part of the history of the event. Wilber's 200-page internal history was written in March 1954. In 1978, according to British Foreign Office papers, London sought to keep material out, and a State Department official warned that it contained "very embarrassing things about the British." The State Department's 1989 volume of Foreign Relations of the United States on the period did not mention the American role at all. The historian Bruce Kuniholm called it "a fraud," and Warren Cohen, chair of the department's Historical Advisory Committee, resigned in 1990. In 1991 Congress passed a statute requiring a "thorough, accurate, and reliable documentary record." In April 2000 Wilber's history was leaked to the New York Times. [cite:rs_cw1_nsarchive_2013]

The acknowledgement came on the coup's sixtieth anniversary. On 19 August 2013 the National Security Archive posted 21 CIA items, including the internal history "The Battle for Iran," with the TPAJAX name now restored; it is believed to be the CIA's first formal acknowledgement of its role. [cite:rs_cw1_nsarchive_2013] [cite:nsarchive_cia_confirms_role] The State Department's retrospective volume, more than 1,000 pages, followed in June 2017. According to Byrne, the Obama administration had held it back out of concern for the nuclear deal with Iran. [cite:rs_cw1_atlantic_byrne_2017] The documentary record of a 1953 decision was thus still being managed for reasons of policy, in London in 1978 and in Washington until 2017.

Whether Iran's later course would have differed without the coup is a question of another kind, because it asks what would have happened, not what did. Historians answer it in two broad ways, and both are interpretations. Those who stress the coup's weight argue that it foreclosed Iran's path toward a secular, nationalist, parliamentary government and replaced it with twenty-six years of increasingly repressive royal rule, whose abuses produced the particular revolutionary coalition, its Islamist wing included, that took power in 1979; on this view a Mossadeq-style government, developing however imperfectly, plausibly would not have produced that specific form of clerical rule. [cite:wiki_1953counterfactual] Those who stress structure point to Iran's religious establishment, the distortions of an oil economy, the pressure of the great powers and the wave of anti-colonial upheaval across the region, forces that would likely have produced some volatile turn against the West under more than one alternative history; on this view the coup shaped or hastened what followed but did not by itself cause it. [cite:cfr_coup_legacy] Neither position can be proved. Both rest on the same documented events. What neither disputes is that the coup happened, that the Shah's long rule followed it, and that the revolution of 1979 followed that. The question persists because it tests whether the hostility of later decades should be read as fixed and inevitable or as the outcome of a chain of documented choices that might have gone otherwise.

One other event of 1953 is often set beside the coup. In the same year Isaiah Kenen founded, in Washington, the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs, the body renamed the American Israel Public Affairs Committee in 1959; its founding, and the uncertainty over its dates, are told in [scene:cw1_lobby|the lobby's founding] and, for its organizational line, in [scene:s07g|From AZEC to AIPAC]. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:britannica_aipac] The two events share a year and nothing else that the record documents: no source shows any operational connection between the coup in Tehran and the committee's founding in Washington. Their later histories, including the lobby's role in Congress in the 2020s, belong to later volumes.

The gaps in this record are specific. The amount of each of the three funding phases is still classified, so the share of the $5,330,000 spent before the coup, rather than on the government after it, cannot be stated. No written presidential authorization has been found, and the June and July approval dates have not been reconciled by any document. The operational cables for 16 to 19 August were largely destroyed, which leaves the decisive days, and with them the balance between foreign direction and Iranian action, the most poorly documented part of the operation. And the counterfactual that gives the coup much of its later weight, what Iran would have become without it, is by its nature beyond documentation.
