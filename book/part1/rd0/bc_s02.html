N M Rothschild & Sons: The London House and Its Legend

The best-known story about the Rothschilds in London is a single episode. Nathan Rothschild, it says, watched the Battle of Waterloo, raced the news to London, and made the family fortune by buying up British bonds before anyone else knew the result. The story is still repeated. The documented record of the London house is a different kind of history, slower and more ordinary: a merchant bank that lent to a foreign state on new terms, supplied the Bank of England in a panic, sent the first Jewish member to the House of Commons, and found the money for Britain's Suez Canal shares while Parliament was not sitting. The legend can be traced to a pamphlet published in 1846, ten years after Nathan's death, and written to stir hostility toward Jews.

The house began as one part of a family design. Mayer Amschel Rothschild of Frankfurt placed five sons in five European financial centres: Frankfurt, London, Paris, Vienna and Naples. The result was a cross-border house that could move capital and information between capitals faster than most governments could. That structure, rather than any single trade, is the documented source of the family's position in the nineteenth century. [cite:wiki_nathan_mayer_rothschild] The father's rise, and the dispersal of the five brothers, are told in [scene:s01|the chapter's opening section].

The London house was founded by Nathan Mayer Rothschild, who lived from 1777 to 1836. Its founding date is given two ways in this book's sources. One account dates N M Rothschild & Sons to 1809, and places Nathan in London from that year. [cite:wiki_nathan_mayer_rothschild] Another gives 1811. [cite:schama_two_rothschilds] The sources do not explain the difference, and both dates are recorded here. This Nathan, the founder, should not be confused with a later namesake, Nathan Mayer Rothschild, 1st Baron, the father of the Walter Rothschild to whom the Balfour Declaration was addressed in 1917.

The house's first great contribution to state finance came in 1818. It arranged a £5 million loan for Prussia, denominated in sterling, with interest payable in London. Until then, British investors who bought a foreign government's bonds carried the risk that the foreign currency would fall. A loan written in sterling and paid in London removed that risk. The Rothschild Archive describes the Prussian loan as "the true precursor of the public borrowing which transformed the international capital market in the nineteenth century." [cite:jwatte_rothschild_myths_record] That description is quoted from the family's own institutional record, and it should be read as such: the archive's judgment of the loan's importance, not an independent one. In this book's sources it reaches the reader through a personal website that reproduces the archive's words, since the archive's own page could not be checked directly.

In 1825, during a banking panic, the house supplied the Bank of England with specie, that is, hard coin. The institution that had been chartered in 1694 to lend to the Crown [scene:rd0_bank1694|as the chapter has shown] now drew on a private house in a crisis. The two were not rivals. They were parts of the same London system of state credit.

The London house's political ties to the state came through Nathan's son, Lionel de Rothschild. Lionel was elected to the House of Commons repeatedly from 1847, but he could not take his seat, because members were required to swear an oath as Christians. When the Jews Relief Act ended that requirement, he took his seat in 1858 as the first Jewish member of the House of Commons. [cite:wiki_lionel_de_rothschild]

Lionel was also head of the house when it carried out its most famous transaction with the British state. The Suez Canal had opened in 1869. It was owned by French financiers and by the Khedive of Egypt, but by 1874 four-fifths of its trade was British. [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023] On 15 November 1875, Benjamin Disraeli learned that the Khedive meant to sell his shares. Ten days later, on 25 November 1875, the purchase was concluded: Britain acquired 44% of the company's shares for £4,000,000. [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023]

The speed is what made the house necessary. Parliament was not sitting, so the government could not vote the money. N M Rothschild & Sons, under Lionel de Rothschild, advanced it. The Rothschild Archive calls it a "technically unsecured loan" and says it was repaid within five months. [cite:rs_rd0_rotharchive_suez] [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023] The mechanism matters more than the drama of the story. An executive that wanted to act before the legislature could meet borrowed from a private house in the City, and asked Parliament to approve the act after it was done.

The purchase did not pass without objection. William Gladstone attacked it for its "lack of reference or consent from Parliament." The purchase also left France as the majority shareholder. [cite:rs_rd0_rotharchive_suez] Disraeli answered his critics in the House of Commons on 14 and 21 February 1876, and his own words, preserved in Hansard, state the government's reason without ambiguity. He defended the purchase "as a political transaction, and one which I believe is calculated to strengthen the Empire," and said it "secures to us a highway to our Indian Empire." He did not recommend it, he said, "as a financial investment." [cite:rs_rd0_disraeli_1876] He also explained why only a house like the Rothschilds' would serve. After the first £1,000,000, he told the House, a further £2,000,000 "might be demanded in gold the next day." [cite:rs_rd0_disraeli_1876] Parliament approved the purchase on 21 February 1876. [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023]

What the house earned is the one point in the Suez story that the sources leave open. Critics in Parliament said the Rothschild commission amounted to 15 per cent interest. Disraeli rejected that reading, and denied that the commission had "anything to do with the interest on the advance." [cite:rs_rd0_disraeli_1876] The critics' case is that a short loan to a government in a hurry was priced at a rate no ordinary borrower would have paid. Disraeli's case rested on the terms: he denied that the commission was interest at all, and set it beside the house's undertaking to produce millions in gold at a day's notice. None of the sources consulted for this book states the actual commission rate. [cite:rs_rd0_rotharchive_suez] Who was right cannot be settled from them. Disraeli's stated aim, the route to India, belongs to the story of why Britain wanted the region at all, which is taken up in [scene:or_s01|a later volume].

Set against that record, the Waterloo story belongs to a different category. It first appears in 1846, in a pamphlet by Georges Dairnvaell, published under the name "Satan." Dairnvaell wrote it after a railway accident on a line the Rothschilds had financed, and he wrote it explicitly to inflame hostility toward Jews. [cite:wiki_dairnvaell_satan_pamphlet] Nathan had died in 1836. He could not answer it. The story's origin is antisemitic. The sources describe that aim as explicit, not as something read into the pamphlet afterwards.

The family's archive rejects the story on the evidence. According to the Rothschild Archive, nothing in the record suggests that Nathan was the first in London to know of the victory. Niall Ferguson, author of the family's authorized history, estimates that any quick profit Nathan could have made on British bonds after the battle could not have exceeded about £7,000. The archive's verdict, as quoted in this book's sources, is short: "The legend of Nathan Rothschild and Waterloo is just that: a legend." [cite:jwatte_rothschild_myths_record] [cite:leonardo_english_rothschild_waterloo] These words reach this book through the same personal website noted above, and are given here as the archive's position as reported there.

Not every account in this book's research tells the story the same way. The chapter's opening section draws on a summary that credits Ferguson with a different verdict: that Nathan's information advantage was real, that the market timing was more complex than the mythology, and that the version in which Nathan "cornered the market" was a nineteenth-century antisemitic caricature by Édouard Drumont, later amplified. That version and the one given here agree that the legend's popular form is an antisemitic invention. They differ on its origin, Dairnvaell in 1846 or Drumont, and on whether Nathan had any advantage in news at all. The record reached for this section, the archive's statement and the £7,000 ceiling, points toward the narrower reading: at most a small and ordinary trade, and no evidence that Nathan knew first.

The story outlived its author because it served a purpose. The claim that the family secretly directed governments rests on the line that runs from the 1846 pamphlet and on later elaborations. The family's own archive, Ferguson's authorized history and the journalist Mike Rothschild's 2023 study each take those claims apart. [cite:publishersweekly_jewish_space_lasers] The Waterloo legend is antisemitic in origin, and naming it as such is part of the record.

The documented house, in short, was a creditor and contractor to states, not a puppeteer of them. Its relationships with the British government were contracts: sovereign loans, a central bank supplied in a panic, the Suez advance, and a seat in Parliament won by election and delayed by an oath. All of them operated inside the City of London that the chapter began with. The documents also show who held the power to decide. The Suez purchase was Disraeli's to make, Parliament's to approve and Gladstone's to attack. Disraeli's own justification for it was the empire, not the bank.

Some things remain open. The founding year of the London house is recorded two ways. The size of the Suez commission, and so the real cost of the advance to the Treasury, is not stated in any source consulted. The Rothschild Archive's verdict on Waterloo reaches this book secondhand, and the summaries of Ferguson that this book draws on describe his findings differently. None of these gaps changes the central finding. The record of the London house is a record of public, contractual business with states, and the legend that replaced it in popular memory was made, from its first printing, as an attack on Jews.
