Resolution 181: The Three-Day Vote

On November 29, 1947, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, recommending the partition of Mandate Palestine. The final tally was 33 in favor, 13 against and 10 abstentions, enough to clear the two-thirds threshold the Assembly required. [cite:un_unispal_186560] [cite:avalon_res181] [cite:britannica_res181] The familiar account ends with that number and calls it the peaceful, democratic creation of a state by international consensus. The procedural record is less tidy. The vote that succeeded on November 29 was not the first count. An initial tally had found 25 in favor, short of two-thirds, and the Assembly President, Aranha, arranged a three-day postponement. [cite:un_181_records] [cite:un_181_text]

Those three days are the hinge of the story. A resolution that lacked the votes, and then passed after a pause, had a specific window in which something changed. The record does not explain every delegation's reasoning, but it does document several of the forces at work inside that window.

The first was the United States executive. The Truman administration pushed for a yes vote, while the State Department and the Pentagon opposed the push, warning of Arab anger and of the stakes for Cold War oil strategy. [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] The most senior internal objection came from James Forrestal, the first Secretary of Defense and formerly Secretary of the Navy. His diaries, published after his death as The Forrestal Diaries (1951), record his distaste for what he called the "bullying" and "coercion" of small states to secure their votes, and his conviction that Middle East policy was being driven by domestic political calculation rather than national interest. [cite:un_unispal_186560] A private diary written at the time, not for an audience, is a primary source of unusual weight. It records a sitting cabinet officer objecting to his own government's methods. It does not, however, give an itemized account of which tactic was used on which country by which official.

The second force is visible in the states that moved. Haiti, Liberia and the Philippines shifted from a no vote or an abstention to yes during the postponement. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] The historian Michael Cohen, in Palestine and the Great Powers (1982), documents the shift and ties it to a broader pattern of pressure on smaller, economically dependent nations. Liberia is the best-documented case. The Firestone Tire and Rubber Company held a rubber concession there of roughly 1.1 million acres, which made the company, in effect, the Liberian economy. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] Cohen connects that dependency to Liberia's change of vote, and Liberia's own ambassador complained that the American delegation had threatened aid cuts to several countries in the same period. These were two separate pressures, one corporate and one diplomatic, converging on one small state; the sources do not describe a single coordinated operation, and the precise mechanics of the Firestone leverage are less fully documented than the economic relationship itself. Firestone had no stake in Palestine. Its importance is that it shows how a great power's commercial hold over a small client could be turned into that client's vote on an unrelated question.

The Philippine and Haitian shifts are less fully documented. According to the sources, the Philippines reversed course after American intervention, and its representative, General Carlos Romulo, later wrote of being recalled and overruled by his own president: "This particular chapter in history had been exceedingly unpleasant to me." Haiti is reported to have changed position after direct communication from the American executive. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] Cuba offers a contrast. Its delegate said publicly that Cuba would vote against partition "in spite of the pressure that has been brought to bear upon us," which suggests the same campaign reached states that resisted it. [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition]

The third force came from Moscow. The Soviet Union and its bloc voted yes, and the documented motive was anti-British. Partition would end the Mandate and weaken Britain's position in the Middle East. [cite:un_unispal_186560] The same logic would lead the USSR to supply arms to the new state through Czechoslovakia in 1948. Soviet-Israeli relations cooled substantially within a few years, which is consistent with a vote driven by strategy rather than lasting alignment.

One group had no part in any of this. The Arab majority of Mandate Palestine had no vote in the General Assembly and no direct representation in the decision that divided its territory. The plan allocated 55 percent of the Mandate to the Jewish state, with about 30 percent of the population, and 45 percent to the Arab state, with about 70 percent, with Jerusalem as an international zone. [cite:britannica_res181] This book does not weigh every argument made at the time for that formula, but the structural fact is plain: the people most affected were the object of the resolution, not participants in it.

Resolution 181 was a real international act with genuine legal weight. It was neither the serene consensus of the familiar account nor a secret plot. It was a contested vote, carried after a three-day postponement by a convergence of different self-interests: an American White House overriding its own State Department and Pentagon, a Soviet Union seeking to push Britain out, and small dependent states moved at the margin. [cite:un_181_records]

Forrestal's warnings were overruled. He left office in early 1949 and weeks later died in a fall from a high window at Bethesda Naval Hospital, where he was being treated for depression and exhaustion. The death was ruled a suicide; some historians consider the ruling contested. [cite:un_unispal_186560] His opposition is documented. This book builds no theory on the circumstances of his death.
