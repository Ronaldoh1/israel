Basel, 1897: The Congress and Its Dissenters

For three days at the end of August 1897, from the 29th to the 31st, delegates filled the Municipal Casino in Basel, Switzerland. Theodor Herzl had called them to the First Zionist Congress. Two hundred and eight came from 17 countries, among them 14 non-voting women.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] Basel had not been the first choice. Herzl had planned to meet in Munich, but the local Jewish community objected, and the German Rabbinical Association, the Allgemeine Deutsche Rabbinerverband, refused to support hosting the congress there. So the founding meeting of political Zionism moved to neutral Switzerland because an organized Jewish religious body had turned it away in advance. Herzl, in top hat and morning coat, was photographed leaning over the balcony of the Drei Könige hotel. The image became iconic.

The Congress founded the World Zionist Organization, a standing body of elected delegates, run like a parliament, with a governing executive. Herzl was its first president. Chaim Weizmann would lead it later, in the decisive years around the Balfour Declaration.[cite:wiki_wzo] What mattered most was that it lasted. The organization ran the Congresses. It acquired its own bank, the Jewish Colonial Trust, in 1899. It managed land purchase through the Jewish National Fund from 1901, and it conducted the diplomacy that produced the Balfour Declaration in 1917. In 1929 it created the expanded Jewish Agency, which became the proto-government of the Jewish community under the British Mandate, the period of British rule in Palestine. When the state was declared in 1948, the Agency's executive effectively became Israel's provisional government. The line from Basel to statehood runs through a public, durable bureaucracy that outlived everyone who built it, not through a hidden plan.

Herzl himself was an assimilated Viennese journalist and playwright. After Basel he wrote in his diary: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State. Perhaps in five years, certainly in fifty, everyone will see it."[cite:herzl_diaries] He was wrong about the five and almost exactly right about the fifty: the state was declared in May 1948, fifty-one years later. He died in 1904 at forty-four. His prediction concerned the outcome, not the path. It does not show that he foresaw the Balfour Declaration, the Mandate or the 1948 war through which statehood arrived. Invented quotations also gather around him. A "Nile to Euphrates" line often attributed to him does not appear in his writings; according to the research behind this book, it originates in a 1994 misattribution that has since been traced and debunked. The documented Herzl was a secular nation-builder who considered Argentina and entertained a British offer in East Africa.

The familiar account holds that Jews embraced Zionism from the start. The record of 1897 does not. It shows a movement contested within Jewish life from its first days, and from several directions at once.

The most famous patron of Jewish settlement was not on Herzl's side. Baron Edmond de Rothschild, of the Paris branch of the family, had funded the struggling First Aliyah agricultural colonies in Ottoman Palestine since the 1880s. These were the first wave of Jewish settlements, among them Rishon LeZion and Zichron Ya'akov, and his sums would amount to tens of millions in today's terms. Yet he personally opposed Herzl and political Zionism. He preferred quiet, philanthropic colonization that would not provoke the Ottoman authorities, and for years he refused to place his settlements under the Zionist Organization.[cite:schama_two_rothschilds] Two Rothschilds must be kept apart here. Edmond in Paris funded settlements and opposed the political movement. Walter Rothschild in London was the ceremonial addressee of the 1917 Balfour letter, and he neither funded settlements nor wrote the policy. Merging two men with opposite roles into a single "Rothschild" actor is exactly how a documented family is turned into an antisemitic myth of a hidden hand.

In October 1897, the same season as Basel, the General Jewish Labour Bund was founded in Vilna. It rejected Zionism outright. Against emigration it set doikayt, "hereness": Jews should secure their rights and culture where they already lived, as autonomous national minorities in democratic, multi-ethnic states. The Bund was socialist and Yiddishist. Through the following decades it was arguably the largest Jewish political movement in Poland and the Russian Empire, and it regularly outpolled Zionist parties in interwar Polish Jewish community elections.[cite:wiki_bund] It did not lose the argument to Zionism. Nazi and Soviet violence destroyed it, and that destruction, not an intellectual defeat, is why it faded from the story.

A third objection was theological. Orthodox anti-Zionist rabbis held that the return to the Land could come only through divine intervention, not through political organizing by secular nationalists. That position survives today in Satmar Hasidism and, most visibly, in the small Neturei Karta group, whose members appear at Palestinian-solidarity protests.[cite:neturei_karta] It remains a minority view within Orthodox Judaism, and most Haredi Jews have reached various accommodations with the state. But the objection has been held continuously since 1897.

A fourth came from inside the movement. Asher Ginsberg, writing as Ahad Ha'am, argued for cultural rather than political Zionism: a spiritual center in the Land rather than a state. In his 1891 essay "Truth from the Land of Israel," he reported that the country already had a substantial Arab population that would not simply accept displacement. He warned Herzl that political maximalism would produce "a state of Jews" but not a "Jewish state."[cite:wiki_ahad_haam] Israelis across the political spectrum still invoke that warning, made at the founding moment rather than in hindsight. Whether his alternative would have produced a better outcome cannot be known.

Herzl's own framing makes the same point. Der Judenstaat presented the project as secular nationalism on the model of nineteenth-century European nation-building, and Herzl was religiously non-observant.[cite:herzl_judenstaat] The First Congress program dealt with political organization, land purchase and diplomatic outreach; religious authority was contested at the Congress and largely absent from its decisions.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] Some delegates may have had religious motives, and Zionism later took on religious dimensions for many of its adherents. But the founders said plainly what they were doing, and the fight over it took place, from the first, within Jewish life.
