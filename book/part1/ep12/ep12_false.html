When the Pattern Was Wrong

A book that sees a hidden ring wherever it looks has stopped investigating. Three cases show the cost of seeing the pattern where it is not, and one of them answers the intelligence question this book has already asked.

In Britain, the Metropolitan Police spent about £2 million investigating the claims of a man named Carl Beech that a ring of politicians and military figures had abused and murdered boys in Westminster. Homes were searched, among them those of a D-Day veteran and a former Home Secretary who was dying. The investigation closed without a single arrest, and in 2019 Beech was convicted of perverting the course of justice and fraud and sentenced to 18 years. [cite:ep12_itv_beech2019] False claims about elite rings do real harm, to the accused and to genuine survivors, who are believed less afterwards.

In the United States in December 2016, a man who had watched online videos claiming that a Washington pizza restaurant hid trafficked children walked into it with a rifle and fired a shot. No one was hurt, he found nothing, and he was sentenced to four years. [cite:ep12_wtop_pizzagate2017]

In Northern Ireland, the Kincora boys' home has long been cited as proof that intelligence agencies protect abusers for blackmail. The abuse there was real: an official inquiry under Sir Anthony Hart found in 2017 that 38 boys had been abused by three staff members, all convicted. But it found no evidence that the security agencies were complicit, that the home was used as a "honey trap" for blackmail, or that prominent figures abused boys there. One survivor said he still believed there was more to it. [cite:ep12_it_kincora2017] It is the closest parallel to the intelligence question in Part Seven, and it was answered the way the book has tried to answer it: by an inquiry with access to the records, which found real abuse and real failures, and no proof of the larger story.
