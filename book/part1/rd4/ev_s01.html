"No One Wants Them": Évian, Bermuda and the Closing Doors

In the summer of 1938, while Woodhead's commission was in Palestine testing whether partition could work, the question of where Europe's Jews could go was put to the world. A common belief about the years before the Holocaust is that the world did what it could for Europe's Jewish refugees. Two conferences, five years apart, show how little that was. The first met in the summer of 1938, about Jewish refugees fleeing the Nazis. The second met in the spring of 1943, with the extermination under way. Both ended the same way. And at both, opening Palestine was kept off the table.

President Roosevelt called the first meeting after Germany annexed Austria. Delegates of 32 countries met at Évian from 6 to 15 July 1938. [cite:wiki_evian] They spoke with sympathy, and almost all of them declined to take more refugees. Britain's delegate, Lord Winterton, said that the United Kingdom "is not a country of immigration." France said it had reached saturation. Brazil asked for certificates of Christian baptism. Canada wanted only experienced farmers. The United States offered only to fill its existing German–Austrian quota, which had gone unfilled in earlier years. [cite:wiki_evian] [cite:newhumanitarian_evian] [cite:facinghistory_evian] The American offer opened no new places. It promised to use places that the law already allowed and that had not been used.

One country made a large offer: the Dominican Republic, under the dictator Rafael Trujillo. The offer came from a regime whose soldiers had massacred thousands of Haitians the year before, in 1937. [cite:wiki_evian] The record here gives no figure for how many refugees the Dominican Republic proposed to take or how many it eventually received, and it does not set out Trujillo's reasons. It records only that the most generous answer at Évian came from a government that had just carried out a massacre.

Palestine was not discussed. At Britain's insistence, any mention of Palestine as a destination was excluded from the agenda. [cite:holocaust_au_evian] [cite:wiki_evian] Golda Meir attended as the Jewish observer from Palestine. She was not permitted to take part. [cite:holocaust_au_evian] The mechanism is plain in the record. Britain held the Mandate and therefore controlled entry to Palestine. The sources here do not give Britain's stated reasons for the exclusion. What they show is the result: the conference was free to discuss any door except the one the Zionist movement was trying to open, and the power that held that door kept it shut.

The conference's failure was noticed in Germany. The Nazi party's newspaper summed it up in three words: "No one wants them." [cite:wiki_evian] Chaim Weizmann gave the Jewish side of the same conclusion. He told the Manchester Guardian that the world was now divided into two kinds of places for Jews: places where they could not live, and places they could not enter. [cite:wiki_evian] The two remarks came from opposite ends of the question and reached the same conclusion. Four months later came the pogrom of November 1938 ([scene:hol_s03|Kristallnacht]).

Five years later the Western powers met again, under far worse conditions. In April 1943, with the extermination of Europe's Jews under way, the United States and Britain held a conference on refugees in Bermuda. A program proposing that Britain open Palestine to refugees was put forward and was not considered. The conference produced little and was widely judged ineffective. [cite:usf_bermuda_evian] Palestine had been kept off the agenda at Évian in peacetime. At Bermuda, in the middle of the killing, it stayed off.

Some things are left open by the record gathered here. It does not list the positions of the other 26 countries at Évian. It does not give the Dominican offer in numbers or say how much of it was carried out. It does not say why Britain insisted on excluding Palestine, or who proposed opening Palestine at Bermuda and on what terms.

The two conferences matter to the story of 1948 in two ways, and both belong in the account. The first is the argument they handed the Zionist movement. When nearly every country refused, the argument that Jews needed a place of their own gained force. Much of what came after in this chapter can be read against that background, among it the clandestine ships ([scene:ab_s01|Aliyah Bet]) and the American campaign for a Jewish commonwealth.

The second is where the decision lay. Britain, which kept Palestine off the agenda at Évian and off the table at Bermuda, held the one door the movement was pushing against. Ten months after Évian, Britain set that policy out in [scene:rd4_white_paper|the White Paper of May 1939], and the clandestine ships of the following years sailed against its limits.
