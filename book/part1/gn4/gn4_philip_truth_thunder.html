Philip, Truth and Thunder: Sacraments, Silence and the Voice of Wisdom

The Gnostic writings found at Nag Hammadi are not all myths of rulers and stolen light. Several are works of devotion, teaching and poetry, and they show a religious life with its own sacraments, its own understanding of death and its own image of the divine feminine. Three are central.

The Gospel of Philip is an anthology of Valentinian teaching, most likely compiled in the third century. [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] [cite:vt25_nasscal_gphil] It describes the Christian life as a series of mysteries: "The Lord did everything in a mystery, a baptism and a chrism and a eucharist and a redemption and a bridal chamber". [cite:vt24_gphil] The bridal chamber stands last and highest in the list; what the rite involved is debated. Philip rejects the bishops' account of the resurrection: "Those who say they will die first and then rise are in error". Resurrection must be received in this life. Its images are homely and startling: "God is a dyer"; and of the initiate, "You saw Christ, you became Christ". [cite:vt24_gphil]

Philip is also the text behind a modern legend. A passage describes Mary Magdalene as the companion of the Saviour and says that he kissed her often; but the manuscript is damaged at the key word, so the place of the kiss is a modern guess, and the Greek loanword for "companion", koinōnos, can mean a partner in any shared enterprise. [cite:vt25_nasscal_gphil] The passage shows that Valentinian Christians honoured Mary as an intimate disciple. It does not show that Jesus was married, and the attempt to supply a document that did, discussed in Part Six, turned out to be a forgery. [cite:gn_wiki_gjw]

The Gospel of Truth, found in two copies, is not a gospel in form but a sermon, written in Greek between about 140 and 180 and possibly by Valentinus himself. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] It describes ignorance as a nightmare, error as the force that nailed Jesus to the cross, and the truth as a "living book" written in the hearts of those who know, who come at last to rest in the Father. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] Irenaeus knew of a Valentinian "Gospel of Truth" and rejected it as unlike the apostles' gospels. [cite:vt25_iren_311]

The Thunder, Perfect Mind is the strangest work in the library. It is a poem spoken by a female divine voice in a series of paradoxes: "For I am the first and the last", "I am the whore and the holy one". [cite:vt25_pbs_thunder] Its translator, George MacRae, and later Hal Taussig doubted that it should be called Gnostic at all. [cite:vt25_pbs_thunder] [cite:vt25_wiki_thunder] It belongs in this book because of where it was found and what it shows: a library kept in fourth-century Egypt held a text in which the divine speaks as a woman who contains all opposites.

The jar held more than Christian texts. Codex VI contains three Hermetic works, the Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth, the Prayer of Thanksgiving and part of the Asclepius, with a scribal note saying they were selected from a larger collection; it also contains a Coptic version of a passage from Plato's Republic. [cite:vt25_wiki_eighthninth] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] The same codex holds the Acts of Peter and the Twelve Apostles, in which the disciples are told to renounce their possessions and care for the poor. [cite:vt25_wiki_actspeter12] Whoever assembled these books read Christian, Hermetic and Platonic writings together, as parts of one search for the knowledge of God. That breadth is the subject of another book on this shelf, The Hermetic Tradition. In the hands of the bishops' churches, it was exactly what the lists of approved books were meant to narrow.
