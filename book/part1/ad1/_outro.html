The Dream began as a claim about what a free society lets its people become; James Truslow Adams, who named it in 1931, said it was "not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely." [cite:ad1_taylor_adams] Its economic logic is older, Lincoln's ladder from wages to savings to ownership, and so is the companion claim that a person who stays on the bottom rung has only himself to blame. [cite:ad1_lincoln_1859] Schools teach the ladder in careful language, that pay "primarily" reflects the value of what workers produce and that skills raise living standards, while the citizenship test names the system in two words, capitalism and free market economy. [cite:ad1_cee2010] [cite:ad1_uscis2025] Presidents of both parties sell the same few outcomes, a decent wage, a home, savings and children who do better, and builders and lenders sell the same promise with a product attached; the one national campaign followed here lifted home ownership to 69.4 per cent in 2004 before it fell back to 63.1 per cent in 2016, amid a dispute over how much the campaign caused the crash between those two points. [cite:ad1_bush_2002] [cite:ad1_fred_hor] [cite:ad1_fcic_angelides] [cite:ad1_hw_wallison] Today about a third of adults say the Dream still holds, with sharp splits by party, age and race. [cite:ad1_apnorc26] Before testing where the promise fails, the book sets out where it holds and how to measure it. [scene:ad2_what_holds|Part Two: What Holds]
