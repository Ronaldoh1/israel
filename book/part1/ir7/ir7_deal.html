The Deal

The agreement that came closest to settling the dispute over Iran's nuclear programme began as a stopgap. On 24 November 2013 Iran and six powers (the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Russia and China) agreed a Joint Plan of Action. Iran agreed to cap enrichment at 5 per cent, freeze the number of its centrifuges, dilute or convert its stock of uranium enriched to 20 per cent, and halt work on the heavy-water reactor at Arak. In return, the United States and the European Union suspended some sanctions on petrochemicals, gold, cars and aircraft parts. When the plan took effect on 20 January 2014, the International Atomic Energy Agency confirmed that Iran had stopped producing 20 per cent uranium. Its monthly reports over the following year recorded Iran carrying out the plan. The one concern it flagged, the testing of a single advanced centrifuge in November 2014, ended when Iran agreed to stop. When the talks were extended that month, Secretary of State John Kerry said Iran had met all its obligations. [cite:ir7_aca_jpoa]

What the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action Limited

The final agreement, the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), was concluded in Vienna on 14 July 2015. Its core limits were concrete and dated. For fifteen years Iran could enrich uranium only up to 3.67 per cent, well below the roughly 90 per cent used in weapons, and could hold no more than 300 kilograms of enriched uranium. For ten years enrichment was confined to 5,060 first-generation IR-1 centrifuges at Natanz. Before the deal Iran had more than 19,000 centrifuges installed, about 10,200 of them enriching; the surplus went into storage under IAEA seal. The underground site at Fordow could enrich nothing for fifteen years. The Arak reactor was to be redesigned to produce about one kilogram of plutonium a year instead of more than seven, and its original core was to be made unusable, with its channels filled with cement. Iran was to apply the IAEA's Additional Protocol, which allows inspections at short notice, and to seek its ratification within eight years. For a disputed request to see an undeclared site, the deal set a procedure lasting up to 24 days: 14 for Iran to answer, 7 for a joint commission to rule, and 3 to comply. [cite:ir7_aca_jcpoa]

The deal's critics fixed on its end dates as much as its limits. The UN arms embargo was to lift after five years and the missile restrictions after eight. The ban on making IR-1 centrifuges was to end after ten years, the enrichment and stockpile caps after fifteen, and monitoring of centrifuge production after twenty. Monitoring of uranium mines and mills would continue for twenty-five years. Its supporters pointed to the arithmetic of "breakout," the time needed to make enough weapons-grade uranium for one bomb. The Arms Control Association put that time at two to three months under the interim deal and more than twelve months under the JCPOA, for over a decade. [cite:ir7_aca_jcpoa] At the end of December 2015 Iran shipped about 25,000 pounds of low-enriched material to Russia. Kerry called it "one of the most significant steps Iran has taken toward fulfilling its commitment." [cite:ir7_cnbc_russia]

On 20 July 2015 the UN Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 2231, which endorsed the deal for ten years and set out a "snapback" procedure for restoring earlier UN sanctions if Iran broke its terms. The US ambassador, Samantha Power, said implementation would be "the only proper measure of this deal." Iran's Foreign Ministry said in return that if sanctions were restored, Iran would treat that as grounds to stop carrying out its own commitments. [cite:ir7_usip_2231] Both statements later proved to describe what happened.

The Campaign Against It

The Israeli government opposed the deal in public, at length and in the United States itself. In January 2015 the Republican Speaker of the House, John Boehner, invited Netanyahu to address a joint meeting of Congress without consulting the White House. The president's spokesman, Josh Earnest, called the invitation a "departure from protocol." The White House said President Barack Obama would not meet Netanyahu, citing the closeness of Israel's election, due on 17 March. [cite:ir7_aje_invite] On 3 March 2015 Netanyahu told Congress, "this is a bad deal." He said that "It doesn't block Iran's path to the bomb; it paves Iran's path to the bomb," and that the agreement "would be a farewell to arms control." He argued that a better deal would keep restrictions in place "until Iran's aggression ends," linking the nuclear limits to Iran's conduct in the region. [cite:ir7_fox_speech] The speech, and the place of its warnings in his three decades of predictions, are examined in Part Five. [scene:ir5_predictions|Always a year away] When the deal was concluded in July he called it "an historic mistake for the world" and said Israel's commitment to preventing an Iranian bomb "still stands." [cite:ir7_toi_historic]

The campaign also had a covert side, as one newspaper reported it. In March 2015 the Wall Street Journal reported that American intelligence had intercepted Israeli communications containing details of the confidential negotiations, and that Israel had passed such details to members of Congress to build opposition to the deal. A senior US official quoted in the report said, "It is one thing for the U.S. and Israel to spy on each other." Israeli officials denied spying on the talks and said the details had come from French officials. [cite:ir7_npr_spied] The account rests on the Journal's anonymous sources and is disputed by Israel; it is recorded here as reported, not as established.

In Washington the main organised opposition came from the American Israel Public Affairs Committee (AIPAC). In July 2015 AIPAC backed a new group, Citizens for a Nuclear Free Iran, which ran a national television and digital advertising campaign. Its advisory board included the former senators Evan Bayh, Mark Begich, Mary Landrieu and Joe Lieberman. Its spokesman, Patrick Dorton, argued that the deal raised the risk of war and would set off a nuclear arms race. [cite:ir7_ap_cnfi] Politico reported a planned $20 million advertising buy in 30 to 40 states. [cite:ir7_wiki_cnfi] The totals actually spent are not fully public. The journalist Eli Clifton reported that AIPAC had committed $20 to $40 million and that FCC broadcast filings to the end of September 2015 showed at least $14.5 million in television advertising. He also cited the JTA reporter Ron Kampeas's figure that the new group raised nearly $30 million. [cite:ir7_972_clifton] These are reported figures from filings and journalists, not audited totals. The other side campaigned too. J Street, a liberal pro-Israel group, ran a full-page advertisement in the New York Times on 23 July 2015 urging Congress not to "sabotage" the deal, as part of what it described as a "multimillion dollar campaign." [cite:ir7_toi_jstreet]

The campaign failed. The Senate's resolution of disapproval received 58 votes on 10 September 2015, two short of the 60 needed to end a filibuster, and the deal went ahead. [cite:ir7_npr_vote] The record shows a foreign head of government lobbying the US Congress directly and a domestic lobby spending heavily against a president's signature agreement. Whether that lobbying changed any senator's vote is a separate question, and the record examined here does not answer it. The money is documented; its effect is a matter of argument.

What the Inspectors Found

On 16 January 2016, "Implementation Day," the IAEA reported that its inspectors had "verified that Iran has carried out all measures required under the JCPOA." Iran began applying the Additional Protocol on a provisional basis, which the agency said increased its ability to verify that Iran's nuclear activities were peaceful. [cite:ir7_iaea_impday] Over the next three years the agency's quarterly reports kept finding Iran within the deal's limits. The day after the American withdrawal, on 9 May 2018, the agency's director general, Yukiya Amano, said, "the IAEA can confirm that the nuclear-related commitments are being implemented by Iran." He described Iran as subject to "the world's most robust nuclear verification regime." [cite:ir7_iaea_may18] As late as 31 May 2019 the agency reported that Iran "continues to stay within the limitations" and that its inspectors had been able to visit all the sites they needed to, though Iran's stocks of low-enriched uranium and heavy water were rising. [cite:ir7_ap_may19]

The verification record answers a narrow question: whether Iran kept the deal's limits while the deal was in force. It did, on the IAEA's findings, from January 2016 until July 2019. It does not answer the deal's critics' wider objections. Those concerned what would happen when the limits expired, the 24-day delay for disputed sites, the missile programme the deal did not cover, and Iran's armed allies across the region, which Netanyahu had asked to be included. Those objections were about the deal's design, not about Iran's compliance, and they are given here as the Israeli government stated them. What happened when the United States acted on them is the subject of the next section. [scene:ir7_withdrawal|The withdrawal]
