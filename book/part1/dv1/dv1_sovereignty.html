What a Sovereign Is, and Why the Word Misleads

The claim this volume examines is usually told with one word doing most of the work. The City of London, Vatican City and Washington, DC are said to be three "sovereign" cities, standing apart from the countries around them, and Israel is said to be a fourth sovereign they brought into being. Before the record of any of the four can be weighed, the word has to be pinned down, because it means several different things, and the popular story moves between them without saying so. A city can be independent of the country around it in law, in practice, in money or in name, and these are not the same. This section sets out the two standard ways of testing sovereignty and applies them to each of the four places, so that the rest of the volume can say precisely which kind of independence each one has.

The legal test. The most widely cited legal definition of a state comes from the Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States, signed on 26 December 1933 by the states of the Americas. Its first article says that a state, as a person of international law, should possess four things: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. [cite:dv1_montevideo] Two further articles matter for this volume. Article 3 says that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states, and Article 8 says that no state has the right to intervene in the internal or external affairs of another. [cite:dv1_montevideo] The convention was a regional treaty, and its definition has been criticized and refined since, but its four criteria remain the usual starting point when lawyers ask whether a place is a state.

The political test. The second way of testing comes from political science, and it is more skeptical. In Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy (1999) the political scientist Stephen Krasner argued that states have never been as sovereign as the textbooks suppose. Rulers, in his account, mainly want to stay in power, and differences of power and interest explain how states behave better than international norms do. He called the result "organized hypocrisy": norms of sovereignty that are long-standing, widely professed and frequently violated. [cite:dv1_princeton_krasner] Krasner also separated the word into four meanings. Domestic sovereignty is exclusive legal authority within a territory. Interdependence sovereignty is the capacity to control what flows across a border, such as money, goods and people. International legal sovereignty is recognition by other states as a juridically independent entity. Westphalian sovereignty is the exclusion of outside powers from a country's own structures of authority. [cite:dv1_holsti_review] A place can have one of these and lack the others.

Krasner's four meanings are the right tool for this volume because each of the three cities is famous for one kind of independence and lacks the rest, and because the fourth sovereign, Israel, has been argued over in precisely his terms: recognized in law by most states, dependent on outside support in ways critics call a loss of independence. The four tests below are applied in that spirit, as questions rather than verdicts.

The City of London fails the legal test outright. It has a small permanent population, a defined territory and a government, but it has no capacity to enter into relations with other states, and its law is made for it by the Parliament of the United Kingdom. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] Its independence is of a different and older kind. Its privileges were granted by royal charter and confirmed by custom, and later Acts of Parliament preserved them by express exception, as [scene:bc_s01|the library's first study of the City] and [scene:dv2_square_mile|Part Two of this volume] show. In Krasner's terms it holds a fragment of domestic authority within its square mile and none of the other three.

Vatican City passes every test, but only from 1929. The Lateran Treaty, signed on 11 February 1929 and in force from 7 June that year, created a sovereign enclave inside Rome, and it is today an elective absolute monarchy in which the pope holds executive, legislative and judicial power. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] Its foreign relations are conducted not by the city but by the Holy See, the pope's authority over the Catholic Church, which is a separate legal person and has full diplomatic relations with 184 states, the European Union and the Order of Malta. [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] That distinction, between a city of a few hundred residents and a church authority with a worldwide reach, is the most important one in this volume's part on Rome.

The District of Columbia is the reverse of a sovereign city. The Constitution gives Congress power "to exercise exclusive Legislation in all Cases whatsoever" over the federal district, and Congress holds over it the combined powers of a local and a national legislature. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] The district's independence is from any one of the states, which is why it was created, not from the federal government, which governs it. The power associated with Washington in this volume does not belong to the district at all. It belongs to the federal government that sits there.

Israel, the fourth, meets the legal test as a matter of record. It declared its independence on 14 May 1948, was recognized by the United States within minutes and by the Soviet Union within days, and was admitted to the United Nations on 11 May 1949. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] Under Article 3 of the Montevideo Convention its existence as a state does not depend on any other state's recognition, a principle that cuts both ways in this volume: it means that Israel's statehood is not in law a gift from its "parents," and it also means that the parents' recognition, money and protection have to be weighed as political facts rather than legal ones. [cite:dv1_montevideo]

The result of these tests is the frame for everything that follows. Only two of the four places are sovereign states, Vatican City and Israel. The other two are cities whose names stand for something larger: the City of London for the financial market and the British state around it, and Washington for the government of the United States. The volume therefore asks, in each part, not what a "sovereign city" did, but what the institutions behind each name did, and it tests each claim against the documents. [scene:bc_s06|The library's earlier examination of the three-enclaves claim] found no documented coordination among the three. This volume does not assume the opposite. It asks a narrower question, city by city: what each one supplied to the making of the fourth.

Two limits on this section should be stated. The Montevideo definition is a treaty among American states and is widely used rather than universally binding, and the meaning of sovereignty is disputed among scholars in ways this section does not try to settle. Krasner's account is one influential argument among several, presented here through his publisher's summary and a scholarly review rather than a full reading of the book. Neither limit changes the plain legal facts on which the comparison rests.
