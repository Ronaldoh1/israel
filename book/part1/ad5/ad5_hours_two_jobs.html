Hours, Second Jobs and the Two-Income Household

The promise as taught says that one full-time job supports a household. Two claims now circulate against it. The first holds that Americans increasingly need a second job to get by. The second holds that a family which once lived on one income now needs two to reach the same place, and is more fragile for it. Both are partly right and partly wrong, and the record lets them be separated. This section looks at how many people hold more than one job and why, how long Americans work, what the law guarantees in time off, and what happened when the second parent went to work, including the cost of the childcare that made it possible.

Second jobs: high in number, not in share

A multiple jobholder, in the Bureau of Labor Statistics' monthly household survey, is anyone employed who held two or more jobs in the reference week. In 2025 there were about 8.8 million, 5.4 per cent of everyone employed; the 2025 figures are averages of eleven months because no survey was taken in October during the federal shutdown. [cite:us22_bls_cpsaat36_25] In September 2026 the seasonally adjusted count was 8.99 million, or 5.5 per cent. [cite:us22_fred_mjrate] The raw count is near the top of its history because the workforce is larger. The share is not. A Monthly Labor Review study of workers aged 16 to 64 found that the rate peaked at about 6.8 per cent in the summer of 1995, then fell steadily to about 5.0 per cent by the end of 2013, with the decline showing up in every education group. [cite:ad5_mlr_mj2015] The age bases differ slightly between the two series, but the direction is clear: holding two jobs is somewhat less common now than in the mid-1990s.

Who holds them also runs against the circulating picture. The rate rises with education: in January 1994 about 7.8 per cent of workers with a college degree held more than one job against about 3 per cent of those without a high-school diploma, and the ordering was the same in 2013. When the survey last asked why, in May 2004, 38.1 per cent said they wanted extra money, 25.6 per cent said they needed it to meet regular expenses or pay off debt, and 17.6 per cent said they enjoyed the second job. [cite:ad5_mlr_mj2015] A quarter working a second job to cover bills is a real finding about strain at the margin. It is not evidence that a second job has become the norm.

Hours: long, but shorter than they were

A widely repeated comparison holds that Americans work hundreds of hours a year more than Germans or the French. The source of such comparisons, the OECD's series on average annual hours, carries a warning that the figures "are intended for comparisons of trends over time" and are "unsuitable for comparisons of the level" across countries, because each country's data come from different sources. [cite:us22_oecd_annhrs_note] On the trend, which the series is built to show, average annual hours per American worker fell from about 1,880 in 2000 to about 1,800 in 2020 and 1,796 in 2024. [cite:us13_jil_hours] The comparable cross-country measure is usual weekly hours on the main job: in 2025 American employees usually worked 38.2 hours a week, 41.0 if full time, and 8.9 per cent usually worked 50 hours or more, down from 9.5 per cent in 2023. [cite:us22_oecd_avgusl] [cite:us22_oecd_lnghrs] Involuntary part-time work, people working part time who want full-time hours, was 0.81 per cent of employment in 2025. [cite:us22_oecd_invpt]

What sets the United States apart is less the length of the week than what the law guarantees around it. The Fair Labor Standards Act "does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations"; paid vacation is a matter between employer and employee. [cite:us13_dol_vacation] In March 2026, 81 per cent of private-sector workers had access to paid sick leave, 88 per cent of full-time and 59 per cent of part-time workers. [cite:us22_bls_ebs26_sick] Federal law guarantees up to twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected family leave to eligible workers under the Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993; paid family leave reached 27 per cent of private workers in March 2023, but 13 per cent in the lowest-paid quarter against 44 per cent in the highest, and ten jurisdictions run public paid-leave programmes. [cite:us18_crs_leave] [cite:us18_bls_leave] Time off, in other words, is distributed by wage level, which places the least of it where hours are least secure.

The second earner

The larger change in American work since the 1970s is not the second job but the second worker. In March 1975, 47 per cent of mothers with children under 18 were in the labour force. By March 2000 the figure was 73 per cent, and it has stayed near that level since. [cite:ad5_ted_mothers] In 2025 it was 73.9 per cent, and 68.0 per cent for mothers of children under six; in 66.3 per cent of married-couple families with children, both parents were employed. [cite:ad5_bls_famee2025] Across all married couples, including retired ones, both spouses worked in 49.1 per cent. [cite:ad5_bls_famee2025]

Why the second earner went to work is the contested question. Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi argued in The Two-Income Trap that rising fixed costs, above all housing in districts with good schools, along with childcare and health insurance, absorbed the second income, so that a two-earner family in the early 2000s had less discretionary money than a one-earner family a generation earlier while carrying twice the risk of losing a paycheque. [cite:ad5_hw_twoincome] [cite:ad5_ppp_bruenig] The book was widely praised; one New York Times reviewer judged its main thesis "undeniable". [cite:ad5_hw_twoincome]

The critique is strongest on the numbers. Matt Bruenig of the left-leaning People's Policy Project argued in 2019 that the book's key comparison used the wrong price index, the standard CPI-U rather than the research series CPI-U-RS, which by his calculation overstated the 1970s family's income by about 13 per cent; that it deflated every cost category by overall inflation rather than by each category's own; that the later family's higher mortgage bought a house about a third larger; and that $9,600 of the later family's extra fixed costs was childcare, which ends when children reach school age. He also cited research by Jonathan Gruber and Julie Berry Cullen finding little evidence that wives entered work to replace a husband's lost earnings, which undercuts the idea that the stay-at-home spouse had served as household insurance. [cite:ad5_ppp_bruenig] The record therefore supports the first half of the story, that the second earner became normal and that housing, childcare and health costs rose, and leaves the second half, that two-earner families are no better off, contested. Where costs rose for each mechanism, housing in [scene:ad6_price_and_rent|Part Six] and health in [scene:ad8_costs_and_coverage|Part Eight], is taken up there.

Childcare: the price of the second paycheque

Childcare is the cost that most directly sets the value of a second job. The Labor Department's National Database of Childcare Prices, covering 2,512 counties in 2022, found that full-day care for one child cost between $6,552 and $15,600 a year depending on the county and the type of care, equal to 8.9 to 16 per cent of the county's median family income for a single child. [cite:us18_dol_childcare] On the OECD's standard family, a couple with children aged two and three, both working full time at the average wage, an American family paid about 20 per cent of its disposable income for full-time centre care in 2022, and a single parent on the average wage 37 per cent; these figures are after any subsidies and tax relief. [cite:us22_statista_ncc] [cite:us22_oecd_pf34]

Federal policy on the price has reversed twice in two years. A rule issued in March 2024 required states using the Child Care and Development Fund to cap subsidised families' copayments at 7 per cent of family income. [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2024] A new final rule published on 12 May 2026 and effective on 13 July 2026 repealed that federal cap, leaving states to set copayments subject only to the older standard that they must not block access to assistance. [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2026] Status as of October 2026: no federal cap applies.

Where it works as promised

On time, the long record is better than the story. Usual weekly hours for full-time American employees sit close to forty a week, the share working fifty hours or more has fallen in recent years, and annual hours per worker have drifted down since 2000. [cite:us22_oecd_avgusl] [cite:us22_oecd_lnghrs] [cite:us13_jil_hours] Involuntary part-time work is below one per cent of employment. [cite:us22_oecd_invpt] Second jobs are held most often by the best-educated and are less common than a generation ago. [cite:ad5_mlr_mj2015] What the record does show is that the guarantees around work, paid leave and affordable childcare, are thin and unequally spread, and that the federal government has twice in two years changed its answer on what childcare should cost a subsidised family. [cite:us18_crs_leave] [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2026] Earlier treatments of the same measures, set beside another country's, are in [scene:us13_wages|US System vs Israel System, Part Thirteen] and [scene:us22_families|its family budgets].
