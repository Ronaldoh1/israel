What the Board Actually Was, Before Any Congress Met

When the First Zionist Congress met in Basel in August 1897, it walked into an arrangement of states, banking houses and churches that had been assembled over the previous two centuries. A familiar assumption runs the other way, reading backward from the twentieth century: that the Zionist movement created the international power structures that later supported Israel. The record documents the reverse. The structures that would fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state were already in place before the movement that asked for one existed.

The first piece was the state. The British government that would later issue the Balfour Declaration governed through Parliament. In that Parliament the Corporation of the City of London held a business franchise and kept its Remembrancer, in effect a seated lobbyist, in the Commons. Finance, in other words, had a formal place inside the legislature of that state.

The second piece was finance, and here precision matters. The Rothschild houses were documented creditors to Britain, to Prussia and, from 1832, to the Holy See. [cite:wiki_nathan_mayer_rothschild] [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] The Paris house was banker to the Pope from that year. Through Edmond de Rothschild, it also financed some thirty colonies in Palestine from 1882, on his own philanthropic terms. A year before Basel, in 1896, Edmond refused to back Herzl's political project. Every connection in this piece is a contract or a loan. None of it supports the puppet-master legend that earlier sections of this book trace to an 1846 pamphlet. The record shows lenders and borrowers and, on the decisive question of political Zionism in 1896, a lender who said no.

The third piece was the church, and it was divided. Protestant restorationists in Britain and the United States had lobbied for a Jewish Palestine since 1839, for reasons of prophecy and empire, some sixty years before Herzl. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The Vatican took the opposite position. For theological reasons and over questions of sovereignty, it would refuse Herzl in 1904, oppose statehood in 1948 and withhold recognition of Israel until 1993. Two confessions held two positions, and both were political.

Taken together, these pieces explain why this book begins where it does rather than at Basel. A City whose financial corporation sat inside Parliament. Banking houses whose relationships with governments and the papacy were matters of contract. A Protestant lobby that wanted a Jewish Palestine, and a Vatican that would refuse one. A Rothschild who had already turned Herzl away. None of this was built by Zionism. The movement entered a game whose pieces had been set before it arrived.

That framing has a limit, and it should be stated plainly. To say the board existed is not to say it determined the outcome. The pieces could be moved in different directions, and some of them, such as the Vatican and Edmond de Rothschild in 1896, were at first set against the political project. What the record supports is narrower and firmer: the structures that would fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state were older than the movement. The chapters that follow trace what the movement did with them, through Basel in 1897, the Sykes-Picot agreement, the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate.
