A Party With an Army

Hezbollah entered Lebanon's parliament in the August 1992 elections, the first held after the civil war, and did best in the east of the country. [cite:hz4_csm_1992] It won eight of the 128 seats in its own name, with four more independents counted in its bloc; sources that give "12 seats" for 1992 are counting the bloc. Its own seat count has stayed in a narrow band since: seven in 1996, ten in 2000, fourteen in 2005, and thirteen in each of 2009, 2018 and 2022. It first took cabinet posts in 2005, after Syria's troops left Lebanon, holding the ministries of labour and of energy and water. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz] In May 2018 Hezbollah and its allies won 71 seats, a majority, and Hassan Nasrallah said: "Today I can say that our goals have been achieved." [cite:hz6_wilson_record] In May 2022 that coalition fell to 61 seats and lost its majority, though Hezbollah and its ally Amal kept all 27 seats reserved for Shia members under Lebanon's confessional system. [cite:hz6_nbc_2022]

The votes rested on services as well as on the claim to be resisting Israel. Hezbollah runs hospitals and clinics in the south, the Bekaa and Beirut's southern suburbs, licensed through an Islamic Health Authority tied to the party, with al-Rasool al-Azam hospital in Bourj al-Brajneh as the flagship. Those services were never wholly separate from the state. In 2020 the health ministry under Prime Minister Hassan Diab added fifteen Hezbollah-sponsored medical centres to the list the state pays for, and state funding to Hezbollah-run hospitals rose from about 16 billion to 30 billion Lebanese pounds. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] By the party's own count, set out by its deputy leader Naim Qassem, it repaired 17,212 homes damaged in Israeli raids between 1991 and 2000; one academic study put the number of people its health network served in 2000 at about 400,000. [cite:hz6_swj_gabrielsen] Support divides sharply by community. An Arab Barometer poll in early 2024 found that 30 per cent of Lebanese trusted Hezbollah and 55 per cent did not trust it at all; trust stood at 85 per cent among Shia respondents and below 10 per cent among Sunni, Druze and Christian respondents. [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz]

The phrase "state within a state" describes the arrangement that made this possible. The 1989 Taif Agreement required every militia to disarm, and the others did; Hezbollah alone kept its weapons, accepted as a "resistance force" while Israel occupied the south. [cite:hz4_wiki_taif] [cite:hz4_lbc_arms] After Israel withdrew in 2000 the weapons stayed, and the rest of this part shows what happened when the state tried to touch them, or tried to hold anyone in the party to account.

The Hariri assassination and the tribunal

On 14 February 2005 a bomb in Beirut killed Rafik Hariri, the former prime minister, and 21 others, and injured 226. A United Nations-backed court, the Special Tribunal for Lebanon, sitting near The Hague, tried four men in their absence. On 18 August 2020 its trial chamber unanimously convicted Salim Ayyash, found "beyond a reasonable doubt" to have taken part in the conspiracy to carry out the bombing, and acquitted the other three. The judges said they had found no evidence implicating Hezbollah's leadership and "no direct evidence" of Syrian involvement, while noting that Syria and Hezbollah "may have had motives" to eliminate Hariri. A fifth accused, the Hezbollah commander Mustafa Badreddine, had been dropped from the case after he was killed in Syria in 2016. [cite:hz6_un_stl2020] Ayyash was sentenced to life imprisonment. In March 2022 the appeals chamber overturned two acquittals and convicted Hassan Habib Merhi and Hussein Oneissi, finding they had circulated a video in which a fictitious group claimed the bombing in order to shield the real perpetrators; on 16 June 2022 both were sentenced to life. [cite:hz6_almon_stl2022]

Hezbollah denied any role. [cite:hz6_un_stl2020] When the tribunal indicted four of its members in July 2011, Nasrallah said they would not be found or arrested "in 30 days or 60 days, or in a year, two years, 30 years or 300 years", and Hezbollah and its allies brought down the government that year in a dispute over the court. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Nasrallah refused to recognise the tribunal and Hezbollah never handed over any of the three men convicted. The court, which cost an estimated $600 million to $1 billion and drew 49 per cent of its budget from Lebanon, ran short of money and wound down with no one in custody. [cite:hz6_almon_stl2022]

May 2008: the guns turned inward

On 6 May 2008 the cabinet of Prime Minister Fouad Siniora declared Hezbollah's private telephone network illegal and removed the head of security at Beirut airport, who was close to the opposition. On 8 May Nasrallah called the decisions "a declaration of war". [cite:hz6_crs_2008] Hezbollah said the network was part of its military apparatus and the decisions were aimed at the resistance against Israel. [cite:hz6_wiki_2008] Its fighters and those of Amal fought pro-government gunmen across the capital, took control of West Beirut on 9 May, forced the Future Movement's television station off the air and attacked an affiliated newspaper. [cite:hz6_voa_2008] [cite:hz6_aje_2008tl] Siniora called it an attempted coup. [cite:hz6_aje_2008tl] The government reversed both decisions on 14 May. Lebanese officials put the dead at 81 by 12 May; a United Nations report counted 69 dead and Human Rights Watch at least 71, and estimates run from about 70 to 110. [cite:hz6_crs_2008] [cite:hz6_wiki_2008]

The Doha Agreement of 21 May 2008 ended the crisis. Michel Suleiman was elected president on 25 May, and the new 30-member cabinet gave the opposition led by Hezbollah eleven seats, more than a third, which Al Jazeera described as an "effective veto" over executive decisions. The parties pledged not to use weapons or violence to settle political disputes; the question of Hezbollah's own arms was left to a later national dialogue. [cite:hz6_aje_2008tl] [cite:hz6_wiki_doha] [cite:hz6_wiki_2008] Hezbollah had turned its weapons on other Lebanese and come away with the blocking third it had long demanded.

The 2019 protests and the port explosion

In October 2019 protests spread across Lebanon against the cost of living and the confessional political class as a whole. Human Rights Watch documented a series of attacks on protesters. On 23 October in Nabatieh more than 400 men, whom protesters identified as Hezbollah supporters, beat demonstrators with sticks and metal objects. On 29 October in Beirut, hundreds of men chanting for Nabih Berri of Amal and for Nasrallah burned and looted the protest tents in Martyrs' Square. No attackers were arrested, and the organisation recorded at least six occasions on which security forces failed to protect protesters. [cite:hz6_hrw_2019] The pattern matched Hamas's response to Gaza's protests the same year (see [scene:hm6_rule|How Hamas Ruled Gaza]).

On 4 August 2020 about 3,000 tons of improperly stored ammonium nitrate at Beirut port exploded, killing more than 220 people. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] [cite:hz6_na_bitar2025] Judge Tarek Bitar, who took over the inquiry in 2021, sought to question former ministers, including Ali Hassan Khalil of Amal; he did not seek to question members of Hezbollah itself. [cite:hz6_obs_bitar] On 20 September 2021 Wafiq Safa, the head of Hezbollah's liaison unit, was reported by Lebanese media to have told senior judges that "should he drift off the course, we will remove him". Bitar reported the warning in a letter to the prosecutor and the justice minister; Safa did not respond to requests for comment, and the remark rests on those reports. [cite:hz6_an_safa] [cite:hz6_obs_bitar] On 11 October Nasrallah said Bitar was using "the blood of the victims to serve political interests" and called his choice of targets "clearly political targeting". [cite:hz6_aje_nasrallah_bitar]

Three days later, on 14 October 2021, Amal and Hezbollah supporters marched on the Palace of Justice against Bitar's warrant for Khalil. Gunfire in the Tayouneh district killed seven people, three Amal members, three Hezbollah members and a woman hit by a stray bullet. Amal and Hezbollah blamed gunmen of the Lebanese Forces party, which denied it; Nasrallah accused that party of seeking a new civil war. A year later no official finding on who fired first had been published. [cite:hz6_olj_tayouneh] What is documented is that Hezbollah publicly demanded the judge's removal and called the protest; who fired first remains unresolved.

The inquiry then stalled for more than three years under lawsuits from the officials it named and a block by the public prosecutor, until Bitar resumed work on 16 January 2025. [cite:hz6_na_bitar2025] He sent his report to the prosecutor in late March 2026, reportedly accusing 70 people; as of August 2026 no indictment had been issued. [cite:hz6_cm_port2026] Elections due in May 2026 did not take place: on 9 March 2026, a week after Hezbollah opened fire on Israel in the wider war on Iran, parliament voted 76 to extend its own term by two years. [cite:hz6_rt_extend] Days earlier the cabinet had banned all military and security activity by Hezbollah (see [scene:hz8_disarm|the disarmament decision]). [cite:hz8_olj_ban]
