Khan Yunis and Rafah, November 1956

Israeli forces entered the Gaza Strip on 2 November 1956, in the first days of the Sinai campaign. The Strip had been under Egyptian administration since 1948, a nineteen-year arrangement told in [scene:egi_src_s02|Egypt and Gaza, 1948–67]; about three-quarters of its people, by Morris's account, were refugees from the war of that year. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] Within ten days of the Israeli entry, two incidents took place in its southern towns that remain among the most disputed events of the war: at Khan Yunis on 3 November and at Rafah on 12 November. What happened in each is known chiefly through one document, the special report that the Director of UNRWA, the United Nations agency for the Palestine refugees, submitted to the General Assembly covering the period from 1 November to mid-December 1956. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] Almost every later figure is measured against it, and its own author was careful to say how much it could not establish.

The document. The report was published as United Nations document A/3212/Add.1, in the official records of the General Assembly's eleventh session (Supplement 14A, 1957). [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] It is not the record of an inquiry. It is the account of an agency that was serving the refugees of Gaza when the Strip changed hands, and that received, in the weeks that followed, lists of names of the dead. Its value lies in the fact that it records both what the Israeli authorities told the Director and what the refugees told him, and that it says plainly where the two diverged.

Khan Yunis, 3 November. The report's paragraph 23 opens with the plain fact: "The town of Khan Yunis and the Agency's camp adjacent thereto were occupied by Israel troops on the morning of 3 November." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] The Director then gives the two accounts he received. The Israeli authorities "state that there was resistance to their occupation and that the Palestinian refugees formed part of the resistance." The refugees state "that all resistance had ceased at the time" and that "many unarmed civilians were killed as the Israel troops went through the town and camp." The Director did not choose between them. He noted only that there was "some conflict in the accounts given as to the causes of the casualties." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212]

On the number, he was equally careful. He had received lists "numbering 275 Individuals, of whom 140 were refugees and 135 local residents of Khan Yunis." In the same paragraph he wrote: "The exact number of dead and wounded is not known." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] The distinction matters for everything that follows. The figure of 275 is the length of the lists the agency received, broken down by whether the person named was a refugee or a resident of the town. It is not a count that the agency itself verified, and the report does not present it as one.

Rafah, 12 November. The second incident took place nine days later and in different circumstances. According to paragraphs 24 to 27 of the report, the Rafah killings "occurred during a screening operation conducted by the Israel forces." Israel said the refugees were "hostile" and that there was "some resistance"; the refugees denied it. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] Here the Director went further than at Khan Yunis and gave his own finding. Loudspeaker announcements "were not heard by some of the refugee population," and "some Israel soldiers apparently panicked and opened fire on this running crowd." The lists for Rafah numbered "111, of whom 103 were refugees, seven local residents, and one an Egyptian." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212]

The Director's finding at Rafah is the closest the report comes to a judgement of cause, and it is worth reading precisely. It rejects neither account in full. It does not accept the Israeli claim of resistance as the explanation, and it does not adopt the language of deliberate killing. It describes a failure of communication followed by fire from soldiers who, in the word he chose, "apparently" panicked. The word suggests an inference from what he was told rather than something his staff had witnessed.

The smaller incidents. The two towns were not the only places where people died. In paragraph 29 the Director recorded a further list of "sixty-six individuals, of whom forty-eight were refugees," killed in smaller incidents between 1 and 21 November: some in the fighting, some for breaking the curfew, some by accident. He described Khan Yunis and Rafah as "the only major ones of their kind." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] The historian Benny Morris adds that dozens more people, mostly captured fedayeen or Egyptian prisoners of war, were killed in the weeks after the conquest. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza]

What the Director could not do. The report is explicit about its limits, and those limits are themselves part of the record. The United Nations Truce Supervision Organization, whose officers would normally have investigated incidents of this kind, could not do so, because their movement in Gaza "had been restricted by the Israel authorities" (paragraph 21). "It has not been possible to verify individually listed death[s]," the Director wrote, and "it is most unlikely that lists of casualties can ever be made complete," because many burials "were made without identification" (paragraph 30). [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] Pressure on the observers was reported at the time. On 7 November the Secretary-General, Hammarskjöld, told the American ambassador, Lodge, that General Burns had reported that Israel intended, and might already have begun, to force the Truce Supervision observers out of Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d534] The body that might have produced an independent count was therefore kept from the place where the count was needed.

The other figures. Every later number for Khan Yunis and Rafah comes from a source with its own standpoint, and each needs to be read as what it is.

Israel's figure for Rafah was 48 dead, which Morris confirms as the Israeli count, against UNRWA's list of 111. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] For Khan Yunis no separate Israeli total appears in the sources used here. Israel's position, as TIME reported it in its coverage of Joe Sacco's Footnotes in Gaza, is that "Israel denies that either event was a massacre," disputes the figures, and says its soldiers killed smaller numbers while suppressing riots and resistance. [cite:rs_cw3_time_sacco]

Palestinian sources give higher totals. For Khan Yunis they give 415 killed and 57 missing; for Rafah, 197 killed and 23 disappeared. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_rafah] Abdel Aziz al-Rantisi, as cited by Filiu, gave 525 Gazans killed. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis] These figures reach this book through encyclopedia articles that attribute them to their named sources; they are reported here as the claims of those sources, not as independent tallies.

Sacco reconstructed both events from the testimony of survivors. Residents told him that about 100 men were shot at the caravanserai in Khan Yunis. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis] TIME notes that Sacco himself describes such testimony as subject to "the frailty of memory." [cite:rs_cw3_time_sacco] The journalist Amira Hass, writing in 2010, reported the United Nations estimates as 275 at Khan Yunis and more than 100 at Rafah, noted that no photographs of the killings appear to exist, and treated one witness, who was six years old in 1956, with explicit caution. [cite:rs_cw3_hass_internazionale]

A British newspaper report pointed lower. For Rafah, The Times of London reported "some 60" dead. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_rafah]

Morris, an Israeli historian, has given the events two different numerical treatments. In work of 1994 and 2011 he put the dead at "about two hundred" across both towns. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis] In 2024 he wrote that at Khan Yunis "some 275 civilians and POWs were murdered" according to the Palestinians and UNRWA, and that at Rafah Israel said 48 were killed and UNRWA 111. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] His later formula attributes the 275 to UNRWA, but the Director's report itself, as quoted above, speaks of lists of 275 "Individuals" whose exact number of dead and wounded "is not known"; it does not describe them as murdered. Morris accepts the word massacre for both events. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis]

Read together, the figures fall into a pattern. The UNRWA lists, made close to the events by an agency on the spot, sit in the middle. Israel's count for Rafah is under half of UNRWA's. The Palestinian counts run above it by roughly half at Khan Yunis and by three-quarters at Rafah. The lowest figure is The Times's "some 60" for Rafah; the highest is al-Rantisi's 525. The spread is wide, but no source puts the dead of either town in single figures, and every source that counts both towns together puts their combined dead in the hundreds.

Israel's context. The Israeli case, stated at full strength, does not begin in November 1956. Gaza was the base from which the fedayeen, the armed infiltrators who crossed into Israel in 1954–56, had operated; Morris writes that their raids were run by Egyptian military intelligence, and he sets the November killings against that background. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] Ben-Gurion had told the Knesset on 15 October 1956 that Egypt had caused Israel 242 casualties in 1954–55 [cite:rs_cw3_knesset_15oct56]; the Jewish Agency counts 1,300 Israelis killed or wounded across 1948–56. [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen] The soldiers who entered Khan Yunis did so in a war, in a town where, by Israel's account, some of the refugees took part in the resistance. These facts, told more fully in [scene:cw3_road_to_war|the road to war], are the context Israel's account invokes. They do not settle the question the UNRWA report leaves open: whether, at Khan Yunis, the killing took place during resistance or after it had ended, and whether the people killed were fighters or unarmed. Context explains the circumstances of an act. It cannot establish what the act was.

The dispute. The two cases can be put in their strongest forms. The first holds that these were massacres of civilians. The refugees told the United Nations at the time that resistance had ended before the killing at Khan Yunis; the Palestinian counts run from 275 to 415, with more missing, there and to 197 at Rafah; and Sacco's witnesses describe men lined up and shot. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_khanyunis] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_rafah] [cite:rs_cw3_time_sacco] The second is Israel's: its troops met armed resistance at Khan Yunis and a riot at Rafah, it put the Rafah dead at 48, and it denies that either was a massacre. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] [cite:rs_cw3_time_sacco] Between the two stand two sources that are neither Palestinian advocates nor Israeli officials. The United Nations Director could verify neither version and judged that at Rafah some soldiers had panicked. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] Morris, an Israeli historian who also documents the fedayeen campaign, uses the word massacre for both. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza]

Was anyone held to account? None of the sources drawn on for this section records an Israeli investigation of either incident, or any prosecution. The contrast with another killing of the same week is direct. At Kafr Qasim, inside Israel, on 29 October 1956, Border Police shot dead villagers who were Israeli citizens; that case went to a military court, as the next sections of this chapter tell. No equivalent proceeding for Khan Yunis or Rafah appears in the record used here. The Truce Supervision officers who might have investigated were kept out, and the agency that did report could not verify its own lists. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] Israel's later occupation of the Strip, of which these were the first weeks, is told in [scene:cw3_gaza_occupation|Gaza under Israeli rule].

What the record leaves open. The number of dead at Khan Yunis is not established and, by the Director's own judgement, never will be completely, since many were buried without identification. Whether resistance had ended there when the killing took place is disputed between the only two accounts given at the time, and the sources used here contain no independent witness to settle it. At Rafah, the Director's finding of panic is the only account of cause from outside the two parties, and he hedged it with the word "apparently." The range between Israel's 48 and the Palestinian 197 for Rafah, and between The Times's "some 60" and al-Rantisi's 525, cannot be closed from these documents. What is not open is that a United Nations agency, working in Gaza in November 1956, received lists naming 275 people at Khan Yunis and 111 at Rafah, and recorded the refugees' statement that many of the dead were unarmed civilians.
