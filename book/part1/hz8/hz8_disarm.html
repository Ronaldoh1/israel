A State Monopoly on Arms, on Paper

The 2024 war changed Lebanon's politics as much as its border. The presidency had been vacant for more than two years. On 9 January 2025 parliament elected the army commander, Joseph Aoun, with 99 of 128 votes. In his acceptance speech he promised to "confirm the state's right to monopolise the carrying of weapons." [cite:hz8_na_aoun] Four days later Aoun asked Nawaf Salam, then president of the International Court of Justice, to form a government; Salam won the support of more than half of the members of parliament, and Hezbollah, which had backed the caretaker prime minister Najib Mikati, did not nominate him. [cite:hz8_aj_salam]

The August 2025 decisions

The pledge became a cabinet decision on 5 August 2025. The government tasked the army with drawing up a plan to restrict weapons to the army and other state forces "before the end of this year," with the plan itself due before the end of August. Two ministers, Health Minister Rakan Nassereddine of Hezbollah and Environment Minister Tamara al-Zein of Amal, walked out. Qassem's answer was that "any timetable presented for implementation under... Israeli aggression cannot be agreed to." [cite:hz8_nn_aug5]

Two days later the cabinet adopted the objectives of a paper submitted by the American envoy Tom Barrack. As reported by AP, its goals were a "gradual end of the armed presence of all non-state actors, including Hezbollah, in all Lebanese territory"; the withdrawal of Israeli forces from the south; a halt to Israeli air strikes; the release of Lebanese prisoners held in Israel; and eventual demarcation of the border. The Shia ministers walked out again. Hezbollah said it would "treat this decision as if it does not exist," and its officials said they would not discuss the rest of their arsenal until Israel left the five hills and stopped its near-daily strikes. [cite:hz8_euronews_aug7] The paper put obligations on both sides in one document; Hezbollah's objection was to the order, since it wanted the Israeli withdrawal and the end of strikes first.

On 5 September the army commander, Rodolphe Haykal, presented the plan. The cabinet "welcomed" it without formally adopting it, and without a timetable; the information minister said implementation depended on the army's logistical, material and personnel capacity and might need "additional time." Five Shia ministers left the room as Haykal came in. A Hezbollah-aligned minister argued that any decision taken without the Shia ministers was void under Lebanon's sectarian power-sharing rules, and Qassem had already warned in August of street protests and raised the prospect of civil war if the government confronted the group. [cite:hz8_aj_sep5] The plan's details were kept secret, but media reports summarized by Security Council Report described five phases: south of the Litani first, then the area between the Litani and the Awali, then Beirut and its surroundings, then the Bekaa, then the whole country. [cite:hz8_scr_nov25]

The first phase

By October 2025, according to a statement by US Central Command, the Lebanese army had removed about 10,000 rockets, 400 missiles and more than 205,000 pieces of unexploded ordnance. [cite:hz8_scr_nov25] On 8 January 2026 the army announced that its plan had "reached an advanced stage" and that it had operational control south of the Litani, except at the five Israeli positions. Netanyahu's office called this an "encouraging start" but "far from sufficient," saying "Hezbollah must be fully disarmed" and citing Hezbollah efforts to rearm with Iranian help, which CNN could not verify. Hezbollah had largely allowed the army to work south of the Litani and refused to give up its weapons north of it, citing the continuing occupation. The government said the army would bring a plan for the area north of the Litani in February. [cite:hz8_cnn_phase1]

That February plan was overtaken by events. In the same months that the army was clearing the south, the Israeli strikes counted in [scene:hz8_violations|the previous section] continued, including the killing of Tabatabai in Beirut, and Israel did not leave the five hills. Each side pointed to the other's failure as the reason for its own. Hezbollah said it could not disarm while Israel struck and occupied; Israel said it could not stop or leave while Hezbollah kept weapons anywhere in Lebanon.

Reconstruction as leverage

Money sat beside the arms question. The World Bank estimated in March 2025 that the war from 8 October 2023 to 20 December 2024 had cost Lebanon $14 billion, $6.8 billion in physical damage and $7.2 billion in economic losses, that recovery and reconstruction would need $11 billion, and that the economy had shrunk 7.1 percent in 2024, on top of a fall of nearly 40 percent since 2019. Housing was the worst-hit sector, at $4.6 billion. [cite:hz8_wb_rdna] The south, the Bekaa and Beirut's southern suburbs, where most of Hezbollah's constituents live, were, Al Jazeera reported in May 2025, Hezbollah's priority for rebuilding; the same report said that major donors, the United States among them, were pushing for Hezbollah's disarmament as a precondition for reconstruction aid. [cite:hz8_aj_donors] The UN rights office, for its part, said Israeli strikes on factories, roads and housing were themselves hindering reconstruction and return. [cite:hz8_ohchr_nov25] This book did not find a published US document setting disarmament as a formal condition; the linkage is reported, not documented in a text.

On the record compiled here, the Lebanese state did more of what it promised than either armed party. That record ended on 2 March 2026, when Hezbollah fired on Israel and the government that had been negotiating its disarmament banned its military activity outright. [cite:hz8_olj_ban]
