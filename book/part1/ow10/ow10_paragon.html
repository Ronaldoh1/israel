Paragon: The Israeli Spyware ICE Bought, Paused and Revived

Where NSO was shut out of the American market, Paragon Solutions was let in. Its tool, Graphite, can take over a phone without the user clicking anything and read messages in encrypted apps. Between September 2024 and April 2026 Immigration and Customs Enforcement signed a contract for it, suspended the contract under a presidential order, saw the company pass into American ownership and then reactivated the deal. Each step is documented, and together they show how an Israeli offensive cyber tool becomes an American one. [cite:ow10_npr_lyons]

The company. Paragon is an Israeli company, operating under the close supervision of Israel's Ministry of Defense, which regulates offensive cyber firms. Its founders, as Globes listed them in December 2024, were Ehud Schneorson, then its chairman, Idan Nurick, its chief executive, Igor Bogudlov, Liad Avraham and Liran Elkayam; its investors included Battery Ventures and Red Dot. Ehud Barak, the former prime minister and defence minister, was reported to hold several percent of the company, worth an estimated $10–15 million before tax, and his involvement was said to have worried buyers that Israel's political leadership might oppose a sale. [cite:ow10_globes_ae]

The ICE contract. On 27 September 2024, according to the federal procurement record reported by the journalist Jack Poulson, ICE signed a $2 million contract with Paragon, number 70CTD024P00000012. In October 2024 a hold was placed on it for review under Executive Order 14093, Biden's 2023 order barring federal use of commercial spyware that poses counterintelligence risks or has been misused by foreign governments against journalists, activists or dissidents. [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] [cite:ow10_eo14093] The order contains no blanket exemption for American-owned vendors. It asks, among other things, whether the vendor is under the control of a foreign government engaged in intelligence against the United States, whether its tools have been used to target activists or journalists, and whether it has taken steps to stop such use, and it allows a one-year waiver in extraordinary circumstances. [cite:ow10_eo14093]

The sale to an American owner. In December 2024 Globes reported that AE Industrial Partners, an American private-equity firm, was buying Paragon for an estimated $900 million, half in cash and half in future milestone payments, and would merge it into REDLattice, a defence contractor it already owned in Virginia; the deal was reported to permit exporting Paragon's technology to the United States, while Paragon itself would remain an Israeli company. [cite:ow10_globes_ae] [cite:ow10_reg_paragon] On the question of Israeli approval, the reporting conflicts. Globes first reported, from unnamed sources, that senior ministry officials had approved the sale weeks earlier; three days later the ministry said the sale had "not yet received the required approval," that no formal report had been filed, that any transfer of technology to foreign hands required a special licence, and that its decision would be "purely professional." [cite:ow10_globes_ae] [cite:ow10_globes_mod] Globes reported on 30 January 2025 that the sale had closed in December 2024, with about $500 million in cash paid, before the Defense Ministry's formal approval; by April 2026 Paragon was described as owned by AE Industrial Partners and merged with REDLattice. [cite:ow10_globes_closed] [cite:ow10_npr_lyons]

Reactivation and use. On Saturday 30 August 2025, under the Trump administration, ICE modified the contract to lift the hold; Poulson reported the change from the procurement record on Monday 1 September, and other outlets confirmed that the deal had been allowed to proceed. [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] [cite:ow10_reg_paragon] In an April 2026 letter to three House Oversight Democrats, answering their questions of October 2025, the acting ICE director, Todd Lyons, acknowledged for the first time that Homeland Security Investigations was using the tool against foreign terrorist organisations, "particularly those involved in the trafficking of fentanyl." He wrote that its use would "comply with constitutional requirements," and certified under the 2023 order that it posed no significant counterintelligence risk and no significant risk of improper use by a foreign government. [cite:ow10_npr_lyons] [cite:ow10_tc_lyons] Representative Summer Lee, one of the letter's recipients, said ICE had given no substantive answer on who could be targeted or under what legal authority inside the United States; an unnamed DHS official said the agency uses such technology to support investigations while respecting civil liberties. [cite:ow10_npr_lyons]

Italy. How a government customer used the tool was tested in Europe. In early 2025 WhatsApp said about 90 journalists and members of civil society had been targeted with Graphite. [cite:ow10_npr_lyons] In June 2025 the Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto confirmed with high confidence that Graphite had been installed on the iPhones of two European journalists, one of them the Italian reporter Ciro Pellegrino; the Italian parliamentary intelligence committee, COPASIR, confirmed that the government had used Graphite against two activists, Luca Casarini and Giuseppe Caccia. Paragon offered to help investigate the targeting of the editor Francesco Cancellato; Italy declined. Paragon suggested it had ended its Italian contracts; Italy's security intelligence department disputed that account. [cite:ow10_is_italy] By April 2026, American reporting described Paragon as having cut off Italy's agencies. [cite:ow10_tc_lyons]

What the case shows. Paragon's path differs from NSO's in one decisive respect: ownership. A tool designed in Israel by Israeli engineers, under Israeli export control, entered American service after an American firm agreed to buy its maker, and after a change of administration in Washington. [cite:ow10_globes_ae] [cite:ow10_reg_paragon] What the United States gains is a capability its agencies say they need against cartels; what is not public is the list of targets, the legal process used for each, and, because Paragon was to remain an Israeli company after the sale, what if anything Israel's licensing authority retains over the tool. The sources used here do not answer that last question. For the ICE side of this story in the context of other Israeli-made tools the agency buys, see [scene:ow6_nypd_ice|Part Six].
