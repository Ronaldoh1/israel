The Data Layer: Brokers, Cameras and the Government's Own Files

Book Five puts the same five questions to every tool it examines: what the tool collects or does, who uses it and under what contract, where it has been misused or got things wrong, what the law and the courts allow, and what its worst use would look like. The last belongs to the book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding. Throughout, capability is not intent: what a tool can do is a documented fact, and its use against a critic needs a document of its own. This section begins with the layer every other tool draws on, the records about people that already exist and can be bought, borrowed or joined. Palantir's platforms and the rule that lets an agency buy what it could not seize without a warrant are in [scene:ow9_ice|ICE, the sole-source contracts and the data they run on] and [scene:us10_us|the United States section of US vs Israel].

What a data broker holds

California, which keeps a public register, defines a data broker as "a business that gathers and sells consumer information that a consumer didn't give them directly." More than 500 were registered there by the end of 2025. [cite:de24_ca_drop] Their raw material is ordinary paperwork: property and court records, vehicle registrations, marketing lists, and "credit header" data, the identifying top lines of credit files (name, addresses, birth date, Social Security number). Brokers match records on shared keys and sell searchable access. The intelligence agencies buy too. A report for the Director of National Intelligence, declassified on 12 June 2023, found that the intelligence community acquires a "significant amount" of commercially available information and that such data "can reveal sensitive and intimate information about individuals." [cite:de24_lawfare_odni]

One episode shows how far an ordinary record can travel. The National Consumer Telecom and Utilities Exchange, a group of utility and telephone companies, licensed the names, home addresses, Social Security numbers and other details of more than 170 million people to the credit bureau Equifax, which sold them on as "utility header data" into investigative databases including Thomson Reuters' CLEAR. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) paid tens of millions of dollars for access to it through CLEAR. After The Washington Post reported it in February 2021 and Senator Ron Wyden pressed the exchange, it told Equifax to stop selling new data in October 2021, and Equifax stopped supplying it to law enforcement on 23 October 2021; data sold before then remained in use. [cite:de24_globe_utility]

The two investigative databases

Two companies, better known for legal research, supply most of the commercial records ICE searches. LexisNexis Risk Solutions holds a contract signed in February 2021, under President Biden, worth about $24.5 million, that gives ICE its Accurint and LexID people-search tools; a 2021 change added jail-booking data. ICE users ran more than 1.2 million Accurint person searches between March and September 2021. The contract was due to end on 31 August 2026, and on 10 August 2026 ICE gave notice of a $6.7 million sole-source bridge whose work statement says the system "must connect through application programming interfaces" to ICE applications including "the Palantir platform". [cite:de24_bu_lexis] An application programming interface, or API, is a standard doorway through which one program asks another for data automatically, without a person typing each search.

Thomson Reuters' CLEAR was renewed for the Department of Homeland Security without competition from 31 March 2026, on the justification that "only CLEAR provides the range of law enforcement data the agency requires"; the data types listed include utility data, credit reporting bureaus and licence-plate records. The company told the legal-technology writer Bob Ambrogi that it does "not comment on specific customer contracts" and that "Palantir is not a customer of CLEAR." [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] Which ICE contracts these are, set beside the Palantir awards, is listed in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|the ICE contracts section].

Taken together, the two databases sit in the middle of a supply chain: ordinary records go in at one end, and ICE's analysts and software query them at the other.

Fusion centres

A fusion centre is a state or city intelligence office where police, federal agents and analysts share reports and records so that, in principle, a clue held by one agency reaches another. The model spread after the attacks of 11 September 2001 with federal money. A bipartisan Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations inquiry, led by Senators Tom Coburn, a Republican, and Carl Levin, a Democrat, reported on 2 October 2012. It counted more than 70 centres and found that the Department of Homeland Security could estimate its spending on them since 2003 only as somewhere between $289 million and $1.4 billion. The intelligence its officers produced there was of "uneven quality – oftentimes shoddy, rarely timely," the subcommittee found,. [cite:de24_psi_fusion] The report also said the reporting was "sometimes endangering citizens' civil liberties and Privacy Act protections." [cite:de24_govexec_fusion]

Licence-plate readers

An automated licence-plate reader (ALPR) is a camera that photographs every passing number plate, reads it by software and stores it with a time and a place. Millions of readings, searched together, show where a car has been. In February 2014 Homeland Security Secretary Jeh Johnson cancelled an ICE request for a nationwide plate-tracking database, saying ICE's leaders had not known of it. [cite:de24_physorg_2014] In January 2018 ICE gained that access through a contract with West Publishing, part of Thomson Reuters, working with Vigilant Solutions; ICE called it "one tool in support of its investigations." [cite:de24_upi_vigilant]

The largest network today belongs to Flock Safety, whose cameras were in more than 4,000 communities by August 2025. On 25 August 2025 Flock paused pilot programmes with Customs and Border Protection and its investigative arm after Illinois' Secretary of State, Alexi Giannoulias, said an audit had found CBP searching Illinois plate data in breach of state law. "We clearly communicated poorly," said Flock's chief executive, Garrett Langley; federal searches would be labelled as such and limited to one police agency at a time. [cite:de24_ap_flock] In Woodburn, Oregon, a city audit found that the Department of Homeland Security and the Border Patrol ran 6,089 searches through a Flock trial between January and November 2025, although the police department's own policy barred immigration use; the city ended its contract at the end of April 2026. [cite:de24_katu_woodburn] The cameras also misread. In August 2020 police in Aurora, Colorado, held Brittney Gilliam's family at gunpoint after a misread, and the city paid $1.9 million. [cite:de24_eff_alpr]

The government's own files, reused

The newest change since 2025 is not a purchase but a reuse: records people gave the government for one purpose opened to another. The Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) system, long used to check benefit applicants' immigration status, was linked to Social Security data in May 2025 and from 15 August 2025 could check voters in bulk with only the last four digits of a Social Security number; election officials had run more than 33 million voters through it by September 2025. The state agreements let DHS use the data "for any purpose permitted by law," and no accuracy testing had been published. [cite:de24_npr_save] In April 2025 the Internal Revenue Service agreed to check names and addresses for ICE; a federal judge later found the IRS had broken the law "approximately 42,695 times," and an appeals panel upheld her block on 8 September 2026, while another panel had declined to block the sharing in February. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] For Medicaid, Judge Vince Chhabria ruled on 29 December 2025 that, for the twenty states that sued, health officials could pass ICE only five fields about people not lawfully present (status, address, phone, date of birth and Medicaid number), and no health records. [cite:de24_wt_medicaid] How each of these walls was built and breached is the subject of [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|the section on the data walls], and the wider set of records the state already holds, on benefits, schooling, work and credit, is mapped in [scene:de24b_benefits|the benefits section], [scene:de24b_schools|the section on schools], [scene:de24b_work_and_vetting|on work and vetting] and [scene:de24b_credit_and_money|on credit and money]; the ICE tool reported to draw on Medicaid data is described in [scene:ow9_ice|the One-Way Mirror]. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite]

The Homeland Advanced Recognition Technology system (HART), meant to replace a 1990s database of faces, fingerprints and irises, was contracted to Northrop Grumman for $95 million in February 2018, under President Trump's first term. [cite:de24_wt_hart2018] In September 2023 the Government Accountability Office found it years late, $354 million over its earlier estimate and meeting 5 of 12 federal privacy requirements; the old system held over 290 million identities. [cite:de24_gao_hart] In August 2026 Kyle Schutt, who had worked with the Department of Government Efficiency, took over the biometrics office, with HART due to reach initial operation "no later than September 2026." [cite:de24_bu_schutt] Whether that deadline was met could not be confirmed as of October 2026.

If it were connected

The book's working lens for Book Five is that these mechanisms need not be joined today to matter: they exist, and in the worst case they could be joined. Each toolkit section therefore ends by naming the joins, what each would let a government do to a person, what step it would take, and what stands in the way, graded documented (it exists, with a source), probable (a contract, interface or legal authority already points to it), possible (technically feasible, with no document) or not supported (the record runs against it). "Possible" is used for joins between systems only, never for facts about people.

Commercial databases to the integration platform: documented as a requirement, probable as a working flow. The LexisNexis bridge's work statement requires interfaces to "the Palantir platform," which would put a person's addresses, vehicles and jail bookings into an ICE case file automatically; the contract is the step. [cite:de24_bu_lexis] Older ICE records, from 2014 to 2022, show the opposite arrangement for CLEAR, which agents were told to search separately because it was "not connected to Palantir tools." [cite:de24_guardian_icm] Medicaid records to the same platform: documented, once, beyond the court's limit. A dataset sent to ICE on 7 January 2026 went on to Palantir, which runs ICE's address tool, over a Microsoft Teams chat, according to a declaration filed by California; Palantir said it was purged "pursuant to government instruction," copies were later found with six users, and Judge Chhabria paused the sharing in late May 2026; after a hearing on 3 September 2026 no further ruling had been located as of October 2026. [cite:de24_npr_medicaid_pal] [cite:de24b_chn_medicaid] Plate readers to federal agencies: documented in pilots and now narrowed; a standing feed from a city network such as Flock into a federal platform is possible, with no document, and would need a new contract or a reversal of Flock's August 2025 limits and of state laws like Illinois'. [cite:de24_ap_flock] [cite:de24_katu_woodburn] Voter checks to enforcement: probable, because the state agreements already let DHS use uploaded data "for any purpose permitted by law"; no use beyond citizenship checks has been reported. [cite:de24_npr_save] How all six layers fit together is drawn in [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|the ecosystem map].

What this layer documents is supply: records on most adults, sold or shared, with doorways built to let one system ask another. It does not document a single master file on every American, or the use of these sources to find a critic for speech. What the joining platform does is argued in [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|the Palantir part].
