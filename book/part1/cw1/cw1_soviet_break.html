From Patron to Rupture: Moscow and Israel, 1947–1953

A common account of Israel's early foreign policy runs in one line: the Soviet Union helped create the state, the state chose America, and Moscow turned hostile in return. The record supports each part of that sentence and complicates the order. Soviet support in 1947–48 was real. Soviet hostility from late 1951 was also real, and it took a form that cannot be described accurately without naming it: show trials and an announced conspiracy in which Jews, Zionism and Israel's own diplomats were cast as agents of Western intelligence. Between those two points lies a dispute that historians have not closed, over whether Moscow pushed Israel away or Israel's own turn to the West drew Soviet hostility on itself. This section follows the relationship from the partition vote to the restoration of diplomatic relations in July 1953.

The beginning was cooperation, and in Cold War terms an unusual one. In 1947 the United States and the Soviet Union both voted for the partition of Palestine, which the historian Avi Shlaim calls "one of rare occasions of agreement between them during the Cold War". The Soviet Union extended recognition to the new state, and in 1948 the Eastern bloc was Israel's main supplier of arms. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Drawing on the work of Uri Bialer, Shlaim argues that the Czechoslovak arms sales "continued for longer than would have been necessary" merely to create a Jewish state. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] That is an interpretation, but it matters to everything that followed. If the arms were meant only to help create a Jewish state, they need not have run on as long as they did. If they ran on, then on this reading Moscow was investing in a relationship with the state itself, and what it expected in return becomes the question.

The first public sign of a cooler Soviet line came over Jerusalem. On 9 December 1949 Moscow voted at the United Nations for the internationalization of the city. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] According to Bialer, as Shlaim reports him, Soviet attitudes shifted further when Israel publicly backed the American position on Korea in 1950. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] How that decision was made, in the cabinet and in the Knesset, is told in [scene:cw1_korea|the section on the Korean decision]. For the Soviet side of the story its significance is simple: the government that had called its policy non-identification with either bloc had now taken a public position with one of them.

The next turn came not in Moscow but in Prague. Rudolf Slánský, the former general secretary of the Czechoslovak Communist Party, was arrested in November 1951. In November 1952 he and 13 others were put on trial, and 11 of them, Slánský among them, were sentenced to death. The charges included that he was a Zionist agent and a Western spy. Most of those condemned were Jews. Slánský was executed on 3 December 1952. [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_slansky] The Jewish Telegraphic Agency, reporting during the trial, gave the count as 12 of the 14 defendants Jewish. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_prague_trial] The two figures measure slightly different things, defendants in one case and the condemned in the other, and they do not conflict.

What made the trial an event in Israel's foreign relations, and not only in Czechoslovakia's internal purge, was the indictment's reach. According to the JTA, it named Ben-Gurion and Sharett. It described Ehud Avriel, Israel's former minister to Czechoslovakia, as "an American spy covered by the immunity of Israel diplomatic passports". And it used the alleged confessions of Mordecai Oren, a leader of Israel's left-wing Mapam party who was then under arrest in Czechoslovakia, to tie Israel to the case. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_prague_trial] Shlaim describes the show trial as denouncing Zionism and calling Israel an agent of American imperialism. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The mechanism, as these sources describe it, was a court in a Soviet-bloc state using alleged confessions to present Zionism, and Israel's own officials, as instruments of Western espionage. The trial's verdicts were not the last word on Slánský himself. He was cleared of the criminal charges in 1963 and restored to party membership in 1968. [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_slansky]

Six weeks after Slánský's execution the same pattern appeared in Moscow. On 13 January 1953 Pravda and Izvestiya announced the arrest of nine doctors, at least six of them Jewish. They were accused of poisoning Andrei Zhdanov and Alexander Shcherbakov, of plotting against Soviet marshals, and of working for American and British intelligence and "the interests of international Jewry." [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_doctors_plot] An American intelligence analysis written that summer read the affair the same way. A working paper of the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence, dated 14 July 1953, records that the announcement tied the doctors to a "Jewish-bourgeois nationalist group" backed by American and British intelligence, and notes the affair's "clearly antiSemitic overtones". It also records that the Soviet journal New Times, on 21 January, called Zionists and Israeli officials the "executors of a US State Department spy ring in the USSR." [cite:rs_cw1_cia_doctors_plot_1953] The charge had moved from Soviet-bloc defendants accused of Zionism, in Prague, to Zionists and Israeli officials as such, in Moscow.

The antisemitism in both cases should be named plainly, as the sources name it. It was the policy of named states and named organs: a Czechoslovak court, the Soviet official press, a Soviet journal. In Prague the condemned were mostly Jews and the charge was Zionism; in Moscow the official announcement invoked "international Jewry", and the CIA's own analysts called the overtones "clearly antiSemitic." [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_slansky] [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_doctors_plot] [cite:rs_cw1_cia_doctors_plot_1953] Israel's foreign minister said so in public. On 19 January 1953 Sharett condemned Soviet antisemitic and anti-democratic practices in the Knesset. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west]

Three weeks later the crisis turned violent. On 9 February 1953 a bomb of about 70 pounds of explosives went off in the garden of the Soviet legation in Tel Aviv. It injured one Soviet diplomat and the wives of two others. The attack was blamed on a group known as the Tzrifin Underground, whose leader, Yaakov Heruti, denied involvement; according to the Center for Israel Education, the government lacked proof. [cite:rs_cw1_israeled_legation] Israel apologized officially. Moscow broke relations anyway. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] According to the Jewish Chronicle's account, the Soviet Foreign Minister, Vyshinsky, summoned Israel's minister in Moscow, Samuel Eliashiv, at 1 a.m. and handed him a note demanding that the legation's diplomats "leave Moscow without delay", citing the "terrorist act". The Chronicle dates the break to 11 February 1953, and Shlaim gives the same day. [cite:rs_cw1_jc_soviet_break] [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The bombing gave Moscow its stated reason. Whether it was the cause, or the occasion for a break that the trials had already prepared, is part of the dispute below.

The rupture lasted five months, and it ended after a death in Moscow and a pledge from Jerusalem. Stalin died on 5 March 1953, before any trial of the doctors had been held. In April 1953 Pravda announced that the charges against them were false and that their confessions had been obtained by torture; the surviving doctors were exonerated. [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_doctors_plot] Relations were restored in July 1953, after Israel said it would not join any alliance against the Soviet Union. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The Center for Israel Education gives the date of the announcement as 21 July 1953. [cite:rs_cw1_israeled_legation] Restoration, in other words, followed a statement about alignment: Israel would not make its Western tilt into a military pact against Moscow. The pledge bore on a question Israel had pursued since 1951, its place in Western regional defence, told in [scene:cw1_mec|the section on the Middle East Command].

The dispute is about causation, and both positions are serious. The first was the view of the Israeli establishment at the time, and Sharett gave it a phrase: Soviet policy followed its own "developmental logic". On this reading, the Prague trial and the Doctors' Plot were products of Soviet internal politics in Stalin's last years, and Israel was swept into them by the identity of the accused and the needs of the prosecutors, not by anything it did. The strongest evidence for it is in the documents themselves: the doctors' confessions were later declared by Pravda itself to have been obtained by torture, Slánský was cleared of the criminal charges in 1963, and the doctors' case collapsed within weeks of Stalin's death, which points to the regime's internal dynamics rather than to Israeli conduct. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] [cite:rs_cw1_britannica_doctors_plot]

The second position, Bialer's, as Shlaim presents it, is that Soviet aid and goodwill had been tied to Israel's foreign-policy orientation more closely than Israeli leaders assumed, and that they cooled as Israel sided with the West, most visibly over Korea. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Its strongest evidence is the sequence before the trials: arms that ran on longer than state-building required, a cooler line on Jerusalem in December 1949, and a further shift after the Korean vote in 1950, all before Slánský's arrest. And the terms of restoration in July 1953, a pledge about alliances, suggest that Moscow cared about Israel's alignment, not only about the bombing it had cited. The two readings are not mutually exclusive. The trials could have had their own Soviet-bloc logic and still have been aimed at Israel more readily because Israel had already chosen a side.

Shlaim's own judgment, which is interpretation, is that the rift was "probably beyond repair", and that by the end of 1953 non-identification had been "replaced by an explicit pro-Western position." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] On his reading, the restoration of July 1953 repaired the formal relationship but not the alignment that had produced the break.

Several matters remain open on these sources. Who set off the bomb at the Soviet legation was not proved: the Tzrifin Underground was blamed, its leader denied it, and the government lacked proof. Whether Moscow would have broken relations without the bombing cannot be shown from the record cited here. The sources do not say what the Soviet government demanded in the talks that led to restoration beyond Israel's statement on alliances, nor what became of Mordecai Oren after the trial in which his confessions were used. And the weight to give each side of the causal dispute rests on interpretation of a Soviet decision-making process that these sources describe only from the outside.
