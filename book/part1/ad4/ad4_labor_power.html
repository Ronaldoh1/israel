Labour Power: Unions, PATCO and the Law That Stayed Still

A union is the mechanism the American system built in 1935 to let workers bargain as a group rather than one by one. Under the National Labor Relations Act, workers at a workplace vote on whether to be represented; if they win, the employer must bargain with the union for everyone in that unit. There is no general way to extend an agreement across a whole industry, so bargaining power stops at the edge of each organised firm, and in a 2017 comparison of 21 rich countries in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) the United States ranked last for the share of workers covered by collective agreements. [cite:us13_eg_bargaining] This section follows what happened to that mechanism in and after the hinge years: how many workers it still reaches, the strike that changed its use, and how the law that governs it is enforced in 2026.

Density: two measures, one direction

"Union density" is the share of workers who belong to a union. The consistent federal series begins in 1983, and it must not be spliced onto older estimates built from different surveys. In 1983, 20.1 per cent of wage and salary workers were union members. The private-sector rate was 16.8 per cent and the public-sector rate 36.7 per cent. [cite:ad4_bls_spotlight2016] By 2025, overall membership was 10.0 per cent, or 14.7 million people; the public sector stood at 32.9 per cent and the private sector at 5.9 per cent. [cite:us13_bls_union2025] The private-sector rate therefore fell by about two-thirds (5.9 divided by 16.8 is 0.35) while the public-sector rate barely moved. In numbers, private-sector members fell from 11.9 million in 1983 to 7.6 million in 2015, during years in which wage and salary employment grew from 88.3 million to 133.7 million. [cite:ad4_bls_spotlight2016] Before the federal series began, about one American worker in three was a union member in 1960, on a different survey basis. [cite:us13_eg_bargaining]

The contrast between sectors matters for interpreting the decline. If shrinking union membership were simply a change in what workers wanted, it would be odd for public employees, who bargain under separate state and federal laws, to keep their rate close to a third for four decades while private employees' rate collapsed. The record points instead to the conditions under which private workers organise and bargain. Economists still dispute how much weight to give to each of those conditions: the shift of jobs from factories to services, competition from imports, employer opposition and the law itself. What the record does settle is that the decline was concentrated where the National Labor Relations Act applies. [cite:ad4_bls_spotlight2016]

Why density matters to the Dream

The economists Henry Farber, Daniel Herbst, Ilyana Kuziemko and Suresh Naidu assembled survey data on union membership back to 1936. They found that American income inequality "has varied inversely with union density over the past 100 years", that the household union pay premium held between 10 and 20 log points (roughly 10 to 20 per cent) throughout, and, using the 1935 legalisation of unions and the wartime labour board as natural experiments, that "unions reduce inequality". [cite:ad4_qje_farber] At the mid-century peak, union households were less educated and more often nonwhite than others: the union contract was one of the main routes into the middle class for workers without college degrees. [cite:ad4_qje_farber] The raw pay difference persists today; full-time union members had median weekly earnings of $1,404 in 2025, against $1,174 for non-members, a comparison that does not adjust for occupation or industry. [cite:us13_bls_union2025]

PATCO, August 1981

The Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO) represented the federal employees who direct the country's air traffic. In June 1981 its negotiators reached a tentative agreement raising pay by as much as 11 per cent, which the members rejected; the union wanted higher pay, shorter hours and earlier retirement. PATCO had endorsed Ronald Reagan over Jimmy Carter in 1980. [cite:ad4_aba_patco] About 13,000 controllers walked out at 7 a.m. on Monday, 3 August 1981. Federal law prohibited strikes by federal employees. Shortly before 11 a.m., Reagan announced from the Rose Garden that strikers had 48 hours to return or be fired. [cite:ad4_reagan_patco] On 5 August he dismissed the 11,345 who stayed out, and the union was decertified, stripped of its legal right to represent the controllers, on 22 October 1981. [cite:ad4_aba_patco] Reagan's stated reason was that government services must not be interrupted: "Government cannot close down the assembly line." [cite:ad4_aba_patco]

PATCO's strike was illegal and the firings were lawful; that was the administration's position, and the law supported it. The significance for private-sector workers lies elsewhere. Since the Supreme Court's decision in NLRB v. Mackay Radio of 16 May 1938, a private employer facing an economic strike "is not bound to discharge those hired to fill the places of strikers": it may hire permanent replacements, so that strikers keep their legal status but may have no job to return to. [cite:ad4_mackay_1938] That rule was designed friction, part of the 1938 settlement, not a product of the 1980s. What changed after 1981 was how often large strikes happened. The BLS counts major work stoppages, those involving 1,000 or more workers. There were 424 in 1974, 235 in 1979, 187 in 1980 and 145 in 1981; there were 54 in 1985, 44 in 1990, 39 in 2000 and five in 2009, the lowest on record. [cite:ad4_bls_stoppages] The firings alone did not cause that fall, and the count had already been sliding before August 1981, but the strike stands at the turning point of the series.

Right-to-work

Section 14(b) of the National Labor Relations Act lets states ban "union-security" clauses, the contract terms that require every worker covered by a union contract to pay toward the cost of bargaining. In a right-to-work state, a worker covered by the contract can decline to pay while still receiving its wages and protections. Michigan became the first state since Indiana in 1965 to repeal such a law; the repeal took effect on 13 February 2024, earlier than the 30 March date first expected, because the legislature adjourned early. [cite:ad4_audit_bt_michigan] As of October 2026, 26 states have right-to-work laws; a New Hampshire bill to become the 27th stalled again in 2025. [cite:us13_littler_rtw] [cite:ad4_audit_nrtw_states] Supporters present these laws as protecting individual choice; unions argue that they let workers benefit without paying and so drain the resources needed to organise. Both are stated positions. The law's documented effect on density is disputed among economists, and this book does not resolve it.

Enforcement: a law without fines

Labour law is only as strong as its enforcement. An employer that fires a worker for organising breaks the law, but the National Labor Relations Act contains no civil penalties or punitive damages. A worker fired illegally is generally owed back pay, minus whatever the worker earned elsewhere in the meantime, and the usual remedies are orders to stop and notices posted at the workplace. [cite:ad4_epi_unlawful] In a 2019 study of elections supervised by the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) in 2016 and 2017, the EPI found that employers were charged with an unfair labour practice in 41.5 per cent of them, and with illegal firing in 19.9 to 29.6 per cent, depending on the definition. In units of more than 60 workers, the figure was 54.4 per cent. A charge is an allegation, not a finding of violation, as the authors note. They also estimated that employers spend nearly $340 million a year on consultants hired to defeat organising drives. [cite:ad4_epi_unlawful] The EPI is aligned with organised labour; the numbers come from NLRB case filings and Labor Department disclosures.

The Board in 2026

The agency that enforces the law was itself in question in 2025 and 2026. After the President removed member Gwynne Wilcox early in 2025, the Board's quorum was restored only on 7 January 2026, when James Murphy and Scott Mayer were sworn in; Crystal Carey became General Counsel the same day. [cite:ad4_butlersnow_nlrb] On 29 June 2026, in Trump v. Slaughter, the Supreme Court held by six votes to three that for-cause removal protection for Federal Trade Commission members was unconstitutional, overruling Humphrey's Executor of 1935. The law firm Proskauer judged it "highly likely" that the equivalent protection for NLRB members would fall as well, and that Wilcox's challenge to her removal was in effect resolved. [cite:ad4_proskauer_slaughter] On 7 August 2026 the Senate confirmed James Macy, a Republican, and David Prouty, a Democrat renominated for a second term, giving the Board four members and a three-to-one Republican majority, with one seat vacant, as of October 2026. [cite:ad4_ogletree_nlrb] A Board whose members can be removed at the President's will is a change in design, not only in personnel: it applies to whichever party holds the White House.

Where it works as promised

The bargain the 1935 law offered still works where it reaches. Union members earn more on the raw comparison, the household union premium has held at 10 to 20 per cent for nearly a century, and public-sector density has stayed near a third. [cite:us13_bls_union2025] [cite:ad4_qje_farber] [cite:ad4_bls_spotlight2016] Strikes have also returned from their low: there were 33 major stoppages in 2023, 31 in 2024 and 30 in 2025, several times the 2009 figure though far below the 1970s. [cite:ad4_bls_stoppages] The mechanism did not disappear. It shrank to a fraction of the private workforce, and the next section turns to what grew in the space it left: the claim of shareholders on what firms earn.
