A Map of the Record

Every stage of this book rests on documents: letters, contracts, votes, treaties, budgets, court records and the accounts of the people involved. Read in order, they tell a story. Laid out together, they form a network: people, organizations, governments and documents, each tied to the others by an act the record names. The companion network map of the Road to 1948 and After is that network drawn whole, from the Rothschild houses of the eighteenth century to the Cold War. Every node can be opened to show who it was and what it did, and every tie shows the sentence and the source that establish it.

The map holds only ties with a source. A tie is verified where the cited page states the act directly, and reported where it states it only indirectly or attributes it to someone else's account; reported ties count for a little over half as much. Names that merely appear together on a page are kept for searching but count for nothing. Ties are never drawn from who someone was, only from what the documents say they did. The same rule applies to every node, whatever its side.

Money counts twice. Every other channel depended on it: land was bought, arms were bought, and the organizations ran on dues and appeals. The record states the sums for the largest flows, and they are converted to 1948 dollars. Edmond de Rothschild was reported in 1934 to have "invested over sixty million dollars in Palestine," which at 1913 prices is about $146 million of 1948. [cite:f_jta_baron] The Keren Hayesod took in £4,714,440 between April 1921 and July 1932, about half of it from the United States. [cite:f_jta_kh33] The United Palestine Appeal's share of the American campaigns was $52 million in 1946 and $74.7 million in 1948. [cite:f_jta_uja46] [cite:f_jta_uja48] Golda Meyerson raised about $50 million on her American tour of early 1948. [cite:m_jwa_golda] Where a money tie gives no sum, it is counted at a nominal $1 million, an assumption the map discloses wherever it is used.

The other channels are weighed by connection: a tie between two well-connected nodes counts for more than a tie between two isolated ones, up to twice as much. In diplomacy and recognition a great power counts for more than a small state. The five permanent members of the UN Security Council are weighed by the size of their economies in 1950, in Angus Maddison's estimates: the United States at 1,455,916 million 1990 international dollars, the Soviet Union at 510,243, Britain at 347,850, China at 244,985 and France at 220,492. [cite:m_maddison] Relative to France, the United States counts 6.6 times, the Soviet Union 2.3, Britain 1.6 and China 1.1; every other state counts once.

With every tie in place the map reads each channel as complete, and the chance of a founding in 1948 as about 93 percent. That figure is a calibration, not a finding: it says only that on the full record the state was founded, with a margin left for what the record does not hold. Every test in this appendix is read against it.
