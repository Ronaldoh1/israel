Money Under Watch: the Euro Agreement and MONEYVAL

A claim runs through much popular writing on the Vatican: that its bank, the Institute for the Works of Religion (IOR), sits outside European banking rules and is "exempt" from the European Union's anti-money-laundering law, with no outside audit until 2013. The documents show something different. Vatican City State uses the euro under a treaty with the European Union that obliges it to apply the Union's anti-money-laundering acts, and since 2011 the Holy See has submitted, as a member, to the Council of Europe's anti-money-laundering evaluators, MONEYVAL, who have published two full evaluations of it and a series of progress and follow-up reports. What is true in the claim is narrower: the IOR's supervisor is a Vatican authority, not an Italian or EU one. [scene:vt9_ior|Part Nine: the IOR] This section sets out the agreement, the supervisor and the evaluations, in that order.

The Monetary Agreement of 2009

Vatican City's use of the euro first rested on an agreement of 29 December 2000 concluded with the European Community, represented by Italy. [cite:vt5_va_monetary] That agreement was replaced by the Monetary Agreement between the European Union and the Vatican City State, signed at Brussels on 17 December 2009 by Commissioner Joaquín Almunia and, for the Vatican City State represented by the Holy See, Archbishop André Dupuy; it entered into force on 1 January 2010. [cite:vt5_va_monetary] [scene:vt4_euro_tax_utilities|Part Four: the euro, taxes and utilities] The agreement makes the euro the city's official currency, bars it from issuing its own notes, coins or substitutes without EU consent, and caps the face value of the coins it may issue each year, with a fixed part (2.3 million euros for 2010) and a part tied to its population. [cite:vt5_va_monetary]

Article 8 is the clause that answers the "exemption" claim. It obliges Vatican City to adopt measures implementing the EU acts listed in an annex on euro notes and coins, on preventing money laundering, on fraud and counterfeiting of cash and non-cash means of payment, on medals and tokens, and on statistical reporting. [cite:vt5_va_monetary] The European Commission updates the annex every year. Its decision of 15 May 2025, for example, listed the Union's 2024 anti-money-laundering package, the Regulation 2024/1624, the Directive 2024/1640 and the Regulation 2024/1620 creating the new EU anti-money-laundering authority, with a deadline of 31 December 2028, and the 2023 regulation on information accompanying transfers of funds with a deadline of 31 December 2026; it also carries a section on entities carrying out financial activities on a professional basis. [cite:vt5_eurlex_2025_898] The deadlines were agreed in a joint committee of the two sides between 2013 and 2024. [cite:vt5_eurlex_2025_898] The Vatican is not an EU member and EU law does not apply in it directly; it applies there because the Vatican undertook by treaty to transpose it.

A supervisor of its own

The first Vatican anti-money-laundering law followed. On 30 December 2010 Benedict XVI established the Financial Information Authority (AIF) by motu proprio, as an institution connected to the Holy See. [cite:vt5_va_aif_profile] Francis widened its powers by a motu proprio of 8 August 2013 and Law XVIII of 8 October 2013, gave it new statutes on 15 November 2013, and gave it prudential supervision of financial activity, the IOR included. [cite:vt5_va_aif_profile] [cite:vt5_wiki_asif] On 5 December 2020 new statutes renamed it the Supervisory and Financial Information Authority (ASIF). [cite:vt5_wiki_asif] It was no rubber stamp in the eyes of its foreign counterparts either. After the Vatican Gendarmerie searched the offices of the Secretariat of State and of the AIF itself on 1 October 2019, in the inquiry into the London property deal, the Egmont Group of national financial intelligence units suspended the AIF on 13 November 2019, and lifted the suspension on 22 January 2020 only after the AIF gave assurances on the handling of shared intelligence, helped by a memorandum with the Vatican's Promoter of Justice. [cite:vt5_cwr_egmont2020] [scene:vt10_london_trial|Part Ten: the London trial]

The evaluations

The Holy See applied to MONEYVAL in 2011, after its first anti-money-laundering law. [cite:vt5_ncreg_aml2017] The first mutual evaluation report, adopted at MONEYVAL's plenary of 2 to 6 July 2012, rated 9 of the 16 core and key recommendations then in force compliant or largely compliant and 7 partially or non-compliant. [cite:vt5_mv_2012es] It found the AIF's role and independence unclear, noted that "no inspections have been undertaken" by it, and "strongly recommended that IOR is also supervised by a prudential supervisor"; it recorded that the 2010 law had been "wholly supplanted and replaced" by a decree of 25 January 2012, too recently for its effect to be assessed. [cite:vt5_mv_2012es] Progress reports followed in 2013, 2015 and 2017. The IOR's clean-up of its client files in this period closed about 4,600 accounts by the end of 2014 and about 4,800 by October 2015, and by late 2017 the AIF had sent 69 reports to the Promoter of Justice since 2013, none of which had yet reached a court. [cite:vt5_ncreg_aml2017] [cite:vt10_ansa_ior2015] [cite:vt10_ansa_aif2016] [scene:vt10_reform_aml|Part Ten: the account closures]

The second evaluation, after an on-site visit from 30 September to 13 October 2020, was adopted at MONEYVAL's plenary of 26 to 30 April 2021 and published in June. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] It rated the effectiveness of the system "substantial" in five of eleven areas and "moderate" in six, with none "low." [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] [cite:vt5_or_barbagallo2021] Its criticisms were specific. The risk assessment underrated the threat from insiders at middle and senior level; the Promoter of Justice's office lacked staff for complex financial cases and investigations had been protracted; sanctions in money-laundering cases appeared minimal; and a large gap separated sums frozen or seized from sums confiscated. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] By the end of the visit there had been two convictions for laundering one's own proceeds, in 2018 and 2019, with a third on 21 January 2021, and confiscation orders of about 1.3 million euros. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] The standard on correspondent banking, the IOR's links to foreign banks, was rated non-compliant. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] The Holy See said it "welcomes the Moneyval Report" and "renews its commitment to continue working towards full compliance." [cite:vt5_pv_moneyval2021]

A follow-up report adopted at MONEYVAL's plenary of 20 to 24 May 2024 raised the correspondent-banking rating from non-compliant to compliant and two others, on wire transfers and on the transparency of legal persons, from partially to largely compliant. [cite:vt5_fatf_fur2024] [cite:vt5_pv_moneyval2024] The next assessment of technical compliance was set for about four years later, in MONEYVAL's sixth round. [cite:vt5_pv_moneyval2024] ASIF's report for 2024 recorded 79 suspicious-activity reports, down from 123 in 2023, and 11 referrals to the Promoter of Justice. [cite:vt5_ewtn_asif2025] No later MONEYVAL report on the Holy See was found as of October 2026.

What the checks reach

The financial checks reach further than the treaty bodies in one respect and less far in another. They reach further because they are tied to things the Vatican uses every day, the euro and its links to foreign banks; the 2012 warning on IOR supervision was followed within a year by prudential supervision, and the correspondent-banking rating was repaired by 2024. [cite:vt5_mv_2012es] [cite:vt5_fatf_fur2024] They reach less far because they test systems, not individual decisions: MONEYVAL rates laws, supervisors and prosecution records, and it was the Vatican's own prosecutors and tribunal, not the evaluators, who brought the London property case to trial. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] [scene:vt10_reform_aml|Part Ten: reform and anti-money-laundering] The record therefore corrects both the claim of exemption and the claim of no accountability, without supporting the opposite claim that the Vatican's money is now fully transparent; the evaluators' own findings on insider risk, prosecution and confiscation say otherwise.
