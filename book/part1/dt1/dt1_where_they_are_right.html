Where They Are Right

Thiel, Yarvin and Land describe a government that most Americans no longer trust, that answers some citizens more readily than others, and in which money buys influence. On each point the record gives them substantial support. This section sets it out, using the same evidence the companion volume weighs in its fuller [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|steelman of the diagnosis], and then states the question the rest of this book asks of it.

Trust has collapsed

When the American National Election Studies first asked in 1958 how often people trusted the federal government to do what is right, 73 per cent said always or most of the time; in 1964 the figure was 77 per cent. In the Pew Research Center's survey completed on 28 September 2025 it was 17 per cent, with Republicans at 26 per cent and Democrats at 9. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] Gallup's average confidence across nine institutions it has tracked since 1979 fell from 48 per cent in that year to 28 per cent in July 2025. [cite:de7_gallup_conf2025]

The public's verdict on the system as a whole is similar. In a Pew survey taken from 23 to 29 March 2026, 69 per cent of adults said they were dissatisfied with the way democracy was working in the United States, including 86 per cent of Democrats and 51 per cent of Republicans. Asked whether American democracy is a good example for other countries, 68 per cent said it "used to be" but has not been in recent years; 24 per cent said it still is, and 7 per cent said it never was. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

The measuring instruments agree

The organisations that grade the world's political systems have reached a similar verdict by different methods. The Economist Intelligence Unit, the research arm of the Economist Group, scores countries from 0 to 10 and calls those at 8 or above "full democracies". It moved the United States into the "flawed democracy" category in its index for 2016, when the score fell from 8.05 to 7.98. The cause it named was not that year's election but "further erosion of trust in government and elected officials"; even without the election, it said, the score "would have slipped below 8.00." [cite:dt1_eiu2016] By 2025 the score was 7.65, the lowest since the index began in 2006. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

The V-Dem Institute, which builds its scores from expert assessments, downgraded the United States in its 2026 report from "liberal democracy" to "electoral democracy" for the first time in more than fifty years. Its liberal democracy index fell from 0.75 in 2024 to 0.57 in 2025, the lowest since 1965 and the lowest in the G7, and it recorded the constraints that the legislature places on the executive at their weakest in over a century. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] [cite:us3_vdem2026] Freedom House scored the country 81 out of 100 for 2025, down from 84, and below 90 every year since 2016; in 2010 the score had been 94. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] [cite:us3_fh_fiw2021] [cite:us3_fh_us2026] The Polity project, which scores regimes from −10 to +10, rated the country +5 in 2020 against +10 in 2015. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

Every index measures democratic qualities: free and fair elections, equal political rights, limits on executive power, a free press. When the United States scores lower, the indices are recording less of those qualities, not too much of them. The critics read the same decline as proof that democracy has failed. The instruments read it as evidence that the country has become less democratic. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

Responsiveness: who government listens to

The most cited evidence that ordinary voters do not steer policy comes from the political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page. Matching 1,779 policy questions asked in surveys between 1981 and 2002 against what government later did, they found in 2014 that average citizens had little or no independent influence once the preferences of the affluent and organised interests were taken into account; business groups carried roughly twice the weight of mass-membership groups. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] The finding has been contested. Peter Enns showed that middle-income and affluent Americans want the same things most of the time, and Omar Bashir found that strongly favoured changes happened 47 per cent of the time for the median-income group against 52 per cent for the affluent. [cite:ad15_enns] [cite:ad15_bashir] Gilens and Page replied that none of the critiques undermined their central claim. [cite:de7_gp_reply] What survives the exchange is narrower than the headline but real: where the affluent and everyone else disagree, the affluent have an edge.

Money

On money, the public and the critics agree. In a Pew survey in July 2023, 72 per cent of Americans said there should be limits on how much individuals and organisations can spend on campaigns, 80 per cent said donors have too much influence on members of Congress, and 70 per cent said the people who live in a representative's district have too little. [cite:dt1_pew_money2023] On 21 January 2010, in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the Supreme Court struck down limits on independent spending by corporations and unions by 5–4, while upholding disclosure requirements by 8–1. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] In 2024 roughly 44 per cent of the money raised to support Donald Trump came from ten donors; for Kamala Harris the top ten gave nearly 8 per cent. [cite:ad15_brennan_cu15] Land's "vote buying" overstates that record, since a donation does not prove a purchase, but the concentration it points at is documented. [cite:de7_land_de] [cite:de7_os_donors2024]

The book's question

The diagnosis, then, is largely right about the symptoms. The question is what they are symptoms of. The American system began as a republic with a narrow electorate and indirect choices, and it has been made more democratic in steps. The Fifteenth Amendment of 1870 extended the vote to men of all races; the Seventeenth, in 1913, gave voters the direct election of senators, who had been chosen by state legislatures; the Nineteenth, in 1920, barred the states from denying the vote on account of sex; the Twenty-fourth, in 1964, abolished the poll tax in federal elections; and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 secured the vote for adult citizens of all races in practice. [cite:dt1_archives_voting] Other features have stayed fixed or moved the other way: the Electoral College still chooses presidents, and the 2010 ruling widened the room for unlimited independent spending. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] [cite:ad15_lii_cu]

Two readings of this history are possible. On the critics' reading, wider participation is part of the problem, which is close to what Thiel argued in 2009 about the franchise of 1920. On this book's reading, the widenings are unfinished, and the remaining departures from equal political weight, in how presidents are chosen, how candidates and districts are selected, and how campaigns are paid for, are where the symptoms concentrate. The next six parts test the second reading point by point. If it is wrong, the reforms will not touch the symptoms, and the critics' case gains ground.

The test

The test asks whether the claim that democracy has failed survives this part's facts. Documented: the symptoms the critics describe are real. Trust in the federal government is at 17 per cent, majorities are dissatisfied with how democracy works, and four separate indices have downgraded the United States. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] Documented: the indices that record the decline measure democratic qualities, and they record fewer of them; they do not show that the country suffers from too much democracy. Probable, in its narrow form: that policy answers the affluent more readily than others when they disagree; the stronger claim that ordinary voters count for nothing is contested by the studies above. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] [cite:ad15_bashir] [cite:de7_branham_ifs] Not supported: that the symptoms prove democracy as such has failed, since nothing in this part separates the effects of democracy from the effects of the features that limit it.

The record also cuts against this book's thesis in three places. Trust in the federal government reached its recorded peak in 1964, the year before the Voting Rights Act, and has never returned there, although the system has since become more democratic in law; that timing proves nothing about cause, but it means that more democracy has not, so far, brought trust back. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] [cite:dt1_archives_voting] The index that first called the United States a flawed democracy blamed falling trust, not the Electoral College or money. [cite:dt1_eiu2016] And judgments of the system track party: the share of Republicans calling American democracy a good example rose from 17 per cent in 2024 to 38 per cent in 2026, while the Democratic share fell from 22 to 12, so public verdicts on whether democracy is working are partly verdicts on who holds power. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] Those findings do not settle the question. They show that it has to be answered mechanism by mechanism, beginning with the one that chooses the president.
