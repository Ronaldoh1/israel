A Secular Movement

Political Zionism was founded at the First Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1897, which adopted a programme for "a home in Palestine secured under public law". [cite:zionism_founding_1897] Its founder, Theodor Herzl, was an assimilated Viennese journalist, and his pamphlet of 1896, The Jewish State, made a political argument: that the Jews were a nation and needed a state of their own. [cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] [cite:w_herzl] David Ben-Gurion, who declared the state in 1948, was a socialist who did not keep the religious commandments. [cite:w_bengurion]

Much of religious Judaism opposed the project from the start. Orthodox rabbis held that the return to Zion could come only through divine redemption, and that forcing it by human politics was a transgression. [cite:neturei_karta] The line runs to the Satmar rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, who argued in 1961 that Zionism is incompatible with Judaism, and to Neturei Karta, which rejects the state outright. [cite:fw_wiki_vayoel] [cite:fw_wiki_nk]

Why the Holy Land

The movement weighed other territories. In 1903 Britain offered land in East Africa; the Zionist Congress rejected it in 1905 because it was not Zion. [cite:a_jvl_uganda] [cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] The network map of the Road to 1948 tests eight alternatives to Palestine, from Argentina to Suriname, and finds that none had better than about a 2 percent chance of becoming a state by 1948. Without the religious pull of the Holy Land on Jews and Christians, there was no draw for the money, the settlers and the powers that a state required. A secular movement made its claim on a sacred map.
