Poverty Over Time: Two Official Measures

The most quoted comparison of the two safety nets sets an American poverty rate of about 10 per cent beside an Israeli rate of about 21 per cent. Both numbers are official and both are current. They do not measure the same thing. The United States counts people below a fixed income threshold; Israel counts people below half the typical income in their own country. This section sets out each country's own series over time, then the one comparison made on a shared definition, and then what each series shows about who is poor. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] [cite:us18_nii_pov2018]

Two definitions

Israel's National Insurance Institute (NII) uses a relative line: a person is poor if the household's disposable income, after taxes and transfers and adjusted for household size, is below 50 per cent of the median. [cite:us18_nii_pov2018] In 2024 that line was NIS 3,547 a month per standard person, NIS 7,095 for a couple and NIS 13,303 for a couple with three children. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024] Because the line rises with median income, the rate falls only when the incomes of the poor rise faster than the middle.

The United States has two official measures. The official poverty measure compares cash income with fixed thresholds updated for prices: in 2025, $16,749 for a single person under 65 and $32,649 for two adults with two children. The Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM) also counts refundable tax credits and non-cash benefits such as food assistance (SNAP). [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] Neither is a relative measure, so neither is directly comparable with the NII's.

The United States, 1959–2025

On the official measure, 22.4 per cent of Americans were poor in 1959. The rate fell to 11.1 per cent by 1973, then rose and fell with the business cycle: 13.0 per cent in 1980, 15.2 per cent in 1983, 13.5 per cent in 1990, 15.1 per cent in 1993, 11.3 per cent in 2000, 15.1 per cent again in 2010 after the financial crisis, and 10.5 per cent in 2019. [cite:us18_epi_povtrend] In 2025 it fell to 10.2 per cent, 34.5 million people, which Stateline reported as the lowest on record; the Census Bureau called child poverty, at 13.4 per cent, a historic low. [cite:us18_census_p60290] [cite:us18_njm_poverty] The 2024 figure appears as 10.6 per cent in the historical table and as 10.7 per cent in the Census Bureau's 2025 report (a fall of 0.5 points to 10.2) and the Congressional Research Service's comparison; the difference is unexplained in the sources this book used. [cite:us18_epi_povtrend] [cite:us18_census_p60290] [cite:us18_crs_pov2025]

The SPM, which counts what government programmes add, stood at 13.1 per cent in 2025, not statistically different from 2024. [cite:us18_census_p60290] It shows what transfers do: Social Security alone lifted 28.8 million people above the SPM line in 2025. [cite:us18_census_p60290] It also showed the effect of the 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit, when child poverty on the SPM fell to 5.2 per cent before rising to 12.4 per cent in 2022 after the credit lapsed. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] The 2025 figures predate most of the cuts to Medicaid and food assistance in the tax and spending law Congress passed in 2025. Experts cited, without names, by Stateline expected "grimmer statistics" in next year's report, and the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, which Stateline describes as left-leaning, counted 1.2 million fewer children receiving SNAP in July 2026 than a year earlier. [cite:us18_njm_poverty]

Israel, 2004–2024

The NII's own reports break Israel's series in 2011–12, when the survey changed from an income survey to an expenditure survey, so figures before and after are not strictly comparable. [cite:us18_nii_pov2018] On the earlier basis, 23.6 per cent of people and 33.2 per cent of children were poor in 2004, and 24.5 per cent of people and 35.8 per cent of children in 2006, after the cuts to child allowances and income support of 2002–2004. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007] On the later basis the person rate was about 21.9 per cent in 2010 and 22.3 per cent in 2015, 21.2 per cent in 2018, 20.1 per cent in 2020 (when emergency pandemic grants cut poverty sharply), 20.8 per cent in 2022 and 20.7 per cent in 2024. [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] [cite:us18_nii_pov2018] [cite:us18_nii_pov2024] Child poverty was 28 per cent in 2024, about 880,000 children. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024]

The one shared yardstick

The NII publishes its own figures recalculated on the OECD's equivalence scale, which allows a comparison with other countries on one relative definition. For 2023 it put Israel's person poverty rate at 19.5 per cent against an OECD average of 11.6 per cent, and its child poverty rate at 23.4 per cent against 12.4 per cent; on both measures Israel ranked second-highest, after Costa Rica. For people aged 66 and over the gap is smaller: 17.8 per cent against 15.1 per cent. [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] The American figure on the same OECD basis appears in the NII report only in a chart this book could not read, so it is not given here. What the shared yardstick does establish is that the common pairing of an American 10 per cent with an Israeli 21 per cent sets a fixed-threshold measure beside a relative one, and so does not measure the gap between the two countries.

What transfers do, and who is poor

Each country's transfers do much of the work. Without NII benefits, 31.5 per cent of Israelis and 34.5 per cent of families would have been poor in 2024, against 20.7 and 20.0 per cent after them. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024] Taxes and transfers together cut person poverty by 36.0 per cent in 2010, 30.1 per cent in 2019, 41.1 per cent in 2020 during the pandemic grants and 33.5 per cent in 2023. [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] Old-age and survivors' pensions do most of it, reducing family poverty by 24.2 per cent in 2023; income support, the NII's last-resort benefit, reduced it by only 1.3 per cent. [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] The erosion of that last-resort layer shows in the NII's own numbers: income support plus child allowances for a couple with two children fell from about 71 per cent of the poverty line in 2002 to 48.9 per cent in 2006. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007]

Older people show the clearest change in Israel. In the mid-2000s about one in five elderly Israelis was poor on disposable income, higher than in any of 21 OECD countries examined. [cite:us18_taub_elderly] The NII's figure for the elderly was 25.1 per cent in 2004 and 18.8 per cent in 2018; its current reports give 12.8 per cent for 2022 and 2023, though the revisions to the series between those reports mean the fall is partly one of method. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007] [cite:us18_nii_pov2018] [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] The NII still counted 158,700 poor older people in 2024 and put their rate above the OECD average. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024]

Poverty in Israel is concentrated in identifiable places and groups, and the units of measure need care. In 2024, 37.6 per cent of Arab families and 32.8 per cent of Haredi families were poor; these are family rates, to be set beside the national family rate of 20.0 per cent, not the person rate of 20.7 per cent. Jerusalem district had a family poverty rate of 36.5 per cent. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024] Part Eight sets out the mechanisms behind these gaps: labour-market access, schooling streams, planning law and the exemptions and stipends tied to Haredi study. [scene:us8_israel|Part Eight: Israel's equality record] In the United States the official rate among full-time, year-round workers was 1.6 per cent in 2025, against 10.2 per cent overall; Part Eight sets out the racial gaps. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] [scene:us8_us|Part Eight: the US record] Food insecurity, a different measure, touched 28.1 per cent of Israelis in 2024, about a million of them children. [cite:us18_nii_pov2024]
