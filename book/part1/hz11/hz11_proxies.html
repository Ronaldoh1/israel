Armed by Israel

Israel's leaders describe Hezbollah, the Houthis and the Iraqi militias as Iran's proxies: armed groups that a state pays, arms and uses to fight where it does not want to fight itself. Part Ten examined how far that description fits each group. Three are set out here. A fourth, Israel's licensing of the Islamist charity network in Gaza from which Hamas later grew, is in the Hamas volume. The South Lebanon Army, 1976–2000

The force that became the South Lebanon Army began in 1976 as a breakaway Lebanese army unit under Major Saad Haddad, which Israel supported through its "Good Fence" policy on the border; in April 1979 Haddad proclaimed a "State of Free Lebanon" from the Israeli border town of Metula, and the Lebanese government dismissed him as a traitor. [cite:hz1_olj_sla] [cite:hz1_wiki_freeleb] Haddad said in 1982 that Israel had contributed about $10 million to his men's salaries since 1978, at about $100 a month each, and supplied weapons and services. [cite:hz1_csm_haddad] After Israel's partial withdrawal in 1985 it formalised the arrangement. In the words of the US Congressional Research Service, Israel established the SLA as "an allied militia that it fully funded and equipped": 2,500 to 3,000 fighters, paid $500 to $800 a month according to media reports, at an estimated cost of $35 million a year, with obsolete tanks and artillery. Most of its commanders were Christian, but by the reported figures about half its members were not; Human Rights Watch reported involuntary conscription, including of children. [cite:hz4_crs_sla]

The SLA ran the Khiam detention centre inside the occupied zone. In a September 1999 affidavit to Israel's High Court, the defence ministry said Khiam was "under the responsibility" of the SLA and its staff were Lebanese, while acknowledging that Shin Bet personnel met SLA interrogators several times a year and gave them "professional guidance and training." Human Rights Watch held Israel, as occupying power, ultimately responsible for abuses there, including torture and detention without charge. [cite:hz4_hrw_khiam99] When Prime Minister Ehud Barak ordered the withdrawal on 23 May 2000, the SLA abandoned its posts rather than fight Hezbollah alone; Hezbollah captured its heavy weapons, and about 1,500 members fled to Israel with their families, some 6,400 to 7,000 people. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] Part Four tells that story in full. [scene:hz4_zone|The security zone]

Syrian rebels on the Golan, 2013–2018

In September 2018 Foreign Policy reported, from interviews with more than two dozen rebel commanders and fighters, that Israel had secretly armed and funded at least twelve rebel groups in southern Syria from 2013 until July 2018. Fighters were paid about $75 a month, and the groups received money to buy weapons on the black market; Israel supplied assault rifles, machine guns, mortar launchers and vehicles through three gates on the occupied Golan Heights. The early weapons were mostly American M16s; later ones included arms from an Iranian shipment to Hezbollah that Israel had seized in 2009. The stated purpose, as the fighters understood it, was to keep Iran-backed forces and the Islamic State away from Israel's border. Israel's embassy in Washington declined to comment. [cite:hz11_fp_rebels] In January 2019 the outgoing chief of staff, Gadi Eisenkot, told the Sunday Times that Israel had supplied the rebels with light arms "for self-defense"; until then Israel had acknowledged only humanitarian aid. [cite:hz11_toi_eisenkot]

The programme ended when the Syrian government, backed by Russia, retook the south in the summer of 2018 and Israel did not intervene. "This is a lesson we will not forget about Israel," one fighter told the magazine. [cite:hz11_fp_rebels] What followed in Syria after the fall of Assad in December 2024 is in Part Ten. [scene:hz10_syria|Syria]

Gaza's militias, 2024–2026

In October 2024 an internal UN memo, obtained by The Washington Post, said armed gangs looting aid convoys in Gaza "may be benefitting from a passive if not active benevolence" or "protection" from the Israeli army, and that one gang leader had set up a "military-like compound" in an area "restricted, controlled and patrolled by the IDF." [cite:hz11_aj_unmemo] On 5 June 2025 the opposition politician Avigdor Lieberman, a former defence minister, disclosed that the government was arming clans in Gaza and said it was giving weapons to "a group of criminals and felons identified with Islamic State"; NPR could not substantiate the Islamic State claim. Israeli media identified one recipient as the gang of Yasser Abu Shabab, whom other Palestinians accused of looting UN convoys. [cite:hz11_npr_clans] Netanyahu confirmed the policy the same day: "Liberman leaked that on security officials' advice, we activated clans in Gaza who oppose Hamas. What's wrong with that? It only saves IDF soldiers' lives." He called the disclosure "a criminal leak." [cite:hz11_ynet_clans]

Abu Shabab's group, the Popular Forces, operated in eastern Rafah under Israeli control. He was killed on 4 December 2025 in a clash with members of a local family; accounts conflict, the group denied that Hamas was responsible, and his deputy, Ghassan Duhine, took over. [cite:hz11_na_abushabab] [cite:hz11_wiki_abushabab] By April 2026 ACLED counted at least five anti-Hamas armed groups operating from Israeli-held areas east of the "Yellow Line," involved in more than 40 violent events and nearly 80 deaths since the October 2025 ceasefire, including the abduction and killing of civilians accused of working with Hamas. ACLED assessed that Israel supplied them with weapons, logistics, intelligence and training and had used drones in their clashes; Israel has not detailed its support beyond Netanyahu's statement. Hamas cited that support when it rejected a full-disarmament proposal. ACLED judged that the groups posed no existential threat to Hamas on their own. [cite:hz11_acled_gaza]

What These Cases Show, and What They Do Not

They show, first, that the practice Israel's leaders describe in Iran is one Israel has used itself. A state paying an armed group's salaries, supplying its weapons and relying on it to fight where its own soldiers would otherwise go is how Hezbollah's patron is documented to operate: Hassan Nasrallah said in 2016 that Hezbollah's budget, "its weapons and rockets, come from the Islamic Republic of Iran," and the US State Department estimated that support at about $700 million a year. [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018] By its own records and statements, Israel did the same in south Lebanon for a quarter of a century, in southern Syria for five years and in Gaza from at least 2025. The difference in scale is large: Iran built standing armies, while Israel's clients were local forces. The mechanism is the same one. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [cite:hz11_toi_eisenkot] [cite:hz11_ynet_clans]

They show, second, a recurring outcome. None of the three created lasting security. The SLA dissolved in two days and its heavy weapons passed to Hezbollah; the Syrian groups were left to the government Israel had armed them against; the Gaza groups, by ACLED's assessment, could not displace Hamas. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [cite:hz11_fp_rebels] [cite:hz11_acled_gaza] The occupation the SLA helped hold is the grievance Hezbollah has named as the reason it exists, as Part Three sets out. [scene:hz3_question|Would Hezbollah exist?]

They do not show that Israel created, armed or funded Hezbollah, the Houthis or the Iraqi militias. The public record examined for this book contains no such case. Nor do they show, on their own, that these programmes were designed to keep wars going. In each case the stated reason was immediate and military: to hold a border zone, to keep hostile forces back, to spare soldiers. That the instruments failed and the wars continued is a documented pattern; that the failure was the purpose would be an inference about motive, and the record does not supply it.
