The Separation of Transjordan and the Claim That "Jordan Is Palestine"

At Cairo in March 1921 Britain chose Abdullah to rule Transjordan. [cite:w_cairo1921] That was the first of four steps by which the territory was separated from the rest of the Mandate: Abdullah's installation in March 1921; the Churchill White Paper of June 1922; the League Council's approval of a British memorandum on 16 September 1922; and completion by 1923. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] [cite:rs_md_transjordan] [cite:wiki_borders_of_israel] The legal instrument was a clause written into the Mandate itself. Article 25 of the Mandate for Palestine let the Mandatory, with the consent of the League Council, postpone or withhold the application of the Mandate's provisions in the territories lying between the Jordan and Palestine's eastern boundary, provided nothing was done in conflict with Articles 15, 16 and 18. [cite:rs_md_avalon] The British government had formulated this arrangement in 1921: Transjordan would fall within the Mandate's scope, but be exempted from the provisions dealing specifically with the Jewish national home. [cite:wiki_borders_of_israel] [cite:wiki_transjordan_memorandum] How Abdullah came to his throne belongs to the story of [scene:me_s05|the Cairo Conference]; this section follows the legal instrument and what was later built on it.

The memorandum of September 1922. The formal act came less than two months after the Mandate text was approved. On 16 September 1922 the Council of the League of Nations approved a British memorandum, submitted under Article 25, declaring that a list of the Mandate's provisions "are not applicable to the territory known as Transjordan." [cite:rs_md_transjordan] The list is precise. It excluded the second and third recitals of the Preamble; part of Article 2; Articles 4 and 6 in full; the part of Article 7 dealing with Palestinian citizenship for Jews; part of Article 11; and Articles 13, 14, 22 and 23. [cite:rs_md_transjordan] In the same document Britain accepted "full responsibility as Mandatory for Transjordan." [cite:rs_md_transjordan]

Read against the Mandate's own articles, the exclusions took in the main national-home provisions. Article 4 had created the recognized Jewish agency; Article 6 required the Administration to facilitate Jewish immigration and encourage close settlement by Jews on the land; Article 2 carried the Mandatory's responsibility for securing the national home; Article 11 allowed arrangements with the Jewish agency for public works. [cite:rs_md_avalon] East of the river, none of that applied. What remained was British responsibility as Mandatory over a territory under Abdullah's rule. The full reading of the Mandate's articles is in [scene:s07|the Mandate text].

Some accounts associate the separation with the Churchill White Paper of June 1922, describing it as the decision by which British Mandate authorities divided Transjordan, the territory that would become the modern state of Jordan, from the part of the Mandate designated for Jewish settlement. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] The League document places the formal step in September, under Article 25. The two fit as successive stages of one policy: the British policy paper of June 1922, then the League's approval in September of the instrument that gave it legal effect, with the separation complete by 1923. Neither was the whole of it. The White Paper's other terms are treated in [scene:s06a|the Churchill White Paper].

Who could act, and who could not. The mechanism concentrated the decision in two places. The Mandatory proposed; the League Council consented. [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_md_transjordan] The separation, roughly three-quarters of the Mandate's land, was carried out by British administrative decision, not by war and not through negotiation with the Zionist leadership. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] On that account the Zionist Organization, the body Article 4 recognized as the Jewish agency, was not a party to the act that switched Article 4 off east of the river.

The Revisionist answer: two banks of the Jordan. The decision produced a political movement defined in part by its refusal to accept it. Jabotinsky founded Revisionist Zionism in the early 1920s, explicitly rejecting the 1922 decision to separate Transjordan from the territory available for Jewish settlement, and made "a Hebrew majority in the Land of Israel on both banks of the Jordan River" a core part of the movement's program. [cite:sunnafiles_jordan] The grievance had a precise origin: a specific, dated British administrative act, not a vague sense of loss.

The program was carried in song and in symbols. While visiting Paris in 1929, Jabotinsky wrote "The East Bank of the Jordan," whose refrain, "the Jordan has two banks, this one is ours and that one too," became an anthem of Betar, the Revisionist youth movement. [cite:jabotinsky_org_etzel] [cite:sunnafiles_jordan] Betar, which traced its beginnings to Riga in the 1920s, grew to more than 70,000 members by the mid-1930s. [cite:grokipedia_betar] The Irgun, a Revisionist paramilitary organization active from 1931 to 1948, adopted in the late 1930s an emblem showing a map of the whole Mandate territory, both banks of the Jordan included, with a hand holding a rifle superimposed on it and the Hebrew words "Rak Kach," "Only Thus." [cite:wiki_irgun] One reading treats the emblem's design as a statement about method as well as territory: paired with the rifle, the slogan said the territorial claim would be achieved by force rather than negotiation, consistent with Jabotinsky's "Iron Wall" doctrine, which held that Arab opposition could be overcome only through unyielding strength. [cite:peoplesworld_greater_israel] The emblem's visual content and slogan are corroborated across several independent sources; what they document is the position of a historical organization, not the policy of any later Israeli government. [cite:wiki_irgun]

The lineage did not end in 1948. Betar is the direct ideological ancestor of Israel's Likud party, itself a Revisionist successor organization. [cite:grokipedia_betar] In 2023 the politician Smotrich, whose Religious Zionism party sits further to the right within the same broad tradition, delivered a both-banks statement in Paris with a map of that territory displayed, a visual echo of the Irgun emblem roughly ninety years later. [cite:grokipedia_betar] [cite:wiki_irgun] Ninety-four years separated the two Paris moments, Jabotinsky's song and Smotrich's speech. Both places and dates are documented; nothing in the record connects the two beyond coincidence, and no source suggests Smotrich chose Paris to echo Jabotinsky. No source suggests Smotrich chose the city to echo Jabotinsky, and this book does not suggest it either. The coincidence matters only as evidence of how deeply the idea is embedded in the movement's own history. [cite:jabotinsky_org_etzel]

"Jordan is already Palestine." From the separation grew a second argument, the reverse of the Revisionist one. The claim runs that the 1921 partition of Mandatory Palestine already gave the Arabs their homeland, so that Jordan is in effect the Palestinian state and a second one would be redundant. It is often treated as a purely pro-Israel argument. The documented record is more complicated, because Jordan's own rulers made versions of the same claim, for their own reasons, before Jordan's government formally reversed course.

In 1948 King Abdullah I declared that "Palestine and Jordan are one, for Palestine is the coastline and Transjordan the hinterland of the same country." [cite:meforum_jordan_west_bank] In 1959 the Jordanian prime minister Hazza Majali stated: "We are the government of Palestine, the army of Palestine, and the refugees of Palestine." [cite:meforum_jordan_west_bank] King Hussein made comparable statements at several points. [cite:meforum_jordan_west_bank] These were not concessions to Israeli arguments. They were Jordanian claims, made to support the Hashemite Kingdom's annexation of the West Bank and its influence over Palestinian politics. [cite:meforum_jordan_west_bank] They show that the idea did not originate as an outside talking point; they do not show that it was ever permanent or unanimous Jordanian policy.

The reversal was formal and dated. On 31 July 1988 King Hussein announced the dissolution of Jordan's legal and administrative ties with the West Bank. [cite:springer_jordan_palestine_history] With that act Jordan's own government renounced any continuing claim to represent or administer Palestinian territory or population, the opposite direction from the claims of 1948 and 1959. The record cited here documents the act and its completeness; it does not set out every strategic consideration behind its timing, or the regional politics of 1988 around it.

The present position is on record from Jordan's head of state. Asked in a televised interview whether Jordan should be considered the de facto Palestinian state, King Abdullah II answered: "Jordan is Jordan." [cite:davidsinger_jordan_palestinian_state] A large share of Jordan's population is of Palestinian origin, from the displacements of 1948 and 1967. [cite:davidsinger_jordan_palestinian_state] That demographic fact is real and remains a significant factor in Jordanian society and politics. It does not change the government's position that this population is not a separate Palestinian polity within Jordan's borders.

The dispute. Two arguments have been built on the same administrative fact, and they point in opposite directions. The Revisionist argument treated the separation as a loss to be undone: the national home had been promised a territory, and three-quarters of it had been removed by a British decision. The "Jordan is Palestine" argument treats the same separation as a settlement already made: the Arabs of the Mandate received the larger share in 1921–23, so the Palestinian question was answered then. The historian Bernard Wasserstein has documented that, in later ideological use, the separation came to be called "the myth of Palestine's first partition." [cite:wiki_borders_of_israel] His phrase does not deny the administrative fact, which is documented. It flags the gap between that fact and the political conclusion later drawn from it.

The strongest form of the case for the claim rests on the documents of 1921–23: the national-home provisions were written for the whole Mandate, Article 25 withdrew them east of the river, and Arab rule was established there. The strongest form of the opposing case rests on the documents of 1988 and after: whatever weight the 1921 separation carries, a state's sovereign status is not conferred by outside argument, and Jordan's own government has formally, explicitly and repeatedly rejected the role of Palestinian state since 1988, most recently in its king's words. That Jordanian monarchs once made the claim themselves complicates any reading of it as a one-sided talking point. That Jordan then withdrew it, formally, is the documented end point of that history.

What the record leaves open. The material here does not say why Britain chose 1921 for the Article 25 formula, or record the reactions of Transjordan's own population to the memorandum. It does not set out the full reasoning behind Jordan's 1988 timing. The administrative history of 1921–23 is settled; its political meaning has been asserted and rejected by different parties at different times for different reasons, and it remains contested.
