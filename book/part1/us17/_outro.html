Part Seventeen established that the two systems spend a similar share of national income on education, about 6 per cent of GDP, but that the United States spent almost twice as much per student, $19,973 against $10,279 in purchasing-power dollars, because Israel has far more children to teach. [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_usa] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_isr] [cite:us17_toi_eag2024] Each system's gaps follow its structure: American spending per pupil ranged from $11,060 in Idaho to $31,918 in New York in 2024, while Israel funds Arab schools at about 12 per cent less per pupil than Hebrew state-religious schools and funds Haredi schools that, by an Israel Democracy Institute study, largely do not teach the core curriculum and are not made to. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] [cite:us17_taub_primary2025] [cite:us17_toi_haredi_core] On PISA, Israel's scores rose to 2012 and then fell, sharply in 2025, to 436 in reading and 433 in mathematics, while American scores held near or above the OECD average with the widest gap between strong and weak readers of any country tested. [cite:us17_wiki_pisa] [cite:us17_ih_pisa2025] [cite:us17_edweek_pisa2025] Israel keeps university tuition low by regulation, NIS 12,017 a year at the Hebrew University in 2025–26, but spends less than half the OECD average per university student; the United States charges more and lends more, with $1.651 trillion in student debt and 9.3 million federal borrowers in default by mid-2026.
