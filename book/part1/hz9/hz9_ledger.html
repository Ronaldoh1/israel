Arms, Fighters and Money

Hezbollah publishes no order of battle and no budget, so every figure for its side of the ledger in this section is an estimate, and the estimates differ by a factor of two or more. They are given here with their source and date. Israel's figures come from the International Institute for Strategic Studies, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute and the US Congressional Research Service, and are far more precise. That difference is itself part of the picture: one side is a state with published budgets and a professional army, the other an armed movement inside a weaker state.

Fighters. On 18 October 2021, in a speech after a gun battle in Beirut that killed seven people, Hassan Nasrallah said for the first time that Hezbollah had 100,000 trained fighters. [cite:hz9_iranintl_100k] This was his claim. The Associated Press reported that most estimates put the force at 25,000 to 50,000, including about 10,000 in the Radwan special forces, and that experts considered the 100,000 figure an exaggeration; Sarit Zehavi, a former Israeli military intelligence officer, said Nasrallah had greatly exaggerated the group's strength. [cite:hz9_ap_100k] If his figure were accurate, Hezbollah would be larger than Lebanon's own army, estimated at about 85,000. [cite:hz9_iranintl_100k] Israel's forces, by the IISS count, number 169,500 active personnel and 465,000 reservists. [cite:hm_almon_idf] On the common estimate, Israel's active army alone is three to seven times Hezbollah's whole force; with reservists, thirteen to twenty-five times.

Rockets and missiles before 2024. Israeli sources estimated Hezbollah's stockpile at about 15,000 rockets before the 2006 war, of which nearly 4,000 were fired at Israel. By 2018 the Center for Strategic and International Studies put it at about 130,000, mostly small unguided artillery rockets. [cite:hz9_csis_2018] By 2024 the estimates ran from 75,000, the figure of Israel's Alma Research and Education Center, to "up to 200,000" from other analysts; the Israeli military's own pre-war estimate was about 150,000, and American and Israeli officials quoted by the New York Times gave 120,000 to 200,000. [cite:hz9_usip_arsenal] [cite:hz9_toi_barrages] [cite:hz9_nyt_half] Observers agreed that most of the arsenal was unguided. [cite:hz9_usip_arsenal] The most dangerous part, by the Israeli military's account, was a smaller stock of precision-guided missiles, which it put at "hundreds" at the start of the 2023–24 fighting, and drones, which Alma estimated at about 2,500 larger models. [cite:hz9_jpost_depleted] [cite:hz9_alma_ceasefire]

Money. Hezbollah does not publish its accounts. In November 2018 Nathan Sales, the State Department's counterterrorism coordinator, said Iran gave Hezbollah "some $700 million a year", out of close to $1 billion a year that Iran spent on its allied armed groups. [cite:hz9_usip_sales] Earlier American estimates were lower, at $100 million to $200 million a year; the Wilson Center has cited $700 million to $1 billion. [cite:hz9_wiki_irfund] In February 2012 Nasrallah acknowledged that Hezbollah had received moral, political and material support from Iran since 1982, but gave no figure, and said the group took support, not orders. [cite:hz9_wiki_irfund] The other parts of Hezbollah's income, including its own businesses and the networks the United States has designated, are set out in Part Six. [scene:hz6_money|Hezbollah's money]

What was destroyed, by whose count. The figures for what Israel destroyed come almost entirely from Israel's military, and they changed over time. On 1 October 2024, senior Israeli and American officials told the New York Times that about half of Hezbollah's rockets and missiles had been destroyed, while tens of thousands remained. [cite:hz9_nyt_half] On 10 November 2024 the Jerusalem Post, citing the Israeli military, reported that 80 percent of rockets with a range up to 40 kilometres had been destroyed, short-range stocks had fallen from more than 44,000 to about 10,000, and precision missiles from hundreds to fewer than a hundred. [cite:hz9_jpost_depleted] By the ceasefire of 27 November 2024, Alma's estimate was that 70 to 80 percent of the arsenal had been destroyed and that 2,500 to 3,000 Hezbollah members had been killed. [cite:hz9_alma_ceasefire] Part Seven sets out the fighting itself. [scene:hz7_war|The 2024 war]

The 2026 war tested those claims. Before Hezbollah resumed fire on 2 March 2026, Alma estimated it had about 25,000 rockets and missiles left. [cite:hz9_toi_barrages] In mid-March the Israeli military said it had now destroyed 85 to 90 percent of the pre-2023 arsenal, that about 30,000 rockets had remained in 2025, and that 10,000 to 23,000 remained, with about 1,000 long-range precision rockets. [cite:hz9_yahoo_8590] On 5 April 2026 the head of Israel's Northern Command, Maj. Gen. Rafi Milo, acknowledged a "gap" between how the 2024 operation had been concluded and the fact that Hezbollah was still standing; Israel's Channel 12 reported that officials now believed Hezbollah had tens of thousands of rockets and hundreds of launchers, enough for about 200 launches a day for five more months. [cite:hz9_toi_milo] [cite:hz9_newarab_milo] The public record does not contain an independent count of Hezbollah's arsenal at any point; the honest range for what it held in 2026 runs from about 10,000 to "tens of thousands", depending on which Israeli estimate is used. Part Eight covers the 2026 war. [scene:hz8_war2026|The 2026 war]

Israel's side. Israel spent $46.5 billion on its military in 2024 by SIPRI's count, 8.8 percent of its economy, and $48.3 billion in 2025, 7.8 percent, the eleventh-largest military budget in the world. [cite:hm_globes_sipri] [cite:hz9_sipri_2025] Its air force has 316 combat aircraft, its navy five submarines, and SIPRI estimates that it holds about 90 nuclear warheads. [cite:hm_almon_idf] It has a layered missile defence of Iron Dome, David's Sling and the Arrow systems, built and paid for in part by the United States. [cite:ow1_csis_imd] [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] Under the 2016 memorandum, the United States provides $3.8 billion a year, $500 million of it for missile defence; it gave at least $21.7 billion more in military aid between October 2023 and September 2025, and the April 2024 supplemental alone added $4 billion for missile defence and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser. [cite:hm_dn_mou] [cite:hm_an_costsofwar] [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

What the air gap means. The difference that decided the shape of both wars is in the air. Hezbollah had no aircraft and no air defence able to contest Israel's air force; Israel could strike anywhere in Lebanon, from the border villages to Beirut's southern suburbs and the Bekaa, and did so more than 8,000 times in the first year of fighting, by the US Institute of Peace's count. Hezbollah, in the same year, launched more than 8,800 rockets, missiles and drones at Israel. [cite:hz9_usip_arsenal] Hezbollah's power was the power to inflict damage and displacement on northern Israel, which Part Seven records [scene:hz7_front|The support front]; it was never the power to defend Lebanon's territory or airspace. Asymmetry of this kind does not make either side's attacks on civilians lawful, and it does not mean Hezbollah's arsenal was harmless: American officials warned in June 2024 that some Iron Dome batteries could be overwhelmed in a full-scale war. [cite:hz9_usip_arsenal] What it measures is the distance between the two forces that met in 2024 and again in 2026.
