Benefits: The Records of Need

A person who asks the government for help with food, health care or rent hands over more about themselves than almost anyone else does: income, employer, household members, address, Social Security number and, for non-citizens, immigration status. This section describes how those records are matched and what crossed from them to immigration enforcement in 2025 and 2026, picking up where [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|the section on silos and subpoenas], on the legal walls around tax, Social Security and the first round of Medicaid sharing, leaves off. As throughout this book, a capability is a fact; its use against a given person needs a document of its own.

How eligibility is checked

"Data matching" means running one list of people against another, usually by Social Security number, name and date of birth, and flagging where the two disagree. In February 2021 the Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's auditor, reviewed six programmes: housing vouchers, Medicaid, the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP, formerly food stamps), the Earned Income Tax Credit, home-energy aid and Supplemental Security Income. It found 34 electronic sources in use to verify income or assets: 13 federal, 14 state and 7 commercial. All six programmes drew on state unemployment records. The cost, GAO noted, falls on applicants, who face delays and extra burden when records disagree. [cite:de24b_gao_21183]

The largest commercial source is a single company's database. The Work Number, run by Equifax Workforce Solutions, holds pay and employment data "collected from employers and payroll processors," and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists government agencies among its users, for public-assistance eligibility and child-support enforcement. [cite:de24b_cfpb_twn] On 2 February 2026 Senators Elizabeth Warren, Ron Wyden and Bernie Sanders wrote to Equifax's chief executive that about half the states use it, that per-query prices had risen by 126 per cent in Colorado and 120 per cent in Kansas over four years, and that the company had called the 2025 budget law's new work requirements "a big positive" for its business. [cite:de24b_warren_equifax] [cite:de24b_warren_letter] The law signed on 4 July 2025 requires adults in the Medicaid expansion group aged 19 to 64 to show 80 hours a month of work, community service, a work programme or half-time education, starting 1 January 2027; as of 5 October 2026 the start date had not been delayed. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] [cite:ad8_audit_hfn_gov] It also extended SNAP's work rule to adults aged 18 to 64. [cite:ad11_mn_snap] Proving hours is a data-matching job. The senators' letter recalled that when Arkansas imposed a Medicaid work requirement in 2018, about 18,000 people lost coverage in under a year. [cite:de24b_warren_letter]

Food aid: the SNAP demand

SNAP is run by the states with federal money, so the federal Department of Agriculture (USDA) did not hold recipients' files. In May 2025 it told states to send them: names, dates of birth, home addresses, Social Security numbers and benefit amounts going back to 2020. By October 2025 at least 27 states, with about 15.7 million enrollees, had complied or were in the process; A USDA official's court filings said the department was running the data through SAVE, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) system built to check immigrants' eligibility for benefits and since linked to Social Security records. USDA's June 2025 notice allowed disclosure, where a record "indicates a violation or potential violation of law," to the appropriate federal, state, local, tribal or foreign agency. USDA said it needed the data to find waste, fraud and abuse. [cite:de24b_npr_snap27]

Twenty-one states and the District of Columbia sued. Judge Maxine Chesney of the federal court in San Francisco found the planned uses "well beyond those permitted" by the SNAP law, issued a temporary order in September 2025 and, on 15 October 2025, barred USDA from withholding administrative funds from the plaintiff states. [cite:de24b_npr_snap27] When USDA renewed its demand in December and threatened again to stop paying states' administrative costs, she said in February 2026 that the government still could not force the states to comply, and on 26 February expanded her order, finding a cut-off likely "contrary to the SNAP Act." [cite:de24b_wcax_snap] [cite:de24b_bl_snapfeb] No later ruling lifting the order had been reported as of October 2026. The map is divided: records in complying states went to USDA and through SAVE; records in the suing states did not.

Medicaid after the December ruling

On 29 December 2025 Judge Vince Chhabria let the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) give Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) five fields about people not lawfully present in the suing states: status, address, phone, date of birth and Medicaid number, and no medical records. [cite:de30_bl_medicaid] The data sent went beyond that. Federal officials admitted that a dataset sent on 7 January 2026 covered millions of people, including people in the country lawfully. On 30 April Chhabria warned: "If the federal government cannot be sufficiently careful then it can't use the information." In late May he paused CMS-to-ICE sharing for enforcement. Court filings in July then showed that ICE had passed the January dataset to Palantir, which runs ELITE, the app ICE agents use to see the addresses of possible deportation targets. The government said the file had been shared in a Microsoft Teams chat and deleted from it. Palantir said: "the dataset in question was purged pursuant to government instruction." CMS then re-sent the same file by mistake, and a wider search found half a dozen users still holding copies. [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] At a hearing on 3 September 2026 the judge said valid visa holders' data should not be shared and that the question had to be settled "category by category"; no formal ruling had been located as of October 2026. [cite:de24b_chn_medicaid]

Cash assistance: TANF

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), the federal cash-aid programme, went the other way in court. The Department of Health and Human Services published a notice allowing recipients' information to be disclosed to other federal, state or private entities, including DHS, to check that states follow programme rules, among them immigration-status rules. Two dozen states and the District of Columbia asked for an injunction. On 28 September 2026 Chief Judge James Boasberg refused it: the states had offered "vague, unsubstantiated predictions" about immigrant communities' fears, and DHS had not said it would use the data for enforcement. The notice took effect on 30 September 2026. [cite:de24b_bl_tanf]

The routes run through a small number of federal holders and one Homeland Security lookup system, SAVE, whose published records notice lists the identifiers it matches on and the systems it draws from. [cite:de24b_arch_save_sorn]

If it were connected

Each join is graded: documented (it exists, with a source), probable (a contract, notice or authority already points to it), possible (feasible, no document) or not supported (the record runs against it). Medicaid records into Palantir's enforcement software: documented, once, by mistake, in court filings, and stopped by a judge. [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] SNAP records into SAVE: documented, from USDA's own filings, for the complying states. [cite:de24b_npr_snap27] SNAP records onward to ICE: probable rather than documented, because the June 2025 notice already permits disclosure to any agency where a record suggests a possible violation, and no transfer has been reported. TANF records to DHS: documented as an open route since the disclosure notice took effect on 30 September 2026; the court noted that DHS has not said it will use them for enforcement. TANF records onward to Palantir's systems: possible, with no document. [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] Payroll data from The Work Number to ICE: possible. No document shows it, but an Equifax-held dataset has reached ICE before: utility records on more than 170 million people flowed through Equifax into a people-search service ICE used, until 2021. [cite:de24_globe_utility]

The joins that matter most for people who are not immigrants run through work requirements. From 2027 the same matching that checks income will check hours, and a mismatch will end coverage unless the person answers in time. That is documented as design; how many eligible people lose cover through errors is not yet known. What stands in the way of the wider joins is specific: the SNAP law's data-security terms, Medicaid confidentiality and Judge Chhabria's categories, and the Privacy Act's rule that a new use be published first. The TANF case shows that last brake is procedural: a published notice can open a route, and a court may decline to stop it before harm is shown.
