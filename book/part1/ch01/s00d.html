The Star and the Menorah

When a political movement chooses one symbol over another, the choice says something about what the movement takes itself to be. Most people assume that the Star of David is the ancient symbol of the Jewish people, and that it stands on Israel's flag as it always has. The dated record shows two symbols of very different ages, and a choice between them made first by a political congress in 1897 and then by a new state in 1948.

The older of the two is the seven-branched menorah. Exodus 25:31–40 describes it as the lampstand of the Tabernacle, and it later stood in the Temple in Jerusalem. [cite:exodus_25_bible] Its oldest visual record is on the Arch of Titus in Rome, completed in 81 CE, which depicts the sack of the Second Temple. [cite:wiki_arch_titus] It is the oldest continuous Jewish symbol, and it is the emblem of the modern State of Israel, though on the state seal rather than the flag. [cite:wiki_menorah]

The star's history is much shorter. The six-pointed star as a shape is old and appears in many unrelated cultures, but the Magen David, the Shield of David, is medieval as a distinctively Jewish symbol. Its documented use as a Jewish communal emblem appears in fourteenth-century Prague. In 1354 the Holy Roman Emperor Charles IV granted Prague's Jewish community the right to its own flag, red with a yellow Star of David. Its adoption as the universal Jewish symbol came later still. European Jewish communities drove it between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, wanting an emblem to match the Christian cross and the Islamic crescent. In some traditions it carried Kabbalistic associations, but it was not widely recognized as the Jewish symbol until the modern period. [cite:unpacked_star_of_david_history] [cite:wiki_star_david] Gershom Scholem's 1949 monograph, Magen David: The History of a Symbol, documents how the modern period, and Zionism in particular, fixed the hexagram as the Jewish emblem. Before the seventeenth century, on his account, the symbol of the people would have been the menorah. [cite:scholem_star_david] According to several historians, Herzl himself called the star a "meaningless symbol of Judaism" in this period. None of this makes the star less meaningful to those who hold it now; symbols gain meaning whatever their age. The point concerns the claim of antiquity, which is often overstated.

The flag came out of the movement. In Der Judenstaat of 1896, Herzl proposed a white flag with seven gold stars standing for a seven-hour workday. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] It never caught on. At the First Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1897, a different design flew instead: two blue horizontal stripes on white, with a blue Magen David between them. [cite:basel_program] It closely followed a design that David Wolffsohn had created in 1885 for the Bnei Zion association in Boston, modeled on the colors of the tallit, the prayer shawl. Wolffsohn, who later succeeded Herzl as president of the World Zionist Organization, is credited with proposing it as the movement's banner. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel]

Why the movement chose the star rather than the menorah is a matter of interpretation. Scholem argued that the star was chosen precisely because it lacked the menorah's religious weight. That left it "available" for a secular political project that needed a Jewish symbol without tying itself to rabbinic authority. [cite:scholem_star_david] The documented record supports a plainer explanation as well. By the 1890s the Magen David was the closest thing to a widely recognized, non-sectarian Jewish emblem, legible across a movement whose members came from many religious and secular backgrounds.

The new state then adopted the movement's banner. When Israel declared independence on May 14, 1948, the Provisional Council of State took the Basel flag as the state flag, a choice formalized on October 28, 1948. [cite:declaration_israel_1948] [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] The menorah went on the state seal in 1949. Neither decision was made without debate. The Provisional Council first tried to create a distinct state flag. Its stated concern was that Diaspora Jews who already used the Zionist flag would face accusations of dual loyalty if their movement's banner became a foreign state's flag. It considered and rejected an alternative seven-stars design proposed by Otto Wassisch. In July 1948 Foreign Minister Moshe Sharett cabled Diaspora Jewish leaders for their views, and the answer came back overwhelmingly in favor of keeping the existing flag. The emblem was more contested still. A selection committee rejected 450 proposals from 164 applicants outright, and a second public competition was held. The winning design, by the Shamir brothers, was sent back twice: once to add the word "Israel," and once to replace their modern menorah with the historical version carved on the Arch of Titus. [cite:crwflags_israel_flag_1948] [cite:wjc_flag_unveiled_1948] [cite:nli_emblem_israel_blog]

Both facts hold at once. The choice was considered and consulted on, and it still placed the movement's symbol on the flag and the Temple's on the seal. The movement never hid that the flag was its 1897 banner; the history is public and uncontested. What it shows is that the flag's lineage runs through a nineteenth-century political movement, not through the Temple period.

Some readers go further. One reading holds that a state emerging from a religious tradition might have led with that tradition's oldest symbol, and that leading with the movement's emblem signals which lineage the state understands itself to continue: the political project of Herzl and the Congress. [cite:basel_program] A related reading holds that displaying the flag in synagogues, schools and community centers teaches, below the level of argument, that the political project of 1897 and the religious tradition of millennia are one thing. That is the same conflation traced in [scene:s00c|the section on Zionism and Judaism]. These are interpretations, not documented findings. Many Israelis and Jews elsewhere experience the flag as embracing both histories, and most who display it do so out of a genuine connection to Israel or to Jewish identity, not as part of any messaging campaign.
