Inquiry and Reversal: The Passfield White Paper and the MacDonald Letter, 1930–1931

British policy in Palestine is often described as moving in one steady direction from the Balfour Declaration to the White Paper of 1939. Between March 1930 and February 1931 it moved twice. After the violence of August 1929, two official inquiries concluded that Jewish immigration and land purchase were feeding Arab fears and creating a landless Arab class. In October 1930 London published a policy built on those findings. Four months later the Prime Minister, in a letter to the leader of the Zionist movement, explained it in terms that removed its restrictions. It is the clearest case before 1939 of a British decision on Palestine reversed within months under political pressure, and the immigration figures of the following years show what the reversal allowed.

The first inquiry was the Shaw Commission, sent to investigate the 1929 violence. It reported in March 1930. It concluded that the fundamental cause was Arab fear, economic and political, of being displaced through Jewish immigration and land purchase, and it recommended that Britain clarify its policy on both [cite:w_shaw] [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d]. Its findings on the Mufti and on the riots themselves are told in [scene:s07a4|the Wall and August 1929]. On land it found that the Land Transfer Ordinances of 1920 and 1921 had failed, that the 1929 Protection of Cultivators Ordinance did not stop evictions, and that a landless class was forming, "a potential danger to the country" [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_e].

The second inquiry went deeper. Sir John Hope Simpson surveyed land and agriculture across the country; his report, Cmd. 3686, carries a covering letter dated 22 August 1930 and was published in October 1930 [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. He found that a large share of rural Arab families had too little land to live on, that evictions after land sales were producing a landless class, and that without development there was no margin for further Jewish agricultural settlement [cite:w_hopesimpson]. It is the most detailed British land study of the Mandate period [cite:w_hopesimpson]. In 104 surveyed villages, 29.4 per cent of families held no land, and he put the land available for new settlement at "100,000 dunams at the outside" [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. His full figures, and the Jewish Agency's challenge to them, are set out in [scene:rd3_land|land and labour, 1922–1933].

On the basis of these two reports, the Colonial Secretary, Lord Passfield, issued a White Paper in October 1930. It made immigration depend on Arab as well as Jewish unemployment, and it tightened control of land transfers [cite:w_passfield]. It was the first serious attempt to slow the machinery of settlement the Mandate had set running. The mechanism lay in two levers the Mandatory already held: the rate at which immigration certificates were issued, and the approval of land transfers. The White Paper proposed to tie the first to the condition of the whole labour market, Arab included, and to hold the second more tightly.

The response was immediate. Chaim Weizmann resigned as president of the Jewish Agency in protest. Senior Conservative and Liberal politicians attacked the policy in Parliament and the press as a breach of the Mandate, and the press campaign was intense. In a closely watched by-election at Whitechapel, a constituency with a large Jewish electorate, Labour's majority shrank [cite:w_passfield]. The pressure fell on a minority Labour government [cite:w_passfield]. Nothing in the record requires a hidden hand to explain the outcome. A government without a majority faced an opposition, a press campaign and an electoral warning, all in the open, and gave way.

On 13 February 1931 Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald sent Weizmann a letter, which was read into the record of the House of Commons [cite:w_passfield]. Its text shows exactly how the reversal was done. The letter did not repeal the White Paper. It presented itself as the "authoritative interpretation" of it, and then interpreted it point by point. On immigration, it stated that the government did not contemplate "any stoppage or prohibition of Jewish immigration", and tied any limits to the principle of absorptive capacity, described as "purely economic considerations". On land, it said the 1930 policy did not bar further Jewish land purchase and referred only to "temporary control of land disposition and transfers"; the duty to settle landless Arabs was confined to those displaced by land passing into Jewish hands. On labour, it accepted the Jewish Agency's right to affirm the preferential employment of Jewish labour, while saying that the Mandatory must take account of any resulting displacement of Arab labour or rise in unemployment [cite:rs_rd3_macdonald_unispal]. And it restated the Mandate itself: "an undertaking to the Jewish people and not only to the Jewish population of Palestine", a double undertaking to which equal weight was to be given [cite:rs_rd3_macdonald_unispal].

The two sides read the letter in opposite ways, and each reading has a solid basis in the documents. The Zionist reading, which leading opposition politicians had also put in Parliament before the letter was written, was that the White Paper had breached the Mandate, and that the letter corrected the breach [cite:w_passfield]. The letter's own language supports that reading on its terms. If the Mandate was an undertaking to the Jewish people as a whole, not only to the Jews already in Palestine, then a policy that measured immigration by the local labour market alone weighed only one side of a double obligation. The Arab reading was summed up in the name Arab leaders gave the document: the "Black Letter" [cite:w_passfield]. Its evidence is the sequence itself. A policy founded on two official inquiries was withdrawn within four months, through a letter to the head of one party to the dispute, after a campaign waged in Parliament, the press and a by-election.

The letter's effect on Arab trust is documented by Arab officials of the Mandate government itself. On 30 June 1936, 137 Arab senior government officials told the High Commissioner that the Arabs had lost faith in official pledges. They cited the 1931 letter, issued as an interpretation of the 1930 White Paper, as a cause, and recommended that immigration be stopped [cite:rs_rd3_officials_memo_1936]. Five years after it was written, men who served the Mandate administration still cited the letter when they explained their loss of faith in British commitments.

Immigration then rose steeply. Recorded Jewish immigration, as compiled by the United States State Department from British returns, was 4,075 in 1931, 9,553 in 1932, 30,327 in 1933, 42,359 in 1934 and 61,854 in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]. A second series, printed from the historian Yehoshua Porath, gives higher figures for the later years; both are set out in [scene:rd3_aliyot|immigration, 1923–1936]. The 1935 figure was the highest of the Mandate years. The letter did not by itself produce that rise. From 1933 the Nazi takeover in Germany drove Jewish emigration on a new scale. What the letter did was keep in place the rules under which that emigration could enter Palestine. The Arab Revolt began the following year.

Several questions the record does not settle. The sources do not show which pressure weighed most with MacDonald: the parliamentary attack, the press, the Whitechapel result, or Weizmann's resignation. They do not separate the letter's effect on immigration from the effect of events in Germany; the two arrived together, and any attempt to apportion the rise between them goes beyond the evidence. Nor can they say what the White Paper would have achieved had it stood, since it was never applied as written. What they do show is a documented sequence: London commissioned two inquiries, adopted their findings, and, under open political pressure, withdrew them through an act of interpretation, leaving the earlier rules on immigration and land in force for the 1930s.
