Palestine at War, 1914–1917

The war reached Palestine before it reached its battlefields. When the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War, the country was still a set of Ottoman districts, centered on the Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem.[cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] Its people were Ottoman subjects, but not all of them. A large share of the Jewish community held foreign nationality. The Ottoman register of 1914–15 recorded 38,754 Jews, apparently counting only Ottoman subjects, while other estimates run as high as 94,000; the counting dispute is set out in the opening section of this stage. In wartime, foreign nationality turned from a bureaucratic detail into a matter of who could stay. Over the next three years Palestine's people, Arab and Jewish, lived through expulsion, a plague of locusts, famine in the wider region, the hanging of Arab nationalists and the emptying of Jaffa. This section tells that story to the end of 1917.

The first blow fell on the Jews of Jaffa who held Russian citizenship. In December 1914 up to 6,000 Russian citizens, all of them Jewish, were expelled from the city, according to a reference account of the period.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] Many went to Egypt. Contemporary reports in the New York Times, cited in a 2021 opinion column, put some 7,000 Jewish refugees from Palestine in British-held Alexandria by the end of January 1915.[cite:rs_rd1_bulut] Those figures come to us through a newspaper column rather than an archive, and they are given here as reported.

In 1915 the Ottoman authorities went further. Non-citizens, Jews included, were required to take Ottoman citizenship or leave.[cite:rs_rd1_yishuv_wwi] The order forced a choice on every immigrant who had kept a foreign nationality. Some of those who left became soldiers. In the same year the British Army formed the Zion Mule Corps from Jews exiled to Egypt, and it served at Gallipoli; that story, and the wider record of Jewish service to Britain, is told in [scene:ww_s01|Services rendered to Britain].[cite:rs_rd1_yishuv_wwi] [cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion]

Then came the locusts. From March to October 1915 swarms covered Palestine, Mount Lebanon and Syria. The historian Zachary Foster estimates that they cut the 1915 winter harvest of wheat and barley by 10 to 15 percent, and destroyed between 60 and 100 percent of the summer and autumn fruit and vegetables.[cite:rs_rd1_locust_wiki] The Ottoman response was a labor quota. Every male aged 15 to 60 in the cities had to collect 20 kilograms of locust eggs or pay a fine of £4.40. By 21 November 1915, the New York Times reported, 800 people had paid the fine rather than collect the eggs.[cite:rs_rd1_locust_wiki]

The locusts struck a region already under strain, and in Greater Syria, the wider territory of which Palestine was a part, the result was famine. The American University of Beirut's documentary exhibit names four causes acting together. The Allies blockaded the eastern Mediterranean in 1915. There was drought. There were the locusts. And the exhibit names Ottoman paper money as a fourth cause.[cite:rs_rd1_aub_famine] The mortality figures given are for Mount Lebanon, and they are disputed. The AUB exhibit says Mount Lebanon lost 20 to 30 percent of its population.[cite:rs_rd1_aub_famine] A higher figure, nearly half of the inhabitants of the Mount Lebanon Mutasarrifate, appears in the reference account of the locust plague, but without a source.[cite:rs_rd1_locust_wiki] The sourced range is the one to rely on. For Palestine itself the record used in this book gives no reliable death toll from the famine, and none is offered here.

The war also brought the state's hand down on Arab nationalists. On Jamal Pasha's orders a group of them was hanged in Marjeh Square in Damascus. One was Shukri al-Asali, the former governor of Nazareth who had tried to stop the al-Fula land sale of 1910 and had then argued against Zionist settlement in the Ottoman parliament; his part in that sale is told with [scene:s04j|the land mechanism].[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] The sources on his life date his execution to 6 May 1916.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] The American University of Beirut's exhibit, however, dates the hangings in Damascus and Beirut to 1915.[cite:rs_rd1_aub_famine] The two accounts differ by a year, and the evidence gathered for this book does not resolve the difference. Either way, an Ottoman official who had opposed a Zionist land purchase in public was put to death by the same Ottoman state, as one of a group of Arab nationalists.

In the spring of 1917 the war emptied Jaffa. On 28 March 1917 Jamal Pasha ordered the evacuation of Jaffa. The deportation took place on 6 April 1917.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] How many people were driven out is disputed, and three figures circulate. One estimate puts those displaced at about 10,000, of whom 8,000 were Jews. Another puts the number evacuated from Tel Aviv, the new Jewish neighborhood beside Jaffa, at up to 16,000.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] A United States consular report of 1917 gave 8,000 Jews evicted.[cite:rs_rd1_bulut] The figures measure different things: the whole city or only its Jewish residents, Jaffa or Tel Aviv. The lower Jewish figures agree with each other, and the highest figure applies only to Tel Aviv.

The deportation did not fall equally. The order covered the city, but Jaffa's Muslim residents were able to return soon afterwards, while the Jewish deportees could not return until the summer of 1918.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] About 1,500 people are thought to have died as a result of the deportation, though the deaths were not properly recorded.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] Reports reached the outside world within weeks. A US consular report, published in the New York Times on 3 June 1917, described looting and killings of deportees; the report survives in that newspaper and is cited in the 2021 column.[cite:rs_rd1_bulut] The evidence here is a contemporary diplomatic report as carried by the press, not a court record, and no primary text of Jamal Pasha's orders is among the sources used for this book.

By the end of the war the Jewish community of Palestine was smaller than in 1914. How much smaller is a real dispute between serious sources. The Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question holds that about a third of the Jewish population left Palestine during 1914–18.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_chron] The demographer Justin McCarthy, as summarized by Roger Owen, argues that the fall was much less than the commonly cited quarter, because many of those who fled to Alexandria came back.[cite:rs_rd1_mccarthy_owen] Both sides have evidence. The expulsions of 1914–15 and the deportation of 1917 were real and large. Return was also real: the Jaffa deportees were allowed back in the summer of 1918, and McCarthy's case rests on returns from Alexandria. The disagreement over the end point is tied to the disagreement over the starting point, since the 1914 baseline itself is disputed between 38,754 and 94,000 depending on whether foreign nationals are counted.[cite:rs_rd1_mccarthy_owen]

Ottoman rule in Palestine ended in December 1917, when Britain occupied the country, and the Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem was dissolved.[cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] By then the British government had already made the commitments that would shape what followed, to the Zionist movement and to others in the region. Those wartime undertakings, and the Balfour Declaration of November 1917 among them, are the subject of Stage Two and are told in [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration and the wartime promises].

Much of this record stays uncertain. The number of famine deaths inside Palestine itself is not established in the sources this book relies on. The death toll in Mount Lebanon is given as a range, and its upper claim is unsourced. The year of the Damascus and Beirut hangings differs between sources. The size of the Jaffa deportation depends on what is counted, and the deaths it caused were never properly recorded. How far the Jewish population fell between 1914 and 1918 remains disputed.
