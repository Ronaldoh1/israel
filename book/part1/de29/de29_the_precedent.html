The Precedent: When the Tools Were Turned Inward

The worst-case reading of Book Five asks whether surveillance built for foreign enemies and for immigration enforcement could be turned on Americans who oppose the government. That question is not hypothetical. It happened, in the United States, under presidents of both parties, between the end of the Second World War and the middle of the 1970s, and the record that proves it is the government's own: a Senate committee, a presidential commission, the National Security Agency's (NSA's) official history and the files of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI). The Senate's inquiry under Frank Church, the vote that created it and its finding that "Intelligence agencies have undermined the constitutional rights of citizens" are set out in [scene:us10_us|the library's account of the American framework] and are not repeated here. [cite:us10_senate_church] This section looks at the programmes themselves, one by one, at who was watched, and at what actually stopped each of them, because that last question bears most directly on the present.

Six programmes

The oldest was the NSA's SHAMROCK. After the Second World War three telegraph companies, Western Union, RCA and ITT, gave the agency and its predecessor copies of the international telegrams that passed through their offices. At its height NSA analysts reviewed about 150,000 messages a month, and in 1966 the NSA and the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) set up a front office in lower Manhattan to handle the traffic. No warrant, no statute and no court was involved at any stage. Church called it "probably the largest government interception program affecting Americans ever undertaken." The NSA's director, Lew Allen, ended it in May 1975, as the Senate investigators closed in. [cite:de29_schneier_shamrock]

The second was MINARET, the NSA's watch list. Other agencies, chiefly the FBI, sent the NSA names, and the NSA searched its intercepts of overseas calls and cables for them. About 1,650 American citizens were on the list. When the National Security Archive at George Washington University forced the release of the relevant pages of the NSA's own Cold War history in September 2013, after some eight years of requests and appeals, the names included Martin Luther King Jr., the civil-rights leader Whitney Young, the boxer Muhammad Ali, the New York Times columnist Tom Wicker, the humorist Art Buchwald, and two sitting senators: Frank Church, a Democrat from Idaho, and Howard Baker, a Republican from Tennessee. [cite:de29_nbc_minaret] [cite:de29_gwt_minaret] The agency's own historian, Thomas Johnson, wrote that officials understood at the time that the programme was "disreputable if not outright illegal" (one account renders the last words "downright illegal"). Accounts differ on its start, from 1961 to the late 1960s; all put its end in 1973. [cite:de29_nbc_minaret] [cite:de29_gwt_minaret]

The third, and the best known, was the FBI's COINTELPRO, short for counterintelligence programme. It began in 1956 against the Communist Party and widened to the Socialist Workers Party in 1961, the Ku Klux Klan and other "white hate groups" in 1964, Black nationalist organisations in 1967 and the New Left in 1968. Its methods went past watching into disruption, through anonymous letters and informants placed inside the groups. On 21 November 1964 the FBI mailed King an anonymous package of recordings with a letter that he read as urging him to kill himself. The Church Committee called the whole effort a "sophisticated vigilante operation." [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro] The programme was officially terminated in April 1971. [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro]

The fourth was the CIA's Operation CHAOS, set up in August 1967. Two presidents, Lyndon Johnson and Richard Nixon, wanted proof that the anti-war and Black political movements were directed from abroad; in the words of the presidential commission that later examined it, "Two Presidents and their staffs made continuing and insistent requests." The CIA built a computer index of about 300,000 names of people and organisations, most of them American, and opened about 7,200 personal files and nearly 1,000 files on organisations. Its own analysts found no evidence of foreign control of the movements, in 1969 and again in 1970. The commission chaired by Vice-President Nelson Rockefeller concluded in June 1975 that "Some domestic activities of Operation CHAOS unlawfully exceeded the CIA's statutory authority", though it held that the stated mission, looking abroad for foreign influence, had been proper. CHAOS was terminated on 15 March 1974. [cite:de29_rock_chaos]

The fifth was the Army's. From the late 1960s plainclothes military intelligence agents attended protests and civil-rights meetings and kept files on civilians. When people named in those files sued, the Supreme Court refused to hear the merits. In Laird v. Tatum, decided 5–4 on 26 June 1972, Chief Justice Warren Burger described the Army's system as "the collection of information about public activities" and held that a claimed chilling effect, without more, was not "an adequate substitute for a claim of specific present objective harm." [cite:de29_laird] The ruling set a pattern that still matters: people who cannot prove they were watched usually cannot get into court to challenge the watching.

The sixth was the Internal Revenue Service's Special Service Staff. By 1969, under pressure from the White House and Congress, the tax agency had a unit that examined about 8,000 individuals and 3,000 groups for their political activity rather than for evidence of tax violations, working from lists of activists supplied by the FBI and the Justice Department. Among the organisations listed were the Ford Foundation and the Urban League. A new commissioner, Donald Alexander, dissolved it in mid-1973. [cite:de29_hnn_irs]

What stopped them

The most important fact about these programmes is how they ended. None was ended by the agency's own compliance system at the point the abuse began. COINTELPRO came to light because of a crime. On the night of 8 March 1971 a group calling itself the Citizens' Commission to Investigate the FBI broke into the bureau's field office in Media, Pennsylvania, took its files and mailed them to journalists. Betty Medsger of the Washington Post published first. Among the papers was a routing slip carrying the word COINTELPRO, which no reporter yet understood. [cite:de29_csm_media] The programme was shut within weeks. [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro] The burglars, among them John and Bonnie Raines and Keith Forsyth, named themselves only in 2014; the FBI had closed its case on 11 March 1976, three days after the limitation period ran out, and none was ever charged. [cite:de29_csm_media] SHAMROCK and MINARET ended under the approach of the congressional inquiry; CHAOS was wound down within the agency in 1974 and then examined by the Rockefeller Commission; the IRS unit ended because a new commissioner found it. [cite:de29_schneier_shamrock] [cite:de29_nbc_minaret] [cite:de29_rock_chaos] [cite:de29_hnn_irs] The Army's programme survived its day in the Supreme Court. [cite:de29_laird]

The sequence that recurs is exposure from outside, then oversight. Internal stops did happen, but under pressure or the threat of exposure. The reforms that followed, a permanent intelligence committee in each house and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) of 1978, which put national-security wiretaps under a special court, were real. [cite:us10_senate_church]

The pattern after the reforms

The reforms did not end the pattern; they changed its form. After the attacks of 11 September 2001 President George W. Bush authorised the NSA, in secret and outside FISA, to collect the content of some Americans' international calls and emails and bulk records of their communications. The programme, code-named Stellar Wind, was examined by five inspectors general in 2009 in a report the New York Times obtained in full through a lawsuit in April 2015. A 2004 FBI review it cited found that only 1.2 per cent of the leads the programme produced between 2001 and 2004 made a "significant contribution" to identifying a terrorist or developing an informant, and none from the content collection between August 2004 and January 2006 proved useful. Its limits were set not by the agency but by the Justice Department's own lawyers, who in March 2004 confronted the White House over its legality. [cite:de29_ap_stellarwind]

The most recent documented misuse is smaller and better recorded. A ruling of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court, released in redacted form on 19 May 2023, found that FBI staff had improperly searched data collected under Section 702 of FISA (described in [scene:us10_us|the library's account]) for more than 100 people arrested at the racial-justice protests of June 2020, for suspects in the attack on the Capitol of 6 January 2021, and in one batch for some 19,000 donors to a congressional campaign, of whom the Justice Department found only eight had a sufficient tie to foreign influence. The FBI blamed confusion over the rules, required written justifications for each search and reported compliance rising from 82 to 96 per cent. [cite:de29_ap_fisc2023] The people searched sat on the left and on the right. That is the honest shape of the modern record: misuse of an existing database by its routine users, caught by the court that oversees it, rather than a programme aimed at political opponents.
