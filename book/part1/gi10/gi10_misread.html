Real Documents, Misread

The second class is made of things that exist and are misdescribed. An essay in a Hebrew quarterly, a think-tank paper and a coin are all real, and each can be read or held in the hand. Each has been presented as evidence of a plan to rule from the Nile to the Euphrates. In each case the text or the object says something else. Two of them, Oded Yinon's essay and "A Clean Break", have already been read in [scene:ir13_papers|the Iran volume] for what they say about Iran; this section reads them for what they say about territory, and keeps to the same facts.

The Yinon essay, 1982

"A Strategy for Israel in the 1980s" was published in February 1982 in Kivunim ("Directions"), issue 14, a Hebrew quarterly on Judaism and Zionism issued from 1978 to 1987 by the World Zionist Organization's Department of Information. Its author, Oded Yinon, was reputedly a journalist and a former Foreign Ministry official, and by some accounts had advised Ariel Sharon. Israel Shahak, an Israeli critic of Zionism, translated it into English; his version was disseminated on 13 June 1982, a week after Israel invaded Lebanon, and circulated as "The Zionist Plan for the Middle East". [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon] [cite:ir13_mb_yinon] The title by which most readers know it was given in translation; the essay's own title describes a strategy, and its author held no office that could make it policy.

What the essay argued was that the Arab states were fragile mosaics of sects and peoples, and that Israel should aim at, and expect, their dissolution. "Syria will fall apart, in accordance with its ethnic and religious structure," it says, and "Lebanon's total dissolution into five provinces serves as a precendent for the entire Arab world." Iraq would divide into Shiite, Sunni and Kurdish parts. [cite:ir13_mb_yinon] [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon] Its programme is fragmentation of neighbours, not the annexation of their land. The only territory it names as an Israeli aim is Sinai, which Israel was then returning to Egypt under the 1979 treaty: "(Regaining) the Sinai peninsula with its present and potential resources is therefore a political priority," Yinon wrote, a priority "obstructed by the Camp David and the peace agreements". In the same passage he added that "Israel will not unilaterally break the treaty". [cite:ir13_mb_yinon] He also called for the "liquidation" of Jordan as a Hashemite state, with Palestinians moved from the West Bank to the east bank. [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon] The English text available online is incomplete and lacks the essay's section on Jordan and its notes, so that passage is reported here from the secondary literature. [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon]

None of this is a Nile-to-Euphrates claim. The essay proposes no Israeli border on either river and no rule over Cairo, Damascus or Baghdad. It is real evidence of something narrower: one writer's view, published under the WZO's imprint, that Israel would gain from the break-up of the states around it. Scholars disagree about how representative that view was. Mordechai Nisan treated it as Yinon's alone and not government policy; Yehoshafat Harkabi found its analysis of Arab weakness largely correct but asked why, if fragmentation was inevitable, Israel would need to interfere; Ilan Peleg called it "an authentic mirror of the thinking mode of the Israeli Right" under Begin; Noam Chomsky placed it outside the Zionist mainstream, nearer the ultranationalist Tehiya party. Yinon himself replied in 1983 that he was not a supporter of Sharon or Begin. [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon]

What it became is a separate matter. From Roger Garaudy's use of it in 1983 to a column in Arab News in 2005 announcing that the plan was "in large part taking shape", the essay has been offered as the script for the 2003 invasion of Iraq, the Syrian civil war and the rise of the Islamic State, most of which happened decades later and none of which Israel's governments set in motion on the documented record. [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon] The record contains no Israeli government decision adopting it. The one aim it stated for Israel's own territory did not happen: Israel completed the return of Sinai on 25 April 1982 and did not go back (see [scene:gi8_sinai|Part Eight]). [cite:gi1_csm_sinai1982] Its call to dissolve Jordan was answered twelve years later by a treaty in which Israel undertook to respect Jordan's "sovereignty, territorial integrity and political independence." [cite:gi8_incore_jotreaty] Becker and Polkinghorn (2017) argue that the essay's ideas were refined in "A Clean Break"; that is an argument about intellectual lineage, and the next document can be checked against it. [cite:ir13_wiki_yinon]

"A Clean Break", 1996

A Clean Break: A New Strategy for Securing the Realm came from a study group of the Institute for Advanced Strategic and Political Studies led by Richard Perle, with James Colbert, Charles Fairbanks Jr., Douglas Feith, Jonathan Torop, David Wurmser, Meyrav Wurmser and the institute's president, Robert Loewenberg, and was addressed to Netanyahu's incoming government in 1996. [cite:ir13_wiki_cleanbreak] Feith says he "neither wrote it nor signed it" and names David Wurmser as its principal author. [cite:ir13_feith_cleanbreak] Its authors were American policy advocates, not Israeli officials.

Its territorial content is real but limited. It urged Israel to drop "land for peace" in favour of "peace for peace", on the basis of "the unconditional acceptance by Arabs of our rights, especially in their territorial dimension"; it recommended "rejecting 'land for peace' deals on the Golan Heights"; and in a draft passage offered for the new prime minister to deliver, it said: "Our claim to the land - to which we have clung for hope for 2000 years - is legitimate and noble." [cite:ir13_km_cleanbreak] That is a programme of keeping the territory Israel then held, the Golan in particular, and refusing to trade it. The text reviewed for this book does not mention the West Bank by name, Judea and Samaria or settlements, and it claims no territory beyond the lines Israel held in 1996. Its phrase about "a redrawing of the map of the Middle East" describes what Syria might fear from the regional changes the paper proposed, not an Israeli border. [cite:ir13_km_cleanbreak] Its strategic proposals concerned Syria and Iraq: weakening and "rolling back" Syria and removing Saddam Hussein, with Turkey and Jordan. [cite:ir13_km_cleanbreak]

The paper is often cited as a Greater Israel blueprint and as the plan behind the Iraq war. The first reading does not survive its text, which is about refusing withdrawal, not about expansion. The second is the argument already weighed in [scene:ir13_papers|the Iran volume]: several of its authors held posts in Washington by 2002, and critics such as John Mearsheimer and Stephen Walt saw a line from the paper to the war, while others, including CBS's David Martin, found no evidence of decisive influence. [cite:ir13_wiki_cleanbreak] Netanyahu did not adopt the paper as a document. His first government signed the Hebron Protocol of 17 January 1997 and redeployed from most of Hebron, and on 23 October 1998 he signed the Wye River Memorandum, which committed Israel to transfer a further 13 per cent of the West Bank from Area C to Palestinian control: land in exchange for Palestinian security commitments, the kind of trade the paper had urged him to refuse. [cite:gi10_crs_wye] [cite:ir13_wiki_cleanbreak] Israel carried out only about 2 percentage points of that transfer before implementation stalled. [cite:wb_wiki_oslo2areas]

The 10-agora coin

The third misread item is an object. At a specially convened session of the UN Security Council in Geneva on 25 May 1990, Yasser Arafat said of Israel's 10-agora coin: "This document is a 'map of Greater Israel' which is inscribed on this Israeli coin". Earlier that spring, at an Arab League committee meeting on 9 April 1990 and in the days after it, he had said the map was stamped "directly under" the menorah. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] [cite:gi10a_pipes] [cite:gi10_wiki_agorot] He relied on a 1989 article in the journal GeoJournal by the British geographer Gwyn Rowley, who wrote that the coin's background shape "seemingly" outlined an area reaching Amman, Beirut, Baghdad and Damascus and framed it as speculation. [cite:gi10_wiki_agorot]

The coin is real and its design is documented. The Bank of Israel describes its face as a "replica of a coin issued by Mattathias Antigonus (37 - 40 B.C.E.) with the seven-branched candelabrum", the last Hasmonean king, whose coin was struck around the Roman siege of Jerusalem in 37 BCE. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The modern coin was issued on 4 September 1985 and its face was designed by Nathan Karp. [cite:gi10_boi_coins] The same design had appeared on the 100-sheqel coin in 1984. [cite:gi10_wiki_agorot] The irregular outline that Rowley and Arafat read as a map is, on the designer's account as Pipes reports it, the general outline of the ancient coin, reproduced with its design. Ya'akov Meshorer of the Israel Museum told the Jerusalem Post in June 1990 that "Karp was astounded" that anyone could see a coastline in it. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] A Hebrew University scholar, S. M. Berkowicz, answered Rowley in the same journal in 1991, calling the claim undocumented and without foundation; Rowley replied that the coin may have been chosen for its map-like shape. [cite:gi10a_wiki_agorot] The Bank of Israel's stated position is that the design was chosen for its historical value. [cite:gi10a_wiki_agorot] The verdict is misreading: a real coin carrying an ancient design, not a map.
