The Ships: What the Immigration Limit Cost, 1940–1947

An immigration quota reads like an administrative matter: a number per year, a rule for exceptions, a procedure for those who arrive without papers. The White Paper of May 1939, whose text and reception are set out in [scene:rd4_white_paper|the preceding section], allowed some 75,000 Jewish immigrants over five years and provided that illegal immigrants would be deducted from the yearly quotas [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text]. In July 1939, citing illegal immigration, Britain suspended the next six-month quota altogether [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841]. A familiar way of telling the story stops there, as though the restriction were a bureaucratic policy whose only cost was a count of people turned away. The record of the war years shows otherwise. The limit was enforced against particular ships carrying particular people, and two of those ships became sites of mass death. Over seven years, three vessels marked the stages of the policy's enforcement: the Patria in Haifa harbour in November 1940, the Struma in the Black Sea in February 1942, and the Exodus 1947 off Palestine in July 1947. Their outcomes were different, and the differences matter as much as what they share.

The mechanism behind all three was the same. Under the White Paper, entry to Palestine came through the quota, and illegal arrivals counted against it. The decision about what happened to refugees who reached the coast without permits lay with the British authorities, who in these cases chose deportation or refused entry outright. The people on the ships had no say in it. The Haganah, as the first case shows, tried to stop one deportation by force.

The Patria, Haifa, November 1940. In the autumn of 1940 British authorities decided to deport roughly 1,800 Jewish refugees, who had reached Palestine without valid entry permits, to Mauritius, a British colony in the Indian Ocean, for the duration of the war. They were to be carried there aboard the Patria [cite:wiki_patria_disaster]. The Haganah's plan to prevent this was narrow and specific. It would place a limited explosive charge below the waterline, damage the ship just enough to delay its departure and force a change in British policy, and do so without endangering the passengers [cite:wiki_patria_disaster].

The plan failed in the worst possible way. The explosion was much larger than intended, and the ship, which had already been damaged and repaired after an earlier incident, capsized far faster than anyone had anticipated, within roughly 16 minutes. Passengers below deck, and those who could not reach the lifeboats in time, drowned. By most documented counts 267 to 268 people died, most of them the refugees the operation had been meant to keep in Palestine [cite:wiki_patria_disaster]. Shorter accounts round the toll to "over 260"; the documented range is 267 to 268.

How the Patria is remembered is part of its history. The Haganah's own postwar historical accounts describe the sinking as a serious operational failure with a devastating and unintended human cost; the organization that carried it out did not conceal or deny it [cite:wiki_patria_disaster]. Every documented account, the Haganah's included, describes a catastrophic accident inside an operation meant to help the people who died, and nothing in the record shows any intent to harm the passengers. To describe it as anything else would misstate the record, and to omit it, or to blame the deaths on Britain alone, would misstate it in the other direction. The policy created the deportation order; the bomb that sank the ship was the Haganah's.

The Struma, the Black Sea, February 1942. Fifteen months later a second ship met a different end. The Struma carried Jewish refugees from Romania. It was denied entry to Palestine under the White Paper's immigration limit and spent weeks stranded at Istanbul before it was forced back out into the Black Sea [cite:wiki_struma_disaster]. On 24 February 1942 it was torpedoed, most likely by a Soviet submarine operating under standing orders to sink any vessel in the area. The sources describe this as a case of mistaken identity, not a deliberate attack on the ship [cite:wiki_struma_disaster]. More than 780 people died, nearly the entire passenger list. There was one survivor [cite:wiki_struma_disaster].

Two questions about the Struma need to be kept apart. The first is who fired the torpedo and why, and the answer the sources give is a submarine acting on general orders that had nothing to do with this ship in particular. The second is how the ship came to be in open water at all, and that answer runs back to the refusal of entry. Saying that the submarine did not target refugees does not clear the policy that left them there, and saying the policy left them there does not make the British government the author of the torpedo. Both statements are documented and both have to stand.

Taken together, the Patria and the Struma killed more than 1,000 people, refugees trying to reach the one place whose doors the White Paper was narrowing [cite:wiki_patria_disaster] [cite:wiki_struma_disaster]. These were not abstract consequences of a policy but named, dated maritime disasters. The combined figure carries a limit that is just as important as the number itself. It does not assign blame for each death to a single actor. The Patria's dead died because of a Haganah miscalculation; the Struma's because of a third party's mistaken attack. What links the two is the policy that created the conditions for both: the deportation order in one case, the refusal of entry in the other.

The deaths also entered the politics of the period. When Lehi gunmen killed Lord Moyne in Cairo in November 1944, the group blamed him for turning back refugee ships, as [scene:lh_s01|the next section] describes [cite:daat_moyne]. The sources do not tie Moyne to either of these two ships by name, and this book does not.

The Exodus 1947: an epilogue. The third ship falls after this chapter's period, and its full story belongs to the postwar years of clandestine immigration told in [scene:ab_s01|the section on Aliyah Bet]. In July 1947 the Royal Navy intercepted the Exodus 1947, which carried roughly 4,500 Holocaust survivors, and forcibly returned its passengers to displaced-persons camps in Germany [cite:wiki_exodus_1947]. Its significance lay less in its immediate human cost, which was real but not comparable to the death tolls of the Patria and the Struma, than in what followed. International press coverage of the interception became a major, documented turning point in Western public sympathy for the Zionist cause, a few months before the United Nations partition vote of November 1947 [cite:wiki_exodus_1947].

How much that coverage moved the vote is disputed, and the case can be put both ways. On one side, historians credit the Exodus coverage as a genuine contributor to the shift in Western opinion ahead of November 1947: the sight of Holocaust survivors being returned to camps in Germany reached a world audience through the press [cite:wiki_exodus_1947]. On the other side, the documented record of the American decision, told in a later volume of this library through the Henderson memorandum, the Joint Chiefs' warnings and Clifford's electoral calculus, shows that the United States' vote turned mainly on domestic political pressure, not international sympathy [cite:wiki_exodus_1947]. Both factors are real. Neither alone explains the outcome, and the more dramatic story should not be allowed to stand in for the better-documented one.

Several things remain unsettled in the sources gathered here. They do not name the passengers of either wartime ship or give a full manifest for the Struma; they describe the Soviet submarine's role as likely, not certain; and they do not record what the Mandatory government itself concluded about either sinking. Nor do they measure the Exodus coverage's effect on any particular government's vote, which is why the weighing above stays a weighing and not a verdict. What they do establish is the answer to the claim this section began with. The White Paper's restriction had a human cost beyond the numbers turned away, and it can be counted ship by ship: more than a thousand dead in two sinkings within fifteen months, each with its own cause and each traceable to a door that was kept nearly shut.
