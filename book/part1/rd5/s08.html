FDR Dies, Truman Chooses

The American decision on Israel was made by a president who had not been expected to make it. That fact begins not in 1948 but in 1944, at the Democratic convention in Chicago.

Henry Wallace had served as Franklin Roosevelt's Vice President since 1941. He was a progressive: pro-labor, anti-imperialist, and a supporter of civil rights well ahead of the American political mainstream. To the party's conservative and machine wings he was unacceptable. At the 1944 convention, with an ailing Roosevelt unlikely to survive another term, the party machine removed Wallace from the ticket and replaced him with a compromise choice, Senator Harry Truman of Missouri. [cite:wiki_choosing_truman] [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] Wallace later ran for president on the Progressive Party ticket in 1948, lost badly, and died in 1965. The substitution matters because of what followed. Had Wallace remained on the ticket, he, not Truman, would have become president when Roosevelt died. What Wallace would have decided about Palestine is unknowable, and this book does not guess. The point is narrower: the sequence that led to recognition ran through a convention back room, not through any inevitability.

Roosevelt himself had left a commitment behind. In February 1945 he met King Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia aboard the USS Quincy in the Suez Canal and assured him that no action hostile to the Arab people would be taken without consultation. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] Two months later, on April 12, 1945, Roosevelt died at Warm Springs, Georgia, before the postwar decisions on Palestine had to be made. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] Whether that promise would have shaped a different policy cannot be tested.

Truman (1884–1972) had been Vice President for 82 days. He had no prior foreign policy experience and had received little briefing on the major questions he now inherited. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The familiar account holds that he recognized Israel out of moral conviction, and his own memoir presented the decision as principled. The documents give a more complicated picture, in which domestic political calculation, a personal friendship and pressure from the American Zionist movement all played a part. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman]

The clearest evidence of the political calculation is a memo from Clark Clifford (1906–1998), the White House Special Counsel. In November 1947 Clifford argued to Truman that the Jewish-American vote was likely decisive in key swing states, New York, Pennsylvania and Illinois, for the 1948 election, and he framed early recognition of a Jewish state as electorally advantageous. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] This is not a reconstruction by later critics. Clifford's own 1991 memoir, Counsel to the President, openly describes the domestic-political framework as part of his advice, and acknowledges the opposition it met. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition]

That opposition was formidable. The career foreign-service establishment lined up against early recognition: Loy Henderson, director of Near Eastern Affairs; Under Secretary Robert Lovett; and Secretary of State George Marshall. They argued that recognition would damage American relations with the Arab world and endanger the oil supply. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Marshall, the wartime architect and one of the most respected public figures in the country, told Truman: "If you follow Clifford's advice and if I were to vote in the election, I would vote against you." He threatened to resign. Truman recognized Israel anyway, and Marshall did not resign. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Not every diplomat necessarily shared the view of these senior officials, but their objections are on the record.

A personal channel ran alongside the official one. Ed Jacobson, a Jewish American, had been Truman's partner in a Kansas City men's clothing store, Truman & Jacobson, which went bankrupt in the early 1920s; he had been best man at Truman's wedding. By March 1948 Truman had refused to see the Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann officially. Jacobson persuaded him to receive Weizmann privately, and the two met on March 18, 1948. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] That meeting is widely credited with securing Truman's commitment to recognition, and it is documented in multiple biographies and in Truman's own recollections. Jacobson's role should not be inflated beyond this one meeting. It was a friendship, and it is recorded in the open.

On May 14, 1948, at 6:11 p.m. Washington time, eleven minutes after Ben-Gurion declared the State of Israel, the United States extended de facto recognition. Clifford had orchestrated the timing. The State Department was blindsided, and so was the American delegation at the United Nations, which at that moment was still working for a trusteeship proposal as an alternative to statehood. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] De jure recognition followed only in January 1949, after Truman's election victory; the Soviet Union, for its own reasons, extended full de jure recognition first, in May 1948.

None of this shows that recognition was illegitimate, or that electoral calculation was its only cause. The record does not show that humanitarian and strategic considerations played no role; several motives likely coexisted, and personal relationships and political pressure shape presidential decisions on many issues. What the record does show is that this decision was not the product of a settled interagency consensus. It was a contested act by one president, taken quickly, over the open objection of his own foreign-policy professionals.
