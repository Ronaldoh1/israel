From Legend to Law: The Emperor Is Sacred

A founding legend usually fades as a state modernises. Ethiopia's did the opposite. In the twentieth century the Kebra Nagast's claim was written into a modern constitution, and it became the centre of a new religion on the other side of the Atlantic.

Menelik II, at the end of the nineteenth century, presented himself as Solomon's literal, genealogical successor, to win over his nobles and to impress European powers. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] Haile Selassie, crowned in 1930, used Solomonic imagery to project an unbroken father-to-son line. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] The revised constitution he granted in 1955 made the claim law. Its second article declares that the imperial dignity "shall remain perpetually attached to the line of Haile Sellassie I", whose descent it traces back to Menelik I, the son, in the Kebra Nagast, of the Queen of Sheba and Solomon. Its fourth article states: "the person of the Emperor is sacred". [cite:et_const1955] A book written in the fourteenth century to legitimise one dynasty had become, six centuries later, the constitutional basis of the state.

The constitutional claim had consequences far from Ethiopia. In the 1930s a small group of Jamaicans, rejecting a Christianised Western culture they saw as built on the exploitation of Africans, came to regard Haile Selassie as the Christ, the Messiah, the Chosen One. "Belief in the divinity of Haile Selassie", the scholar Monique Bedasse writes, "is foundational to the Rastafarian movement." [cite:et_jstor_rasta] When the emperor visited Jamaica in 1966, his short stay galvanised the movement, although he kept his distance from it. [cite:et_jstor_rasta] The Ethiopian legend of a Solomonic king had become, in the Caribbean, a theology of a living messiah.

It ended in a coup. On 12 September 1974 Haile Selassie was deposed by the military committee known as the Derg, and he died less than a year later; the government attributed his death to natural causes, while many of his supporters believed he had been murdered. [cite:et_jstor_rasta] Solomonic descent, which World History Encyclopedia describes as central to state ideology until the communist takeover of 1974, ceased to be the foundation of the Ethiopian state. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] Some Rastafarian leaders refused to believe the reports of his death. [cite:et_jstor_rasta]

The arc from 1270 to 1974 shows the strongest form of the pattern this shelf traces. In the West, the institution that guarded the scriptures claimed a line of succession from Peter. In Ethiopia, the throne claimed a line of succession from Solomon, and the church that kept the scriptures was the church of the kings who made the claim. The book that proved the line was not scripture, but it decided who ruled the people who heard scripture read. When the throne fell, the next thing the revolution took, as Part Seven shows, was the church's land.
