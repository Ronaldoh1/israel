The Definition Fight, Where It Stands

The previous section set out the three competing definitions of hatred of Jews and how one of them came to be used as a shield. This section maps where the contest stands as of November 2026: who has adopted which definition, who has refused, what the adoption does in law, and where it has been reversed. The point is not to decide which definition is right; scholars of the subject still disagree. It is to show how far one side of an open argument has been written into the rules of governments, universities and schools.

The scale of adoption

The working definition of the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA), with its eleven examples, seven of them about Israel, has been adopted far more widely than any rival. According to the Combat Antisemitism Movement, an advocacy group that tracks adoptions and promotes the definition, it had been adopted 1,334 times worldwide by the end of 2025: by 47 national governments, 37 American states (18 by legislation and 19 by executive order or proclamation), 386 educational institutions and hundreds of regional, municipal and other bodies. In 2025 Ireland became the twenty-sixth member of the European Union to adopt it, leaving Malta as the only one that had not, and Harvard, Yale and Columbia adopted it on their campuses. The European Commission, the European Parliament, the Council of Europe and the Organization of American States have also adopted it. [cite:def_cam_2025]

The rival texts

The main alternative, the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism, was published on 25 March 2021 after nine months of drafting by scholars of Jewish history, the Holocaust and the Middle East, and was first signed by about 200 of them. Its core definition reads: "Antisemitism is discrimination, prejudice, hostility or violence against Jews as Jews (or Jewish institutions as Jewish)." Its fifteen guidelines are grouped in three sets: general forms of hatred of Jews; speech about Israel and Palestine that is hateful on its face, such as holding Jews collectively responsible for Israel's conduct; and speech about Israel and Palestine that is not hateful on its face, such as supporting Palestinians or opposing Zionism. [cite:def_wiki_jda] Its authors said it was meant as a guide to judgment, not a legal code. [cite:jwa_jda]

The Declaration has its own critics. Scholars such as David Hirsh and Cary Nelson argued that it overlooks hatred of Jews on the political left and accommodates anti-Zionism that crosses the line; others said that by rebutting the IHRA definition it undermined a shared standard. [cite:def_wiki_jda] Unlike IHRA, it has not been adopted into law by any government in the sources located for this book. The third text, the Nexus Document, takes an intermediate position and has been cited by members of Congress as one tool among several. [cite:def_wiki_jda]

Who refused, and why

Opposition has come from human-rights organizations and from inside Jewish and Israeli civil society. In April 2023, as the United Nations considered its approach to hatred of Jews, 104 organizations signed a letter asking it not to adopt the IHRA definition. The thirteen founding signatories included the American Civil Liberties Union, Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, the Israeli groups B'Tselem, Gisha and Physicians for Human Rights–Israel, and Palestinian groups such as Al-Haq. Jewish Voice for Peace, IfNotNow and the Israeli veterans of Breaking the Silence joined later. The letter asked world leaders to condemn hatred of Jews and protect Jewish communities, while warning that the definition's examples were being used against criticism of Israel. [cite:def_hrw_un2023] The definition's own lead drafter, Kenneth Stern, said in March 2025 that the US administration was "absolutely weaponizing antisemitism." [cite:ow11_npr_stern]

What adoption does in law

In the United States the definition reaches law through civil-rights enforcement. A December 2019 executive order told federal agencies to consider it when enforcing Title VI of the Civil Rights Act. On 29 January 2025 a new order, "Additional Measures to Combat Anti-Semitism," expanded that one: it told agencies to report on every tool available against hatred of Jews, ordered an inventory of Title VI complaints since 7 October 2023, asked officials to familiarize universities with the grounds for refusing visas, and said institutions could monitor and report activities by foreign students and staff. [cite:def_wh_eo2025] The Antisemitism Awareness Act, which would write the definition into Title VI by statute, passed the House in May 2024 but stalled in a Senate committee in April 2025, and was not law when this edition was checked in November 2026. [cite:ow2_jlink_aaa] [cite:politifact_aaa_2024]

The case of Mahmoud Khalil, a Columbia graduate and lawful permanent resident arrested in March 2025 after negotiating on behalf of campus protesters, shows how the policy reaches individuals. An immigration judge found him removable in April 2025 solely on the Secretary of State's determination that his presence harmed US foreign policy; a federal judge found that policy likely unconstitutional, and he was released in June 2025. [cite:de34_tribune_khalil] [cite:de34_ccr_khalil] As of October 2026 he had petitioned the Supreme Court after an appeals court ruled that he could not bring his claims before a judge until immigration proceedings ended; he has not been charged with any crime. [cite:de40_abc_khalil]

Where it has been reversed

Adoption has not run only one way. On 2 January 2026, his first day in office, New York's mayor, Zohran Mamdani, revoked his predecessor's executive order adopting the IHRA definition, together with an order barring city agencies from boycotting Israel, saying that "a number of leading Jewish organizations have immense concerns" about the definition. He kept the city's Office to Combat Antisemitism. Israel's consul general in New York said the decisions posed "an immediate threat" to Jewish New Yorkers; the New York chapter of the Council on American-Islamic Relations welcomed them. [cite:def_abc_mamdani2026] It is a prominent reversal, and the group that counts adoptions noted it in its own report. [cite:def_cam_2025]

What the record shows

Two facts stand side by side. The definition that lists criticism of Israel among its examples has been adopted by 26 of the 27 European Union governments, the United States and many universities, faster than any scholarly agreement about it has formed. And the people warning against it include the definition's own drafter, the leading human-rights organizations and Jewish and Israeli groups. The adoption figures come from an organization that campaigns for adoption; the opposition comes from organizations that campaign against it. A reader can weigh both. What cannot be said, on this record, is that the question has been settled.
