# Volume II, Prelude (1694–1896): research notes

Verified 2026-10-09. Every claim in `rd0.json` comes from a page fetched in this session. WebFetch returns a model-written summary of each page, not the raw text, so quoted phrases are short. Check any quotation that will be printed against the original page before publication.

**Limits of this pass.** The shared web-search budget ran out partway through, and pages could be fetched only after a search had surfaced them. So several planned topics could not be sourced (see "Not reached" at the end). Several claims rest on Wikipedia summaries of named scholarship; those are marked `reported` and the scholar is named in the claim so the writer can attribute.

## 1. The Bank of England and the national debt (`rd0_boe_1694`)
**Agree:** The Bank's own museum page and its 1970 booklet agree: chartered 27 July 1694; £1.2 million lent to William III for the war with France; 8% interest; the Bank as the government's banker and manager of the national debt. The museum gives 1,268 subscribers in 11 days.
**Disagree:** Who founded it. The museum credits William Paterson's proposal; the reference summary (citing Roseveare) credits Charles Montagu with devising it. The two accounts fit together.
**Not verified:** The museum's 'about £200 million today' is the Bank's own estimate. The £1 billion debt peak in 1815 (more than 200% of GDP) comes from a reference summary citing ukpublicspending.co.uk, not from the Bank or the Treasury.

## 2. Palestine, 1799–1840 (`rd0_ottoman_1799_1840`)
**Agree:** Napoleon besieged Acre in 1799 and did not reach Jerusalem. Ibrahim Pasha invaded at the end of October 1831. The 1834 revolt followed the conscription order of April 1834. The rebels entered Jerusalem on 19–20 May, and the first day's dead were 50, 16 and 5. Ottoman rule returned in 1840 after the Royal Navy's bombardment of Acre on 3 November 1840.
**Disagree:**
- The 1799 'proclamation to the Jews' is the main dispute. The 1940 German text is called a forgery by Schwarzfuchs, Laurens, Schechter and Popkin, and Akadem names the Frankists. Gichon reads a real intent; Schur reads propaganda; the JVL calls it 'debated'. A short May 1799 newspaper report of an invitation to Jews is not itself in dispute.
- The 1834 revolt as an early sign of Palestinian identity: Kimmerling and Migdal argue for it; Morris argues against. Shamir and Manna offer framings of their own.
- Hebron's toll: Rood gives 630 men sent to Egypt; the reference summary gives about 500 killed and 750 conscripted.
**Not verified:** Morris's argument is known here only through the reference summary. The overall 1834 toll ('thousands') and the figure of 10,000 deported come from the summary alone. The JVL gives 22 May 1799 for the retreat from Acre, the same date as the Gazette report; one of the two may be a conflation.
**Name caution:** Tahir Afandi al-Husayni (the mufti in 1834, in Rood) and Muhammad Tahir al-Husayni (the mufti from the 1880s, in Beška) are named separately in the sources. Do not merge them.

## 3. Tanzimat and the first parliament (`rd0_tanzimat_parliament`)
**Agree:** Gülhane on 3 November 1839; the Reform Edict on 18 February 1856; the constitution on 23 December 1876; parliament opened 19 March 1877; suspended February 1878. Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi was Jerusalem's only deputy, sat in the opposition, and was expelled from Istanbul in 1878 (PalQuest and Jerusalem Story agree).
**Disagree:** Chamber size (120 with 14 Arabs per PalQuest; 115 per Wikipedia). Suspension date (13 or 14 February 1878). Al-Khalidi's mayoralty (1870–74 per PalQuest; from September 1865 for nine years per Jerusalem Story).
**Not verified:** Whether non-Jerusalem districts (Nablus, Acre) sent their own deputies in 1877. Jerusalem Story calls al-Khalidi 'the only member representing Palestine'. The Wikipedia claim that he was chamber president is tagged 'citation needed' and is left out.

## 4. Land law (`rd0_land_law_1858_1867`)
**Agree:** The 1858 Land Code and Tabu law began registration and mapping, and land concentrated in urban effendi families (Kark; Porath via Wikipedia). The 1867 law put foreign landowners on equal terms with Ottoman subjects (quoted in the 1898 US dispatch). That concentration eased purchase by Jewish and German Christian buyers (Kark: 52 and 15 estate cores).
**Disagree:** Emphasis rather than fact. The Marmara thesis frames 1867 as a loophole Jews exploited; Kark frames the laws as a privatization that eased purchase.
**Not verified:** Only Kark's abstract was read, not the full chapter. The Marmara thesis's author and year were not shown on the page.

## 5. Ottoman restrictions (`rd0_ottoman_restrictions`)
**Agree:** From 1882 the Porte tried to keep foreign Jews from settling in Palestine. It admitted them as pilgrims for one month, later three. The rules were weakened by the Capitulations and consular protection, and the US and Britain protested on grounds of religious equality. The US and British diplomatic documents (1887, 1888, 1893, 1898) are primary and consistent with Barın's thesis.
**Disagree:** When the land-sale ban began: 1892 (Wikipedia citing Porath and Ocak), 1893 and the following April (Barın), or the note of 27 March 1894 (US dispatch). A German firm said in March 1893 that a ban had been 'abolished some time ago' (Beška). The red card: Barın's footnote dates it to 1900, which is later than the 1880s pilgrim rules.
**Not verified:** The text of the June/July 1882 decisions; the 'Sultan's permit' of May 1882 (Mandel via Wikipedia).

## 6. Arab responses (`rd0_arab_responses`)
**Agree:** The first opposition came from peasants. A petition or telegram of June 1891 from Jerusalem notables asked the Porte to stop Russian-Jewish immigration and land purchase. Three sources independent of each other agree on 1891: a reference timeline citing Mandel, a Jewish Agency overview, and a 1978 review of Mandel. The Jerusalem mufti, Muhammad Tahir al-Husayni, worked against land sales from at least 1893 (Beška, from German consular files).
**Disagree:** Whether early opposition was political (the Jewish Agency doubts it; Beška and Mandel document petitions and an official campaign). Petition or telegram; Jerusalem alone or with Jaffa.
**Not verified:** The 1891 text, its signatories and date (24 June comes only from the timeline); the 1886 Petah Tikva clash (no source reached); Najib al-Hajj's 1895 article (seen only in an excerpt of a related paper). Left out: the 1978 review's claim about Haj Amin al-Husseini's father in 1899 (single press source, outside the period).

## 7. Old Yishuv and First Aliyah (`rd0_yishuv_first_aliyah`)
**Agree:** The Jewish population was about 23,000–24,000 in 1880, more than half of it in Jerusalem (13,900, Barın). The settlement founding dates are given as the First Aliyah summary has them.
**Disagree:** First Aliyah size: 20,000–30,000 (Barın), 25,000 (Jewish Agency figure via Wikipedia), up to 60,000 (Shapira), with 50–70% leaving (Shapira). End-of-period population: about 50,000 by 1903 (Barın), about 55,000 (uncited), 80,000 by 1907 (Elia).
**Not verified:** Yemenite numbers ('a few hundred', uncited). Whether the 1870 AIU school is Mikveh Israel (the archive record does not say). Montefiore, the halukka and Old Yishuv institutions: not reached.

## 8. Suez, 1875 (`rd0_suez_1875`)
**Agree:** £4 million advanced by the London Rothschilds while Parliament was not sitting; 44% of the shares (Hefler) or 'nearly half' (Disraeli); Parliament's approval in February 1876; Disraeli's stated reason was the route to India.
**Disagree:** The cost of the advance: critics said 15 per cent; Disraeli denied it. No fetched source gives the commission.
**Not verified:** The share count (176,602) and the exact price (£3,976,582) often cited elsewhere; the Montagu Corry anecdote.
