A Church With a State Attached: How Vatican City Was Made

Most states begin with a territory and later acquire a government to run it. Vatican City began the other way round. The Holy See, the central government of the Catholic Church, was a recognized actor in European diplomacy for centuries before 1929, and for most of that time it also ruled a large territory, the Papal States, across central Italy. In 1860 the new Kingdom of Italy took Romagna, and in 1870 it occupied and annexed Latium and Rome itself. [cite:dv3_wiki_lateran] The popes did not accept the loss. Italy's Law of Guarantees of 1871 offered the pope the use of the Vatican and Lateran palaces and a yearly income, and the Holy See refused it; for nearly sixty years each pope described himself as a prisoner in the Vatican. [cite:dv3_wiki_lateran] This was the "Roman Question," and it is the reason a church government has a state attached to it today.

The 1929 settlement. The question was closed on 11 February 1929 at the Lateran Palace, when Benito Mussolini, signing for King Victor Emmanuel III, and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, signing for Pope Pius XI, concluded three instruments: a treaty that created the state, a concordat that governed the Church's position inside Italy, and a financial convention that settled the money. The Italian Parliament ratified them on 7 June 1929. [cite:dv3_wiki_lateran] Under the convention Italy paid 750 million lire at once and handed over government bonds with a nominal value of 1,000 million lire more, in settlement of the Holy See's claims for what it had lost in 1870. [cite:dv3_wiki_lateran] The Holy See's own negotiator was Francesco Pacelli, brother of the future Pius XII, and the money was placed under a new Special Administration run by a lay financier, Bernardino Nogara. [cite:nybooks_pope_who_tried] [cite:wiki_bernardino_nogara] Part of how the Vatican works today, and the subject of a later section, begins with that payment.

The treaty's own words show what kind of state was being made. In Article 2 Italy recognized "the sovereignty of the Holy See in international matters" as an attribute inherent in its nature, not something Italy was granting. In Article 3 it recognized the Holy See's "full ownership, exclusive dominion, and sovereign authority and jurisdiction" over the Vatican, and in Article 4 it promised that the Italian government would not intervene there. [cite:dv3_lateran_text] The order matters. The sovereignty belongs to the Holy See, the government; the city is the territory over which that government exercises it. That is why diplomats are accredited to the Holy See and not to Vatican City, and why the Holy See can be a party to a treaty while the city is a member of the Universal Postal Union. [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex]

A state that leans on its neighbour. The treaty also shows how little of an ordinary state's machinery the new territory was expected to carry by itself. Italy undertook to supply "an adequate water supply," rail links, and connections by telegraph, telephone, wireless, broadcasting and post (Article 6). [cite:dv3_lateran_text] St Peter's Square was to stay open to the public and remain "subject to supervision by the Italian police authorities" (Article 3). [cite:dv3_lateran_text] Goods for Vatican institutions were to cross Italy free of customs (Article 20). And crimes committed inside Vatican City would be prosecuted by Italy whenever the Holy See asked it to (Article 22). [cite:dv3_lateran_text] The arrangement still works that way. The city has no prison beyond a few cells for pre-trial detention; people convicted by its courts serve their sentences in Italian prisons, at the Vatican's expense. [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city] Visitors walk in from Rome without passport checks, the square is normally patrolled by Italian police, and the city has no airport, only a heliport and a short railway spur that joins the Italian network. [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city]

Some of what the treaty granted lies outside the walls altogether. Articles 13 to 16 recognize the Holy See's ownership of the basilicas of St John Lateran, Santa Maria Maggiore and St Paul, and of the papal palace at Castel Gandolfo, give those buildings immunities like those of an embassy, and exempt them from expropriation. [cite:dv3_lateran_text] The Holy See's property in Rome is therefore a scatter of protected enclaves inside an Italian city, not a single block.

What it gave up. The treaty also bound the Holy See. Article 24 says the Holy See will take no part in temporal conflicts between other states, and that Vatican City is "considered as neutral and inviolable territory." [cite:dv3_lateran_text] Article 26 declares the Roman Question settled and recognizes the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital. [cite:dv3_lateran_text] The neutrality pledge is worth holding on to. It reappears, in nearly the same spirit, in the Holy See's 1993 agreement with Israel, and it is part of the answer to the question this part asks at the end: what the Holy See was able to do, and chose not to do, in Palestine.

Who belongs to it. Vatican citizenship is not inherited. It goes with an office: cardinals resident in Rome, the Holy See's diplomats, and others who live in the city because of their work, together with spouses and children living with them. It normally ends when the appointment ends, and anyone left without another nationality becomes Italian automatically under the Lateran Treaty. [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city] On 26 June 2023 there were 618 citizens, 104 of them Swiss Guards, and 764 inhabitants. [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] The state levies no taxes. It lives on donations and on what it sells: stamps, coins, medals, publications and museum tickets. [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city] It has used the euro under agreements with the European Union, the first concluded on 29 December 2000; the current one, signed on 17 December 2009 and in force from 1 January 2010, lets it give legal tender status to the euro and issue a capped number of its own coins, which Italy's state mint strikes, and obliges it to adopt the EU's rules against money laundering, fraud and counterfeiting. [cite:dv3_eu_monetary] It runs its own post office (founded 13 February 1929), telephone service, radio and internet domain. [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city]

Why this is not a city in the ordinary sense. An ordinary city, whether Rome around it or Madrid, Chicago or Atlanta, draws its powers from a state above it and can have them changed by that state's legislature. Vatican City draws its powers from no one above it. Its ruler is chosen by the cardinals for life and answers to no electorate; its legal system takes canon law, the Church's own law, as its first source; and the only outside body that can alter its basic position is the Holy See's treaty partner, Italy, and only by agreement. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] At the same time it is far less self-sufficient than any city of its size would be inside a country, because it rests on Italian water, Italian prisons, Italian police in its main square and an Italian mint. Part One described the city's sovereignty as complete in law and tiny in scale. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] The 1929 texts add a third feature: it is a sovereign state built to be dependent on its neighbour for almost everything except authority.

The concordat after 1984. The concordat signed alongside the treaty made Catholicism Italy's only state religion and set out the Church's legal position inside Italy. It was rewritten in 1984: the new text, signed on 18 February 1984 and in force from 3 June 1985, ended Catholicism's status as the sole state religion and replaced direct state support for the Church with the otto per mille, under which taxpayers assign 0.8 percent of their income tax to a recognized religious body or to a state programme. [cite:dv3_wiki_lateran] [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] That change belongs to Italy's relations with the Church, not to the Vatican's statehood, but it is a reminder of where the Holy See's real weight lies. Its sovereignty is measured in hectares; its reach is measured in the institutions of a church that operates in nearly every country, which is the subject of the next section.
