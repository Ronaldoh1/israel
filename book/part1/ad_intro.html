This volume is about a promise most Americans were taught before they could vote: that anyone who works hard and plays by the rules can own a home, hold a secure job, see a doctor when sick, retire without fear and give their children a better life than their own. It sets that promise, as schools, presidents of both parties and popular culture have taught it, beside the system that was actually built to deliver it, one mechanism at a time: wages and the minimum wage, unions and the shareholder turn, housing and zoning, schools and student loans, health insurance and medical debt, credit and fees, pensions and 401(k)s, taxes and benefit cliffs, wealth and inheritance, the courts and the poverty line, the lobbying that writes the rules, and the belief that keeps the promise alive. For each it asks who designed the mechanism and when, how it works step by step, who gains, who pays, and what the documented outcome is.

The book tests a thesis set out on the Sophia platform: that the Dream now works as an illusion, a story told to keep people working inside a system that extracts from them. It tests that thesis fairly. Where a mechanism really does move money from those who have less to those who have more, the book shows it with the numbers, as a money trail from input to who pays. Where the taught version holds, it says so, and early: Part Two sets out what has genuinely improved, and every part carries a passage on where the mechanism works as promised. Where the record is disputed, it shows both sides with their sources. Interpretations, including the illusion thesis itself, appear only in passages labelled as interpretation, each followed by the strongest readings against it.

The record it assembles is mixed, and specific. About nine in ten Americans born in 1940 went on to earn more than their parents; about half of those born in the early 1980s did. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] The chance of climbing from the bottom fifth to the top has held near 8 or 9 in 100, and is about a quarter as high for Black children as for white. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] Where federal law was built to keep the promise, it largely did: poverty among Americans over 65 fell from 35.2 per cent in 1959 to 9.9 per cent, and the share without health insurance is less than half what it was in 2009. [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:us16_census_2025] [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] Where costs outran incomes, the promise narrowed: a typical home now costs about 5.0 years of median household income against 3.2 in the late 1980s, and the largest federal college grant covers about a quarter of a year at a public university, against more than three-quarters in 1975–76. [cite:ad6_jchs_2018] [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell]

The book ends with a verdict in the open. Seven promises are scored on a scorecard whose every rule and weight is printed beside it, so that a reader who weighs things differently can recompute the result; the total is the book's editorial synthesis, not a discovered fact. The final part then sets out the levers with documented effects, from the 2021 child tax credit to automatic retirement enrolment and Medicaid expansion, with the evidence for and against each, and answers the volume's question in three registers: what the record documents, what it makes probable, and what it does not support. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad18_ucd_wherry] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26]

Each part stands on its own and links to the parts that explain the figures it uses. Readers who want the numbers first can start with Part Two, which defines the measures used throughout (absolute and relative mobility, income and wealth, median and mean, real and nominal dollars), or with Part Seventeen, which follows three ordinary households through the whole machine in a single year.
