From Prayer to Program: The Thinkers and Groups Before Herzl, 1840–1890

The debate over the Three Oaths turned on whether people might bring about the return themselves. A common account has Zionism springing fully formed from the mind of Theodor Herzl in 1896. The record shows something slower and more interesting. The idea passed through stages over half a century: a hope held in prayer, then an argument by two rabbis that people might begin the return themselves, then a political program written by a secular socialist and a doctor, then small societies, a conference and a handful of pioneers, and finally a name. Herzl came last. What he added was not the idea but the organization.

For centuries before any movement, the return to Zion lived in the liturgy. It was prayed for daily and awaited as a messianic event, an act of God rather than a matter for politics. It was a hope, not a plan. That distinction matters for everything that follows, because the religious argument over the Three Oaths, set out in the previous section, turns on exactly this question: whether the return is something people may bring about, or something they must wait for.

The first break came from inside that religious world. Yehuda Alkalai, a Sephardi rabbi in Semlin, urged practical settlement in the land as a step toward redemption. He did so after the Damascus affair of 1840, a blood-libel case. [cite:w_alkalai] Zvi Hirsch Kalischer, a rabbi in Thorn, made a parallel case in a book of 1862: redemption, he argued, could begin by natural means, through settling and farming the land. [cite:w_kalischer] These two were the first to argue that human settlement could begin redemption. [cite:w_alkalai][cite:w_kalischer] In the terms of the older debate, they stood on the side that treated action in the land as part of a religious process rather than a forcing of the end. The earliest step from prayer toward program was taken by rabbis and argued in religious terms.

The step into secular politics came from two very different men, whose work is told in full in the next section [scene:s02|the idea appears, 1862–1885]. In the same year as Kalischer's book, 1862, the secular socialist Moses Hess published Rome and Jerusalem. [cite:wiki_moses_hess] Twenty years later, after the pogroms of 1881, the Odessa physician Leon Pinsker published Auto-Emancipation (1882), which argued that Jews needed a territory of their own, not necessarily Palestine. [cite:wiki_auto_emancipation] Between them, a socialist and a doctor turned a religious hope into a national program.

Ideas became groups in the 1880s. Local societies calling themselves Hovevei Zion, "Lovers of Zion", spread across the Russian Empire. In 1884 they met in conference at Kattowitz, under Pinsker's leadership. [cite:w_hovevei] The record holds a small irony here. Pinsker's own pamphlet had not insisted on Palestine, and he and other early organizers discussed several territories before the movement settled on it. [cite:wiki_leon_pinsker] Yet the man whose argument was territorially open chaired the conference of societies named for Zion. Two years earlier, in 1882, a student group called BILU had sent its first pioneers to the land. [cite:w_bilu] The settlements they and others founded, and the numbers who stayed and left, are counted in [scene:rd0_aliyah|the Old Yishuv and the First Aliyah]. Finally, in 1890, Nathan Birnbaum coined the word "Zionism". [cite:w_birnbaum] By then there were societies, a conference and colonists. There was still no single organization.

Why did people move from prayer to program in these decades? The motives recorded across these writers are four: persecution and pogroms; the failure of emancipation to end antisemitism; the age of European nationalism; and religious longing. A fifth current pushed from outside the Jewish world altogether: Christian restorationism in Britain and America. That story is told in [scene:bc_s05|restoration before Zionism]. The motives did not all point the same way. Alkalai and Kalischer argued from redemption; Hess from nationalism and socialism; Pinsker from physical safety. What they shared was the conclusion that waiting was no longer enough.

The familiar account deserves its strongest form. Herzl's role was real and decisive in one respect: he did not invent the idea, but he gave a scattered movement an organization, a diplomacy and a bank, beginning in 1897. Before him there were groups without a single organization; after him there was a movement with its own organization, its own diplomacy and its own bank. That is a large contribution. It is a different claim from saying that the idea began with him, and the record of the half-century before 1896 does not support that second claim.

The record available here leaves several things unsettled. It names Kalischer's 1862 book but does not give its title or its argument beyond its central claim. It dates Alkalai's call after the Damascus affair of 1840, but does not establish how directly the one produced the other beyond that sequence in time. It gives no membership figures for the Hovevei Zion societies and no account of what the Kattowitz conference decided. And it does not show whether Hess, Pinsker or Herzl read the two rabbis; the four stages followed one another in time, but the record does not trace a single line of influence running through them.
