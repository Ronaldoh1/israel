Peel, 1937: A Partition Map and a Letter to Amos

In July 1937 a British royal commission produced the first official map dividing Palestine. The Palestine Royal Commission, chaired by Lord Peel, proposed to end the Mandate and create two states. The Jewish state would consist of Galilee, the Jezreel Valley and most of the Beth-Shean valley, and the coastal plain from near the Lebanese border south to Be'er Tuvia: about 17 percent of the country. The Arab state, about three-quarters of it, would take in the hill country of Samaria and Judea and the Negev, and would be united with Transjordan. Britain would keep a mandate zone of about 8 percent, covering Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth and a corridor from Jerusalem to the sea at Jaffa. [cite:gi2_jvl_peel] [cite:gi2_ej_partition] The commission also proposed an "exchange of population", compulsory in the last resort, which would have moved far more Arabs than Jews; that recommendation, and the record of how the Zionist leadership received it, are set out in [scene:pl_s01|Peel's "exchange of population"] and are not repeated here. The commission's work and the revolt that prompted it are in [scene:s07a6|the Peel Commission].

For the question of territorial aims, the Peel map is a test. It offered a Jewish state on less than a fifth of western Palestine and none of the east bank. How each party answered it is a direct record of what each would accept. [cite:gi2_jvl_peel]

Three answers

The Twentieth Zionist Congress met in Zurich from 3 to 16 August 1937. It called the Peel scheme "unacceptable", principally because the proposed state was too small, but it authorised the Zionist Executive to negotiate with the British government over a Jewish state, with any resulting arrangement to be submitted to a newly elected Congress. [cite:gi2_ej_partition] [cite:gi2_israeled_20th] Most of the Yishuv and the movement thus accepted partition in principle after a vigorous controversy, while rejecting this partition. [cite:gi2_ej_partition] The Revisionists' New Zionist Organization, outside the Congress since 1935, "opposed and combated" partition outright, because it gave up the claim to the whole land. [cite:gi2_ej_revisionists] The Arab Higher Committee rejected the plan, warning that "partition would create in Palestine two neighboring hostile states." [cite:gi2_jvl_peel]

The plan did not survive. A technical commission under Sir John Woodhead found it unworkable, and in November 1938 the British government told Parliament that its Partition Commission had advised "unanimously against the adoption of the scheme of partition outlined by the Royal Commission" and that partition was impracticable. [cite:gi2_unispal_cmd5893] That episode is in [scene:rd4_woodhead|Woodhead and St James's Palace].

The letter

The single document most often quoted on what the mainstream leadership intended is private. On 5 October 1937 David Ben-Gurion, chairman of the Jewish Agency Executive, wrote to his son Amos. The handwritten original is held in the IDF Archive; the English translation most cited was obtained from the Ben-Gurion Archives and published by the Institute for Palestine Studies. [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter]

Much of the letter is not in dispute. Ben-Gurion argued for accepting partition as a stage, not a settlement: a Jewish state was "not the end but the beginning." "The establishment of a state, even if only on a portion of the land," he wrote, would be "a powerful boost to our historical endeavors to liberate the entire country." [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter] He wanted the Negev, which Peel had given to the Arab state, arguing that the Arabs "already have an abundance of deserts but not of manpower, financial resources, or creative initiative", and he wrote that force might be needed to secure Jewish settlement there. [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter] On this reading the letter is evidence that the leader of the movement's majority accepted a small state while intending, in time, to enlarge it. It is not evidence of a border he named, and "the entire country" here meant the land of the Mandate, not the biblical frontiers.

One sentence is disputed, and the dispute concerns what the manuscript says. In the original, several words in the middle of a sentence are crossed out. Read without them, the sentence says "We must expel the Arabs"; restored, it says the opposite. [cite:gi2_comm_morris] [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter]

The first reading came from Shabtai Teveth's English version of 1985, which Benny Morris quoted in 1988: "We must expel Arabs and take their places." [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter] [cite:gi2_comm_morris] Efraim Karsh argued in 2000 that the quotation left out the crossed-out words and that restoring them reversed the meaning; writing in 2008, he gave the original as "We do not wish and do not need to expel Arabs and take their place," and said it "unequivocally settled the matter." [cite:gi2_hnn_karsh] [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter] Morris, in an exchange in Commentary in 2011, said he had consulted the original, that the crossing-out was in it, and that Ben-Gurion "rarely made corrections," while others had suggested the deletion was made later by another hand. He said he had since either dropped the quotation or used the version the restoration allows, and called the focus on it "nothing more than (an essentially mendacious) red herring," because, he argued, Ben-Gurion endorsed transfer elsewhere, "either 'voluntarily' or by compulsion." [cite:gi2_comm_morris] [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter]

This book does not decide between the readings. It records that the undisputed text supports partition as a stage toward "the entire country"; that the disputed sentence cannot carry any finding on its own; and that the wider question it is used for, Ben-Gurion's views on transfer in 1937–38, rests on other documents, discussed in [scene:pl_s01|Peel's "exchange of population"]. A separate quotation sometimes confused with this one, "The Arabs will have to go, but one needs an opportune moment for making it happen, such as a war", comes from Ilan Pappé's writing of 2006, not from the letter. The Journal of Palestine Studies, which had published it, acknowledged in 2012 that its punctuation presented a paraphrase as a quotation, while rejecting the charge that it misrepresented Ben-Gurion's position of the late 1930s. [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter]

"Expand to the whole Land of Israel"

The same view, partition as a stage, appears in remarks Ben-Gurion is reported to have made to the Jewish Agency Executive in June 1938: that after building a strong force "we will abolish the partition of the country and we will expand to the whole Land of Israel." The wording comes from the historians Nur Masalha (1992) and Tom Segev (2000), citing the Executive's minutes; this book has not checked the minutes. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] It is consistent with the undisputed passages of the Amos letter and should be read with the same limits: it is a statement of long-range aim by the leader of the mainstream, made privately, about the territory of the Mandate.

What Peel settles

Three points can be stated as findings. The mainstream Zionist movement accepted the principle of partition in 1937 and negotiated on a state far smaller than its 1919 claim. [cite:gi2_ej_partition] [cite:gi2_israeled_20th] The Revisionists refused it because they held to both banks. [cite:gi2_ej_revisionists] And the leader of the mainstream wrote privately that a partial state would be a beginning, not an end. [cite:gi2_wiki_bgletter] Whether that last view was a programme of expansion or a negotiating posture is an argument, with both cases resting on documents. The argument for a programme points to the letter, the 1938 remarks and the later expansion of 1948. The argument against points to what the same leadership did when it held power and the choice was real: it accepted the 1947 partition plan, signed the 1949 armistice lines, and in 1957, under American pressure, withdrew from Sinai and Gaza, as [scene:gi3_borders|Part Three] shows.
