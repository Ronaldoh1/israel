From RAGE to Schedule Policy/Career

In 2012 Curtis Yarvin proposed that a new American leader begin by retiring every government employee. In 2020 a president created a new category of federal job whose holders could be removed with fewer protections. In 2021 J.D. Vance said a future president should fire every mid-level civil servant. In 2026 the government finished the rule that brought the 2020 category back and moved its first 8,000 senior officials into it. Commentators have often run these four things together as one plan carried out in stages. This section sets them side by side, clause by clause, separates what each actually says from what it is said to say, and asks which links in the chain are documented. It also places the change beside the Supreme Court's ruling of June 2026 on the president's power to remove officials, which moved the same way at the top of the government. Status is as of October 2026.

Three proposals

Yarvin's version came first and went furthest. At a technology "unconference" called BIL in 2012 he told the audience that Americans would have to "get over their dictator phobia" and that the first step of a takeover was to "Retire All Government Employees," which he abbreviated RAGE. [cite:de6_tnr_duran] [cite:de6_pein_2014] He returned to the idea in an interview with the New York Times in January 2025, saying the government should be treated "like a company that goes out of business, but sort of more so," and that its former officials should be treated as ordinary people who had served a bad regime. [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] RAGE is a proposal to remove everyone, not to sort anyone by loyalty, and the program it belongs to, a single executive answerable to no electorate, is set out in [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six].

The second version was an executive order. In October 2020 President Trump signed Executive Order 13957, which created "Schedule F" for career positions of a "confidential, policy-determining, policy-making, or policy-advocating character" that are "not normally subject to change as a result of a Presidential transition." [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] A schedule, in the federal civil service, is a category of job with its own rules for hiring and removal; most career staff are in the "competitive service," where removal for poor performance or misconduct follows set procedures and can be appealed to the Merit Systems Protection Board, an independent tribunal. Schedule F took those procedures away from the jobs placed in it. President Biden revoked the order in January 2021, before it had been fully put into effect, and in April 2024 the Office of Personnel Management (OPM) adopted a rule guaranteeing that employees moved into such a category kept their right of appeal. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] [cite:de30_fedsmith_order]

The third version was spoken. In September 2021, then a Senate candidate, Vance told the podcast Jack Murphy Live that a future President Trump should "fire every single mid-level bureaucrat, every civil servant in the administrative state" and "replace them with our people." [cite:de5_nextgov_vance] In the same interview he named Yarvin: "There's this guy, Curtis Yarvin, who's written about some of these things," adding, "I'm sympathetic to that project. But another option is that we should just seize the administrative state for our own purposes." [cite:de5_wbur_vance] The second sentence matters. Vance described Yarvin's project and then offered a different one: not retiring the state, as RAGE proposed, but taking control of it and staffing it with allies. The place of the remark in his life is set out in [scene:de12_the_thiel_years|Part Twelve].

The rule and the order

On his first day back in office, 20 January 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14171, "Restoring Accountability to Policy-Influencing Positions Within the Federal Workforce." It reinstated the 2020 order with amendments, renamed Schedule F "Schedule Policy/Career," and revoked Biden's revocation. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] OPM published its final rule on 6 February 2026, and it took effect on 8 March. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] [cite:us2_govexec_schedf] Under it, positions judged to influence policy lose the statutory procedures for removing employees for poor performance and misconduct, and the 2024 appeal-rights rule is rescinded; the president, not OPM, decides which positions move. Holders keep competitive hiring, trial periods and veterans' preference, and the rule forbids patronage and loyalty tests. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] [cite:us2_opm_schedpc] Officials estimated that up to 50,000 employees could eventually be covered. [cite:us2_govexec_schedf]

The first move came on 3 June 2026. Executive Order 14410, "Implementing Schedule Policy/Career in the Excepted Service," placed the positions listed in its appendix into the new schedule, declared them "exempted from the adverse action procedures," let agencies remove their holders for unacceptable performance or misconduct by written notice, and stated that they "will be filled based on merit and not political affiliation." Its stated purpose is that the president "relies on subordinates in the executive branch to help him faithfully execute the laws." [cite:de30_wh_eo14410] About 8,000 employees were moved, nearly all at GS-15, the top grade of the general civil service: heads of policy offices and their chiefs of staff, regional directors, programme managers and senior communications staff. For comparison, the government has about 4,000 political appointees. [cite:de30_npr_8000]

Each side stated its case in plain terms. OPM's director, Scott Kupor, said: "This is very much about accountability," and called it "a restoration, in our mind, of the democratic process"; he said no loyalty tests would be used and whistleblower protections remained, though enforcing the ban on political tests falls to the agencies themselves. [cite:de30_npr_8000] He also said the employees moved could be "removed effectively at will." [cite:de30_fedsmith_order] Donald Moynihan, a public-policy scholar at the University of Michigan, warned that "It creates bubbles around policymakers," and the president of the largest federal union called the order, if it stands, "one of the largest acts of political corruption in American history." [cite:de30_npr_8000] [cite:de30_fedsmith_order]

What the comparison shows, and what it does not

Read clause by clause, the three are not one plan. RAGE removes the whole state; the 2026 rule removes procedures from about 0.4 per cent of the civil service and keeps merit hiring. Vance's 2021 remark sits between them: it shares RAGE's mass firing and Schedule Policy/Career's aim of a staff responsive to the elected president, but on the documented record it was a podcast answer, not a policy he has since put in writing. The resemblance is real and runs in one direction, toward a civil service more easily directed by the president. [cite:de30_npr_8000] [cite:de5_nextgov_vance]

The line of descent is another matter. Schedule F has documented defenders, and none of them cites Yarvin. James Sherk, a former special assistant to President Trump who later worked at the America First Policy Institute, argued for it in 2023 on grounds of accountability: "Every federal employee should serve at the pleasure of the president." [cite:de30_govexec_sherk] That claim is broader than the rule that followed, and it is Sherk's, not Yarvin's. The only reported link between Yarvin and the people who designed the 2025 personnel changes is an anonymous adviser's remark to the Washington Post that everyone designing policy had read him. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] Yarvin himself said in 2025 that the administration was "barely scratching the surface" and that his influence ran "more through the kids in the administration." [cite:de6_cnn_2025] Neither remark is a document showing that the order or the rule came from his writing. The book's rule holds here: rhyme is not provenance ([scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One]).

The removal power at the top

The civil-service rule changed who can be removed below the political level. A ruling of the Supreme Court changed it above. On 29 June 2026, in Trump v. Slaughter, the Court overruled its 1935 decision in Humphrey's Executor by six votes to three and held that the president may remove members of the Federal Trade Commission at will; it left open offices traditionally outside the executive branch and named the Federal Reserve, and the same day it declined, in Trump v. Cook, to let the president remove a Fed governor pending her case. [cite:de0_slaughter] Taken together, the ruling and the rule extend the president's control over the people who carry out the law, at the commissions and in the career service. Both moved through channels that remain open to challenge, a court's judgment and a rule under existing statute, which is why [scene:de21_the_ending|Part Twenty-One] describes them as a real shift of power toward the presidency, documented as such, rather than an executive without a veto. [cite:de0_slaughter] [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412]

The checks still in play

The rule has been in court since the day the 2025 order was signed. The National Treasury Employees Union sued in Washington on 20 January 2025, and in March 2026 the judge allowed it to amend its complaint once positions were actually moved; a separate suit by Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility and other unions, joined by the American Federation of Government Employees, challenges the order, the rule and the reclassification in federal court in Maryland. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] [cite:de30_afge_lit] No court had blocked the conversions as of October 2026, and the June order is in effect. [cite:de30_afge_lit] Congress has a bill before it, the Saving the Civil Service Act, which would limit the president's power to reclassify positions; it had not passed. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] Whether the 8,000 are removed for performance, as the order's text provides, or for their views, as its critics fear, is a question of practice that the record does not yet answer; [scene:us2_integrity|the library's account of the civil service] sets the same rule against the wider cuts, and [scene:de21_the_ending|Part Twenty-One] places it among what DOGE left behind.
