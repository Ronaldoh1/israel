The Serpent Lifted Up

In the Book of Numbers the people complain in the wilderness, "fiery serpents" bite them and many die. God tells Moses to make a serpent and set it on a pole, and anyone bitten who looks at it lives; Moses makes it of bronze. [cite:cn_esv_num21jn3] [cite:sp_wiki_nehushtan] The image is not condemned. It is the means of healing.

It did not stay a means. The Second Book of Kings records that King Hezekiah, in his reform of Judah's worship, removed the shrines, cut down the sacred post and "broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made", because the people of Israel had been making offerings to it; it was called Nehushtan. [cite:sp_esv_jn3_2k18] [cite:sp_wiki_nehushtan] Serpent objects have been excavated at Bronze Age Canaanite sites, including the temple at Hazor, Megiddo, Gezer and Shechem, so the image belonged to the land's older religion as well as to the story of Moses. [cite:sp_wiki_nehushtan]

The New Testament takes the image back. In John's Gospel Jesus says, "as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up." [cite:sp_esv_jn3_2k18] The serpent that Hezekiah broke as an idol becomes, a few centuries later, a figure for the crucifixion.

The Christians who took the serpent's side

From the second century, groups of Christians that their opponents called Ophites, from the Greek word for serpent, read Eden the other way round. Britannica describes them as Gnostic sects, among them the Naassenes and the Cainites, that set a spiritual God against a corrupt material world and saw the God of the Hebrew Bible as a lesser maker, a demiurge. For them the serpent of Genesis was a liberator, who gave humans the knowledge of good and evil and urged them to seek the true, unknown God. [cite:sp_brit_ophites]

The pattern in this part is the first of several in the booklet: an image that heals is kept, the practice that grows around it is broken, and those who read it most boldly are named heretics.
