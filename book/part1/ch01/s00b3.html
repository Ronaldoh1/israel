Opposition to Zionism Across Lineages

Earlier sections showed that Zionism was contested inside Jewish life from its first year. A further point deserves to be stated plainly. That opposition was never confined to one community. It is sometimes said that Jewish opposition to Zionism is small and marginal, or that it comes from only one particular group. The documented record from 1885 to the present shows Zionism-critical and anti-Zionist positions in both Ashkenazi and Mizrahi lineages, two of the communities described in [scene:s00b2|the section on Ashkenazi, Sephardi and Mizrahi Jews], on religious and secular grounds alike.

The earliest of these was Ashkenazi and religious. American Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform of 1885, adopted by a movement founded and led by Ashkenazi immigrants and their American-born successors, defined Jewish identity as a religious community rather than a nation awaiting territorial restoration. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] The twelve-year gap before Basel matters. This was not a reaction against Zionism but a position that existed first and simply never adopted Zionism's framework when it appeared. Reform Judaism's institutional position shifted considerably over the twentieth century; the 1885 document records its founding stance.

The second was Ashkenazi and secular. The General Jewish Labour Bund, founded in Vilna in 1897, the same year as the First Zionist Congress, organized Jewish workers across the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement. It rejected emigration to Palestine and argued for Jewish cultural and political autonomy where Jews already lived. [cite:wiki_bund] Its members were not indifferent to antisemitism; they lived through the same pogroms that gave Zionism its urgency. Their disagreement was over strategy.

The Bund's later history shows how its story ended, which is not the same as showing its argument was refuted. When the Bolsheviks took power in October 1917, the Bund split. A majority faction, believing it was joining an ally on workers' rights, merged into the Communist Party. A minority led by Vladimir Medem rejected Bolshevik rule as undemocratic and hostile to Jewish national rights. In 1921 the Bolsheviks dissolved the Bund entirely. Remaining members were imprisoned or fled, and Mark Liber, one of its leading figures and its representative on the Executive Committee of the Petrograd Soviet during the revolution, was executed in the Stalinist purges in 1937. In Poland the Bund survived independently between the wars and into the Holocaust; Bundists fought alongside Zionists and Communists in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. Survivors who tried to regroup after the war were suppressed again, this time by Poland's new Communist government. [cite:jewishcurrents_bund_rise_fall] [cite:britannica_bund] Within a single generation the organization was dissolved by two governments, one of which its own members had helped bring to power. A movement suppressed by force is not thereby proven wrong; the record documents what happened to the organization, not a verdict on its ideas.

The third current is Ashkenazi, Haredi and active today. Neturei Karta, the most publicly visible anti-Zionist Haredi community, rests on the argument set out in Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum's VaYoel Moshe of 1958: that the ingathering of exiles and the restoration of sovereignty are meant to come through divine action in the Messianic era, so that a state established beforehand by human political and military effort is itself the transgression. [cite:wiki_satmar] Its members still take part in public demonstrations. They are a specific and relatively small community, and most Haredi and Orthodox institutions hold other positions.

A separate kind of critique came from Mizrahi Jews inside the state. Scholars including Ella Shohat, Sami Shalom Chetrit and Yehouda Shenhav documented discrimination against Mizrahi immigrants after 1948 by an Ashkenazi-dominated establishment: placement in peripheral development towns, discrimination in employment, and the marginalization of Middle Eastern Jewish culture in favor of an Ashkenazi-coded national culture. [cite:chetrit_mizrahi_struggle] This is a critique of the state's conduct from within Mizrahi intellectual life. It is not a claim about Mizrahi opinion as a whole; many Mizrahi Israelis identify strongly with the state.

A further strand sat inside Zionism itself. Martin Buber and Judah Magnes, with colleagues including Gershom Scholem and Henrietta Szold, founded Brit Shalom in 1925 and later Ihud in 1942, the latter in response to the Biltmore Conference's demand for a Jewish Commonwealth. Both called for a binational state with full civic equality between Jews and Arabs. In 1947 Buber and Magnes testified against partition before UNSCOP, to Ben-Gurion's documented fury. Buber called the treatment of the Arab population "the moral acid test of Zionism." Hannah Arendt's 1944 essay "Zionism Reconsidered" separately warned that an exclusively Jewish sovereign state risked permanent conflict and dependence on a great-power patron. [cite:wiki_ihud] [cite:britainpalestine_buber_magnes] [cite:researchgate_arendt_zionism_reconsidered] Brit Shalom was a small circle of intellectuals, never a mass movement, and drew fierce hostility from Labor and Revisionist Zionists, some of whom called its members traitors; Albert Einstein publicly supported it. It dissolved around 1933. Historians tie the project's failure to the 1929 Hebron violence and the Arab Revolt of 1936–39, which undercut its premise that Arab leaders would accept binational equality. [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom] [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] After May 1948 Magnes turned to proposing a confederation between the Jewish state and a future Palestinian Arab state, until his death that October. Ihud's publication, Ner, continued until 1964. [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism] Precision matters here: Buber and Magnes were Zionists who rejected the sovereignty-and-partition model, not anti-Zionists in the sense of the Bund or Neturei Karta.

The dividing line, then, does not run between lineages. Some of the earliest and most organized anti-Zionist traditions were Ashkenazi, and a sustained critique of the state came from Mizrahi scholars. The line runs through Jewish life as a whole, between those who hold the political position of Zionism and those who contest or reject it. This book has no current polling on what share of any community holds which view. What the record establishes is that both support and opposition have had serious, long-standing representation for more than 140 years.
