The Sun Gods of the Americas

The Inca state was organised around the sun. Inti was the most important deity of its official religion, and its highest priest, the Willaq Umu, was a priest of the sun, usually a brother of the ruler. [cite:vt35_wiki_willaqumu] The temple of Inti in Cusco, the Coricancha, had walls covered in sheets of gold. The first act against it was not religious: in 1533, when Francisco Pizarro's company demanded a ransom for the captive ruler Atahualpa, most of the gold was collected from the Coricancha and melted down. Atahualpa was killed anyway. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] Then the Dominicans built the church and convent of Santo Domingo directly on the Inca walls, which still stand inside it. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha]

The holiest image of the sun cult, the Punchao, a gold figure holding the remains of the hearts of dead Inca kings, was kept by the last Inca rulers in the forests of Vilcabamba. In 1572 Viceroy Francisco de Toledo's expedition captured the last ruler, Túpac Amaru, and seized the image; Túpac Amaru was executed in Cusco on 24 September 1572, while Catholic clergy knelt to plead for his life. [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru] The historian Catherine Julien traced the Punchao to Spain and to the estate of Philip II: the sun of the Inca may have ended in a king's treasury, as a trophy. [cite:vt35_julien_punchao] The festival of the sun, Inti Raymi, ceased under Spanish rule; at the same season the Church held its own procession of Corpus Christi, which the art historian Carolyn Dean describes as staging "the triumph of Christian orthodoxy over the imperial Inka patron (the Sun)". [cite:vt35_wiki_intiraymi] [cite:vt35_dean_muse]

In central Mexico the sun had two faces: Tonatiuh, the sun of the present age, the Fifth Sun, and Huitzilopochtli, patron god of the Mexica, a god of the sun and of war. [cite:vt35_wiki_tonatiuh] [cite:vt35_wiki_huitz] The record must say plainly what this religion asked. In the Mexica account the sun had to be kept moving across the sky, sustained by human sacrifice; excavations behind Mexico City's cathedral had uncovered more than 600 skulls in the great skull rack by 2020. [cite:vt35_wiki_tonatiuh] [cite:vt35_smith_tzompantli] The Templo Mayor, with Huitzilopochtli's shrine on its summit, was destroyed by the Spanish in 1521, and a church was built on the site with its stones. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor] [cite:vt35_wiki_cathedral]

The great carved disc called the Sun Stone has its own story. According to the Dominican chronicler Diego Durán, Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar ordered it buried so that "the memory of the ancient sacrifice that was made there would be lost". It was found in 1790, and in 1791 the cathedral mounted it on its west tower, where it stayed on public view until 1885. [cite:vt35_wiki_sunstone] The Maya kept their sky in books of bark paper. Most were destroyed by priests; four survive, and about 65 per cent of the Dresden Codex is astronomical tables tracking eclipses and the cycles of Venus and Mars. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayacodices] The full account is in [scene:vt35_inti_and_the_andes|The Vatican Architecture: Inti and the Andes] and [scene:vt35_mexico_and_the_maya|Mexico and the Maya].

The sun was not only lost. On the pilgrimage of the Lord of Qoyllurit'i in the Cusco region, which UNESCO describes as combining "elements from Catholicism and worship of pre-Hispanic nature deities", the procession carries the sacred images to a village "to greet the first rays of sunlight". [cite:vt35_unesco_qoyllur] Among the Tzotzil Maya of Chamula, Christ and the sun are joined in a single figure, Our Father Sun. [cite:vt35_everyculture_chamula]
