The Messiah of 1666, and the Baptisms of 1759

Shabbetai Tzevi was born in Smyrna, in the Ottoman Empire, on 23 July 1626, and as a young man "steeped himself" in the Kabbalah. [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] In 1665 a twenty-year-old student, Nathan of Gaza, took the role of a new Elijah and proclaimed him the messiah, prophesying victory and naming 1666 as the year. When Shabbetai returned to Smyrna that autumn he was wildly acclaimed, and the movement spread to Venice, Amsterdam, Hamburg, London and the cities of North Africa. [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] Lurianic Kabbalah, with its teaching that the world's repair was at hand, "played a central role" in it. [cite:kb_brit_luria]

The end came quickly. Shabbetai was imprisoned on arriving in Constantinople early in 1666. In September he was brought before the sultan at Adrianople and, having been threatened with torture, converted to Islam. The sultan renamed him Mehmed Efendi, made him his personal doorkeeper and gave him "a generous allowance". [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] Most of his followers were devastated. A faithful minority explained the conversion as a hidden step in his mission, and kept the movement alive after his death in 1676. [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] A popular Jewish history site calls him the chief example of the misuse of Kabbalah the rabbis had feared: a mystic who claimed to be the messiah partly on the strength of a kabbalistic calculation, and whose failure caused a crisis of faith. [cite:kb_mjl_danger]

A century later the movement produced a second claimant. Jacob Frank, born around 1726 in Podolia, in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, "proclaimed himself as a direct successor to Sabbatai Zevi". [cite:kb_wiki_frank] After a disputation with rabbis at Lwów in 1759, presided over by a Catholic canon, Frank was baptised on 17 September 1759, and again in Warsaw the next day. More than five hundred of his followers were baptised in Lwów within a year, and nearly a thousand the year after. [cite:kb_wiki_frank] The church did not hold him long in favour. He was arrested in Warsaw in February 1760, convicted of heresy and held in the monastery at Częstochowa for over twelve years, until a Russian general released him in 1772. He ended his life at Offenbach in Germany, styled the "Baron of Offenbach". [cite:kb_wiki_frank]

For the rabbis, these were the orchard story at the scale of a nation. A teaching that promised the repair of the world had, in the hands of a charismatic claimant, led communities from Smyrna to London to expect redemption and then led some of them out of Judaism altogether. [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] [cite:kb_wiki_frank] The response, which Part Five follows, was to close the gate: to restrict who could study the books that had carried the movement.

The record should be read carefully here. The two movements show what can happen when a mystical teaching becomes a mass political hope; they do not show that the teaching itself caused the outcome, and the same Lurianic ideas later fed a movement, Hasidism, that kept its followers within the tradition. [cite:kb_brit_luria] What they do show is why the guardians of the tradition believed the danger was real.
