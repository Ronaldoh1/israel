A Quarter of a People on One Percent of the Land

The armistice line closed around about 80,000 people who had lived in that part of the Gaza district before the war. By the time it was drawn, more than 200,000 others had arrived, from the rest of the district and from as far north as the Lydda area. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] The strip that the war made was, from its first day, a place of refugees, and it has remained one. This section sets out how many came, where they were housed, who took responsibility for them, and the legal limbo in which they and the strip's original residents were left.

The numbers. The total number of Palestinian refugees from the war of 1948 was disputed from the start, and the dispute is set out in [scene:rd6_exodus|the library's account of the exodus]: Israel's government put it at 520,000 to 530,000, Arab officials spoke of 900,000 or a million, and the British estimated 810,000 in February 1949, of whom they placed 210,000 in Gaza. [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix] The Institute for Palestine Studies' encyclopedia puts the refugees who reached the strip at "more than 200,000", against a resident population of 80,000. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] Taken together, the figures mean that the strip's population roughly tripled in a matter of months, and that refugees outnumbered the original residents by more than two to one. The historian Jean-Pierre Filiu's summary is starker: by the war's end, a quarter of Mandate Palestine's Arab population was in Gaza, on about one percent of its land. [cite:gz_wiki_history]

The camps. The first refugees slept wherever there was a roof or none: in mosques, schools and private homes, in military barracks and on open ground. The American Friends Service Committee, a Quaker relief body, then set up camps on land allocated by the Egyptian administration, named after the nearest towns. The Institute's encyclopedia dates Jabaliya and Nuseirat to 1948; al-Maghazi, Deir al-Balah, Khan Yunis and Rafah to 1949; Shati, the Beach camp, to 1951; and al-Bureij to 1952. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] Those eight camps still exist. On the eve of the war of 2023, AFP reported, citing UNRWA, that more than two-thirds of Gaza's 2.4 million people were registered refugees. [cite:gz_afp_camps]

UNRWA. On 8 December 1949 the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 302, which established the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees in the Near East to carry out "direct relief and works programmes" with the host governments. It recognized that continued assistance was necessary "to prevent conditions of starvation and distress", and it was framed as temporary: the agency was also to plan, with the governments of the region, for the time when international relief would end. [cite:gz_un_res302] UNRWA began operating on 1 May 1950 and took over the camps. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] Its first report listed Gaza as one of its five fields of operation. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] The relief was meant to be temporary because the refugees were meant to go home or be resettled. A year before, on 11 December 1948, the General Assembly had resolved in paragraph 11 of Resolution 194 that refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so, with compensation for those who chose not to, and had charged a Conciliation Commission with facilitating "repatriation, resettlement and economic and social rehabilitation." [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] Israel did not implement the return; in 1951 the Conciliation Commission recorded that Israel was not prepared to implement paragraph 11, as [scene:rd6_return_barred|the library's account of the barred return] sets out. [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] The camps became permanent.

The last expulsion into Gaza: al-Majdal, 1950. The movement of people into the strip did not end with the armistice. About 1,000 Arabs had remained in al-Majdal after its capture in November 1948, and by 1950, with returnees and natural growth, there were about 2,700. In June 1950, according to the historian Benny Morris, the Israeli government began moving them out, with Prime Minister Ben-Gurion's authorization and at the urging of Moshe Dayan, many of them across the line into Gaza. Ben-Gurion suspended the operation in October 1950 after objections from the Histadrut, the labour federation; by the end about twenty remained. [cite:gz_oup_morris_majdal] [cite:gz_stein_majdal] Jewish immigrants had begun moving into the town's empty houses in 1949; it is today part of the Israeli city of Ashkelon. [cite:gz_stein_majdal]

A people without a state. The residents and refugees of Gaza were left without a state of their own or anyone else's. Egypt governed the strip through a military governor but never annexed it, and it did not offer its people Egyptian citizenship; they travelled, if they travelled at all, on documents of the All-Palestine Government, which by then had no power and had moved to Cairo, where Nasser dissolved it in 1959. Egypt also restricted movement in and out of the strip, which limited the work its population could find. [cite:gz_wiki_history] In 1955 Egypt issued a Basic Law for the strip, which took effect in 1958 and reserved four seats on its Legislative Council for refugees. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] Part Two takes up those nineteen years of Egyptian rule, and the occupation that followed them.
