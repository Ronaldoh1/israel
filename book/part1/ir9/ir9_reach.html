Money, Weapons, Plots and Hostages

Iran's leaders did not confine their hostility to speeches. The institutions they commanded financed and armed allied groups across the region, planned killings on American soil, and held foreigners as leverage. Each strand below rests on court records, official estimates or the groups' own statements, with Iran's stated position given beside it.

Words were matched with money. The US State Department estimated in 2018 that Iran gave Hezbollah about $700 million a year, "the overwhelming majority" of its budget, and up to $100 million a year to Palestinian groups including Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad (PIJ). [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018] [cite:hm_state_iran] Hezbollah's leader Hassan Nasrallah said on 24 June 2016 that its budget, salaries, weapons and rockets "come from the Islamic Republic of Iran." [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] The US lists PIJ as receiving "financial assistance and training primarily from Iran." [cite:hz10s_state_pij] UN expert panels traced Iranian-made or Iranian-designed missiles and drone engines to the Houthis in Yemen, concluding in 2024 that without foreign support the movement could not build such complex weapons; Iran's UN envoy rejected US and British claims that it supplied them. [cite:hz10_un_poe2024] [cite:ir9_iranintl_houthis] In Iraq, the Pentagon attributed at least 603 US deaths between 2003 and 2011 to Iran-backed militias, about 17 per cent of the total, many from explosively formed penetrators that it said Iran supplied; Kata'ib Hezbollah and allied groups mounted more than 160 attacks on US forces after 7 October 2023, including the drone strike that killed three US soldiers at Tower 22 in Jordan on 28 January 2024. [cite:ir9_mt_603] [cite:hz10_at_farida] [cite:hz10_abc_tower22] The Hezbollah volume follows each of these flows in detail, including the view of analysts such as the former CIA officer Norman Roule that Iran sought the product of these groups' actions rather than control of them. Plots on American soil

US federal courts have recorded several IRGC plots inside the United States. In 2011 Manssor Arbabsiar, a US citizen, arranged with a man he believed to be a Mexican cartel member, in fact a DEA informant, to kill the Saudi ambassador in Washington for $1.5 million, wiring a $100,000 down payment. He pleaded guilty, admitting he acted for senior Quds Force officials, and was sentenced to 25 years on 30 May 2013. [cite:ir9_doj_arbabsiar] On 10 August 2022 the Justice Department charged an IRGC member, Shahram Poursafi, with offering $300,000 for the murder of the former national security adviser John Bolton, probably in revenge for the killing of Qassem Soleimani. [cite:ir9_nbc_bolton] [cite:ir9_iranintl_bolton] On 8 November 2024 it charged Farhad Shakeri, described as an IRGC asset in Tehran, alleging he had been tasked on 7 October 2024 with planning the killing of Donald Trump; Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called it a fabricated "scenario." [cite:ir9_usip_shakeri] The Iranian-American journalist Masih Alinejad was the target of an intelligence ministry kidnapping plot in 2020–21 and then of an IRGC murder-for-hire plot; two men were convicted in March 2025 and sentenced to 25 years on 29 October 2025. [cite:ir9_doj_alinejad] Plots in other countries are covered in Part Eight. [scene:ir8_iranside|Iran's side of the shadow war]

Hostage diplomacy

The use of detainees as leverage began on 4 November 1979, when students seized the US embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans for 444 days. [cite:ir9_usip_hostages] It has continued with dual nationals. On 18 September 2023 five Americans, among them the businessman Siamak Namazi, held since 2015 on espionage charges his family rejects, were freed as $6 billion of Iranian funds frozen in South Korea were moved to Qatari banks and five Iranians in the US received clemency; Washington said the money was restricted to humanitarian purchases and was not a ransom, while Tehran said it could spend it as it chose. [cite:ir9_newarab_swap] [cite:ir8_voa_swap] In March 2022 the British-Iranian aid worker Nazanin Zaghari-Ratcliffe was released after six years as Britain paid a £393.8 million debt dating from a cancelled pre-1979 tank contract; neither government said the two were linked. [cite:ir9_forces_nazanin] In both cases the detainees had been held on charges brought by Iran's courts, and in both cases their release coincided with the transfer of money that Iran said was its own.
