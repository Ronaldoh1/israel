The Architecture

Americans vote for president every four years, but the Constitution does not give the office to the candidate with the most votes. It gives it to the candidate who wins a majority of electors, people chosen in each state for the single task of casting the state's presidential votes. That body is known as the Electoral College, though its members never gather in one place; they meet and vote in their own states. [cite:dt2_lii_12th] This section describes how it is built: what the Constitution requires, what the states added, and how a single ballot becomes part of a national result. The rules are documented and, apart from a few contested details, uncontroversial. What they produce is the subject of the two sections that follow.

What the Constitution requires

Each state receives as many electors as it has members of Congress: its seats in the House of Representatives, which are apportioned by population, plus its two senators. The Twenty-third Amendment gives the District of Columbia three electors, the same as the least populous state. That makes 538 electoral votes in all, and a candidate needs a majority, 270, to win. For the elections of 2024 and 2028 the allocations run from 3 for the smallest states to 54 for California. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] Because every state starts with two electors for its senators whatever its population, the smallest states carry more electoral weight per resident than the largest.

The Constitution leaves the method of choosing electors to each state. Article II says only that the state shall appoint them in the manner its legislature directs, and in the first elections many legislatures simply chose the electors themselves; supporters of reform today rely on the same clause, which they describe as the states' plenary power over their electors. [cite:dt2_fairvote_wta] [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia] Nothing in the text requires a popular vote for president at all. Since Colorado's legislature made the last such choice in 1876, every state has held one, but each does so because its own law says so. [cite:dt2_fairvote_wta]

The original design had a flaw that showed within twelve years. Each elector cast two votes without saying which was for president and which for vice-president. In 1800 the Republican electors, voting as a party, gave Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr the same number of votes, and the tie went to the House of Representatives. The Twelfth Amendment, ratified in 1804, made electors cast "distinct ballots" for the two offices. [cite:dt2_lii_12th] [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo] It kept the rule that a winner needs "a majority of the whole number of Electors appointed." If no candidate reaches it, the House chooses among the top three, with "the representation from each state having one vote", so that Wyoming's delegation and California's count the same; the Senate chooses the vice-president from the top two. [cite:dt2_lii_12th] This fallback is called a contingent election. It has decided a presidency once, in 1824, when the House chose John Quincy Adams. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026]

What the states added: winner-take-all

The rule that most shapes modern campaigns is not in the Constitution. In forty-eight states and the District of Columbia, the candidate who wins the most votes statewide takes all of the state's electors, a system known as winner-take-all or, in the older term, the general ticket. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] It spread for partisan reasons. In 1800 Virginia, the state with the most electoral votes, switched to a statewide popular vote so that its whole slate would go to Jefferson, who wrote that it was "folly & worse than folly" for other states not to do the same. Massachusetts, controlled by John Adams's party, responded by having its legislature choose the electors itself. Once some states used the method to deliver a solid block for their candidate, others followed so as not to dilute their own influence. By 1836 every state except South Carolina used statewide winner-take-all; South Carolina's legislature chose its electors until after the Civil War, and Colorado's legislature chose in 1876, the last to do so. [cite:dt2_fairvote_wta]

Two states have since moved away from it. Maine adopted the district method for the 1972 election and Nebraska for 1992. Each gives two electors to the statewide winner and one to the winner of each congressional district. [cite:dt2_270_split] [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] The districts sometimes split from the state. Nebraska's 2nd district, centred on Omaha, gave Barack Obama an electoral vote in 2008, the first for a Democrat from Nebraska since 1964; Maine's 2nd district went to Donald Trump in 2016, 2020 and 2024; and in 2024 Nebraska's 2nd went to Kamala Harris. That year some Nebraska Republicans tried to restore winner-take-all, and the attempt failed when a Republican state legislator declined to back it. [cite:dt2_270_split] [cite:dt2_spectrum_split2024]

From one ballot to a president

The path from a voter's ballot to the presidency runs through several stages, each set by a different body of law. The voter's choice is counted in the state; the state's law decides how the result becomes a slate of electors; the electors meet in their own states in December, on the first Tuesday after the second Wednesday, which in 2024 was 17 December; and the certified votes are opened and counted before both houses of Congress in a joint session. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] [cite:dt2_lii_12th] Under the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022, the governor submits the certificate identifying the state's electors unless state law names another official, and the vice-president's role at the count is ministerial: the presiding officer opens the certificates and cannot decide disputes alone. [cite:dt2_bp_ecra]

What the design does to a vote

Winner-take-all means that within a state, a margin of one vote and a margin of a million produce the same result: all of the state's electors. Votes beyond what a candidate needs to carry a state add nothing to the national total, and votes for the losing candidate in a state add nothing at all. The effect is that the national outcome turns on a few close states. In 2020 Joe Biden won the national popular vote by more than 7 million, but his Electoral College margin rested on fewer than 50,000 votes in a few closely divided states. [cite:dt2_pew_ec2024] In 2024 Trump won 49.8 per cent of the national vote to Harris's 48.3, a lead of about 1.5 points, and took 312 of 538 electoral votes, 58 per cent. [cite:dt2_bp_2024] In both elections the result turned on a handful of states, and in some elections, as the third section of this part shows, the same arithmetic has given the office to the candidate with fewer votes.

Defenders of the design make a federal argument for it. The Congressional Research Service summarises their case as resting on the Framers' choice to give the states a central role and on the voters' "central, if indirect" part in the outcome; critics call the system "antiquated and undemocratic" because it does not provide for direct popular election and sometimes elects the candidate with fewer votes. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] Both descriptions are accurate as far as they go, and the choice between them is a judgment about what a presidential election is for. What is not a matter of judgment is that the design gives each state, not each voter, the unit of account. A national majority is neither necessary nor sufficient to win.

Two parts of this design are often confused, and the distinction matters for any reform. The allocation of electors, with its two-senator floor for every state, is in the Constitution and can be changed only by amendment. Winner-take-all is state law, and each state can change it by statute, as Maine and Nebraska did. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] [cite:dt2_270_split] The second fact is what makes the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact, discussed in the third section, legally possible at all. The first is why its critics argue it is not enough.
