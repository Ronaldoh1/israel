Plumbing a Mandate Through London

From 1920 to 1948 Britain governed Palestine, and the earlier volumes of this library tell that government's politics: the High Commissioners, the commissions, the riots and revolts, the White Papers. This section looks at something less often described, the financial plumbing beneath the politics. Where was the country's money issued, where were its reserves kept, which banks carried its payments, and which London companies built its largest pieces of infrastructure? The answer to each question leads back to London, and the last of them leads to a single order of the British Treasury in February 1948 that cut the plumbing off.

The currency. In 1926 the British Colonial Secretary appointed a Palestine Currency Board, and from 1 November 1927 the board issued the Palestine pound, fixed at par with sterling and divided into 1,000 mils. [cite:wiki_palestine_pound] [cite:israelmint_pcb_notes] No body in Palestine, Jewish or Arab, issued the country's money or set its value. [scene:us_s06|An earlier volume] noted that its sources did not say how the board's reserves were held. A working paper prepared by the secretariat of the United Nations Conciliation Commission for Palestine after the Mandate ended supplies the answer. Palestine's currency was backed by British government securities and sterling, held in London by the Currency Board. The board's assets made up more than half of all of Palestine's sterling balances, which the UN Palestine Commission put at more than £50 million. [cite:dv2_unccp_assets] Every Palestine pound in circulation was matched, in other words, by assets lodged in the capital of the governing power.

The board did not operate its own branches in Palestine. According to the Conciliation Commission's note on currency and banking, it worked there "through the agency of Barclay's Bank." [cite:dv2_unccp_banking] Barclays had arrived through a merger. The Anglo-Egyptian Bank, which often acted as banker to British authorities in the Mediterranean, had opened branches in Jerusalem and Jaffa in 1918; in 1925 Barclays merged it with two other overseas banks to form Barclays Bank (Dominion, Colonial and Overseas). [cite:dv2_wiki_anglo_egyptian] A London clearing bank, in short, was the channel through which the Mandate's London-managed currency reached the country.

The banks. Within that system three kinds of bank did business. There were foreign banks, Barclays and the Ottoman Bank among them. There was the Zionist movement's own bank, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, still a subsidiary of the London-incorporated Jewish Colonial Trust, as the previous section showed. And there were Arab banks. The Conciliation Commission named the Arab Bank and the Arab National Bank as "the two big Arab banks" and estimated Arab bank deposits at about £12,500,000 at the end of 1945. [cite:dv2_unccp_banking] The figure is an estimate in a working paper, and the scanned document is partly illegible, but it is a useful corrective to accounts in which the Mandate economy had only one community with money in the bank.

The oil line. One of the major works built across Mandate Palestine was not built by the Mandate government or by the Zionist movement. It was built by the Iraq Petroleum Company, in which Anglo-Iranian and Shell each held 23.75 percent. Between 1932 and 1934 the company laid a pipeline from the oilfields of Kirkuk to the Mediterranean, splitting into two branches, one to Tripoli in Lebanon and one to Haifa. [cite:dv2_wiki_kirkuk_haifa] [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline] The transit agreements with Palestine and its neighbors had been signed in the first three months of 1931. [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline] The first oil reached Haifa in October 1934, on the 14th by one account and the 16th by another, and opening ceremonies were held in January 1935. [cite:dv2_wiki_kirkuk_haifa] [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline] At Haifa, Consolidated Refineries Ltd, owned jointly by Anglo-Iranian and Shell, built a refinery that began production on 29 November 1939. [cite:dv2_wiki_kirkuk_haifa]

The pipeline drew the country's politics into its path. During the Arab revolt of 1936–1939 it was attacked repeatedly, and Captain Orde Wingate's joint British and Jewish Special Night Squads were formed largely to protect it. The Irgun later attacked it too. [cite:dv2_wiki_kirkuk_haifa] An oil company's pipeline, in other words, was a main reason for a British unit that trained Jewish fighters alongside British soldiers. The revolt itself is told in [scene:s07a5|the Arab revolt].

The cut-off. As the Mandate ended, the plumbing was closed from London. On 22 February 1948 a British Treasury order excluded Palestine from the sterling area and blocked its accumulated sterling balances. The blocked sums included the Currency Board's assets and more than £3 million left over from three bond issues made in Palestine since 1944, which were invested in British government securities "pending a general settlement of the question." [cite:dv2_unccp_assets] The Conciliation Commission's later note described Palestine's sterling credits as largely "blocked in London." [cite:dv2_unccp_banking] The question of what became of those balances after 1948, and how they were divided between Israel and the Arab states and individuals with claims on them, is not answered in the sources used here, and it is left open.

Two months later the refinery stopped. On 12 April 1948 the Haifa refinery shut down indefinitely after a strike by its Arab workers. After the war, the Haifa branch of the pipeline was cut off from Iraqi oil and never restored; deliveries resumed on a much smaller scale in October 1949, using crude from Venezuela. [cite:dv2_wiki_kirkuk_haifa] The Mandate's currency outlived its government by three months: the Currency Board's notes stayed in circulation until 18 August 1948, when the new state's own notes took their place. Those first Israeli notes were printed not in London but in New York, by the American Bank Note Company, at the request of the Anglo-Palestine Bank's chairman. [cite:wiki_palestine_pound] [cite:israelmint_aplb_series] [cite:jhom_anglo_palestine_notes]

The detail is a fitting end to the Mandate's financial story. For twenty-one years the country's money had been issued, backed and banked through London. When the state that succeeded the Mandate needed a currency of its own, the bank that supplied it had been chartered in London fifty years before, but the notes came from America. The handover from one patron to the next, told in the parts of this volume on Washington, can be read in the printer's imprint.

What the record leaves open has already been noted in passing and should be gathered here. The Conciliation Commission's documents are scanned working papers, partly illegible, and some of their figures cannot be read reliably; this section uses only those that can. The sources do not say what the peg to sterling cost or gained the country's economy, how the blocked balances were finally settled, or how the burden of the February 1948 order was shared among Jewish and Arab depositors. The two dates for the first oil at Haifa, 14 and 16 October 1934, are both recorded, and the difference is not explained.
