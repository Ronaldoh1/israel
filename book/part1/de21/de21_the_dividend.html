The Dividend That Was Never Paid

For a few weeks in February 2025 the government cuts came with an offer attached. If the Department of Government Efficiency found the savings it promised, part of the money would come back to taxpayers as a cheque. The idea, called the "DOGE dividend," was proposed by an investor, endorsed by Elon Musk within a day and by President Trump within two, and polled well. It was never legislated and never paid. This section sets out where the idea came from, the arithmetic it rested on, what happened to it, and what its fate does and does not show. It is a small episode, but it is the clearest case in Book Three of a public promise that can be checked against a calendar and a ledger.

The proposal

The proposal came from James Fishback, chief executive of an investment firm, Azoria Partners. On 18 February 2025 he posted a four-page memo on X proposing that 20 per cent of whatever DOGE saved be returned to taxpayers. Taking the $2 trillion figure Musk had used during the campaign, he put that share at $400 billion, enough for a cheque of about $5,000 to every tax-paying household, to be sent after DOGE's scheduled end in July 2026; the rest of the savings would go to paying down the national debt. [cite:de21_reuters_dividend] [cite:de21_usat_dividend] The household count he used was about 78 million. [cite:de21_usat_dividend] Musk replied the same day: "Will check with the President." [cite:de21_reuters_dividend]

The next day, 19 February, Trump spoke in Miami at an investment conference hosted by Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund. He said a "new concept" was "under consideration," under which 20 per cent of DOGE savings would go to American citizens and 20 per cent to paying down the debt. [cite:de21_reuters_dividend] Asked about it aboard Air Force One, he said: "I love it." [cite:de21_ap_dividend] He also presented the cheque as a reason for citizens to report waste themselves: "They participate in the process of saving us money." [cite:de21_ap_dividend] Reuters reported that Fishback was in contact with the administration about the plan. [cite:de21_reuters_dividend]

The idea was popular. A poll of 1,001 registered voters taken on 24 and 25 February 2025 found two-thirds in favour of $5,000 cheques, 46 per cent strongly. [cite:de21_usat_dividend] The people whose votes would have been needed were cooler. A cheque from the Treasury requires an appropriation, which only Congress can pass. Speaker Mike Johnson said he would be "happy" to consider it "once we balance the budget." [cite:de21_bi_dividend] At the Conservative Political Action Conference he put the objection plainly: "Politically, that would be great for us, you know, because everybody gets a check," but "I think we need to pay down the credit card." [cite:de21_usat_dividend] Senator Thom Tillis preferred to reduce the debt, Senator Josh Hawley wanted the money for a child tax credit, and Republican House members Celeste Maloy and Riley Moore said savings should go to the deficit. [cite:de21_bi_dividend] [cite:de21_usat_dividend] Musk, for his part, said the decision was "entirely up to" the president. [cite:de21_bi_dividend]

The arithmetic it rested on

Everything in the proposal followed from the size of the savings, and the savings never approached the figure the cheque was built on. When Fishback wrote, Musk was already describing $2 trillion as a best case and $1 trillion as the goal, and a Reuters analysis of the partial data DOGE had published found that its contract cancellations to that point had saved $8.5 billion. [cite:de21_reuters_dividend] By April 2025 Musk told the cabinet that he anticipated $150 billion in savings in the following fiscal year, and at his farewell on 30 May the DOGE website estimated $175 billion. [cite:de11_fortune_150b] [cite:de21_wt_farewell] When the website stopped being updated, on 1 January 2026, it claimed $215 billion, or $1,335.40 per taxpayer. [cite:de21_ee_end]

Even on DOGE's own final claim the dividend would have been a fraction of what was floated. Twenty per cent of $215 billion is $43 billion; spread over 78 million households, it comes to about $550 each, roughly a ninth of the $5,000 figure. And the $215 billion was itself a claim the official auditor could not confirm. In August 2026 the Government Accountability Office examined the $110.34 billion of it that DOGE attributed to cancelled contracts, grants and leases. It found that DOGE had followed its own stated method for only 27.5 per cent of the contract savings; that 2,503 contracts, carrying $27.4 billion of claimed savings, showed no termination action at all in the federal procurement database; that it could not verify how 96 per cent of the $49.21 billion in grant savings had been calculated, because DOGE "did not provide sufficient information"; and that a $1.7 billion saving on a military health contract was no saving, since "no action was taken to terminate the contract, or to reduce scope, value, or funding." [cite:de21_gao_full] GAO also noted that money released from cancelled awards "could reduce federal spending or be spent on higher priority agency needs"; a cancellation is not the same as a dollar returned. [cite:de21_gao_full] A dividend paid out of such figures would have been paid out of savings that, on the auditor's account, could not be shown to exist in the amounts claimed.

What became of it

The dividend did not fail by vote. It stopped being discussed. No bill to create it was enacted and no cheque was issued. DOGE's temporary organization reached its legal end on 4 July 2026, the date on which Fishback had proposed the cheques should go out, without a final account of its savings: the director of the Office of Management and Budget, Russell Vought, told a House subcommittee on 30 June 2026 that the administration had "no plans to do kind of a closing DOGE report." [cite:de21_ee_end] [cite:de21_fnn_vought] In the language of a promise ledger, its status is "not attempted": endorsed by the president in public, never sent to Congress in a form Congress could pass.

Two features of the episode should be kept in view. The first is that the dividend was never a formal promise. Trump called it a concept "under consideration" and said he loved it; he did not sign anything, and Musk handed the decision to him. [cite:de21_reuters_dividend] [cite:de21_bi_dividend] The second is that the obstacle that stopped it was ordinary and visible: the leaders of the president's own party in Congress said they would rather reduce the debt, and the power of the purse sat with them. [cite:de21_usat_dividend] That is the constitutional design working as it was taught, in the case of a popular idea that a president endorsed.

Against the record

On the book's red-flag scale, the dividend is ORANGE: a public claim that was not delivered, resting on a savings figure the official auditor could not verify. [cite:de21_gao_full] The grade attaches to the endorsement and the arithmetic behind it, not to any finding about intent. The explanation on the record from those who floated it is the conditional built into the proposal itself: the cheques were to come out of savings, and the savings were not found. No statement by Trump or Musk shows that either knew, when endorsing it, that the money would not appear; the word "lie" is therefore not used.

For the hypothesis this book tests, which treats the cuts as an occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination, the dividend bears in a limited way. Documented: a cash payment was publicly endorsed while the cuts were most contested, and was never paid. [cite:de21_ap_dividend] [cite:de21_ee_end] Not supported: that it was offered as bait in bad faith; the proposal came from an outside investor, the endorsement was conditional, and no document shows a decision to use it to sell the cuts. The record runs against the worst case on one point. The dividend stalled on objections from the president's own party in Congress, which holds the power of the purse; an executive acting without a check would not have needed them.
