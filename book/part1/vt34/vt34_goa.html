Goa: The Inquisition in Asia, 1560–1812

Portugal's tribunal in Goa was the only inquisition to work for any length of time in a society where most people were neither Christian, Jewish nor Muslim. It sat for two and a half centuries in the capital of the Portuguese Estado da Índia, and its main business, by the end, was the religious life of Hindus who had become Catholics, or whose parents had. It is also the tribunal whose history is hardest to tell from the record, because most of that record was burned. This section sets out who asked for it, what the crown and the Church did to Hindu worship in the territory before and alongside it, what the surviving numbers show, and where historians disagree.

Before the tribunal: the crown against the temples

Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa for Portugal in 1510. The destruction of Hindu worship there began as royal and colonial policy, two decades before the Inquisition arrived. An order of 30 June 1541 directed that the temples be destroyed and their property handed to the missionaries, and a royal order of 8 March 1546 from John III forbade Hindu worship, ordered temples destroyed and banned Hindu festivals in Portuguese territory. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] By 1566 about 160 temples had been razed on the island of Goa itself; a Franciscan campaign destroyed about 300 more in Bardez in 1566–1567, and Portuguese officials and missionaries are reported to have destroyed a similar number in Salcete, though the evidence for that figure conflicts. A royal letter of 1569 stated that all the Hindu temples in the colony had been burned and razed. No exact count exists. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] The acts were ordered by the king and carried out by viceroys, captains and members of religious orders, Franciscans and Jesuits; the Inquisition was one part of a wider system, not its author.

The communities did not simply lose their gods. In 1560, according to the government of Goa's own account of the shrine, devotees of Shri Mangesh carried the linga by night from Kushasthali, the present Cortalim in Salcete, across the border to Priol, then ruled by the Adil Shahi sultans of Bijapur, to save it from destruction. [cite:vt34_goagov_mangueshi] The Mangueshi temple at Priol is today one of the best known in Goa. Other village deities made the same journey into the hinterland of Ponda, beyond Portuguese rule, which is why so many of Goa's great temples stand there rather than where they were founded.

Who asked for the tribunal

Francis Xavier, the Jesuit missionary later canonised (12 March 1622), wrote to John III asking, in the words quoted by the historian Stephen Neill, that "your highness should institute the holy Inquisition" in India. [cite:vt34_wiki_xavier] Sources date the letter to 1545 or 1546, and historians disagree about what he meant: some read it as a request for the tribunal that followed; others read it as a plea for an official to promote and protect the mission. He died in 1552, eight years before the tribunal opened. [cite:vt34_wiki_xavier] [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Goa's vicar general, Miguel Vaz, also appealed for it. The tribunal was established in 1560; its first inquisitor, Aleixo Dias Falcão, was sent by Cardinal Henrique, head of the Portuguese Inquisition. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq]

Whom it tried

The first target was the same as in Lisbon: New Christians of Jewish descent and Old Christians suspected of Protestant ideas. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Over time the largest category became converts and their descendants accused of keeping Hindu customs, which the records call gentilismo, though the tribunal also often prosecuted local people who had never been baptised, which the historian Célia Tavares calls "quite disconcerting at first sight" for a court built to judge Christian heresy. [cite:vt34_alzulaij_tavares] Antonio José Saraiva put the share charged with crypto-Hinduism at about 74 per cent; Ulrich Lehner finds that more than three-quarters of the cases concerned public Hindu worship. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq]

The laws behind those cases were made by the colonial state and church councils together. Laws of 1567 restricted Hindu life in Portuguese territory, among other things forbidding Hindu priests to officiate at weddings. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Language came under the same regime. On 27 June 1684 the viceroy, at the urging of Franciscan friars, decreed that within three years the people of Goa should give up Konkani for Portuguese, on pain of imprisonment; the king confirmed the order on 17 March 1687, and it was renewed in 1745 and 1812. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Konkani survived it as the everyday language of Goa's Hindus and Catholics alike.

What the surviving numbers show

Most of the tribunal's archive was burned by Portuguese officials after it was abolished in 1812. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Every count is therefore a reconstruction. Lauren Benton gives 3,800 cases between 1561 and 1623; a widely repeated figure of about 16,000 people tried between 1561 and 1774 has no clear source. Seventy-one autos da fé are recorded. The most cited count of executions, drawn from Portuguese archival summaries by Saraiva and others, is 57 people burned in person and 64 in effigy, the effigies standing for people who had fled or died in custody. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] Agostino Borromeo, presenting the Vatican's 2004 symposium volume, gave a rate of 5.7 per cent condemned to death among more than 13,000 people tried by the Portuguese tribunals in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, a figure that does not isolate Goa. [cite:vt34_icn_borromeo] For converts convicted of Hindu practice the ultimate penalty was to be handed to the secular arm and burned; far more often, Tavares finds, the sentence was whipping, compulsory attendance at Sunday Mass and other corporal penalties, or four or five years in the galleys, a sentence that often ended in death, each imposed in public at an auto da fé. People convicted of sodomy received some of the harshest sentences. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] [cite:vt34_alzulaij_tavares]

The last auto da fé was held on 7 February 1773. The Marquis of Pombal's government suspended the tribunal in 1774; it was restored in 1778, kept prosecuting, and was abolished for good in 1812. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq]

The other Christians: Diamper, 1599

The same Portuguese church that ran the Goa tribunal also acted against an ancient Christian community. The Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala, whose liturgy was in Syriac and whose bishops came from the Church of the East in Mesopotamia, were not under Rome. In June 1599 Aleixo de Menezes, archbishop of Goa, convened a synod at Diamper (Udayamperur), attended by 153 local priests and 660 lay representatives, which passed more than 200 decrees to bring them into line with post-Tridentine Rome and cut their tie to their own patriarch. [cite:vt34_gedsh_diamper] One decree forbade anyone to keep, translate or read Syriac books "written by Nestorian heretics", under pain of excommunication, and a list of banned works followed, including an infancy gospel and the law code of the fourteenth-century metropolitan Abdisho bar Brika. [cite:vt34_hmml_diamper] During Menezes's tour of the churches afterwards, books judged heretical were burned. How many is unknown: Hidemi Takahashi concludes that a large share of Kerala's Syriac manuscripts was probably lost, while the Hill Museum and Manuscript Library has catalogued banned works that survived, including a copy of Abdisho's law code made in 1291. [cite:vt34_gedsh_diamper] [cite:vt34_hmml_diamper] This was not the Inquisition's work but the archbishop's and his synod's; it belongs here because it was the same colonial church acting on the same principle of control over books.

How the story is told, and argued

The modern account in India rests heavily on A. K. Priolkar's The Goa Inquisition (1961), published as Goa joined the Indian Union. C. R. Boxer, the leading historian of the Portuguese empire, called it a "dispassionate and objective account"; Délio de Mendonça and Dale Luis Menezes have criticised its reliance on Claudius Buchanan, a nineteenth-century Protestant polemicist, and on the French physician Charles Dellon, whose memoir of his own imprisonment shaped the tribunal's European reputation. [cite:vt34_wiki_priolkar] [cite:vt34_alzulaij_tavares] The Brazilian historian Célia Tavares argues that the tribunal's power has been overstated but its violence was real, that it heard far more cases than the tribunals in Portugal itself with similar conviction rates, that no Jesuit ever served as an inquisitor in Goa, and that Brahmin converts were punished more harshly than lower-caste ones. [cite:vt34_alzulaij_tavares] The argument has present-day weight: on 21 December 2021 Goa's chief minister, Pramod Sawant, said the temples destroyed by the Portuguese should be rebuilt. [cite:vt34_dh_sawant]

The documented core is not in dispute. A Catholic crown, its viceroys and religious orders destroyed the public temples of the territory it ruled, outlawed Hindu rites and tried to suppress the local language, and a tribunal licensed by the pope and staffed by the crown punished converts for continuing the practices of their families. What is disputed is scale, which the burned archive makes uncertain, and whether the tribunal or the wider colonial order did most of the damage. The Hindu worship of Goa survived in the temples carried over the border, and in households. Part Thirty-Four returns to Goa's temples in its survey of suppressed solar cults.
