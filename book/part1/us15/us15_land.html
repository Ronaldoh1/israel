Who Owns the Land, and Who May Build on It

The two countries begin from opposite ends. In Israel almost all land is public and households lease it from the state; in the United States most land is private and the decisive public power is local zoning, which says what an owner may build. Each arrangement serves some people well and leaves others out, and in each the people left out are concentrated in identifiable places. This section sets out the law in each country, what the record shows about who gains access to land and building rights, and where the arrangement has been challenged in court or reformed. Land in the West Bank, which runs under a separate legal system, is treated in Part Nine and in the volume on the West Bank. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] [scene:wb4_land|The West Bank: land]

Israel: 93 per cent public, held on lease

The Israel Land Authority (ILA) manages 93 per cent of the land inside Israel, about 19.5 million dunams. That land belongs to three owners: the state, the Development Authority and the Jewish National Fund (JNF). The remaining 7 per cent is privately owned or held by religious bodies. An Israeli who "owns" a home usually holds a 49- or 98-year lease from the ILA; urban land is typically leased for 49 years with an option of a further 49. [cite:us15_wiki_ila] The system was set up in 1960 by four instruments: Basic Law: Israel Lands, the Israel Lands Law, the Israel Land Administration Law, and a covenant between the state and the World Zionist Organization for the JNF. The JNF's place in that covenant is visible in the governing body: the Israel Land Council has 22 members, 12 from government ministries and 10 from the JNF. [cite:us15_wiki_ila] A reform in 2009 replaced the old Israel Land Administration with the Israel Land Authority and converted long-term leases on some residential land into private ownership, with the stated aim of reducing bureaucracy for homeowners. [cite:us15_wiki_ila]

The JNF is older than the state. It was founded at the Fifth Zionist Congress in 1901, bought land to lease rather than sell, and held 936,000 dunams by the eve of statehood. [cite:dv2_wiki_jnf] Today it owns about 13 per cent of Israel's land and describes itself as the caretaker of the land on behalf of the Jewish people everywhere. [cite:us15_aj_jnf2007] That self-description is the source of the main legal question in the Israeli land system: whether land the state manages may be allocated by religion or nationality.

The courts and the JNF: Ka'adan, Mazuz and admissions committees

The courts have answered that question in principle. In the Ka'adan case of 2000, brought by an Arab family refused a home in the community town of Katzir, the High Court held that the state may not allocate land on the basis of religion or nationality, even through a third party such as the Jewish Agency. [cite:us15_wiki_ila] In 2004 the ILA barred non-Jews from a housing tender in Carmiel because the land was JNF land, and the rights group Adalah petitioned. On 26 January 2005 Attorney General Menachem Mazuz decided that all land the ILA manages, JNF land included, may be marketed to Jews and non-Jews without discrimination. A JNF spokesman objected that "it's only fitting our land would be solely for Jewish settlers" while Israel's security was not secured. [cite:us15_ynet_mazuz2005] The compromise was a swap: when a non-Jew wins JNF land, the ILA may compensate the JNF with other state land, so that the JNF's total holding is not reduced. Petitioners said in 2007 that the decision was still not being followed. [cite:us15_aj_jnf2007]

The Knesset then gave small communities a different tool. The Admissions Committees Law of 2011, an amendment to the Cooperative Societies Ordinance, lets admissions committees in small communities in the Negev and the Galilee reject applicants for incompatibility with the community's "social-cultural fabric". Adalah counted 434 localities covered. On 17 September 2014 the Supreme Court dismissed the petitions against the law by five votes to four. [cite:us15_aj_admissions2014] The majority did not hold the law valid in every use; it held the challenge was not yet ripe, because the law was not discriminatory on its face, included an anti-discrimination clause, and had produced no concrete case of discriminatory use before the court. [cite:us15_toi_fuchs_admissions] One of its 2009 sponsors, MK David Rotem, had described the purpose as letting communities be "established by people who want to live with other Jews"; a regional council head who used the committees said they had "nothing to do with Arab citizens". [cite:us15_aj_admissions2014]

Building in Arab towns: planning, permits and demolition

Most Arab citizens do not live in the small communities the admissions law covers. About 90 per cent live in 139 Arab towns and villages whose combined jurisdiction covers less than 3 per cent of the state's area, by the figures six Israeli civil-society groups compiled from official sources in 2017. Those jurisdictions had not grown with the population, and density within them had risen about elevenfold. [cite:us15_acri_kaminitz] Until 2000 the Arab localities had no up-to-date outline plans, the documents without which a building permit cannot be issued. The same paper, citing the Finance Ministry's 2015 "120-Day Team" report, put the Arab population's need at about 13,000 new homes a year against about 7,000 built, and found that from 2005 to 2009 about 20 per cent of planned units in Arab localities had been marketed, against 70 per cent in Jewish and mixed towns. [cite:us15_acri_kaminitz]

Building without a permit followed, and so did enforcement. By figures of the Knesset Research and Information Center, 97 per cent of administrative demolition orders issued from 2012 to 2014 were in the Arab sector, as were 97 per cent of the 1,348 judicial demolition orders in force in July 2015. [cite:us15_acri_kaminitz] On the night of 5 April 2017 the Knesset approved the Kaminitz Law, an amendment to the 1965 Planning and Building Law. Its stated aim was stronger enforcement against illegal building: it moved enforcement powers to a national body, widened administrative powers to demolish and evict while limiting court review, and sharply raised fines. Adalah called it discriminatory in effect against Arab citizens. [cite:us15_adalah_kaminitz] [cite:us15_acri_kaminitz] In the Negev, about 120,000 Bedouin citizens lived in 46 villages the state did not recognise in 2018; the village of Umm el-Hiran was demolished in January 2017. [cite:us15_jpost_bedouin2018]

The government's answer on the planning side has been money and plans. Resolution 922 of 30 December 2015 funded outline plans for 20 Arab local authorities among much else, and Resolution 550 of 24 October 2021, worth NIS 30 billion over five years, set targets of marketing 5,000 homes on state land in Arab towns in 2022 and up to 9,000 by 2026. [cite:us15_inss_922] Part Eight covers the plans' wider results. [scene:us8_israel|Part Eight: equality among Israel's citizens] The money has been cut since the war: the Israel Democracy Institute recorded a 15 per cent cut after October 2023 and, in December 2025, a draft decision to move about NIS 3 billion from the plan to the Ministry of National Security, much of it from the housing and interior budgets. The institute advised against it. This book did not find the outcome of that proposal or of the plan's renewal due in 2026. [cite:us15_idi_550cut]

The United States: private land, federal West, local zoning

The federal government owns about 640 million acres, roughly 28 per cent of the country's 2.27 billion acres, but that land is concentrated in Alaska (61 per cent federal) and the eleven western states (46 per cent), against about 4 per cent in all other states. Four agencies hold almost all of it: the Bureau of Land Management (244 million acres), the Forest Service (193 million), the Fish and Wildlife Service (89 million) and the National Park Service (80 million). [cite:us15_crs_fedland] The rest of the country was transferred out: about 1.29 billion acres left federal ownership between 1781 and 2024, some 816 million of them to private owners, nearly all before 1940; homestead entries peaked in 1910. The Federal Land Policy and Management Act of 1976 declared that the remaining public domain would generally stay federal. [cite:us15_crs_fedland]

Where Americans live, therefore, the land is mostly private, and the binding rules are local. In Village of Euclid v. Ambler (1926) the Supreme Court upheld a town's power to exclude apartment buildings from residential districts, with Justice Sutherland writing that "very often the apartment house is a mere parasite". By a 2019 New York Times analysis, about three-quarters of residential land in many American cities is zoned for detached single-family homes only, from 94 per cent in San Jose to 15 per cent in New York City. [cite:us15_wiki_sfzoning] Such rules name no group; their effect is to set a price of entry into a neighbourhood. Part Eight covers America's racial gaps and Part Fourteen the price of housing. [scene:us8_us|Part Eight: the United States] [scene:us14_housing|Part Fourteen: housing against income] Since 2018 a reform movement has loosened them: Minneapolis ended single-family-only zoning in December 2018, Oregon required duplexes and more in most cities from 2019, California's SB 9 allowed two to four homes on single-family lots from 2021, and Washington State followed in 2023. [cite:us15_wiki_sfzoning]

The comparison has limits that should be stated. Israel's land figures are mostly snapshots, not series; the demolition data stop in 2015 and the unrecognised-village count in 2018, and this book did not find a current official count of either. The American zoning share is a city-level estimate from 2019, not a national series. What the record does show is that both systems have outlawed open allocation by group while keeping lawful screening devices, a community's "fabric" in one case and a lot size in the other, whose effects fall unevenly.
