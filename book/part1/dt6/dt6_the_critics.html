The Critics' Case

The strongest objections to ranked-choice voting come from political scientists and mathematicians, not only from partisans. This section takes the main criticisms one at a time: that its majorities are partly an artefact of discarded ballots, that it is harder to use and harder on some voters than others, and that it can eliminate the candidate most voters prefer. Each is set beside the research and the supporters' reply, before the section turns to proportional representation.

The majority claim

The system guarantees a majority of the ballots still counting in the final round, not of all ballots cast. Craig Burnett and Vladimir Kogan examined more than 600,000 ballots from four local instant-runoff elections and found "a substantial number of votes being discarded in each election" through exhaustion, and that "the winner in all four of our cases receives less than a majority of the total votes cast." [cite:dt6_burnett_kogan] The pattern appears in the cases in this part. In Maine's 2nd District in 2018 the 8,253 exhausted ballots outnumbered the winning margin more than twice over, and the winner finished with about 49.2 per cent of all ballots cast. [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] In New York's 2021 mayoral primary 140,202 ballots were inactive by the final round. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] In Alaska's 2022 special election the winner had 51.48 per cent of continuing ballots but 48.40 per cent of the ballots counted in the first round. [cite:dt6_clelland_ak]

The supporters' reply is a comparison, not a denial. Under plurality voting, the alternative in nearly every American race, the same Maine election would have gone to the first-round leader with 46.3 per cent, the 2021 New York primary to a candidate with 30.7 per cent, and the Alaska race to a candidate with 40.2 per cent. [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_clelland_ak] Ranked choice does not deliver a majority of all voters, but in each of these cases the winner's final share was larger than a plurality winner's would have been. The fair description is "majority of continuing ballots", and the critics are right that the shorter claim overstates it.

Complexity, spoiled ballots and turnout

The second criticism is that ranking is harder than choosing, and that the difficulty falls unevenly. The political scientist Jason McDaniel of San Francisco State University found that after San Francisco adopted the method, turnout fell among infrequent voters and rose among the highly educated, and that spoiled ballots increased, concentrated in neighbourhoods with more Black, Latino, elderly, foreign-born and less affluent residents, without fading over time. "The ranking process is cognitively more difficult than just choosing one candidate," he said. [cite:dt6_mcdaniel_sf]

Other research points the other way. A 2022 collection of fifteen studies for the think tank New America, introduced by Lee Drutman and Maresa Strano, found that initial confusion largely faded after voters had used the system a second time, that voters penalised candidates of colour at similar rates under ranked and plurality rules, and that none of the studies found that replacing plurality with ranked choice disadvantages minority voters or candidates. Among respondents aged 65 and over, 6.5 per cent preferred ranked choice to a single vote; among Republicans fewer than 7 per cent did, against 28 per cent of Democrats. [cite:dt6_na_review] A 2025 study in Social Science Quarterly, using voter files from local elections across the country, found that ranked-choice cities were not associated with lower turnout among people of colour, and that several groups voted at higher rates there, with the largest proportional gains among White voters. [cite:dt6_dowling_ssq] The evidence on complexity is mixed, and the honest summary is New America's own: "adopting RCV will not hurt as much as you might fear, but it may not help as much as you might hope." [cite:dt6_na_review]

The Alaska problem: eliminating the consensus candidate

The strongest criticism is mathematical. A candidate who would beat every rival in a two-way contest is called the Condorcet winner. Instant runoff can eliminate that candidate early, because elimination depends on first choices alone. Alaska's special election for its House seat in August 2022 is the documented case. In the first round Mary Peltola, a Democrat, had 40.19 per cent, Sarah Palin, a Republican, 31.27 per cent and Nicholas Begich, also a Republican, 28.53 per cent. Begich was eliminated. Of his ballots, 27,053 moved to Palin, 15,467 to Peltola, and 11,290 were exhausted, and Peltola won with 51.48 per cent of continuing ballots. [cite:dt6_clelland_ak]

The mathematician Jeanne Clelland used the ranked-ballot record to reconstruct each head-to-head contest. Begich would have beaten Palin with about 61 per cent and Peltola with about 53 per cent. He was the Condorcet winner, and he finished last in the first round. Clelland also reports that the election showed two further anomalies known to voting theorists: a "monotonicity" paradox, in which ranking a candidate higher can cause that candidate to lose, and a "no-show" paradox, in which some voters would have obtained a better result by not voting at all. [cite:dt6_clelland_ak] Supporters reply that plurality voting would have elected Peltola too, on 40 per cent, and that no single-winner method avoids every paradox. The second does not cure the specific failure: a method that reads only first choices until someone is eliminated can miss the candidate a majority would accept. Alaska's repeal campaign, now on the 3 November 2026 ballot, is in part a response to this election; Begich, the eliminated candidate, now a member of Congress, backs repeal. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026]

The larger reform: proportional representation

The New America review reached a conclusion that supporters do not often quote: "RCV alone likely cannot deliver the representational diversity that many advocates promise." The studies found that multi-seat districts and larger assemblies produce more diverse representation, and that hostility between parties declined as the number of parties grew under ranked and proportional systems but not under plurality. [cite:dt6_na_review] Proportional representation means electing several members from one district in rough proportion to the votes each party or group receives, rather than awarding a single seat to whoever wins.

The Fair Representation Act, introduced in the House on 23 July 2025 by Representative Don Beyer with Representatives Jamie Raskin, Scott Peters, Jim McGovern and Ro Khanna, all Democrats, would combine the two. It would require ranked-choice voting for the House and Senate. States with six or more House seats would elect members from districts of three to five seats each; states with five or fewer would elect all their members at large. It would bar maps that materially favour or disfavour a party, ban mid-decade redistricting except by court order, and have a federal three-judge court draw the map if a state misses its deadline. The House provisions would apply only after the 2030 census. [cite:dt6_fra_text] The route is an ordinary statute, relying on Congress's power under Article I to regulate how House elections are held, and it would require ranked choice in federal races even in the eighteen states that now ban it. [cite:dt6_baber] [cite:dt6_bp_bans2026]

The test

Thiel wrote in 2009 that he had little hope "that voting will make things better." [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] Yarvin said in January 2025, "I don't believe in actually voting at all," and the model Land summarises from his writing gives residents "no voice, free exit." [cite:de1_audit_nyt_voting] [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Their charge, in its strongest form, is that counting votes cannot produce a coherent public will, so the effort should be abandoned rather than improved; the companion volume sets out how their model would choose rulers instead. [scene:de35b_how_real_systems_choose|How real systems choose]

This part's facts cut both ways. Documented against the Dark Enlightenment: voting rules are not fixed. Within ten years voters, cities, legislatures and courts changed how votes are counted in two states and eleven cities, and in New York, Maine and Alaska the new rule produced winners whose final support was broader than a plurality winner's would have been. That is a system improving its own machinery, which a claim that democracy has simply failed does not predict. Documented against this book's thesis: the Alaska election shows that a reformed count can still eliminate the candidate a majority prefers, the San Francisco research shows that a harder ballot can fall hardest on the voters reform is meant to help, and the 2024 results show that voters themselves rejected most proposals to adopt the system. None of it answers Yarvin's objection about what voters know. Graded, the claim that elections cannot be improved is not supported; the claim that no counting rule fully captures what a majority wants is documented, and holds for ranked choice as for plurality; whether finishing democracy with better counting rules would answer the critique is, on this part alone, probable at best, and depends on reforms beyond the ballot that the next part examines.
