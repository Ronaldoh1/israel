The Roman Question: Porta Pia to the Prisoner Years, 1870–1929

For fifty-nine years after 1870 the bishop of Rome lived inside a city that belonged to a state he refused to recognise. Italians called the dispute the "Roman Question." The Lateran Treaty of 1929 named it in those words and declared it "definitively and irrevocably settled and therefore eliminated." [cite:vt2_lateran_text] This section sets out how the dispute began, what Italy offered in 1871, why Pius IX refused it, and how the refusal shaped papal conduct and Catholic politics until 1929.

20 September 1870

The French garrison was what had kept Rome papal after 1860. When the Franco-Prussian War broke out in July 1870, Napoleon III recalled it, and the French defeat at Sedan removed any prospect of its return. Italy offered to send troops into Rome on the pretext of protecting the pope; Pius IX refused. Italy declared war on 10 September 1870. Its army crossed the frontier the next day and reached the Aurelian Walls on 19 September. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates_rq]

On 20 September Italian artillery opened a breach beside the Porta Pia, in a stretch of wall in poor repair, and the city was taken. Papal resistance was token. The stated reason was that Pius IX wanted the record to show that Italy had taken Rome by force and not with his consent. A plebiscite followed in October, and the annexation of Rome to the Kingdom of Italy is dated 9 October 1870. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates_rq] That choice, to lose visibly rather than to negotiate, set the pattern for the next half-century: the Holy See preserved its legal claim by refusing every arrangement that implied acceptance of the loss.

The Law of Guarantees, 13 May 1871

Italy needed to show Catholic governments abroad that the pope would remain free to govern the Church. It could have convened an international congress on the pope's status, but Catholic governments showed no interest, so the Italian parliament legislated on its own. The Law of Guarantees, passed on 13 May 1871, declared the pope's person "sacred and inviolable," granted him royal honours and guards, left him the use of the Vatican and Lateran palaces and the villa at Castel Gandolfo, guaranteed free communication with the Catholic world and the freedom of a conclave, and assigned him an annual endowment of 3,225,000 lire. [cite:vt2_ce_guarantees] The law did not recognise papal sovereignty over any territory: the palaces remained, in Italian law, Italian. [cite:vt2_ce_guarantees]

Pius IX rejected it. Two days after its passage, in the encyclical Ubi Nos of 15 May 1871, he wrote that he "never can and never shall allow or accept those 'guarantees' devised by the Piedmont Government." His argument was that only civil sovereignty could secure the Church's freedom: Providence had given the popes civil rule so that the pope "may never be subject to any ruler or civil power." [cite:vt2_ewtn_ubinos] A privilege granted by an Italian statute could be withdrawn by another Italian statute; that was the core of the objection, and the later popes repeated it. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner] Pius IX expressly refused the endowment on 13 November 1872. Neither he nor his two successors ever drew on it, and the Holy See lived instead on donations from Catholics abroad. [cite:vt2_ce_guarantees]

The prisoner years

The popes of the period described themselves as prisoners of the Italian state. In practice this meant a self-imposed confinement. Pius IX, Leo XIII, Pius X, Benedict XV and Pius XI, until 1929, did not set foot outside the Vatican. They did not appear on the loggia of St Peter's facing the square, which lay in Italian Rome; the blessing Urbi et Orbi was given from a balcony facing an inner courtyard or from inside the basilica, and papal coronations were held in the Sistine Chapel. Pius IX had excommunicated King Victor Emmanuel II. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner] No physical barrier held the popes in; the confinement was a legal statement made with the body of the pope, and it lasted fifty-nine years. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner]

The same refusal applied to Catholic voters. Under the non expedit ("it is not expedient"), adopted by the Holy See in 1868 and confirmed by Pius IX in 1874, Italian Catholics were not to take part in national politics, summed up as "neither elected nor electors." Pius X largely relaxed the ban in 1904–05 to improve relations with the government, and Benedict XV formally lifted it in 1919, when he approved the new Partito Popolare Italiano (PPI). [cite:vt2_britannica_nonexpedit] The PPI took 20.5 per cent of the vote and 100 seats in 1919 and 108 seats in 1921. [cite:vt2_wiki_ppi] For the first time since unification a mass Catholic party sat in parliament; within seven years it had been destroyed, and how that happened is part of the story of the 1929 deal. [scene:vt2_pacts_signed|The pacts signed, 1929]

What the dispute was and was not

Two misreadings of the period circulate in the sources this volume replaces. The first is that the Vatican has been "a sovereign enclave since the fourth century." The record does not support it. The papacy held a territorial state from 756 to 1870; from 1870 to 1929 it held no territory at all in its own understanding of the law, only palaces whose use an Italian statute granted and which the pope refused to accept on those terms. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin] [cite:vt2_ce_guarantees] Whether the Holy See itself remained a subject of international law in those years is a separate question, examined in Part One of The Divine 3+1; the Lateran Treaty later described his sovereignty "in the international field" as "an attribute inherent to its nature," not a gift of Italy. [cite:vt2_lateran_text] [scene:dv1_sovereignty|The Divine 3+1: the tests of sovereignty]

The second misreading is that the popes were literally held captive. They were not. They chose not to leave so as not to appear to accept Italian rule over Rome, and the Italian state had offered them guarantees they declined. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner] [cite:vt2_ce_guarantees] The Holy See's stated position at the time was that any arrangement short of sovereignty would leave the head of a worldwide Church subject to one government; Italy's position was that its law gave the pope everything he needed except a state. The 1929 settlement split the difference: Italy conceded sovereignty, and the pope accepted that the sovereignty would be very small.

The end of the prisoner years was staged as deliberately as their beginning. On 25 July 1929, seven weeks after the treaty took effect, Pius XI led a eucharistic procession into St Peter's Square. The New York Times reported the next day that the pope had left the Vatican for the first time in 59 years, watched by 300,000 people. [cite:vt2_umich_procession1929] After the treaty the popes resumed visits to the Lateran basilica and summers at Castel Gandolfo. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner]
