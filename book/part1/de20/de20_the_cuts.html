The Cuts: Agencies, Firings and the Size of the Workforce

Musk stated the aim in public before the office existed and repeated it after. At Madison Square Garden on 27 October 2024 he said, "I think we can do at least $2 trillion" in cuts; on 13 February 2025 he told the World Governments Summit, "I think we do need to delete entire agencies," because "if we don't remove the roots of the weed, then it's easy for the weed to grow back." [cite:de11_fortune_2t] [cite:de11_cbs_wgs] This section sets out what was actually cut, agency by agency, how the courts handled the main firing programmes, and what the workforce figures show when each is read on its own definition. Who bore the cost is the subject of [scene:de20_who_paid|the next section]; whether the savings were real is graded in [scene:de11_claims_vs_record|Part Eleven] and [scene:de21_the_ending|Part Twenty-One].

USAID: an agency closed

The Agency for International Development (USAID), which ran most American foreign aid, was the first and largest target. Musk called it "a criminal organization" on 2 February 2025, as two of its security chiefs were put on leave for refusing his team classified material. [cite:de19_pbs_usaid] On 10 March Secretary of State Marco Rubio wrote, "After a 6 week review we are officially cancelling 83% of the programs at USAID": about 5,200 contracts ended, and the remaining 1,000 or so programmes were to be run by the State Department. He thanked DOGE. [cite:de20_ewn_rubio83] He then announced that "as of July 1st, USAID will officially cease to implement foreign assistance." [cite:de20_ap_usaidjuly] A federal judge found in March 2025 that the shutdown had likely "usurped the authority" of Congress, which created and funded the agency, but his order was stayed within ten days; [scene:de19_the_courts|Part Nineteen] follows that case. [cite:de19_upi_chuang] [cite:de19_ch_does] USAID did not supply data for the government auditors' workforce count, so its staff losses are not in the official totals below. [cite:de19_gao_workforce]

Probationary firings

Probationary employees, usually those in their first year in a job, have fewer protections against dismissal. On 14 February 2025 OPM emailed agency personnel chiefs asking them to separate probationary staff not identified as mission-critical. [cite:de20_crs_probation] About 25,000 people were targeted. [cite:de20_reuters_alsup] Judge William Alsup in San Francisco found on 13 March 2025 that OPM's direction was beyond its legal authority and ordered reinstatements at six agencies; a Maryland judge separately ordered reinstatements at eighteen agencies for nineteen states and Washington, D.C. [cite:de20_crs_probation] Both orders were stayed within days. On 8 April 2025 the Supreme Court stayed Alsup's order because the nonprofit plaintiffs it rested on had not shown standing, without deciding whether the firings were lawful, over the dissents of Justices Sotomayor and Jackson; the next day the Fourth Circuit stayed the Maryland order. [cite:de20_crs_probation] On 12 September 2025 Alsup ruled on the merits that OPM had unlawfully ordered the firings, but he did not order reinstatement, citing the Supreme Court's emergency rulings and the fact that many of those fired had found other work; he ordered nineteen agencies to correct their personnel files instead. [cite:de20_reuters_alsup] The firings were found unlawful and stood.

Reductions in force, and the agencies that reversed

A reduction in force (RIF) is the formal procedure for laying off permanent federal staff. A February 2025 executive order told agencies to prepare "large-scale" RIFs. Judge Susan Illston blocked the plans; on 8 July 2025 the Supreme Court lifted her order, adding that "we express no view on the legality of any" particular plan. Justice Jackson dissented, calling the decision "hubristic and senseless." [cite:de20_npr_trumpvafge] On 14 July the Court likewise allowed the layoff of more than 1,000 Education Department staff; Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, wrote that it "hands the Executive the power to repeal statutes by firing all those necessary to carry them out," and Secretary Linda McMahon called the president "the ultimate authority to make decisions about staffing levels." [cite:de20_rollcall_mcmahon] By January 2026 Education's workforce had fallen by more than 45 per cent, the steepest decline of any large agency. [cite:de19_gao_workforce]

The Health and Human Services department eliminated about 10,000 posts, including 3,500 at the Food and Drug Administration, 2,400 at the Centers for Disease Control and 1,200 at the National Institutes of Health, with total departures of up to 20,000 by its own figures; Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. later said upwards of 2,000 people had been "cut by mistake," and hundreds were rehired. [cite:de20_biospace_hhs] Veterans Affairs announced in 2025 a cut of more than 80,000 and then, on 7 July 2025, dropped it: "A department-wide RIF is off the table," Secretary Doug Collins said, and about 30,000 positions were to go through attrition and voluntary departures instead. [cite:de20_bl_va] The National Weather Service lost more than 550 staff, mostly through probationary firings and early retirements, and in August 2025 was given permission to hire 450 meteorologists, hydrologists and technicians back. [cite:de20_cnn_nws]

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the regulator for lenders and payment firms, is the case that did not resolve. Its acting leadership planned to cut staff from about 1,175 to 556; a court injunction obtained by the employees' union blocked the layoffs, and on 19 June 2026 the full appeals court in Washington refused to lift it and sent the revised plan back to the trial judge. [cite:de20_orrick_cfpb] [cite:de20_ab_cfpb] In July 2026 the parties agreed, and Judge Amy Berman Jackson ordered, that nothing would move until 60 days after a new director, Brian Johnson, is confirmed, or until 3 January 2027. [cite:de20_ab_cfpb] The Senate Banking Committee advanced his nomination 13 to 11 on 17 September 2026, with no floor vote scheduled when last reported. [cite:de20_cfm_johnson] Status as of October 2026: the injunction stands, and the bureau's 2026 budget was capped at $446.8 million, against $785.4 million in 2024. [cite:de20_ab_cfpb]

Grants sorted by a chatbot

At the National Endowment for the Humanities, two DOGE staffers with no prior government experience, Justin Fox and Nate Cavanaugh, used ChatGPT to flag more than $100 million in grants as related to diversity, equity and inclusion; the grants were then cancelled. Their prompt, quoted in court filings, began: "From the perspective of someone looking to identify DEI grants, does this involve DEI?" [cite:de20_abc_depos] Asked under oath why a documentary about Holocaust survivors had been flagged, Fox answered, "It's a gender-based story that's inherently discriminatory to focus on this specific group." [cite:de20_abc_depos] The deposition is sworn testimony in a pending civil suit; it shows how the list was made, not a court finding about the cancellations.

The size of the workforce: three measures

Three figures for the shrinking federal workforce are widely quoted, and they measure different things. GAO, using data from 22 major agencies, counted about 378,000 separations during 2025 and about 127,000 hires, including temporary staff, for a net decline of nearly 256,000, more than 11 per cent, between December 2024 and January 2026; losses ranged from about 1 per cent at Homeland Security to more than 45 per cent at Education. [cite:de19_gao_workforce] OPM's director, Scott Kupor, counted about 317,000 employees who left during 2025, a gross figure of departures: some 144,000 deferred resignations, about 17,000 layoffs, about 7,000 probationary dismissals, about 21,000 early retirements and buyouts, and about 129,000 ordinary attrition. [cite:us2_bloomberg_317k] Reuters, measuring from September 2024 to January 2026, reported a 12 per cent fall. [cite:us2_reuters_workforce] A figure of about 386,800 also circulates, sometimes attributed to GAO; it is the headcount behind Reuters' 12 per cent fall over September 2024 to January 2026, a net measure over a longer period, and not GAO's count of separations, which is about 378,000. [cite:de20_audit_reuters_386k] [cite:de19_gao_workforce] One press account of the GAO report added that 83 per cent of those who left did so voluntarily and that about 129,000 left through the deferred-resignation offer known as the "Fork in the Road"; GAO's own summary does not state those figures, and the 129,000 is not the same count as OPM's 144,000 deferred resignations. [cite:de20_cs_256k] [cite:us2_bloomberg_317k]

None of these figures can be added to another. The net figure is the honest answer to how much smaller the government became; the gross figures describe how people left. The cuts were also uneven by design or by result: Education lost almost half its staff while Homeland Security, where immigration enforcement sits, was nearly flat. [cite:de19_gao_workforce] That contrast matters to the worst-case reading this book tests, and [scene:de20_who_paid|the next section] takes it up with the question of who bore the cost.
