Thirty Years of Steady Money, 1990–2020

For three decades before AIPAC entered campaign spending, pro-Israel money in American elections followed a steady, modest course. It came from individual donors and from several dozen independent political action committees, most of them small, some of them regional, and it went mostly to Democrats. This section sets out that long record from the oldest consistent series, kept by the Center for Responsive Politics, and looks closely at the one cycle, 2018, for which a full analysis was published. It is the baseline against which the changes of the 2020s have to be measured.

The PAC series. OpenSecrets' series of contributions from pro-Israel PACs to federal candidates begins in 1990. It shows about $4.0 million in that cycle, a range of between about $2.1 million and $4.3 million a cycle through the 1990s and 2000s, and then a slow rise: $5.1 million in 2012, $5.9 million in 2014, $7.5 million in 2016, and $8.7 million in both 2018 and 2020. In thirty years the PACs gave $71.3 million. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs2020] PAC money was always the smaller part. From 1990 to 2012, when the Center also published totals for individual donors, individuals gave about twice as much as the PACs, $71.5 million against $34.5 million, figures from an earlier release of the Center's data that do not match the PAC table above. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs]

Who gave. Over the thirty years the largest pro-Israel PAC was not AIPAC, which gave nothing, nor a hawkish group. It was J Street's PAC, which describes itself as "pro-Israel, pro-peace" and supports a two-state solution, with $14.5 million in contributions; it gave about $4 million in 2018 alone. Next came NORPAC, a long-established committee based in New Jersey, with $8.1 million, then National PAC with $4.5 million, the Republican Jewish Coalition with $3.4 million and the National Action Committee with $2.9 million. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs2020] [cite:pm_jstreet_2018] The largest giver in the long series was thus the most dovish of the groups, a fact that sits awkwardly with both the defenders and the critics of "the lobby" as a single bloc.

The 2018 cycle in full. The Guardian's analysis of OpenSecrets data for 2018 gives the fullest picture of a single pre-2021 cycle. Pro-Israel donors and groups spent more than $22 million that cycle on lobbying and campaign contributions together. About $5 million went to lobbying, the most since tracking began in 1998, AIPAC accounting for about $3.5 million of it. Just under $15 million went to candidates' campaigns, the most since 1990, reaching 269 members of the House and 57 senators, with Democrats receiving about twice as much as Republicans. [cite:pm_guardian_2019] The largest recipients were Senators Bob Menendez of New Jersey ($548,507), Ted Cruz of Texas ($352,894), Sherrod Brown of Ohio ($230,342) and Tammy Baldwin of Wisconsin ($229,896), and the Texas Senate candidate Beto O'Rourke ($226,690). [cite:pm_guardian_2019]

The scale, in proportion. In 2018 pro-Israel donors ranked thirty-fourth among interest groups giving to members of Congress. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] The securities and investment industry spent $389 million in the same cycle and the real-estate industry $186 million. [cite:pm_guardian_2019] By that measure pro-Israel campaign money was a middling interest, large for a foreign-policy cause and small beside the great economic interests. Its distinctive feature was not size. It was organization: a single-issue constituency, active in both parties, whose donors, as Part Two describes, gave early to promising candidates and signalled to one another whom to back. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying]

The long series has two limits that matter for what follows. It counts contributions only: before 2010 there were no super PACs, and before 2022 pro-Israel independent spending was comparatively small. And it depends on OpenSecrets' classification of which PACs and donors are pro-Israel, a judgment other trackers make differently. The next section turns to the cycles after 2021, when both limits stopped holding.
