The State's Own Channel: What Israel Spends Abroad

Everything in Parts Two to Eight concerns American organizations spending American money. This part turns abroad, and it begins with the one actor whose money appears in every country studied: the government of Israel itself. Unlike the American organizations, it may not give or spend in American elections. [cite:fec_foreign_nationals] What it may do, in the United States and elsewhere, is pay for public diplomacy, as every government does: advertising, hosted delegations, cultural programs and, in the United States, the work of agents who register their contracts under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. Since the war that began in October 2023 that spending has grown faster than any other figure in this volume.

The budget. Israel's budget for 2026 sets aside about $730 million for public diplomacy, known in Hebrew as hasbara, more than four times the roughly $150 million of 2025, which was itself about twenty times what Israel had spent before the war. The foreign minister, Gideon Sa'ar, argued that the money should be thought of "like investing in jets, bombs and missile interceptors." [cite:pm_jta_730m] The Quincy Institute, a Washington research group that favors restraint in American foreign policy, estimates that Israel has likely spent more than $1 billion on public diplomacy since 7 October 2023, more than $100 million of it through contracts registered under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. [cite:pm_quincy_eighth]

What it buys. In 2025 the spending included a $50 million advertising campaign on Google, YouTube, X and Outbrain; about $40 million to host some 400 foreign delegations of lawmakers, pastors, influencers and university presidents; a $4.1 million campaign aimed at evangelical churches; and the paid influencer network known as the Esther Project. [cite:pm_jta_730m] The Quincy Institute counted 300 delegations sponsored by the Foreign Ministry in 2025, with 6,000 participants from 70 countries. [cite:pm_quincy_eighth] The trip for 250 American state legislators described in Part Seven was one of them. [cite:pm_idcap_50states]

The registered contracts. In the United States the largest contracts are public because the contractors registered. In September 2025 two firms registered as agents of Israel's government, both working under contracts with the advertising group Havas: Bridges Partners, which ran the Esther Project, a campaign of up to $900,000 to pay 14 to 18 American social-media influencers, none of whom has been named or has registered; and Clock Tower X, owned by Brad Parscale, Donald Trump's campaign manager in 2020, for a campaign aimed at young Americans and at how artificial-intelligence chatbots describe Israel. [cite:pc_fara_complaint] [cite:dc_parscale_fara] The size of the Clock Tower X contract is reported differently by different sources: a contract of record of $6 million, later raised to $9 million, with $15 million received in its first six months according to a supplemental filing, and $46.5 million over a year in the Quincy Institute's account. [cite:dc_parscale_fara] [cite:intercept_parscale] [cite:pm_quincy_eighth] The Quincy paper also lists smaller registered contracts, among them $3.2 million for a firm aimed at evangelical Christians. [cite:pm_quincy_eighth] The simulation's accounts of [scene:ip_s01|the Esther Project] and [scene:ip_s02|Clock Tower X] give the filings in detail.

Does it work? Israeli and American specialists quoted by the Jewish Telegraphic Agency doubted that it would. Nicholas Cull of the University of Southern California, Shibley Telhami of the University of Maryland and Ilan Manor of Ben-Gurion University argued, in different words, that messaging cannot overcome objections to policy; Israel's consul general in Los Angeles defended the spending and called for more of it on American film and television. [cite:pm_jta_730m] The question for this volume is narrower. The state's spending is the one part of the pro-Israel effort abroad whose size is known with any precision, because the United States requires its agents to report. In most other countries, as the following sections show, there is no equivalent requirement, and the record is thinner.
