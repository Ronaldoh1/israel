The Fundamental Agreement: Recognition on Terms, 1991–1994

The Holy See recognised the State of Israel in a treaty signed in Jerusalem on 30 December 1993, forty-five years after Israel's independence. The document, the Fundamental Agreement between the Holy See and the State of Israel, is short, legal in tone and explicit about what each side wanted. Read with the dates around it, it is the best single test in this volume of what drove the Holy See's position: Israeli or Zionist pressure, Arab and Palestinian sensitivities, or the Holy See's own doctrine and interests. This section sets out how the negotiation began, what the treaty says, how it was received, and what its timing shows.

From Madrid to the working commission

The reasons the Holy See gave for withholding recognition were political and legal, and by the 1980s it said so openly. Rabbi David Rosen, who later served on the bilateral commission that drafted the treaty, wrote that the Holy See had for years said there was no theological barrier to relations, and that its reluctance was "the consequence of secular political considerations." [cite:vt17_rosen_1994] He listed them: the Catholic Church's communities, institutions and property in Arab and Muslim countries, which it feared would suffer a backlash; the weight of the Arab world in its work across the global South; and the position of the local church, most of whose members identified as Palestinian and whose leadership opposed any advance with Israel "until Palestinian political claims had been satisfied." [cite:vt17_rosen_1994] Israel's foreign ministry gives a similar list: Israel's sovereignty over Jerusalem, the absence of recognised borders, the holy places, the needs of local Catholics, the Palestinian refugees, and the fear of reprisals against Christian minorities in Arab states. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] When the treaty was signed, the Associated Press summarised the Holy See's long-standing grounds as Israel's treatment of Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, church property and the status of Jerusalem. [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993]

The political setting changed after the Gulf War. The Madrid Conference opened on 30 October 1991, co-chaired by Presidents George H. W. Bush and Mikhail Gorbachev, and for the first time brought Israel face to face with Syria, Lebanon, Egypt and a joint Jordanian-Palestinian delegation. [cite:vt17_state_madrid] In April 1992, according to Israel's foreign ministry, John Paul II directed the Curia to open negotiations toward full relations, and Archbishop Tauran informed Ambassador Pazner of the decision about ten days later. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] The Bilateral Permanent Working Commission agreed its agenda in Jerusalem on 15 July 1992 and confirmed it at the Vatican on 29 July. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] Rosen, from inside the process, described the Holy See's aim as turning into binding law what it already enjoyed in practice under Israeli rule: the legal status of its institutions, freedom of worship, schools, welfare work and tax treatment. [cite:vt17_rosen_1994] The Holy See's spokesman, Joaquín Navarro-Valls, put the change of setting more simply, as Rosen reports him: "The Palestinians are talking with Israelis, why shouldn't we?" [cite:vt17_rosen_1994]

The negotiation took about a year and a half. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] In the middle of it, on 13 September 1993, Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization signed the Declaration of Principles in Washington, together with letters of mutual recognition. [cite:vt17_state_madrid] [cite:vt17_wiki_oslo1] Julian Schvindlerman of the Jerusalem Center for Public Affairs, a pro-Israel institute, read the sequence directly: "Only after the PLO agreed to formally recognize Israel in September 1993 did the Holy See decide to do the same." [cite:vt17_jcpa_schvindlerman] That is his reading of the order of events; the negotiation itself had begun fifteen months before Oslo.

What the treaty says

The agreement was signed at Israel's Foreign Ministry in Jerusalem by Monsignor Claudio Maria Celli, the Holy See's under-secretary for relations with states, and Deputy Foreign Minister Yossi Beilin, in English and Hebrew texts of equal authority. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993] Its preamble records the parties' awareness of "the unique nature of the relationship between the Catholic Church and the Jewish people." [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] Its fifteen articles fall into three groups.

The first group concerns the Church in Israel. Israel affirmed its commitment to freedom of religion and conscience (Article 1); recognised "the right of the Catholic Church to carry out its religious, moral, educational and charitable functions, and to have its own institutions" (Article 3); agreed to "maintain and respect the 'Status quo' in the Christian Holy Places" (Article 4); and the two sides reaffirmed the Church's right to property while listing "unclear, unsettled and disputed issues, concerning property, economic and fiscal matters" for further negotiation (Article 10). [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] These were the institutional interests the Holy See had named since 1948.

The second group concerns the Jewish people. In Article 2 both parties committed themselves to combat "all forms of antisemitism and all kinds of racism and of religious intolerance", and the Holy See reiterated its "condemnation of hatred, persecution and all other manifestations of antisemitism directed against the Jewish people" and deplored "attacks on Jews and desecration of Jewish synagogues and cemeteries." [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] This article carried the theology of Nostra Aetate into a treaty for the first time. [scene:vt17_nostra_aetate_notes|Nostra Aetate and the Notes]

The third group concerns the state and the region, and it is here that the Holy See set its terms. Article 11 reads, in its second paragraph:

"The Holy See, while maintaining in every case the right to exercise its moral and spiritual teaching-office, deems it opportune to recall that, owing to its own character, it is solemnly committed to remaining a stranger to all merely temporal conflicts, which principle applies specifically to disputed territories and unsettled borders." [cite:vt1_va_fa1993]

The clause echoes Article 24 of the Lateran Treaty of 1929, in which the Holy See declared that it would remain extraneous to temporal disputes between states. [cite:vt1_va_lateran] [scene:vt2_pacts_signed|Part Two: the pacts signed] In the 1993 setting it did two things at once. It reserved the Holy See's right to speak on moral grounds, and it stated that recognising Israel did not mean taking a position on the territories Israel had held since 1967 or on its borders. Article 13 added that the treaty did not prejudice either party's existing treaties with other states. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] Article 12 committed both sides to continue negotiating, and Article 14 provided for an exchange of special representatives on signature and full diplomatic relations at the level of nunciature and embassy after entry into force. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993]

The Holy See also issued a statement on the day of signing that it continued to seek an internationally guaranteed "special status" for Jerusalem, with freedom of worship and access to the holy places for all religions. [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993] The 1967 position on the city survived recognition intact.

Reception and the 1994 protocol

Beilin said the agreement opened a new era; President Ezer Weizman hoped for a papal visit. [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993] About eighty religious Israelis demonstrated outside the ministry with placards about the Inquisition, and the daily Haaretz wrote that the Church "should not be forgiven." [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993] A Syrian Orthodox Christian interviewed in Jerusalem called it "against the Palestinian people"; the Latin Patriarch, Michel Sabbah, a Palestinian, placed it within a "general process of reconciliation in the whole area." [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993]

The nunciature in Israel and Israel's embassy to the Holy See opened on 19 January 1994; the agreement entered into force on 10 March 1994. [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] A joint protocol signed in Jerusalem on 14 June 1994 established full diplomatic relations from 15 June, and Israel's first ambassador, Shmuel Hadas, presented his credentials on 29 September. [cite:vt1_jvl_protocol1994] [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] A Legal Personality Agreement followed on 10 November 1997; neither it nor the Fundamental Agreement has been ratified by the Knesset. [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] The agreement on property and fiscal matters foreseen in Article 10 is followed, with its status as of October 2026, in Part Eighteen. [scene:vt18_relations_since_1994|Part Eighteen: relations since 1994]

What the timing shows

Laid out by date, the shifts do not follow the needs of the Zionist movement or of Israel. The Holy See withheld recognition from 1948 onward, through the whole period before 1967 and for a quarter-century after, and held to its own Jerusalem demand against Israel's stated position throughout. Public Israeli pressure, in 1982 and 1987, did not move it. The steps it did take came in two forms: doctrinal changes about the Jewish people that were explicitly kept apart from the state, and diplomatic changes that followed the Palestinian track, opening talks after Palestinians sat at the Madrid table and signing after the PLO recognised Israel. The stated interests named in 1948 to 1949, the holy places and the legal security of Catholic institutions, are the ones written into Articles 3, 4 and 10 of the treaty, and the reservation on "disputed territories and unsettled borders" in Article 11 kept recognition from becoming a position on the territories Israel had held since 1967. This reading rests on the published record; the Holy See's own files for 1958 to 1993 are closed, and the Secretariat of State's negotiating papers cannot yet be checked against it.
