How the Vatican Governs: One Ruler, Two Governments, a Worldwide Service

The previous section described the territory. This one describes the government, and here the Vatican differs from every other city in this volume in a basic way. In the City of London power is divided among a Lord Mayor, aldermen, councillors and liverymen, and in Washington among a mayor, a council and the Congress above them. In Vatican City it is not divided at all. The Fundamental Law that Pope Francis promulgated on 13 May 2023 restates that the pope holds "the fullness of legislative, executive and judicial powers." [cite:dv3_vn_fundlaw_2023] Everything else in the structure is delegated by him and can be taken back by him.

The state's government. For the territory, the pope delegates legislative work to the Pontifical Commission for Vatican City State. Until 2023 its members were cardinals; the new law added "other members" appointed by the pope for five-year terms, and these may be lay men and women. [cite:dv3_vn_fundlaw_2023] The Commission's cardinal president is also President of the Governorate, the administration that runs the city day to day: the museums, the Apostolic Palace apart from the basilica, the post office, the gardens and the shop. A legal guide to the city compares the office to a mayor's, and its holder can call on the Gendarmerie and ask for help from the Swiss Guard. [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] The Commission must pass a provisional and a final budget each year and a three-year financial plan, balanced and following "principles of clarity, transparency and fairness," and send them directly to the pope for approval. [cite:dv3_vn_fundlaw_2023] There is no assembly, no election and no opposition. The only people who choose the ruler are the cardinals in conclave, and once chosen he serves for life; the present pope, Leo XIV, was elected on 8 May 2025. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican]

The law and the courts. The city's legal order is built in layers. A law of 1 October 2008, in force from 1 January 2009, makes the Church's canon law the first source of law, and admits Italian law only where it does not conflict with canon law, divine law or the Lateran agreements. Criminal cases are still tried under the Italian penal code of 1889, kept in force until the city adopts its own. [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] The courts run from a single judge for small matters to a three-judge Tribunal, a Court of Appeal whose members are mostly judges of the Roman Rota, and a Court of Cassation whose three judges are cardinals. [cite:dv3_nyu_globalex] Every judge holds office from the same sovereign who also makes the law and runs the administration. The largest test of that system in modern times, the trial over the Secretariat of State's London property, is still unresolved in November 2026; the section on the Vatican's money takes it up.

The Church's government. The territory is small; the government that matters is the Holy See's. Its executive is the Roman Curia, which Pope Francis reorganized by the apostolic constitution Praedicate Evangelium, promulgated on 19 March 2022 and in force from 5 June 2022. The constitution arranged the Curia into sixteen dicasteries, the departments of the Church's central government, and opened their leadership to "any member of the faithful," lay people included. [cite:dv3_cwr_pe] At its centre sits the Secretariat of State, which coordinates the departments and conducts the Holy See's relations with governments; the communications dicastery, for example, is required to work "in particular with the Secretariat of State." [cite:dv3_cwr_pe] The Secretary of State since 15 October 2013 has been Cardinal Pietro Parolin, a career diplomat who in December 2008 had led the Holy See's delegation to its permanent bilateral commission with Israel. [cite:dv3_press_parolin] His comments on Gaza in October 2025 drew a sharp public rebuke from the Israeli government, and Pope Leo XIV publicly backed him. [cite:dv3_catholicherald_parolin]

A diplomatic service larger than most countries'. No city in the world, and few states, maintains a foreign service on the Holy See's scale. At the start of 2026 it had full diplomatic relations with 184 states, together with the European Union and the Order of Malta. It kept 128 nunciatures, the Holy See's embassies, and 93 diplomatic missions were accredited to it in Rome. [cite:dv3_aleteia_dipl_2026] Only about a dozen countries had no formal relations, among them China, North Korea and Afghanistan; Saudi Arabia was served by an apostolic delegation instead. [cite:dv3_aleteia_dipl_2026] The Holy See has been a permanent observer at the United Nations since 6 April 1964 and has chosen not to seek membership, while belonging in its own right to several specialized agencies. [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] [cite:dv1_wiki_holysee_un] Pope Leo XIV addressed the assembled diplomatic corps on 9 January 2026, the yearly occasion on which the Holy See states its positions on the world's conflicts. [cite:dv3_aleteia_dipl_2026]

This is the instrument through which "the Vatican" acted on Palestine for a century, and it should be measured accurately. A nunciature is an embassy, not a garrison or a bank. The Holy See has no army, no trade to cut off and no aid budget comparable to a state's. Its power is the power to recognize or withhold recognition, to speak, to negotiate agreements on the Church's own position, and to move the governments of Catholic countries, insofar as they choose to be moved. Every one of those tools appears in the record that follows; none of them is military or financial in the way Washington's and London's were.

The budget. The Holy See publishes consolidated accounts through its Secretariat for the Economy. For 2024, published on 26 November 2025, they showed operating income of €1.23 billion and operating expenses of €1.275 billion, an operational deficit of €44.4 million that investment returns, one-off asset sales and higher donations turned into an overall surplus of €1.6 million. [cite:dv3_america_budget_2024] Excluding its hospitals, 43 percent of revenue came from donations and 40 percent from the Holy See's own activities; of the spending on its mission, 37 percent went to support local churches and 10 percent to the nunciatures. [cite:dv3_america_budget_2024] These are the accounts of a large charity and a small foreign ministry combined. They are not the accounts of a financial power able to underwrite a state, and the next section shows that the money ran the other way for much of the papacy's modern history.

What the structure means for the question. Two conclusions follow. First, because power is undivided, the Holy See's policy toward Zionism and Israel at any moment was the pope's policy, carried out through the Secretariat of State; there was no parliament or lobby inside the Vatican that could have overruled him. When the record shows a position held from 1904 to 1993, it is a position held by six popes and their secretaries of state, each of whom could have changed it. Second, because the Holy See's tools are diplomatic, its contribution to any state, Israel included, has to be looked for in recognition, in words and in the votes of Catholic governments, not in money or weapons. That is where the rest of this part looks.
