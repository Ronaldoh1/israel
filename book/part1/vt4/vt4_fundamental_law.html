The Fundamental Law: One Ruler, Delegated Powers

Vatican City State has a written constitution, called the Fundamental Law. It has had four versions in under a century, and the most recent was signed less than three months before this volume was completed. Each version starts from the same principle, which makes the state unlike any other in Europe: every power of government belongs to one person, the Pope, and every other office exercises power because he has delegated it. The working machinery of the state, a commission that makes laws and an administration that carries them out, is real and has its own rules, but it rests on that delegation and can be overridden by it.

Four versions, 1929 to 2026

The first Fundamental Law was published on 7 June 1929, the day the Lateran Treaty came into force, under Pius XI. John Paul II replaced it with a new text promulgated on 26 November 2000, which took effect on 22 February 2001, the feast of the Chair of St Peter. Francis published a third version on 13 May 2023, in force from 7 June 2023. [cite:vt4_wiki_fl] Leo XIV signed a fourth on 31 July 2026, the memorial of St Ignatius of Loyola, with immediate effect, replacing the 2023 text. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2026] Catholic News Agency counted it as the fourth constitution since the state was created. [cite:vt4_cwr_fl2026]

The Pope's three powers

The 2000 text, whose structure later versions kept, opens by stating that the Pope, as sovereign of Vatican City State, holds "la pienezza dei poteri legislativo, esecutivo e giudiziario", the fullness of legislative, executive and judicial power. When the papacy is vacant these powers pass to the College of Cardinals, which may legislate only in urgent cases and only for the period of the vacancy. Representation of the state abroad and the making of treaties are reserved to the Pope and exercised through the Secretariat of State. Amnesties, pardons and remissions of sentence are reserved to him alone. [cite:vt4_wipo_fl2000] Francis's 2023 law confirmed the same "fullness of legislative, executive and judicial powers" in the Pope. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2023] [cite:vt4_ncr_fl2023]

In the vocabulary of political science this is an elective absolute monarchy: the ruler is chosen for life by the cardinals in conclave, and no other body can bind him. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] There is no parliament of residents, no separation of powers in the modern sense, and no court that can review the sovereign's acts. The judges themselves, under the 2020 judicial law, are "hierarchically dependent on the Supreme Pontiff", and the Pope may remove any case to a particular court at any stage. [cite:vt4_cwr_judicial2020] [cite:vt4_vcs_judicial] What the Fundamental Law does supply is a set of ordinary channels through which those powers are normally used.

The Commission and the Governorate

Under the 2000 law, legislative power was exercised by a Pontifical Commission for Vatican City State made up of cardinals, with a cardinal president, all appointed by the Pope for five years, except in matters the Pope chose to reserve to himself. Executive power belonged to the president of that commission, assisted by a Secretary General and a Vice Secretary General. [cite:vt4_wipo_fl2000] The executive offices together are called the Governorate, and its president is in effect the head of the state's administration: the state's directorates, among them those for security and for infrastructure and services, report to it. [cite:vt4_vcs_geography] The same person is president of both the Commission and the Governorate. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2023]

The 2023 law changed the composition. The Commission would consist of cardinals, including the president, and "other members", appointed by the Pope for five years, which allowed laymen and laywomen to sit on the state's legislative body. It also expanded the budget rules: the Commission must adopt annual budgets and a three-year financial plan, the plan to be approved directly by the Pope, and the budget must aim at balance and follow "principles of clarity, transparency and fairness". [cite:vt4_vn_fl2023] [cite:vt4_ncr_fl2023]

The presidency was still reserved to a cardinal. In 2025 Francis nonetheless appointed Sister Raffaella Petrini as President of both the Commission and the Governorate, the first woman and the first non-cardinal in the post. Catholic News Agency described the appointment as "legally irregular" until the law caught up. [cite:vt4_cwr_fl2026] On 19 November 2025 Leo XIV issued a motu proprio repealing the paragraph of the 2023 law, Article 8(1), that limited the presidency to cardinals. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2026] The episode illustrates the system: the sovereign appointed first and amended the constitution afterwards, and no body in the state could have objected.

What the 2026 law changed

Leo XIV's Fundamental Law of 31 July 2026 repeals the 2023 text in full and restates, in line with the Lateran Pacts, that the state exists to guarantee the independence and international sovereignty of the Holy See. [cite:vt4_infovat_fl2026] According to Vatican News, its Article 8 describes the Commission as "composed of Cardinals and other Members, including the President", all appointed by the Pope for five years, so that the presidency is no longer reserved to cardinals. The Governorate is described as directly accountable to the Pope. The law sets out more precisely how the President and the Secretary General divide their work, stresses that the Secretary General's post is an institutional office, and allows it to be entrusted to more than one person. Article 22(1) refers the organisation of the courts to the separate law on the judicial system. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2026] Catholic News Agency added that a General Councillor may attend the Commission's meetings in a consultative role. [cite:vt4_cwr_fl2026] When the law was promulgated, Sister Raffaella Petrini held both presidencies, and no change had been announced as of October 2026. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2026] [cite:vt4_cwr_fl2026] The reports consulted for this section summarize the 2026 law; they do not reproduce its full text, and this section claims nothing about articles they do not describe.

What the law does not cover

The Fundamental Law governs the 44 hectares. It does not govern the Curia, the body that serves the Holy See and the Church worldwide: that is ordered by a separate apostolic constitution, whose 2022 version Part Six describes. [scene:vt6_curia_2022|Part Six: the Curia since 2022] Nor does it govern the Holy See's finances as a whole. The Governorate has its own budget, from the museums and the state's commercial services among other things; the Holy See's investment arm, APSA, and the Institute for the Works of Religion are separate bodies whose accounts Part Nine reads. [scene:vt9_apsa_governorate|Part Nine: APSA and the Governorate] A reader trying to work out who runs "the Vatican" therefore has to ask which part: the territory is run by the Governorate under a commission, the Holy See by the Secretariat of State and the dicasteries, and both by the same Pope.

The design also explains a feature that outsiders sometimes take for secrecy. A state with a single sovereign and no electorate has no parliament to publish debates and no opposition to force disclosure. The 2023 law's language on "clarity, transparency and fairness" in the budget, and the judicial reforms described in the next section, were introduced by the sovereign himself; the record shows the rules changing by papal decision, not by any outside check. [cite:vt4_vn_fl2023] Part Five describes the outside checks that do exist, through treaties and international bodies. [scene:vt5_moneyval_euro|Part Five: MONEYVAL and the euro]
