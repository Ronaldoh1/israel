### Script 3 — Book III: DOGE
[HOOK: "They promised every household a $5,000 check."]
They promised every household a $5,000 "DOGE dividend". Here's what the government's own auditor found.

DOGE was created by executive order on 20 January 2025. Musk was its public face. But in court the White House said he was a senior adviser with no formal authority, not DOGE's head.

The pitch was $2 trillion in savings. DOGE claimed $215 billion. The Government Accountability Office reviewed $110 billion of it and couldn't verify 96 per cent of the grant savings. The dividend was never legislated and never paid. Even on DOGE's own number, it works out to about $550 a household, not $5,000.
[ON SCREEN: $5,000 promised → ~$550 on their own math → $0 paid]

What did it reach? Treasury's payment systems, federal personnel files, Social Security, the IRS. Courts and inspectors general documented the access, and some of it was found unlawful. What did it cut? USAID was closed on 1 July 2025, and large parts of the federal workforce were gone, with different agencies counting it differently. The book shows each count with its definition, and the studies that estimate deaths from the aid cuts, with their methods and the criticism of them.

Now the worst-case question. Was "cutting waste" the excuse, and a government without checks the goal? Here's the honest grade. The cuts as the occasion: documented. A deliberate plan to use them as a pretext: not documented. What actually lasted wasn't DOGE, which ended on 4 July 2026. It was a rule that lets presidents turn career civil servants into at-will employees, and a Supreme Court ruling that lets presidents fire officials Congress tried to protect.
[ON SCREEN: what outlived DOGE → the power shift]

That's a real shift of power toward the presidency. It isn't, yet, a ruler without a veto. Courts still ruled against it, and Congress never voted for the dividend. Watch that line.

### Script 4 — Book IV: Exit
[HOOK: "What if you could buy your own country?"]
What if the rich could simply leave the country, and build their own?

That's "exit", the Dark Enlightenment's favourite word. Book Four follows what happened when people tried it.

First, seasteading: floating cities in international waters, with early money from Peter Thiel. The deal with French Polynesia lapsed.

Then charter cities. In Honduras a law created zones with their own rules, and on the island of Roatán a project called Próspera was built. Here's how its charter works. The company names four of the nine council seats, and big decisions need six votes, so the company's seats can block any of them. Remote "e-residents" can't vote at all.
[ON SCREEN: company seats can block any major decision]

Honduras repealed the law in 2022. Próspera answered with an international arbitration claim of up to $10.775 billion, now asking for restitution or $1.63 billion. As of October 2026 there is still no award. Thiel is one step removed: he put up more than half of the fund that invested.

Trump's "Freedom Cities", new cities on federal land? A campaign pledge, rated "stalled" in 2026. No law.

Then the digital exit. Yarvin's own network, Urbit, has identities arranged in tiers, with "galaxies" at the top. In 2010, on his own blog, he called the design "digital feudalism". And in October 2025 the galaxy owners voted to replace the foundation's board, and Yarvin's identity stepped aside.
[ON SCREEN: even the founder got voted out]

So here's the verdict. Rule by owners: documented, in the design itself. Exit as an enclave for the few: probable. Owners ruling without a veto: not supported. A country repealed the zone, courts pushed back, and the owners checked the founder. The exit dream keeps running into the thing it was built to escape: other people.

### Script 5 — Book V: The Eye
[HOOK: "You don't need a secret police if you can buy the data."]
You don't need a secret police if you can buy the data. That's Book Five.

These tools were built or bought for immigration enforcement. Book Five shows what each one does, who used it under both parties, where it failed, and what the law allows. Then it asks the worst-case question: what if they were pointed at everyone?
[ON SCREEN: capability is not intent — but capability is documented]

Your phone: apps sell location through advertising systems, and brokers resell it to agencies. Reporting describes ICE tools that watch phones across whole neighbourhoods. Your face: ICE's Mobile Fortify app scans faces in the street, you can't refuse, and the scans are kept for 15 years, even for citizens. Your bills: utility records on more than 170 million people reached ICE through data brokers. Your taxes: the IRS gave ICE addresses, and a federal judge found 42,695 of those disclosures unlawful. Your health coverage: Medicaid data went to ICE, a court cut it back, and reporting from court filings says a dataset was shared with Palantir and later purged.

And "anonymous" data? A landmark study found that zip code, birth date and sex alone identified 87 per cent of Americans in 1990 census data.

Palantir is the glue. It doesn't collect your data. It joins whatever an agency is authorised to feed it, and ICE paid $29.9 million for a system called ImmigrationOS.
[ON SCREEN: a connector is not access — but access can be granted]

Your car, your watch, your doorbell, your school records, your credit file, your travel bookings: Book Five maps them all, including who can already stop you boarding a plane.

And history says the worry is fair. The FBI's COINTELPRO targeted activists; the NSA watched senators of both parties. The people who exposed later programmes were prosecuted, and the officials who misled Congress were not.

Verdict: the capability is documented. Using it on critics as a programme is not documented. The book tells you exactly where that line sits today.

### Script 6 — Book VI: The Pathway and the Force
[HOOK: "Every system needs someone to knock on the door."]
Data doesn't arrest anyone. Somebody has to knock on the door. Book Six asks who.

First, the pathway. A Supreme Court ruling in June 2026 let presidents fire officials Congress tried to protect. A new civil-service rule makes career experts easier to remove. Seventeen inspectors general, the internal watchdogs, were fired in a single week in 2025. The book traces each change: what it removed, who benefits, and which ones courts blocked.
[ON SCREEN: brakes removed / brakes still working]

Then the force. ICE is the template: the 2025 budget law gave it about $75 billion over four years, including about $45 billion for detention. National Guard troops were federalised and deployed in Los Angeles, Chicago and Portland. Those deployments ended after the Supreme Court refused the Illinois one, while the D.C. Guard was extended to 2029.

And the part almost nobody talks about: the detention business. About nine in ten people in ICE detention are held in privately run facilities, and that was true under Biden too. Two companies dominate. Both gave $500,000 to the inauguration. One runs the ankle monitors and the face-check phone app for about 184,000 people. Detainees in the "voluntary work program" get $1 a day, a rate Congress set in 1950, and a Washington jury ordered one company to pay about $23 million in back wages.
[ON SCREEN: the jailer also runs the tracker]

So could critics be swept into this system? Legally, it would take a criminal charge, a "terrorism" label, or a new law. The book shows the tools that already exist for each, and what still stops them: courts, governors, juries, and the agents themselves.

Verdict: the force exists and the incentives exist. Turning it on citizens for their speech is not documented. Book Six shows the exact switches.

### Script 7 — Book VII: The Test (rights, consequences, who picks the CEO)
[HOOK: "In a CEO-run country, how would you choose the CEO? You wouldn't."]
In a country run like a company, how do you choose the CEO? You don't.

In Yarvin's design the owners pick the board and the board picks the CEO. Citizens are customers, and customers don't vote. Who the owners are, and who fires a bad CEO, is the question his own texts never close.
[ON SCREEN: voter → customer]

So Book Seven runs the test. What would you lose, compared with a democratic republic? Your vote. Your ability to remove leaders. Independent courts. A free press. Protest. Due process. Privacy. And here's the twist: even "exit", their promise, fails without a passport, money or a job.

Then the consequences, as a ladder: watched, denied, fined, prosecuted, removed, confined, forced labour. The scary part isn't that this is new. Pieces of it already exist in US law, each for something else. The government can deny your passport over tax or child-support debt. It can seize your tax refund. About eighteen states, as of 2018, could suspend a professional licence over student-loan default. There's a no-fly list. Benefits carry work requirements. Banks can close your account. Sanctions can cut a person off from every bank and card, and in 2025 that was used on a UN investigator critical of a foreign government's war.
[ON SCREEN: each piece exists — for something else]

The worst case is simply wiring them together, with one trigger: "critic". China already runs a version: a court blacklist that bars people with unpaid judgments from flights and fast trains. Canada froze protesters' bank accounts in 2022, and its own courts later called that unreasonable.

Book Seven also covers what doesn't fit: where the record runs against the fear, and where the theorists disagree with each other. Then the final scorecard and a watch list of the triggers that would move each grade from "probable" to "documented".

Because the point isn't to panic. It's to know exactly where the line is, and to notice the day someone crosses it.
