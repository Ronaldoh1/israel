The League of the Perfectible

Adam Weishaupt was professor of natural and canon law at the University of Ingolstadt. On the night of 1 May 1776 he and four students founded a society first called the League of the Perfectible, renamed the Order of the Illuminati in April 1778. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] Its stated aims were to oppose superstition, to reduce the influence of religion over public life and to check abuses of power by rulers; Weishaupt wrote of wanting freedom from "religious prejudices". He had become strongly anticlerical after years of conflict with the Jesuits at his university. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt]

The order borrowed its structure from the Masonic lodges. Members passed through grades, later a thirteen-degree system designed by Baron Adolph von Knigge, who joined in 1780 and recruited heavily inside existing lodges. Members took classical code names: Weishaupt was "Spartacus", Knigge "Philo". Goethe was among those who joined. [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] Estimates of its size vary: about 650 members can be verified by the end of 1784, its leaders claimed 2,500, and historians have estimated about 1,300. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] What it taught was not a secret gnosis but the reading programme of the German Enlightenment, passed down a hierarchy that asked members to report on their moral progress to their superiors.

The suppression was the work of the Elector of Bavaria, Charles Theodore, not of Rome. An edict of June 1784 banned all societies formed without government authorisation; one of March 1785 banned the Illuminati by name; a third, in August 1787, made recruiting punishable by death. [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] The authorities seized the order's papers in 1786 and 1787 and published them, which is why so much of its internal correspondence is known. Weishaupt lost his chair and lived the rest of his life in exile in Gotha. Present-day groups that claim descent from the order have no documented continuity. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati]

The order would be a footnote but for the French Revolution. In 1797 and 1798 two writers, working separately, argued that the Revolution had been planned. The French Jesuit Augustin Barruel traced it to a decades-long conspiracy of the philosophers, the Freemasons and the Illuminati; the Scottish natural philosopher John Robison published a book with a similar thesis. Both sold widely. Contemporaries rejected the conspiracy claims, and historians reject them still, while treating Barruel as the father of the modern conspiracy theory. [cite:vt30_wiki_barruel_memoirs] The order had been dismantled by its own government before the Revolution began. The full account of both the order and the myth is in [scene:vt30_illuminati|The Vatican Architecture: the Bavarian Illuminati].
