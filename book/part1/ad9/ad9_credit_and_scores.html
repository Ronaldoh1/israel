Credit, Interest and the Score That Follows You

The promise as taught treats debt as a ladder. A mortgage buys the house, a car loan gets the worker to the job, a student loan buys the credential, and a credit card smooths the months when pay and bills fall out of step. Pay on time, the lesson goes, and a good credit record opens the next door at a lower price. Much of that is accurate, and this section says where. It also follows three things the lesson leaves out: how large household debt has become and what it is made of, how the price of revolving credit was freed from state limits by two Supreme Court decisions, and how a three-digit number built by private companies came to decide who borrows and at what cost.

What American households owe

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York counts household debt from a large sample of anonymised credit records. At the end of June 2026 American households owed $18.771 trillion. Mortgages made up $13.117 trillion of it, auto loans $1.713 trillion, student loans $1.651 trillion, credit cards $1.263 trillion, home-equity lines $0.459 trillion and other debts $0.568 trillion. Of the total, 4.7 per cent was in some stage of delinquency, meaning at least 30 days late. [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] The figures are nominal, that is, in the dollars of each year without adjustment for prices; Part Two explains the difference between nominal and real amounts. [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two]

The total has grown in most years since the bank began the series. It peaked at $12.68 trillion in the third quarter of 2008, fell to $11.28 trillion by the third quarter of 2013 as families paid down and defaulted on mortgages, passed the old peak again in 2017, and reached $14.15 trillion at the end of 2019. [cite:ad9_nyfed_q3_2013] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2019] It rose to $16.90 trillion at the end of 2022 and $18.776 trillion at the end of 2025, then edged down by $13 billion in the second quarter of 2026. [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2022] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2025] [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] Credit-card balances followed their own path: $672 billion in the third quarter of 2013, $0.93 trillion at the end of 2019, a fall to $856 billion at the end of 2021 as pandemic aid let families pay cards down, and then a climb to $986 billion in 2022, about $1.13 trillion in 2023, $1.277 trillion at the end of 2025 and $1.263 trillion in June 2026. [cite:ad9_nyfed_q3_2013] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2019] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2022] [cite:ad9_aba_q4_2023] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2025] [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026]

A rising total is not by itself a sign of strain, because incomes and the population also grow. The Federal Reserve's debt service ratio measures the burden directly: required payments of principal and interest on mortgages and consumer loans as a share of income after taxes. It stood at 11.11 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, well below its peak of 15.85 per cent at the end of 2007 and above its low of 9.05 per cent in early 2021. [cite:ad9_fred_tdsp] The burden is not evenly spread, however. The share of card balances moving into serious delinquency, 90 days or more late, was 6.97 per cent a year in the second quarter of 2026, against 5.32 per cent at the end of 2019. [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] [cite:ad9_nyfed_q4_2019]

How the price of a card was set free

For most of the twentieth century each state set a usury ceiling, a legal maximum interest rate on consumer loans. In 1978 a Minnesota bank sued to stop the First National Bank of Omaha from charging Minnesota cardholders the rate Nebraska allowed, 18 per cent a year on the first $999.99 of a balance, when Minnesota's own cap was 12 per cent. On 18 December 1978 a unanimous Supreme Court, in an opinion by Justice William Brennan, held in Marquette National Bank v. First of Omaha that the National Bank Act of 1864 let a national bank charge the rate permitted in the state where it was located, wherever the borrower lived. The Court added that any concern for protecting state usury laws was "better addressed to the wisdom of Congress than to the judgment of this Court." [cite:ad9_marquette]

The decision made a bank's home state worth choosing. South Dakota, already lifting its own ceilings, passed a law in a single day with an emergency clause declaring a shortage of financial capital; its governor, Bill Janklow, later said Citibank helped draft it and moved its card operation to Sioux Falls in 1981, promising 400 jobs, and that the operation grew to about 3,000 employees. Asked in 2004 whether he had helped foster the consumer-credit boom that followed, he answered, "The answer is yes, I do." [cite:ad9_janklow] In 1996 the Court extended the logic from interest to fees. In Smiley v. Citibank, decided on 3 June 1996, it accepted a regulation that the Comptroller of the Currency had adopted that February defining "interest" to include late fees, insufficient-funds fees, over-limit fees, annual fees and cash-advance fees, so that these too could be charged under the home state's law. [cite:ad9_smiley]

The fight over this design is still live. Colorado used a 1980 federal opt-out in 2023 to require out-of-state, state-chartered banks to obey its own rate limits when lending to Colorado residents. Bank trade associations sued; a district court blocked the law, a divided appeals panel upheld it on 10 November 2025, and on 2 April 2026 the full Tenth Circuit agreed to rehear the case, vacating the panel decision. The court's order left the injunction against the Colorado law in place while the rehearing was briefed, and no ruling from the full court had been reported by October 2026. [cite:ad9_cfm_colorado] The exportation rule is designed friction in the protocol's sense: it is the National Bank Act as the Supreme Court read it, open to change by Congress at any time.

The rate, the margin and the minimum payment

An annual percentage rate, or APR, is the yearly cost of borrowing expressed as a percentage of the amount owed, including interest and certain fees. The Federal Reserve's survey of commercial banks puts the average card APR across all accounts at 11.85 per cent in November 2013 and 14.87 per cent in November 2019. It jumped to 19.07 per cent in November 2022 and has stayed near 21 per cent since: 21.47 per cent in November 2024 and 21.19 per cent in August 2026. On the accounts that actually paid interest, the rate was 22.36 per cent in August 2026. [cite:ad9_fred_ccall] [cite:ad9_fred_ccint] Part of the rise followed the Federal Reserve's own rate increases. Part did not: the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) reported in February 2024 that the average margin of card APRs over the prime rate had reached an all-time high of about 14 percentage points in 2023 and attributed it to issuers widening margins. [cite:ad9_orrick_margins] The Consumer Bankers Association answered that the bureau's comparisons mixed in credit unions, which are capped at 18 per cent by law, and that large issuers lend more to riskier customers and fund rewards programmes. [cite:ad9_cba_math] Card holders paid $160 billion in interest and $31.3 billion in fees in 2024 on the bureau's figures. [cite:us22_cfpb_ccmr25]

The minimum payment is where the rate compounds. Take a $5,000 balance at 22.36 per cent and assume a minimum payment equal to the month's interest plus 1 per cent of the balance, with a $25 floor, a common form of the formula. The monthly rate is 22.36 ÷ 12 = 1.86 per cent, so the first month's interest is about $93 and the first minimum about $143. Paying only the minimum and charging nothing new, the balance takes 231 months, more than 19 years, to clear, and the borrower pays about $13,240 in all, of which about $8,240 is interest. Paying a fixed $192 a month clears it in 36 months for about $1,910 in interest. At the 2013 rate of about 12.9 per cent, the same minimum-payment path costs about $4,570 in interest. (Book arithmetic, monthly compounding, no new purchases or fees.) Since 22 February 2010 the CARD Act has required every statement to show how long paying only the minimum would take, what it would cost, and the fixed payment that would clear the balance in three years. [cite:ad9_crl_card] [cite:ad9_fred_ccint]

The score

Fair, Isaac and Company, which built its first scoring system in 1958, released the first general-purpose credit score in 1989, a single number meant to work across all kinds of lending, and all three national credit bureaus were selling FICO scores by 1991. [cite:ad9_kgw_fico] FICO says its score weighs payment history at 35 per cent, amounts owed at 30 per cent, length of credit history at 15 per cent, new credit at 10 per cent and the mix of credit types at 10 per cent, and does not consider race, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, income or employment. [cite:ad9_myfico] Because it measures borrowing behaviour rather than ability to pay, a person who has never borrowed has no score at all, and a person who earns well but missed payments during an illness scores low.

The underlying files contain errors. In a study for Congress published in December 2012, the Federal Trade Commission had 1,001 consumers review nearly 3,000 of their own credit reports. Twenty-six per cent found at least one potentially material error; 13 per cent had an error that changed their score; and 5 per cent had errors that lowered their score enough to make credit likely to cost more. [cite:ad9_ftc_2013] A widely repeated claim holds that one American in four has an error that hurts their score; the study's figure for errors serious enough to raise borrowing costs is one in twenty.

Some people have no file at all. In 2015 the CFPB estimated that 11 per cent of adults, about 26 million people, were "credit invisible". In June 2025 it corrected that estimate after finding that its data had excluded records containing only collections, closed accounts or deferred student loans: the 2010 figure fell to 5.8 per cent, about 13.5 million, and the 2020 figure was 2.7 per cent, about 7 million adults. Another 9.8 per cent of adults in 2020 had a file too thin or stale to score. [cite:ad9_cfpb_invis2025] Mortgage scoring is changing as well. After years of review, the Federal Housing Finance Agency announced in April 2026 that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac would accept VantageScore 4.0, a rival model built by the three bureaus, and on 9 September 2026 Fannie Mae opened it to all its approved lenders, alongside the classic FICO score. [cite:ad9_bhfs_fhfa] [cite:ad9_fnma_ll2026] A CFPB rule to remove medical debt from credit reports was vacated by a federal court in Texas in July 2025, leaving in place the bureaus' voluntary 2023 decision to drop paid medical debts and those under $500. [cite:ad9_checkbook_med] Part Eight covers medical debt in full. [scene:ad8_medical_debt|Part Eight]

Where it works as promised

Scoring did what its designers said it would in several measurable ways. The Federal Reserve's 2007 report to Congress found that automated scoring cut mortgage decision times "from several weeks to a matter of a few minutes", lowered some lenders' origination costs by as much as half, and brought "objectivity and consistency" that could reduce room for discrimination by individual loan officers. The same report found "very little evidence" that the spread of scoring had especially helped groups that historically borrowed little, and called that conclusion tentative. [cite:ad9_fed_scoring2007] The burden of debt payments, measured against income, is lower now than before the 2008 crisis. [cite:ad9_fred_tdsp] And cardholders who pay in full each month borrow free for weeks and often collect rewards; the interest in the money trail above is paid by those who carry a balance.
