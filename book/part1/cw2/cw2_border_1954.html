The Year After Qibya: Scorpion Pass, Nahhalin, Byroade and the Bat Galim, 1954

The censure of Israel over Qibya in November 1953, told in [scene:cw2_unit101_qibya|Unit 101 and Qibya], did not end the killing across the armistice lines, and it did not settle how the lines would be policed. The months from March 1954 to January 1955 tested the armistice system on three fronts at once. On the Jordanian line, a massacre of Israeli bus passengers and an attack on a Jordanian village eleven days apart led Israel to walk out of the joint commission meant to investigate both. In Washington, a senior State Department official told Israelis in public what he thought of their state's policies and premises, and drew a formal protest. At Suez, Egypt seized an Israeli ship and held its crew for three months. In each case the record preserves both sides' accounts, and in two of the three the United Nations' own officers declined to say who was responsible.

The first event was the worst. On 17 March 1954 an Israeli bus on the road from Eilat to Beersheba was attacked at the Scorpion Pass, Ma'ale Akrabim. Israel's letter to the Security Council described "the murder of eleven Israeli citizens". [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3196] The United Nations observer who reached the scene counted four bodies outside the bus and seven inside. Israel's draft resolution named the dead as nine men and two women among the passengers, and two more passengers, a young woman and a nine-year-old boy, were seriously wounded. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3196] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] The UN count and Israel's count agree: eleven dead.

What divided the parties was responsibility. Israel brought a draft resolution to the Jordan–Israel Mixed Armistice Commission condemning Jordan for the attack. The vote divided along national lines: two for, Israel's delegates; two against, Jordan's; and the chairman abstaining. The draft failed. The chairman explained that "the evidence brought out is far from being conclusive", and said that people from outside Jordan could also have been responsible. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] Israel's case was that infiltrators from Jordan had killed eleven civilians on an Israeli road, and that the body charged with keeping the armistice would not even name the state from whose territory, in Israel's view, they had come. The chairman's case was that the evidence did not show who the killers were or where they had come from. Both positions are in the UN's own report, and the record does not resolve between them.

Israel's response was to leave. After the vote its delegation stated that it was "not in a position, under the present circumstances, to continue its participation". From 23 March 1954 Israel cut all connection with the Commission and stopped attending the local commanders' meetings that handled day-to-day incidents along the line. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] The walk-out removed one party from the only standing body in which both sides' complaints were heard together, five days before the next attack.

On the night of 28–29 March 1954 Nahhalin, a Jordanian village, was attacked. Jordan's complaint gave nine killed, eight men and one woman, and fourteen injured. The Commission's resolution gave a slightly different breakdown: five National Guards and one woman killed in the village, and three Arab Legion soldiers killed when their truck hit a mine on the way to it, with fourteen villagers and five soldiers wounded. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3251] The two counts reach the same total of nine dead by different routes: Jordan counted eight men and a woman, the Commission six in the village and three soldiers on the road. UN observers found footprints leading from the village to the demarcation line, with similar tracks on the Israeli side, and unexploded devices, some marked with Hebrew letters. Seven house doors and the doors of the mosque had been blown open. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3251]

The Commission met in emergency session on 30 March 1954. Israel, which had walked out a week earlier, did not attend. Jordan's resolution was adopted with Jordan and the chairman voting in favour; it condemned Israel "in the strongest terms" and called on it to punish those responsible. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3251] The contrast with the Scorpion Pass vote is the mechanism at its plainest. With the parties' delegations voting against each other, the chairman's vote decided the outcome. At Scorpion Pass he found the evidence inconclusive and abstained, and no one was condemned. At Nahhalin he found physical evidence leading to the Israeli side and voted with Jordan. Israel's absence meant that its own account of Nahhalin was not heard in that forum, and the sources fetched here do not record what that account was.

Israel took its case to a forum it was still attending. On 5 April 1954 it asked the Security Council to take up its complaints against Jordan, including Jordan's refusal to attend a conference under Article XII of the Armistice Agreement. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3196] The UNTSO chief of staff's reports on both incidents followed in June 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3251]

While the Council took up the Jordanian file, the argument moved to American platforms. Assistant Secretary of State Henry Byroade spoke to the Dayton World Affairs Council in April 1954. The date is recorded two ways: the State Department's own memorandum, in Eban's account, places it on 9 April; Rafael Medoff, writing in 2014, places it on 19 April. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_byroade] [cite:rs_cw2_medoff_byroade] On 1 May 1954 he spoke again, in Philadelphia, to the American Council for Judaism. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_byroade] [cite:rs_cw2_medoff_byroade] As Medoff quotes him, Byroade urged Israelis to "look upon yourselves as a Middle Eastern state, rather than as a headquarters… of a world-wide grouping of people of a particular religious faith", to "drop the attitude of the conqueror", and to halt "retaliatory killings". In Philadelphia he named Israel's calls for "greatly expanded immigration" as feeding Arab fears of expansion. [cite:rs_cw2_medoff_byroade] Byroade's reasoning, as he gave it, was regional: Israel's integration into its neighbourhood, an end to reprisals, and the removal of what he saw as a source of Arab fear.

The Israeli reply came at the top. In May 1954 Abba Eban told Dulles, in a meeting recorded by Parker T. Hart with Shiloah also present, that the Dayton reference to Israel's ties with Jews elsewhere "wounded the feelings of millions outside Israel and hundreds of thousands within that country", and that raising immigration had been "entirely unnecessary". The Department had issued a corrective, Press Release 231 of 5 May, but Eban said it had not undone the impression. He also acknowledged that Israeli papers had run headlines such as "U.S. Threatens Israel", which he called distortions. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_byroade] Israel's government protested formally against "unjustified interference in matters which are purely Israel's own concern". The Zionist leader Emanuel Neumann denounced what he called "Byroadeism". According to Medoff, the administration stood by Byroade. [cite:rs_cw2_medoff_byroade]

The exchange shows two governments arguing over where the line between foreign policy and a state's self-definition lay. For Byroade, immigration and Israel's relation to Jews abroad were matters of regional security, because they shaped Arab perceptions. For Eban and the Israeli government, they were Israel's own concern and, in Eban's words, touched the feelings of millions outside the country. The quarrel was between named officials stating their reasons, and the record keeps it there. The Anglo-American search for a settlement that followed is told in [scene:cw2_alpha|Operation Alpha].

The third front opened at Suez in the autumn. On 28 September 1954 Egyptian authorities seized the Israeli-flagged Bat Galim at the southern end of the Suez Canal; the ship was bound for Haifa. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_batgalim] [cite:rs_cw2_jta_batgalim] Egypt complained to the armistice machinery on 6 October that the crew had attacked two Egyptian fishing boats with automatic fire in the Gulf of Suez, killing two fishermen. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3323] On 19 November 1954 the Egyptian–Israeli Mixed Armistice Commission rejected Egypt's draft and adopted Israel's, which found the complaint "unfounded"; the chairman said "conclusive evidence has not been produced" of an attack. Six days later, on 25 November, the Commission's Special Committee ruled that the word "unfounded" should not be used, and that the decision was "not intended as a judgment on the facts". [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3323] The UN thus declined to find that the fishermen's deaths had happened as Egypt alleged, and also declined to find that they had not.

The crew paid for the deadlock. The ten crewmen, the captain and nine sailors, were held for 94 days and released on 1 January 1955, sent across the Gaza Strip on foot. The captain said they had been beaten. Egypt offered to release the ship only if it sailed south, away from the Canal. [cite:rs_cw2_jta_batgalim] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_batgalim] That condition was the substance of the dispute: the seizure was a test of whether an Israeli vessel could use the Canal at all, a question the Security Council had addressed in its resolution of 1 September 1951, told in [scene:cw1_suez_blockade|the 1951 resolution]. When the Council discussed the case in January 1955, the United States, Britain, France and Brazil said Egypt's action violated that resolution, and on 13 January the Council's President stated that members agreed it had "continuing validity and effect". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_batgalim] No new resolution was adopted in the sources fetched; the Council reaffirmed the old one and the ship stayed where it was.

What did the year establish? On the Jordanian line, the armistice commission proved able to condemn only when its chairman was persuaded, and Israel's withdrawal from it after Scorpion Pass meant that the next incident was judged without one party in the room. In Washington, a public line critical of retaliation and of Israel's self-description was stated by a named official and defended by his administration against a formal Israeli protest. At Suez, the Council restated the principle of passage and left the ship and the Canal as they were.

The record leaves the central questions of responsibility open, as the UN's own officers did. Who killed the passengers at the Scorpion Pass is not established; the chairman's words, "far from being conclusive", remain the last official word in these sources, and Israel's position that the killers came from Jordan remains its position. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] Who carried out the Nahhalin attack is not stated in Israel's own voice anywhere in the material fetched; the Commission's finding rests on physical evidence, and Israel was not present to answer it. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3251] Whether the Bat Galim's crew fired on Egyptian fishermen is likewise unresolved: Egypt charged that they did, the Commission found no conclusive evidence, and then withdrew the word that would have cleared them. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3323] And the exact date of Byroade's Dayton speech differs between the State Department record and Medoff, by ten days.
