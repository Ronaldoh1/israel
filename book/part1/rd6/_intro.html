The declaration of May 1948 ended one story and began another. In the years that followed, the new state consolidated what the war had produced. More slowly, it was made to account for it. This stage follows both tasks. It traces the state's relationship with Washington through an election year, a loan, a formal recognition and a threatened change of policy. It traces the laws that turned wartime flight into permanent title. It goes to the conference at Lausanne, where return and borders were negotiated and left unresolved, and to the single paragraph of a United Nations resolution on which every later negotiation would turn.

It also steps back. The same week in December 1948 that produced the modern human-rights order also produced the refugee resolution. This stage asks what that order could do for the people already displaced, and what it could not. It traces how the flight of 1948 was explained to Western readers, and how long the archives took to correct that account. Finally, it closes the books on the years before the state: who paid, through which channels, who was tied to whom by what documented link, and what the record can and cannot show about money and politicians.

One question runs through every section. Once the state existed, what did its founding actually rest on, and what did it leave unsettled?
