Wannsee: Ninety Minutes, Fifteen Officials

The Wannsee Conference is often remembered as the meeting at which Hitler decided to murder the Jews of Europe. The surviving documents show something more precise, and in its way more disturbing. The decision had already been taken, months earlier, at the highest level of the regime. Wannsee was the meeting at which the machinery of the German state was brought together to carry it out.

The written trail begins on 31 July 1941, shortly after the German invasion of the Soviet Union. On that date Reichsmarschall Hermann Göring ordered Reinhard Heydrich, head of the RSHA, the Reich Security Main Office, "to make all the necessary preparations... for the Final Solution of the Jewish problem in the German sphere of influence in Europe" [cite:yadvashem_wannsee_final_solution]. The order is dated and documented, and it predates Wannsee by nearly six months. It is the point at which the policy decision appears in the written record, given by one man directly to another. Nothing about this chronology lessens Wannsee's importance. It changes what that importance was.

On 20 January 1942 fifteen high-ranking officials of the Nazi Party and the German government met at a villa in the Berlin suburb of Wannsee. The meeting lasted about ninety minutes, and Heydrich chaired it. He opened by announcing his appointment as Plenipotentiary for the Preparation of the Final Solution, and he indicated that approximately 11 million Jews across Europe would fall under its provisions, including Jewish populations in countries not yet under Axis control [cite:ushmm_wannsee_conference] [cite:ww2museum_wannsee]. A gathering of roughly an hour and a half set in motion the coordination of what would become the murder of millions. The brevity is itself part of the record.

What is known of the discussion comes from the Wannsee Protocol, compiled and heavily edited by Adolf Eichmann from notes taken during the meeting. The attendees represented the SS, the Nazi Party and several Reich government ministries. The Protocol records no voiced opposition to the plan from any of them. The men in the room did not deliberate over whether the policy should go forward; they discussed how to implement a decision that had already been made [cite:ushmm_wannsee_protocol] [cite:ushmm_wannsee_final_solution].

The limits of that evidence should be stated plainly. Because Eichmann edited the Protocol afterward, it documents the meeting's substance and outcome, not a verbatim transcript of every exchange. It shows that no objection was recorded. It cannot prove that no attendee held a private reservation that went unwritten. What it does establish is that fifteen officials from across the German government heard a plan covering some eleven million people, and that the written record of the meeting contains not one documented objection.

The Protocol had a second life. It was reproduced and circulated to leading Nazi Party and government officials at the time. After the war it became key documentary evidence in the Subsequent Nuremberg Proceedings, and it was cited again at Adolf Eichmann's trial in Jerusalem in 1961 [cite:ushmm_wannsee_protocol]. The record created to coordinate the genocide later helped to prosecute the people who wrote and received it.

Put precisely, Wannsee's significance lies in coordination rather than origination. Göring's order to Heydrich had already set the policy. What Wannsee did was formally bring the civil government, through named ministries and named officials, into direct and coordinated participation in carrying it out, and no one present is documented as having objected. The distinction matters for understanding how the genocide was actually organized. It was not the product of a single conversation, but of a state apparatus whose ministries took part in carrying it out. The scale of what that coordination produced is the subject of the section that follows.
