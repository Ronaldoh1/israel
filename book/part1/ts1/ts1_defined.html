What Christian Zionism Is, and What This Volume Means by a Theology Shield

Christian Zionism is support for the Jewish return to the land of Israel, and since 1948 for the State of Israel, on Christian theological grounds. Definitions differ on one point, which turns out to matter. Encyclopaedia Britannica defines it as "a religious and political Christian movement that supports the return of the Jewish Diaspora to a homeland in Palestine as a prerequisite for the end-time." [cite:ts1_brit_cz] That definition ties the movement to prophecy about the last days. The historian Samuel Goldman, in God's Country: Christian Zionism in America (2018), draws it more widely. His publisher describes the book as telling the story "from the Puritans to 9/11," and it names three sources of American Christian support for a Jewish state: covenant, prophecy and cultural affinity. [cite:ts1_penn_goldman] On the wider account, prophecy about the end of the world is one motive among three, not the definition of the thing.

This volume uses the wider definition and keeps the narrower one in view. Christian Zionism, as used here, means the belief, held by some Protestants, that God's promises to Abraham still apply to the Jewish people and to the land, that the modern state stands in some relation to those promises, and that Christians therefore have a duty to support it. The belief is older than the system of prophecy most often linked to it. It grew out of English Puritan "restorationism" in the seventeenth century and later out of John Nelson Darby's dispensationalism, and not every Christian Zionist is a dispensationalist. [cite:fw_wiki_christianzionism] Part Two follows the older line from the Puritans to Lord Shaftesbury, and Part Three follows Darby's. [scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two: before Darby] [scene:ts3_darby|Part Three: Darby]

What it is not

Christian Zionism is not a church. It has no creed of its own, no clergy and no membership roll. It lives inside denominations, seminaries, broadcasters and organisations that hold it in different forms and to different degrees. Its largest organised vehicle in the United States is Christians United for Israel (CUFI), founded by the San Antonio pastor John Hagee in 2006, which says it had more than seven million members by 2019 and more than ten million by 2023; those figures are CUFI's own. [cite:ts1_jvl_cufi] Hagee has stated the organisation's ground in one sentence: "We believe in the promise of Genesis 12:3 regarding the Jewish people and the nation of Israel," which he calls "an eternal covenant between God and the seed of Abraham." He has also said the support is not aimed at converting Jews and is owed as "a debt of gratitude to the Jewish people." [cite:ts1_jvl_cufi] Part Eight takes CUFI and its predecessors in turn. [scene:ts8_cufi|Part Eight: CUFI]

Nor is Christian Zionism the same thing as dispensationalism, though the two overlap heavily. Dispensationalism is a system for reading the whole Bible. The Reformed theologian Keith Mathison, a critic of the system, puts its centre plainly: "at its heart is the distinction between Israel and the church." It began with Darby in the nineteenth century and spread through the American Bible conferences and the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909. [cite:ts1_lig_dispens] A dispensationalist will usually be a Christian Zionist; a Christian Zionist need not be a dispensationalist.

It is not the position of American evangelicals as a whole, still less of American Christians. Belief that the land was a divine gift is widespread among white evangelical Protestants: in a Pew Research Center survey published in October 2013, 82 per cent of them said God gave the land of Israel to the Jewish people, against 40 per cent of American Jews. [cite:ts1_pew_2013] In a LifeWay Research poll of 2,002 Americans with evangelical beliefs in September 2017, 80 per cent said Israel's founding in 1948 fulfilled biblical prophecy, yet only 23 per cent said Israel should sign a treaty giving the Palestinians a state, and 46 per cent were unsure. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] A poll share describes the answers a sample gave to the questions it was asked. It does not describe every believer, and it does not show which policies respondents want. Part Eleven sets out the large body of evangelical, mainline, Catholic, Orthodox and Palestinian Christian opinion that rejects the belief. [scene:ts11_churches|Part Eleven: the churches]

It is not a Catholic doctrine. The Holy See's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews asked Catholics in 1985 to understand the Jewish "religious attachment which finds its roots in biblical tradition" without adopting "any particular religious interpretation of this relationship," and said that "the existence of the State of Israel and its political options should be envisaged not in a perspective which is in itself religious," but by reference to "the common principles of international law." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] Rome's earlier record, from Pius X's refusal to Herzl in 1904 to its rejection of millenarian teaching, is in the opening volume. [scene:gx_s05|One verse, two readings]

Finally, it is not Zionism among Jews, which has its own history, its own internal arguments and, in the religious Zionist current, its own reading of the land promise; that reading is treated in The Greater Israel Architecture. [scene:gi11_religious|The religious engine] Christian Zionism is a non-Jewish support for Jewish return, argued on Christian grounds. [cite:fw_wiki_christianzionism]

The theology shield: two earlier meanings

The title of this volume borrows a phrase that earlier books in Ron's Israel Architecture series used in two different senses, and neither of them was simply another name for Christian Zionism. In Book Three, the theology shield is a substitute argument. Where the historical case for the founding of 1948 is contested, that book argues, a biblical claim, "of divine promise, of an eternal covenant", is offered instead and "deployed as an argument that closes discussion"; it calls this "the second-line argument." In Book Four, the theology shield is a recasting: "the treatment of political criticism of the Israeli state as if it were religious hostility to Judaism." The first sense is about what is said in defence of a state's claim. The second is about what is done to its critics.

Book Four's main example shows why the second sense needs care. At a hearing of the White House Religious Liberty Commission on antisemitism on 9 February 2026, the commissioner Carrie Prejean Boller told Rabbi Ari Berman of Yeshiva University: "I'm a Catholic and Catholics do not embrace Zionism. Just so you know." The commission's chair, the Texas lieutenant governor Dan Patrick, removed her two days later, writing that "No member of the Commission has the right to hijack a hearing for their own personal and political agenda on any issue." She refused to resign and wrote that she "will never bend the knee to the state of Israel." [cite:ts1_hill_boller] Jewish Insider reported that at the same hearing she pressed the Jewish witnesses on whether they would consider her antisemitic for believing that Jews killed Jesus, and that she had defended the commentator Candace Owens. [cite:ts1_ji_boller] Book Four reads the removal as the shield at work against a religious position. Patrick's stated reason was her conduct of the hearing, and her own remarks included the ancient deicide charge. Both halves belong to the record, and Part Ten takes the case up in full. [scene:ts10_mechanism|Part Ten: how the shield works]

The definition used in this volume

The two senses describe one move seen from two sides, and this volume joins them. Here, the theology shield means the use of a scriptural claim, above all Genesis 12:3 read as applying to the modern state, to move a question about that state's policies off ground where it can be argued on evidence (history, law, the record of conduct) and onto ground where disagreement becomes a matter of faith. It works in two ways. It can substitute a promise for an argument, which is Book Three's sense. It can recast an argument as an offence against God or against the Jewish people, which is Book Four's sense.

Christian Zionism is the largest organised constituency that carries this move in American politics, but the two are not the same. Many Christian Zionists argue their case on strategic or moral grounds as well, and the shield is also used by people who are not Christian Zionists at all: religious Zionist Jewish arguments from the land promise belong to Book Three's sense, and the charge that criticism of a state is hostility to a faith can be made by anyone. [scene:gi11_religious|The religious engine] The term is this volume's analytical frame, not a finding. Book Three goes further and argues that the whole pro-Israel apparatus exists because without it the consensus would not hold. That is that book's argument, and this volume does not adopt it. Whether the shield has worked, where and on whom, and where it has failed are questions Parts Nine to Twelve test against the record. [scene:ts10_failures|Part Ten: where it fails]

Two cautions follow from the definition. First, it describes an argument, not a people. Statements are attributed in this volume to named organisations and named individuals, with their own stated positions beside any criticism, and survey results are given with their dates and populations. Second, it does not decide the theology. Whether God's promise to Abraham applies to a state founded in 1948 is a question about how scripture should be read, and the next section sets out the two main answers and who holds each.
