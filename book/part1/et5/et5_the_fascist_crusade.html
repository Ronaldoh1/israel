The Second Attempt: Fascist Italy, the Monks of Debre Libanos and the Obelisk

Three centuries after the Jesuits, an army from Catholic Italy invaded Ethiopia. The invasion of 1935 and 1936 was Mussolini's, not the pope's, and the record of the Vatican's own role is mixed. But the Italian occupation attacked the Ethiopian church directly, and the worst single atrocity of the occupation was committed against a monastery.

The context was the relationship between the Fascist state and the Catholic Church. The Lateran Pacts of 1929 had ended the long breach between the papacy and the Italian state. [cite:et_lrb_holywar] According to Christian Century, the Vatican "had good (if not uncritical) relations with Mussolini's regime", and the Italian church "passionately favored the war effort in Ethiopia"; some Catholics and clergy presented the war as a civilising mission. [cite:et_cc_debrelibanos] [cite:et_lrb_holywar] The pope himself, Pius XI, seems to have been reluctant to lend his support, according to the historian John Foot's review of the most detailed study of the subject, Ian Campbell's Holy War. [cite:et_lrb_holywar] [cite:et_wiki_campbell] The documented position, then, is a national church that backed the war, a Vatican on good terms with the regime, and a pope who held back.

On 19 February 1937 Ethiopian resistance fighters attacked the Italian viceroy, Rodolfo Graziani, in Addis Ababa. [cite:et_wiki_campbell] Italian forces killed at least 19,000 people in the city over the next three days, according to Foot's summary of Campbell's research. [cite:et_lrb_holywar] Graziani then turned on the church. On 19 May he ordered the execution of "all monks without distinction" at Debre Libanos, one of the most important monasteries in Ethiopia. The killings, which Campbell dates between 21 and 29 May, coincided with the monastery's feast day, when pilgrims had gathered; Campbell estimates that between 1,200 and 1,600 pilgrims and clergy were killed. [cite:et_lrb_holywar] [cite:et_wiki_campbell] Churches were burned and looted elsewhere in the country. [cite:et_lrb_holywar]

The occupation also took Ethiopia's heritage. When Graziani returned to Italy in 1938, he took 79 crates of stolen material with him, and a 1939 exhibition at the Museo Coloniale in Rome displayed what appear to have been Ethiopian crowns, almost certainly taken from Debre Libanos. [cite:et_lrb_holywar] The largest object taken was a monument from Aksum itself, the city of Ezana and of the church that claims the Ark: a fourth-century stele 24 metres tall and weighing 160 tonnes. It was cut into three pieces, shipped to Italy and unveiled in Rome on 28 October 1937 to mark the fifteenth anniversary of the March on Rome. [cite:et_wiki_obelisk]

Italy agreed in a United Nations agreement of 1947 to return it. The return took fifty-eight years. The three pieces arrived back in Ethiopia between 19 and 25 April 2005, at a cost to Italy of 7.7 million dollars, and the obelisk was re-erected in Aksum and unveiled on 4 September 2008. [cite:et_wiki_obelisk] Graziani, who ordered the massacre at Debre Libanos, was sentenced after the war to nineteen years for collaborating with the Nazis and served only a few months. [cite:et_lrb_holywar]

The occupation did not try to change the Ethiopian canon any more than the Jesuits had. What it attacked was the institution that kept the canon: its monks, its monasteries and its treasures. The pattern is the one Part Six follows in a British case: the holders of the Ethiopian scriptures were killed or robbed by an outside power, and the objects that survived went to the outsider's capital, to be returned, if at all, decades later.
