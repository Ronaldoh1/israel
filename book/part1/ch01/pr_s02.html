Who Are "the Palestinians"? One People, Several Legal Situations

When a sentence in this book, or in a newspaper, refers to "the Palestinians," it may mean any of several groups whose rights and daily lives differ sharply. A common picture has them all living in one place under one government. The record shows something else: one people spread across several territories and several legal systems, so that which laws apply to a given Palestinian depends on where that person was born and where he or she now lives. The map in the preceding section showed the lines; this section shows who lives on each side of them, and in what legal standing.

Begin with the total. The Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics estimated about 14.8 million Palestinians worldwide in 2024.[cite:alquds_pcbs_refugees] They fall into four legal situations, and beyond these lies a larger population of registered refugees.

The first situation is citizenship of Israel. Palestinian Arab citizens make up about a fifth of Israel's population. They vote, and they sit in the Knesset.[cite:jta_nsl_upheld] The Statistical Yearbook of Jerusalem, published by the Jerusalem Institute for Policy Research, gives the scale precisely. At the end of 2023 Israel's population was 9,699,200, of whom 7,631,800, or 78.7 percent, were counted as "Jews and others" and 2,067,300, or 21.3 percent, as Arabs.[cite:rs_ch01_jiis_pop] In 1967 the population was 2,776,300, of whom 392,700, or 14.1 percent, were Arabs.[cite:rs_ch01_jiis_pop] The table records population groups, not legal status, so it does not by itself say how many of those it counts as Arabs hold citizenship and how many hold residency only.

Citizenship has not meant one uniform standing over time, and two episodes, each told in full elsewhere in this library, show how its terms have been contested. The first came before Israel governed a single Palestinian in the West Bank or Gaza. From 1949 to 1966 most of Israel's Arab citizens lived under a military government: a separate administration that required permits to travel outside their localities, imposed curfews, and designated closed areas. It was a distinct legal regime for one category of citizens, and it was abolished in 1966.[cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] Its worst single event came on 29 October 1956, the eve of the Sinai War. Brigadier Issachar Shadmi moved up a dusk curfew on villages near the Jordanian border without adequate notice. When Major Shmuel Malinki asked how to treat villagers returning from work who did not know of it, Shadmi's answer, according to the court record, was that anyone who left his house would be shot. Two of the three units given the order disregarded it.[cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The Border Police unit at Kafr Qasim, about a kilometer from the Green Line, did not. It killed 49 people returning home after a curfew they had not been told of: 19 men, 6 women and 23 children, a count that includes an unborn child.[cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60]

The accounting afterward was documented in two directions at once. Eight officers and men were convicted in 1958, and all were released within a few years. Shadmi, tried separately, was fined one-hundredth of an Israeli lira, a sum Palestinian citizens remember as "Shadmi's penny." The same trial established a doctrine that the Israeli army still teaches: a soldier must refuse a "manifestly illegal" order, one over which "a black flag flies."[cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The claim that Arab citizens have enjoyed equal civil rights since 1948 does not survive these eighteen years. The record also shows the regime's limits: the military government was abolished, the soldiers were convicted, and the principle of refusing an illegal order came out of the case. One reading of the period, made in [scene:mg_s01|the full account of the military government], holds that it was a template, a separate administrative regime for one population, alongside the absentee laws that kept its people from their property, that preceded the occupation of 1967 by eighteen years and was inherited by it. That is an argument about continuity, and the record of 1949–1966 is its evidence; whether the later regime was modeled on the earlier one is a question of interpretation rather than of documents.

The second episode is constitutional. On 19 July 2018 the Knesset passed Basic Law: Israel, the Nation-State of the Jewish People, by 62 votes to 55, with two abstentions.[cite:asianews_nsl_passed] Its eleven articles state that "the exercise of the right to national self-determination in the State of Israel is unique to the Jewish people." They make Hebrew the state's language and give Arabic "a special status," adding that this does not harm the standing Arabic held before the law. They declare the state open to Jewish immigration, and they state that "the development of Jewish settlement" is "a national value" that the state will encourage. The law contains no equality clause; a draft clause that would have permitted Jewish-only communities was dropped.[cite:jta_nsl_upheld] [cite:asianews_nsl_passed] Because Israel has no written constitution, its Basic Laws carry constitutional weight, which makes this text the closest thing Israel has to a defining clause on nationhood.

On 8 July 2021 the Supreme Court rejected the petitions against the law by 10 to 1. The majority held that it does not negate Israel's democratic character and that individual rights are protected by other laws. The lone dissenter was Justice George Karra, the court's only Arab member.[cite:jta_nsl_upheld] [cite:gktoday_nsl_upheld] The two readings of the law are both on the record. Its defenders say it merely enshrines what Israel already is, codifying the state's Jewish character. Its critics, among them Arab citizens' rights groups and some Jewish organizations, say it relegates Arab citizens, about a fifth of the population, to a lesser standing.[cite:christiancentury_nsl] [cite:jta_nsl_upheld] A familiar claim holds that Israel's founding commitment to equality is written into its constitution. The law that comes closest to a constitutional statement of who the state is for names one people and contains no equality clause; the court that upheld it located the protection of individual rights in other laws. The fuller story is told in [scene:nsl_s01|the account of the Nation-State Law].

The second situation is East Jerusalem. Roughly 360,000 Palestinians live in the part of the city Israel annexed after 1967, most of them holding Israeli residency rather than Israeli citizenship.[cite:minorityrights_palestine] They live under Israeli rule, but most without Israeli citizenship. The same yearbook shows how the city's population has changed since 1967. At the end of that year the city had 266,300 residents: 197,700, or 74.2 percent, counted as Jews and others, and 68,600, or 25.8 percent, as Arabs. At the end of 2022 the figures were 968,700, with 585,700 (60.5 percent) and 383,000 (39.5 percent). At the end of 2023 Jerusalem had 980,700 residents: 589,300, or 60.1 percent, Jews and others, and 391,400, or 39.9 percent, Arabs.[cite:rs_ch01_jiis_pop] Between the end of 1967 and the end of 2023, the Arab share of the city's population rose from about a quarter to about two-fifths.

The third situation is the West Bank, where about 3.4 million Palestinians lived in mid-2025. The Palestinian Authority governs them in Areas A and B, and the territory as a whole is under Israeli military rule. Israeli settlers in the same territory live under Israeli civil law.[cite:minorityrights_palestine] [cite:alquds_pcbs_refugees] Two populations thus live in the same territory under two legal systems.

The fourth situation is Gaza, home to about 2.1 million people in mid-2025. About two-thirds of them are refugees or their descendants.[cite:alquds_pcbs_refugees] [cite:minorityrights_palestine]

Beyond these four lies the largest single category. About 5.9 million Palestinians are registered with UNRWA, the United Nations agency for Palestinian refugees. About 40 percent of them are in Jordan, and the others are in Lebanon, Syria, the West Bank and Gaza.[cite:alquds_pcbs_refugees] [cite:minorityrights_palestine] About 6.6 million Palestinians live in Arab countries overall. Registration as a refugee is a status in its own right, and it overlaps with the others: a registered refugee may live in Gaza or the West Bank, under one of the regimes above, or in Jordan, Lebanon or Syria.

These differences are not only descriptive. Say that Palestinians vote and sit in the Knesset, and the record shows it of citizens of Israel, not of residents of East Jerusalem, who mostly hold residency rather than citizenship. Say that they live under Israeli military rule, and the record shows it of the West Bank, not of Israel's Arab citizens since 1966. A refugee in Jordan, a resident of Gaza, a villager in the West Bank, a resident of East Jerusalem and an Arab citizen of Israel share a national identity; they do not share a legal status. That is why so much of the legal argument over Israel's conduct, including the argument over whether its policies amount to apartheid, turns on these distinctions. What courts, United Nations bodies, human rights organizations and governments have actually found on that question, and Israel's rejection of those findings, is the subject of [scene:pr_s03|the section on the contested legal words]. This section leaves the finding there.

Some of what a reader would want to know next is not in these figures. The population tables count groups, not citizenship, so they do not say exactly how many Arabs in Israel are citizens and how many are residents. Whether a Palestinian refugee, or the Palestinian spouse of an Arab citizen, can become a citizen of Israel is a question of Israel's citizenship laws, taken up in the next section. And the numbers themselves are estimates from different bodies at different dates, from 2023 to mid-2025; they describe the shape of the population, and they should not be added together as though they were a single count.
