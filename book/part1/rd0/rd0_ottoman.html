Palestine in the Ottoman Order, 1799–1840

Two opposite misconceptions are often told about Palestine before the twentieth century. One holds that the Middle East has always been a set of hostile nations locked in permanent war. The other comes in two forms that contradict each other: that the Ottomans "occupied" Palestine the way later settlers did, or that Palestine was an empty Ottoman backwater. The record supports none of these. For four centuries the land that this book follows was neither a nation among nations nor an empty space. It was a group of districts inside one large, multi-religious empire.

From 1516 to 1917 the Ottoman Empire governed most of the Arab Middle East as provinces of a single state, without international borders between them. Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem and Beirut were provincial cities of the same state, and people and goods moved among them without passports. The states that define the region's later conflicts, Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, Jordan and Israel, did not exist. Iran stood apart: it was never Ottoman, and it had its own dynasty, its own language and a Shia majority. Almost every border that matters in today's conflicts was drawn later, in a six-year window between 1916 and 1922, by two European powers, Britain and France.

Inside the empire, religion rather than territory organized much of law. Under the millet system, recognized religious communities (Muslims, Christians of several churches, and Jews) administered their own religious law, courts and schooling, and governed marriage, inheritance and education under their own leaders. [cite:w_ottoman_millet] The arrangement was not a golden age, and nothing in the record presents it as one. Its costs were real: heavy taxation, conscription, periodic violence, and a second-class status for non-Muslims, who paid special taxes and held lesser standing. Revolts were suppressed. The point the record makes is narrower than nostalgia. The order was imperfect, but it was not the patchwork of rival states the twentieth century created.

Palestine sat inside this order as a set of administrative units rather than as a country with its own frontier. Apart from a period of Egyptian rule from 1831 to 1840, it was administered as Ottoman provinces, chiefly the district of Jerusalem and parts of the provinces of Beirut and Damascus, with no border separating it from what are now Syria, Lebanon or Jordan. The precise word for that rule is imperial. Governors, garrisons and tax collectors ruled over a population that remained largely Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians, with long-established Jewish communities in Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias. Rule came through administration and taxation, not through replacing the population. It ended when British forces captured Jerusalem in December 1917, and the Republic of Turkey, founded in 1923, formally renounced all rights over the former Arab provinces in the Treaty of Lausanne that year. [cite:w_lausanne] Modern Turkey is the empire's successor state, not a claimant to the land. Lausanne belongs to a later volume. It is mentioned here only to mark where the Ottoman story ends.

Two maps drawn for this library begin at this point. In the first, the Ottoman order of 1516 to 1917, with no internal borders, is the root from which the later layers of the region grow, beginning with the Anglo-French carve-up of 1916 to 1922. In the second, Ottoman rule is the first station in the long relationship between Turkey and Israel, and the next is the 1923 renunciation at Lausanne. Only their first box belongs to this chapter. Every later station is told in later volumes.

The period from 1799 to 1840 shows how this imperial order behaved under pressure from outside. Within a single generation, a French army, an Egyptian army, a peasant revolt and a coalition of European fleets all fought over the coast and hills of Palestine. On this section's reading, the decisive choices were made each time in Paris, Cairo, Istanbul or London.

The first pressure came from France. During his Egyptian and Syrian campaign, Napoleon Bonaparte besieged Acre in 1799, and the campaign was defeated by combined British and Ottoman forces. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_wiki] He never reached Jerusalem. The Jewish Virtual Library's account dates his retreat from Acre to 22 May 1799. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_jvl] That is also the date given for a French newspaper report about him. A reference summary of the scholarship records that the Gazette nationale of 3 Prairial, Year VII (22 May 1799) printed a short report that Bonaparte had invited the Jews of Asia and Africa to gather under his flag. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_wiki] Whether one source has run the two events onto one date has not been settled in this book's research.

That newspaper item stands beside one of the most contested documents in the prehistory of Zionism. A much fuller text, a "Letter to the Jewish Nation," headed "General Headquarters, Jerusalem, 1st Floréal [Year VII], April 20th, 1799" and addressed to the "rightful heirs of Palestine," was first published only in 1940, when Franz Kobler announced a German translation in The New Judaea. No original has ever been found. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_jvl] [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_akadem] The letter is dated and placed in Jerusalem, a city its supposed author never reached.

Historians have divided sharply over what, if anything, Napoleon proclaimed. The case for forgery is made by four of the scholars the sources name. Simon Schwarzfuchs (1979), Henry Laurens, Ronald Schechter and Jeremy Popkin regard the German text as forged. Schechter goes further and reads even the 1799 newspaper report as a rumour. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_wiki] A French Jewish educational site calls the forgery "almost certainly established" and attributes it to Frankist circles. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_akadem] The strongest form of this case rests on the document's history: it surfaces only in 1940, in translation, with no original, and its heading places Napoleon where he never was.

The other side does not dismiss the episode. Mordechai Gichon argues that Napoleon did conceive of a Jewish national home in the Land of Israel. Nathan Schur reads the 1799 report as propaganda aimed at local Jews. [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_wiki] The Jewish Virtual Library prints the letter and says only that its authenticity "has been debated ever since." [cite:rs_rd0_napoleon_jvl] The strongest form of this side's case is that a contemporary French newspaper did print a report of an appeal to Jews in May 1799, so that something was said or rumoured at the time, whatever became of the text. What the record does not settle is whether Bonaparte himself issued any appeal, what it said, and who composed the letter that appeared in 1940.

The second pressure came from Egypt, whose ruler, Muhammad Ali, fielded his own army against the Sultan. Ibrahim Pasha, son of Egypt's ruler Muhammad Ali, invaded Ottoman Syria on 31 October 1831 and made Haifa his main base from December 1831. According to a reference summary, Palestine remained under Egyptian rule until 1840. [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki] Ibrahim's conscription order of 25 April 1834 required one Muslim man in five, beginning with 200 from Jerusalem, and conscripts were to serve for life. He also disarmed Nablus and the Hebron district and imposed new taxes, including one on the Muslim administrative class. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834] [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki]

The result was a revolt. Judith Mendelsohn Rood reconstructed it from the records of Jerusalem's Islamic court. In her account, the revolt was led from Nablus by Qasim al-Ahmad, with 'Isa Amar leading the Hebron district, while the mufti of Jerusalem, Tahir Afandi al-Husayni, acted as mediator and was later exiled. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834] He should not be assumed to be the Muhammad Tahir al-Husayni who appears later in the [scene:rd0_limits|campaign against land sales in the 1890s]; the sources name the two muftis separately and do not connect them.

On 19 and 20 May 1834, about 36 rebels entered Jerusalem through an abandoned sewer near the Dung Gate and opened the gate to the rest. The first day's fighting killed 50 peasants, 16 townspeople and five soldiers. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834] [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki] Rood reports that Ibrahim lost 1,500 of his 9,000 soldiers in rebel ambushes. The violence did not run along a single line: 33 Christian men and women of Bayt Jala were killed on suspicion of looting. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834]

The sources differ on Hebron, and both versions are given here. In Rood's account, Hebron was besieged on 24 July, and 630 young men were afterwards sent to Egypt for the army. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834] A reference summary instead dates Hebron's defeat to 4 August and gives about 500 killed and 750 men conscripted. [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki] Rood's study rests on the Jerusalem court records, but neither figure can be confirmed against the other with the material in hand. Qasim al-Ahmad was captured in Transjordan in August 1834 and beheaded in Damascus. His sons were beheaded in Acre. [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834]

The wider cost is known only from the reference summary, and it is reported here as that source's figure. It states that some 10,000 peasants were deported to Egypt, and that deportation, conscription and flight together reduced Palestine's male population by about one-fifth. The same source records the rebels' stated aim: to expel the Egyptians and restore Ottoman rule. [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki]

That stated aim lies at the centre of a real argument among historians. The question is whether 1834 was an early moment of Palestinian national identity. Baruch Kimmerling and Joel Migdal, in The Palestinian People (2003), treat the revolt as formative, because it joined disparate groups against a common enemy, and those groups later re-emerged as the Palestinian people. The strongest form of their case, as this section reads it, rests on the shape of the revolt itself: peasants and townspeople from Nablus, Jerusalem and the Hebron hills, with the city's religious leadership as intermediary, acting together against one ruler. Benny Morris argues the opposite. The rebels demanded no independence, they likely saw themselves as Ottoman subjects, Arabs or Syrians, and they left no lasting national tradition. The reference summary that reports his view adds that the revolt is largely absent from Palestinian collective memory, and that its declared purpose was to restore the Ottomans, not to replace them. [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki]

Two further readings complicate both positions. Shimon Shamir sees the Egyptian period as the first application of the territorial state in Palestine. Adel Manna argues that the way twentieth-century writers felt about the Ottomans and about Muhammad Ali shaped how the revolt was later written about. [cite:rs_rd0_revolt1834_wiki] On Manna's point, the debate over 1834 is partly a debate about the twentieth century carried back into the nineteenth. What the sources do not settle is what the rebels thought they were: the sources preserve their acts and their stated aim, but not a statement of identity in their own words.

The third pressure came from Europe, and it ended the Egyptian interlude. On 15 July 1840, in the Convention of London, Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia backed Sultan Abdülmecid I against Muhammad Ali. They offered him hereditary rule of Egypt if he withdrew from Syria. [cite:rs_rd0_oriental1840_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_oriental1840_ellison] British and allied ships then shelled Beirut and Sidon on 11 September 1840 and bombarded Acre on 3 November 1840. On 27 November 1840 Muhammad Ali accepted terms. He withdrew from Syria and the Holy Land in return for hereditary rule of Egypt and Sudan. [cite:rs_rd0_oriental1840_wiki] Acre, where a French campaign had been defeated in 1799, was bombarded by European ships in 1840.

Britain's reason for acting was stated plainly, and it was not about Palestine as such. In William Erich Ellison's 2024 analysis of the crisis, Britain sought to "preserve Turkey" as a route to India and as a bulwark against Russian expansion. [cite:rs_rd0_oriental1840_ellison] The same years saw a separate, religious current in British policy towards Jerusalem, which is told in [scene:bc_s05|the section on Protestant restorationism]. The two converged in 1840 without being the same thing. The interest that moved the fleets was imperial and strategic. In the same years, Istanbul began its own programme of reform, the subject of [scene:rd0_tanzimat|the next part of the Ottoman story].

Seen as a mechanism, the four decades follow a single pattern. The power to decide who ruled Palestine lay with the Sultan in Istanbul, with the ruler of Egypt in Cairo, who could raise his own army, and, by 1840, with a concert of European powers that could settle the question by treaty and enforce it with ships. The people of the land had one instrument of their own, revolt, and in 1834 they used it against conscription, disarmament and new taxes. They could make Egyptian rule costly, at the price of deportations, executions and a lost fifth of the male population, if the reference figure is right. They could not decide the outcome. That was settled in London in July 1840 and enforced off Acre in November, and the settlement returned the land to the empire that the rebels of 1834 had said they wanted back.

Several things remain open. No original of Napoleon's supposed letter has been found, and the scholars who have studied it disagree about whether even the 1799 report reflects anything he said. The Hebron figures differ between the court-record study and the reference summary. The deportation total and the one-fifth estimate rest on a reference summary rather than on a study of the records. And whether the revolt of 1834 was the first stirring of a Palestinian national identity, a provincial rising for the old order, or both at once remains a matter of interpretation on which the historians named here continue to disagree.
