Paying for the New State, 1949–1953

While the American organization was reorganizing itself, the state it supported had bills to pay. The state that emerged from the war of 1948 had to house and feed a mass immigration, a story told in [scene:rd6_ingathering|the mass immigration of 1948–1952]. The money for that effort came from three outside channels that opened in quick succession: loans and grants from the United States government, bonds sold to buyers abroad, and reparations from West Germany. Each worked differently, each was paid for by different people, and one of them divided Israel's own public. A common assumption is that American military support flowed to Israel from the beginning. The documents of the period show something else: American help in these years was economic, modest and uneven, and the largest single agreement of the early 1950s was made with West Germany.

The first channel was the American government. After Truman's re-election an Export-Import Bank loan of $100 million was earmarked for Israel, a decision placed in its political sequence in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's decisions after the election]. The Congressional Research Service's table of US aid to Israel, compiled by the Jewish Virtual Library, records the year-by-year sums in millions of dollars: 100.0 in 1949, from the Export-Import Bank and other sources; nothing in 1950; 35.1 in 1951; 86.4 in 1952; 73.6 in 1953; 74.7 in 1954; 52.7 in 1955; 50.8 in 1956; 40.9 in 1957; 85.4 in 1958; and 53.3 in 1959, the last including a first military loan of 0.4. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_usaid_table] Added together, the table's eleven years come to $652.9 million, an average of under $60 million a year, with the largest single figure in the first year.

The character of that aid mattered as much as its size. From Israel's founding through the early 1960s, US assistance was economic aid and loans only. According to one study of the aid relationship, Washington avoided direct arms sales because it did not want to alienate the Arab oil-producing states. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990]

Among the American-dollar flows in the record from 1921 to 1949, the $100 million loan of 1949 is the largest single sum, double the $50 million of Golda Meyerson's 1948 American tour and a thousand times the $100,000 that Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann provided for Truman's whistle-stop campaign, as the chart in the section on Truman shows. The largest flow was not the most politically disputed: it was a government credit, open to view and recorded in the official tables.

When American military aid began is a point on which the sources differ, and both versions should be stated. The CRS table shows a military loan of $0.4 million in 1959. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_usaid_table] The study of the aid relationship cited above dates the first US military aid, itself in the form of loans rather than grants, to 1962. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] The sources do not explain the difference; a reader can say only that a very small military loan appears in the official table three years before the date that study gives for the start of military aid.

The later shape of the relationship belongs to later chapters and can be summarized in a few dates. Congress first designated a dedicated earmark for Israel in 1971. [cite:everycrsreport_israel_foreign_assistance] The 1973 war was the turning point: President Nixon ordered an emergency military airlift and requested $2.2 billion in emergency aid, a rise of roughly 800 percent over prior annual levels. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] [cite:legalclarity_when_did_us_start_funding] Economic aid became all grants in 1981, and military aid in 1985, the year of a separate $1.5 billion supplemental economic package, the largest aid package for a single country since the Vietnam War era. [cite:legalclarity_when_did_us_start_funding] [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] The often-quoted cumulative total of $300 billion is the Council on Foreign Relations' inflation-adjusted estimate; the Congressional Research Service, in its most recent tally, using the same appropriations data without adjusting for inflation, places cumulative bilateral assistance and missile defense funding at about $174 billion. [cite:cfr_aid_four_charts] [cite:legalclarity_how_much_money_given] Those totals were built almost entirely after the period of this chapter, as told in [scene:iaa_s01|how the aid relationship grew].

The second channel was private, but organized by the state. According to the Jewish Virtual Library's history of the program, David Ben-Gurion conceived an overseas bond issue in 1950, amid economic crisis and mass immigration. In September 1950 he convened American Jewish leaders at the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, and sales were launched in the United States the following spring with a rally at Madison Square Garden. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_bonds] The 1951 Independence Issue paid 3.5 percent, and first-year sales topped $52 million. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_bonds] Within six years, the same source reports, bond sales accounted for 35% of Israel's special development budget, financing the National Water Carrier, the Dead Sea Works, power plants, ports and immigrant towns. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_bonds]

The mechanism differed from the charitable campaigns of the pre-state years. The 1951 issue paid its buyers interest rather than asking for a gift, it was sold in the United States, and its proceeds went to the state's special development budget and the projects named above. The record cited here does not give the month of the first sales, saying only that they began the following spring.

The third channel was the most contested. On 12 March 1951 Moshe Sharett presented a claim of $1.5 billion against Germany. It was calculated as the cost of absorbing 500,000 victims of the Nazis at $3,000 each. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations] The method framed the claim as compensation for a concrete burden on the new state: the cost of resettling survivors. On 9 January 1952 the Knesset voted 61–50 to negotiate directly with West Germany, while thousands demonstrated outside and police used tear gas. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations]

The opposition was led by Menachem Begin, who spoke at mass demonstrations in the spring of 1952. Opponents called the payments "blood money" from the "land of the murderers." [cite:rs_rd6_nli_reparations] The dispute was not between Israel and its adversaries but within Israel. The case for the agreement, in the government's own terms, was that the new state was bearing the cost of absorbing hundreds of thousands of the Nazis' victims and that Germany should meet it. The case against, in the opponents' own words, was that money from the "land of the murderers" was "blood money" and should not be taken. The narrow vote, 61 to 50, shows how evenly the Knesset was divided.

The agreement was signed in Luxembourg on 10 September 1952 by Sharett, Nahum Goldmann and Konrad Adenauer, and took effect on 21 March 1953. [cite:rs_rd6_nli_reparations] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations] Payments ran in annual instalments from 1 April 1953 to 31 March 1966. They came not as cash transfers but in kind: 30% for crude oil bought in Britain and 70% for German goods. Over the decade they made up 12–14% of Israel's annual imports. [cite:rs_rd6_nli_reparations] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations]

How much the agreement was worth is itself disputed between the two sources. The Jewish Virtual Library, citing Israeli Foreign Ministry documents of 1953 and the Encyclopaedia Judaica, gives a total of $845 million, of which $100 million went to the Claims Conference, paid over 14 years from 1953 to 1966. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations] The National Library of Israel gives goods and services valued at 3.5 billion marks over 12 years, plus 450 million marks more. [cite:rs_rd6_nli_reparations] The two figures are expressed in different currencies and over periods described differently; the sources do not reconcile them, and this book does not either.

Taken together, the three channels answer the question of who paid for the new state in its first years only in part. The American government lent and granted modest sums, without arms, for reasons tied to its relations with Arab oil producers. Private buyers abroad lent money through bonds that built the state's water, power and ports. West Germany paid in oil and goods, over the objections of a large part of Israel's own public. What the record cited here does not settle is the whole: it gives no single account of the state's budget in these years, no measure of how much of the cost of absorption fell on Israel's own taxpayers and on the immigrants themselves, and no agreed figure for the largest of the three transfers. The month the first bonds were sold, and the reason the two sources date the first American military loan three years apart, also remain open.
