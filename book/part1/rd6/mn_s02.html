Did Money Buy the Politicians?

The question of whether money bought American policy toward Palestine is usually answered in one of two ways: that Zionist money bought Congress and the White House, or that money played no role at all. Both answers treat a bundle of separate claims as if it were one. Taken apart, the claims have very different evidentiary standing, and one of them cannot be settled either way.

The first claim is that there was organized political pressure. This is documented in detail. The American Zionist Emergency Council's local committees, its letter and telegram campaigns, its book America and Palestine, the 1944 party platforms and the Wagner–Taft resolution are all recorded, much of it in the movement's own files. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]

The second claim is that money changed a specific decision. This is documented once. The historian John Judis records that in mid-1948 Truman backed off pressing Israel over territory and refugees in part because his nearly broke campaign needed $100,000 from Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann. The case is set out in full in [scene:s07f2|Abraham Feinberg].

The third claim is that members of Congress were systematically paid before 1948. This cannot be established in either direction, because the records that would establish it were never required to exist. Until the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, the governing law was the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925. It required disclosure mainly from party committees operating across several states rather than from candidates, carried few penalties, left enforcement to Congress, and was rarely enforced. It was riddled with loopholes, and candidates' donors were largely undisclosed until 1971. [cite:wiki_fcpa_1925] [cite:sage_fec_history] [cite:crs_campaign_finance] Without disclosure records, any claim that specific legislators were paid is unsupported. So is any claim that they were not.

The fourth point is that other documented drivers pushed in the same direction without money. They include the moral urgency of the Holocaust years; concentrations of Jewish voters in states such as New York; sincere Christian restorationist conviction going back to 1839; Henry Wallace's personal Zionism; and the 1922 Lodge–Fish resolution, which is credited to lobbying with no payment documented.

The counter-record belongs here too. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense opposed the policy in writing, and they were overruled. The pressures that prevailed over them are documented as political; a financial one is documented only in the single case above.

The result is a finding with a visible gap in it. What can be shown is an organized, well-funded political movement with direct access to decision-makers, plus one documented case of money shifting a decision. Wider claims of bought politicians are not in the record. That silence is itself a finding: it reflects not evidence of innocence, nor evidence of guilt, but a legal regime under which the evidence was never required to be kept.
