Abraham Feinberg: One Donor, One Decision, 1945–1958

The one decision in that sequence that a historian ties to money runs through Abraham Feinberg, a name absent from most popular accounts of 1948. It belongs in them, for a specific reason. He is the person through whom the documented record comes closest to showing a campaign contribution changing a particular decision of the American president. That claim needs to be stated at exactly its documented size, no larger and no smaller, and the rest of his career helps show why.

The man. Abraham "Abe" Feinberg was born in 1908 and died in 1998. He was born and raised in the Bronx and built his fortune in the hosiery business. From 1945 he played a significant role behind the scenes in the relationship between the United States and Israel, a role that lasted until 1968. Across those years he served as a confidant and a key financier to three presidents: Truman, Kennedy and Johnson. [cite:emory_feinberg_thesis] [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb] He was also one of the original seventeen members of the Sonneborn Institute, the arms-procurement network founded in 1945, whose place in the wider American structure is mapped in [scene:nw_s02|the network of the American build-up]. [cite:emory_feinberg_thesis]

That second fact matters for what follows. Feinberg was not a donor who appears once in the record and then disappears. He links two structures that are often told separately: the private arms-procurement network that operated on American soil from 1945, and the presidential campaign of 1948.

The $100,000. In mid-1948 Truman's campaign was broke, and Thomas Dewey was widely expected to make him a one-term president. Truman needed $100,000, a large sum in 1948, to pay for a whistle-stop tour by train through the Midwest. Feinberg and the jewelry magnate Ed Kaufmann led the fundraising that produced that amount, at that moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] [cite:prnewswire_feinberg_fbi]

Feinberg later described his own method plainly. "My path to power," he said, "was cooperation in terms of what they needed — campaign money." [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] [cite:prnewswire_feinberg_fbi] The words are his, on the record, not an outside reading of his motives. They describe an exchange in general terms: he supplied what politicians needed, and that was, in his words, his path to power. They do not, by themselves, say what any president gave him.

The decision. The link between the money and a decision comes from the historian John Judis. In mid-1948, by Judis's account, Truman wanted Israel to stop seizing territory by military force beyond the UN partition lines, and he was personally "disgusted" by Israel's refugee policy. He is reported to have said that Jews had turned their own narrative on its head by denying Palestinians the right of return. Judis documents that Truman folded on these impulses in part because he needed the $100,000 from Feinberg and Kaufmann for the whistle-stop tour at that moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] The dated sequence of what Truman did and did not do on territory and refugees, from the Bernadotte plan to the threat he sent Ben-Gurion in May 1949, is set out in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's decisions after recognition].

The familiar account holds that Truman's support for Israel was a matter of personal conviction and of a broad political calculation about Jewish voters, with no instance in which financial dependence changed a specific decision. Judis's account is such an instance. It names a donor, a sum, a moment in a campaign, and an objection the President stopped pressing. That is a different and more precise kind of claim than the general statement that money influenced politics.

It is also a limited one, and the limits are in Judis's own words. He says Truman folded "in part" because of the financial need. Money was one cause among others, not the only one. The account is one historian's reading of one decision. It does not show that Feinberg's money bought American policy toward Israel in general, and it does not show what Truman thought he was giving in return. When Truman later threatened Israel over the same two questions, territory and refugees, the money of 1948 did not prevent him. Some accounts go further and place the recognition of Israel, eleven minutes after its declaration of independence, inside the same financial relationship. The sources gathered here cite no document linking the money to recognition itself; they tie the $100,000 to the summer retreat and to nothing else.

Why the smallest sum matters most. On the chart of dollar flows in the previous section, the $100,000 is the smallest bar, one-thousandth of the $100 million Export-Import Bank loan of 1949. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] Yet it is the only sum in that record that a historian ties to a specific policy decision, because, by Judis's account, it was raised at the moment a decision was being made. Its importance comes from its timing, not its size.

The FARA order of 1952. The federal government did eventually reach Feinberg, not for arms but for publishing. In 1952 he was formally ordered to register as an agent of a foreign principal under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. The order concerned Israel Speaks, a bimonthly publication he ran on behalf of Israeli interests, the successor to an earlier publication aligned with the Haganah. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file] [cite:israellobby_feinberg_fbi] Feinberg briefly considered selling the publication to the Jerusalem Post. Instead he shut it down entirely rather than register. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file] The outcome was compliance by closure: there was no prosecution and no fight in court.

The order corrects a claim sometimes made about the Sonneborn network, that none of its figures was ever required to register as a foreign agent and that the network enjoyed complete legal immunity. At least one was ordered to register. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file] It was the first documented order of its kind against a figure from that network, seven years after the network's founding.

The same FBI file, released decades later, shows more. It documents Feinberg's continuing meetings with Sonneborn, and it shows that the Israeli government tasked him with intervening in the criminal prosecutions of members of the arms-smuggling network who had been caught. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file] It is the clearest documented link in the whole chronology between the smuggling network of 1945–48 and a named figure to whom the foreign-agent law was applied.

"Call Abe," 1958. Feinberg's role did not end with the 1948 campaign. According to Avner Cohen's history Israel and the Bomb, David Ben-Gurion secretly named Feinberg his chief coordinator for raising private American funds for Israel's clandestine nuclear weapons program, beginning in 1958. Ben-Gurion is said to have told his staff simply to "call Abe." [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb] [cite:israellobby_feinberg_fbi] On that account, the man who helped fund a whistle-stop tour in 1948 became, a decade later, the coordinator of private American funds for a clandestine nuclear program. The account is Cohen's. It establishes Feinberg's role and the instruction; it does not give the program's finances or the sums he raised, and that story belongs to a later volume.

What the record shows, and what it does not. Feinberg's career runs from the arms network of 1945 through a presidential campaign, a foreign-agent order and, by 1958, a nuclear fundraising role. Over those years he is documented as a channel: for money into a campaign, for a publication on Israel's behalf, for Israel's government into an American prosecution, and for private funds into a secret program. The one point at which the record ties his money to a specific American decision is the summer of 1948, and it does so through one historian's account, qualified by the words "in part." The record does not show how much weight Truman gave that need against votes, conviction or advice. It does not show any later president changing a decision because of Feinberg's money. And it does not document the size of his later fundraising. Those limits are part of the finding. The case is real, named and specific, and it is one case.
