What Was Lost, and How the Ascent Survived

The closings of 391 to about 537 ended institutions. Whether they also erased knowledge depends on which knowledge is meant. The rites of the mysteries were transmitted by performance and oath, not by books, and when the performances stopped, the rites were lost. The written philosophy of ascent was a different matter. Much of it was copied, adapted and taught by Christians and Muslims for the next thousand years, often under false names, and returned to the Latin West in full in the fifteenth century. This section sets out both outcomes: what the record shows was destroyed or is now missing, and the routes by which the doctrine of the soul's ascent came through.

What is gone

The inner content of the mystery rites is gone, and in most cases it was never written down to be lost. No text of the Eleusinian ceremony survives, only the initiates' testimony that it gave them a better hope at death. [cite:vt33_wh_eleusis] The cult of Mithras, which left hundreds of sanctuaries and had ceased to exist by the end of the fourth century, left no scripture; its structure is known from mosaics, inscriptions and a summary by its critic Celsus preserved by its other critic Origen. [cite:vt21_wiki_mithraism] [cite:vt33_harland_celsus] The Samothracian cult left lists of initiates and no doctrine. [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] For these, the loss was the end of the practice itself. The laws of Theodosius and the acts of bishops and emperors described in the previous section stopped the practice where the old cults were still alive; at Samothrace and, on Dijkstra's reading, at Philae, the practice was already fading. [cite:vt33_gozzoli_dijkstra]

Some losses were ordered in writing. The most explicit concerns not a mystery text but a philosopher's attack on Christianity. Porphyry, the pupil of Plotinus, wrote a work known as Against the Christians. Constantine banned it, and the emperor Theodosius II ordered every copy burned in 435 and again in 448. No copy survives; its arguments are known only from the roughly thirty Christian writers, among them Eusebius and Jerome, who quoted it in order to refute it. [cite:vt33_wiki_againstchristians] This is the clearest documented case in late antiquity of the Christian state destroying a pagan philosopher's book by name, and it belongs beside the orders against Arian, Montanist and Nestorian books set out in Part Twenty-One. [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Church and Empire]

The schools lost their institutional line. After Justinian's decree, the succession of heads of the Platonic school at Athens, from Plato through Proclus to Damascius, ends in the record. Many of Proclus's own works survive only in fragments, preserved by the later Neoplatonists Damascius, Olympiodorus, Simplicius and the Christian John Philoponus, who quoted them. [cite:vt33_sep_proclus] [cite:vt33_hannam_academy] What is missing here is less a body of secret knowledge than the continuous teaching community that had carried it and the many commentaries that community would have produced.

The first route: inside Christianity

The most important survival route ran through the church itself. In the late fifth or early sixth century, an author writing under the name of Dionysius the Areopagite, the Athenian whom Paul converted in Acts 17, produced four treatises, On the Divine Names, On the Celestial Hierarchy, On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy and the Mystical Theology, with ten letters. The first clear citation of them is by Severus of Antioch between 518 and 528. They describe the soul's path in the triad of purification, illumination and perfection; they call theurgy "the consummation of theology"; and the Mystical Theology describes Moses' entry into the darkness of the unknowable God as the model of union. [cite:vt33_sep_dionysius] Modern scholarship places their author in Proclus's time and finds him drawing heavily on Proclus, so much that the classicist E. R. Dodds described Proclus as "conquering Europe in the guise of an early Christian". [cite:vt33_sep_proclus]

Because the church took the Areopagite to be Paul's disciple, his writings carried near-apostolic authority. John Scottus Eriugena translated them into Latin in 862 for Charles the Bald, and they shaped the Rhineland mystics Meister Eckhart, Johannes Tauler and Jan van Ruusbroec, as well as Nicholas of Cusa and the English Cloud of Unknowing. Doubts about the attribution were raised in the sixth century and by Lorenzo Valla in 1457, and Erasmus publicised them from 1504. [cite:vt33_sep_dionysius] The doctrine of ascent that Theodosius's laws had forced out of the temples therefore re-entered Christian monasteries under a borrowed name. The institution that kept it also, in time, policed it: Eckhart, one of its heirs, had propositions condemned by Pope John XXII in 1329, a story told in Part Twenty-Nine. [scene:vt29_mystics|Mystics and the stake]

The second route: through Arabic

The second route ran through the Islamic world. In the ninth century a paraphrase of parts of Plotinus's Enneads was made in Arabic by Ibn Na'ima al-Himsi, a Christian, and edited by the philosopher al-Kindi. It circulated as the Theology of Aristotle, an attribution modern scholars regard as certainly false, and it shaped how al-Kindi and later Avicenna, who wrote a commentary on it, understood the relation of the soul to the One. [cite:vt33_wiki_theologyaristotle] A second Arabic adaptation, of Proclus's Elements of Theology, became the Latin Liber de causis, also read in the medieval universities as Aristotle's until Thomas Aquinas identified its source in Proclus. [cite:vt33_sep_proclus] The Neoplatonic doctrine of emanation and return thus reached Paris and Oxford partly through Baghdad, under the name of a philosopher who had not written it.

The third route: Florence, 1463

The third route was the return of the Greek texts themselves. In Florence, Marsilio Ficino finished a Latin translation of the Hermetic treatise known as the Pimander in 1463, from a Greek manuscript brought from Macedonia for Cosimo de' Medici; eight printed editions appeared before 1500. Ficino went on to translate Plato and the writings of Dionysius the Areopagite. [cite:vt33_brandeis_pimander] He had interrupted his work on Plato to translate the Hermetica first, because they were believed to be older; in 1614 Isaac Casaubon showed from their Greek that they belonged to the Roman period. [cite:ts13_wiki_hermetica] The revival of Hermetic and Platonic ascent in Renaissance Europe, and the Church's later handling of it in the trials of Bruno and in the Index of Forbidden Books, are set out in Parts Twenty-Nine and Thirty. [scene:vt29_bruno_galileo|Bruno and Galileo] [scene:vt30_forbidden_arts|The forbidden arts]

Interpretation, not finding: closing the rite, keeping the map

The Sophia platform reads this sequence as a pattern rather than a set of separate events. In this reading, what the Christian empire closed was the independent practice of ascent: the rite a person could undergo at Eleusis or in a mithraeum, or the philosophical school where a teacher could claim, as Plotinus did, direct union with God. What it kept was the map of ascent, rewritten as a hierarchy of angels, sacraments and clergy under the name of an apostle's disciple. The platform holds that the knowledge was not so much destroyed as relocated, from free-standing schools into an institution that alone could administer it, a reading that connects this part to the "two economies" argument of Part Thirty-One. [scene:vt31_two_economies|Two economies of the sacred]

The strongest counter-readings come from the same record. First, not every ending was an act of suppression: Samothrace faded without a recorded closure, and at Philae the cult was, on Dijkstra's evidence, already dying when Justinian's general arrived. [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] [cite:vt33_gozzoli_dijkstra] Second, not every actor was the church: Eleusis was wrecked by a Gothic army, and the decisive laws were imperial. [cite:vt33_wiki_telesterion] Third, much of the survival was the work of Christians and Muslims who valued the philosophy, not of pagans hiding it; the Pseudo-Dionysian writings were embraced by the hierarchy, not smuggled past it. [cite:vt33_sep_dionysius] Fourth, enforcement of the anti-pagan laws was uneven, as the archaeology of temple destruction shows. [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] What the record supports without interpretation is narrower than either reading: emperors outlawed the rites, bishops and soldiers destroyed specific sanctuaries, a named philosopher was killed and a named book burned, and the doctrine of ascent survived because people inside the victorious religions chose to keep it.
