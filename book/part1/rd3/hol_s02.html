The Nuremberg Laws, 1935: Persecution Written into Statute

On 15 September 1935 the Reichstag met in special session at Nuremberg, where the Nazi Party was holding its annual rally. It passed two laws, and it passed them unanimously [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_laws]. The parliament that voted had already surrendered its own authority two and a half years earlier, when the Enabling Act of March 1933 allowed Hitler to make law without the approval of the Reichstag or the President, a measure carried after Communist deputies had been forcibly kept from voting [cite:history_weimar_republic]. The two statutes it approved that day did not burn a synagogue or open a camp. They did something that came first and made the rest administrable: they wrote antisemitism into the law of the German state, defining Jews as a separate category of person by descent and removing them from the body of citizens.

A familiar way of telling this history begins with violence: the mob, the broken windows, the deportations, with laws following later to tidy up what force had already done. The order in the documents runs the other way. By the autumn of 1935 Germany had a complete legal framework that defined who was a Jew, stripped Jews of citizenship and made marriage between Jews and other Germans a crime. That framework stood three years before the mass violence of Kristallnacht, told in [scene:hol_s03|the next stage]. Exclusion was legislated first and then enforced through escalating violence. Law came before the pogrom, not after it.

The first of the two statutes, the Reich Citizenship Law, turned on a single definition. A citizen of the Reich was a person "of German or related blood" who proved through conduct a willingness to serve the German people and Reich [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_laws] [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. German Jews were reduced from citizens to "subjects" of the state, without full political rights [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_laws]. One sentence of law, defining citizenship by blood rather than by birthplace, residence or belief, removed an entire population from political membership in the state. No shot was fired to do it.

The second statute, the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honor, passed the same day, reached into private life. It banned marriage between Jews and "subjects of the state of German or related blood," and it declared such marriages void even when they had been concluded abroad for the specific purpose of evading the law. It made "extramarital intercourse" between Jews and non-Jewish Germans a crime. It barred Jews from employing non-Jewish German women under the age of 45 in their households. Violations were punishable by imprisonment [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. The regime had its own name for the new offence: Rassenschande, "race defilement" [cite:wiki_rassenschande]. The word carried the ideology of the statute in a single term. It presented ordinary love, marriage and domestic employment between Jews and other Germans as a contamination that the state was entitled to punish.

Both laws depended on knowing who was a Jew, and here the regime made its most consequential choice. It did not define a Jew by religion. Under the Nuremberg Race Laws a person with three or four Jewish grandparents was a Jew, whatever that person believed or practised [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. Germans who had not practised Judaism in decades fell within the definition. So did people who had formally converted to Christianity. Belief, conversion and long non-observance offered no exit, because the test was not what a person did or believed but who that person's grandparents had been. The definition was racial in the plainest sense, and it is the clearest evidence that the target of the laws was not a faith but a people the state had decided to classify by blood.

The bureaucracy then extended the classification downward. On 14 November 1935 the First Supplementary Decree to the Reich Citizenship Law created the category of Mischling, "mixed blood," for people with one or two Jewish grandparents, and attached its own separate legal restrictions to it [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. The decree distinguished degrees of Jewish ancestry, each carrying its own legal consequences. Charts were produced so that officials, and ordinary citizens, could work out a person's racial status under the law [cite:facinghistory_nuremberg_laws]. A person's legal standing became the answer to a genealogical sum. The arithmetic was the mechanism: once ancestry could be counted, a clerk could place any German in a category, and the category decided whom that person could marry, whom he or she could employ, and whether he or she was a citizen at all.

The structure of power behind this is simple to state. The decision was made inside the Nazi state and ratified unanimously by a parliament that had already given up its law-making authority. The instrument was statute, followed by decree. The people classified had no means of acting against it: they had lost the standing of citizens by the very law that classified them. The burden fell on German Jews, including converts and the non-observant whom the ancestry test caught, and, through the Mischling decree, on people with one or two Jewish grandparents.

What the 1935 laws meant for Palestine can be stated only as far as the sources go, and they do not go far. Jewish immigration to Palestine reached its highest recorded level of the Mandate years in 1935. The figures compiled by the US State Department from British returns give 61,854 Jewish immigrants that year, against 42,359 in 1934 and 29,727 in 1936 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]; a second series, printed by the Jewish Virtual Library after Yehoshua Porath, gives 66,472 for 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_porath_table]. The difference between the two series, and the composition of the 1935 arrivals, are set out in [scene:rd3_aliyot|the immigration section]. The research does not break the year's arrivals down by country of origin or by month, so it cannot show how many German Jews came, or whether any of the rise followed September rather than preceding it. The surge had begun in 1933, two years before Nuremberg, when the same series records 30,327 arrivals [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]; and the British labour schedule, which governed one channel of entry, was cut from 8,000 places for April–September 1935 to 3,250 for October 1935–March 1936 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d531]. The most that can be said is that the laws were passed in the year of the peak, and that the transfer agreement through which German Jews had been moving to Palestine since 1933 [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] was upheld by the Zionist Congress at Lucerne in August 1935, a month before the Nuremberg session, as told in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement]. The sources here do not measure the effect of the laws themselves on emigration.

The text of the two statutes and of the November decree is documented, and it is unambiguous; on what the laws said, the sources present no dispute. What they leave unsettled is the record of enforcement. The documentation here establishes the provisions and the penalties, not a count of prosecutions for Rassenschande, which varied in intensity across regions and years; it establishes that the Mischling system existed and how it worked, not the full detail of the distinctions it drew between its categories. Nor do the sources say how many people the classification touched in 1935. What they do establish is the sequence, and the sequence is the finding: by the end of 1935 a modern state had defined a part of its own population by ancestry, removed it from citizenship and criminalized its private life, all by unanimous statute and orderly decree, years before the violence that the next stage records.
