What the Diagnosis Gets Right

An argument is only tested fairly in its strongest form. The Dark Enlightenment texts make two separate claims, and they stand or fall separately. The first is a diagnosis: that American democracy no longer does what it says it does, that the people who formally govern are not the people who actually decide, and that the machinery has grown too large and too diffuse for anyone to answer for it. The second is a cure: a state run like a company, under a chief executive answerable to its owners rather than to voters. This part takes the diagnosis on its own terms and asks how much of it the record supports. The next part tests the cure.

Curtis Yarvin, writing as Mencius Moldbug, put the diagnosis in two words. "The Cathedral" was his name for the universities, the press and the permanent civil service, which in his account govern together as an unelected establishment through shared assumptions rather than a chain of command. "Formalism" was his name for the remedy's first step: making the official map of power match the real one, so that whoever actually rules does so openly and can be held to account. Nick Land, summarising the neoreactionary case in 2012, wrote that "winning elections is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying" and that democracy drives the state ever larger. [cite:de7_land_de] In an interview with the New York Times in January 2025, Yarvin offered a milder version: "it's not even that democracy is bad, it's just that it's very weak." [cite:de7_yarvin_nyt2025] That second sentence is the strongest form of the diagnosis, and it is the one tested here.

Trust has collapsed

The first piece of evidence is that most Americans agree with the symptom, whatever they think of the cause. When the American National Election Studies first asked in 1958 how often people trusted the federal government to do what is right, 73 per cent said always or most of the time; in 1964 the figure reached 77 per cent, the highest in the series. In the Pew Research Center's survey completed on 28 September 2025 it was 17 per cent: 2 per cent "just about always" and 15 per cent "most of the time." Among Republicans it was 26 per cent; among Democrats, 9 per cent. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025]

Confidence in particular institutions has followed the same path. Gallup's average across nine institutions it has tracked since 1979 stood at 48 per cent in that first year and 28 per cent in its July 2025 reading, with Democrats' confidence at a record low of 26 per cent. [cite:de7_gallup_conf2025] In September 2026, 54 per cent of Americans told Gallup they had not very much or no trust in the judicial branch; trust in the courts first fell below half in September 2022, never before in a series that began in 1972. [cite:us2_axios_gallup2026]

The state struggles to deliver

The second piece of evidence is that the federal government often fails at things it sets out to do, and its own auditors say so. When the Government Accountability Office, the investigative arm of Congress, examined the launch of HealthCare.gov in October 2013, it found that the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services had built the site and its supporting systems "without effective planning or oversight practices." Obligations for the core marketplace system rose from $56 million in September 2011 to more than $209 million by February 2014, and a replacement contract awarded for $91 million in January 2014 had passed $175 million by June. [cite:de7_gao_hcgov] In February 2025 the GAO listed 38 areas of government operations with serious vulnerabilities to waste, fraud or mismanagement, and reported that federal agencies had recorded an estimated $2.8 trillion in improper payments since 2003. [cite:de7_gao_highrisk2025]

What the GAO describes is not a government that has grown in people. Elaine Kamarck of the Brookings Institution reported in January 2025 that federal civilian employment "has stayed the same since the mid-1960s," falling from 1.1 per cent of the population in 1967 to 0.6 per cent in 2018, while the population grew by about two-thirds. The work moved to contractors, who by one estimate outnumber federal employees by more than two to one; federal contract spending was $694.2 billion in fiscal 2022. At the Social Security Administration, staff fell from nearly 67,000 in 2010 to 56,645 in 2024. [cite:de7_kamarck_size] This complicates the diagnosis in an important way. The texts describe a bloated bureaucracy; the record describes a thin one, carrying more work through contractors it struggles to oversee.

The rules grew; the people writing them did not

What did grow was the volume of rules. The Code of Federal Regulations, the compilation of standing federal rules, ran to 22,877 pages in 1960, 71,224 in 1975, 157,972 in 2008 and 188,346 at the end of 2021, by the count of the Competitive Enterprise Institute, a free-market group, from Office of the Federal Register data. [cite:de7_cei_cfr] Page counts measure length, not burden, and many pages replace older rules rather than adding new ones. But the direction is not in dispute, and it supports a real part of the diagnosis: much of what governs daily life is written by agencies rather than voted on by Congress.

The Dark Enlightenment texts did not discover the accountability problem that follows from this. In 1980 the political theorist Dennis Thompson named it "the problem of many hands" in the American Political Science Review: when outcomes are produced by many officials each doing a small part, it becomes hard to say who is responsible for any of them. [cite:de7_thompson_hands] The idea is a staple of mainstream public administration. Moldbug's version adds a claim Thompson did not make, that the diffusion is the purpose of the design rather than a cost of it; that further claim is his, and the record does not show it.

Who government listens to

The most cited evidence for the diagnosis is a 2014 study by the political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page. They matched 1,779 policy questions asked in surveys between 1981 and 2002 against what the government later did. They found that average citizens had little or no independent influence on outcomes once the preferences of the affluent and of organised interests were taken into account: a change opposed by economic elites passed about 18 per cent of the time, one they strongly favoured about 45 per cent, and business groups had roughly twice the influence of mass-membership groups. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens]

The finding has been seriously disputed, and the dispute matters for what it can carry. Peter Enns showed that the preferences of middle-income and affluent Americans were correlated at 0.94 across 1,836 survey questions, so the middle usually gets what it wants because the affluent want the same thing; he called this "coincidental representation." [cite:ad15_enns] Omar Bashir found that the model explained only about 7 per cent of the variation in outcomes, and that when median-income Americans strongly favoured a change it happened 47 per cent of the time, against 52 per cent for the affluent. [cite:ad15_bashir] J. Alexander Branham, Stuart Soroka and Christopher Wlezien found that the two groups agreed about 90 per cent of the time and that when they disagreed the rich won 53 per cent of the time, "only a little more often than the middle." [cite:de7_branham_ifs] Gilens and Page replied that none of these critiques, "individually or collectively," undermined their central claims. [cite:de7_gp_reply] What survives the exchange is narrower than the headline: when the affluent and everyone else disagree, the affluent have an edge. The stronger reading, that ordinary voters count for nothing, is contested.

The precedent: Roosevelt in 1933

The diagnosis comes with a precedent, and Yarvin returns to it often. On 4 March 1933, with the banking system collapsing, Franklin Roosevelt said in his first inaugural address that he hoped "the normal balance of executive and legislative authority" would be enough. If Congress failed to act on his measures and the emergency remained critical, he said, he would ask Congress for "broad Executive power to wage a war against the emergency, as great as the power that would be given to me if we were in fact invaded by a foreign foe." [cite:de7_fdr_1933] Yarvin told the New York Times that he reads this passage aloud in talks, and summarised it as Roosevelt saying, "hey, Congress, give me absolute power or I'll take it anyway." Asked whether Roosevelt took it, he said, "Yeah, he did." [cite:de7_yarvin_nyt2025]

The precedent is real, and it is the honest core of the case for an executive-centred fix: in a genuine emergency, Americans accepted a president who acted fast and asked Congress to follow. But the record is narrower than the paraphrase. Roosevelt's request was conditional and addressed to Congress, and it was never made in that form, because Congress acted. The Emergency Banking Act was introduced and passed on 9 March 1933, five days after the speech; it expanded the president's powers over banking and approved the bank holiday after the fact. [cite:de7_frb_eba] The powers that followed came statute by statute, and they stayed subject to the other branches. On 27 May 1935 the Supreme Court unanimously struck down the codes of the National Industrial Recovery Act as an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power. [cite:de7_brit_schechter] When Roosevelt answered with a plan announced on 5 February 1937 to add up to six justices, the Senate Judiciary Committee called it "an invasion of judicial power such as has never before been attempted in this country," and the Senate shelved it that July. [cite:de7_ncc_courtpack] After he won a third and a fourth term, the country amended the Constitution so that no president could be elected more than twice. [cite:de8_annenberg_22]

Taken together, the diagnosis holds in its weaker form. Americans have lost faith in their government; the government's own auditors document failures of delivery; rule-making has outgrown direct democratic control; responsibility is genuinely diffuse; and on the best evidence the affluent are heard more than everyone else when they disagree. What the record does not show is the further step the texts take: that the cause is democracy itself, rather than a particular way in which American democracy has come to be run.
