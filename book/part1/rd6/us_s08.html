Fifty-One Years From a Bank to a State: The Whole Sequence, Step by Step

A familiar account treats Israel's creation as a sudden event: a response to the Holocaust, ratified by a postwar decision at the United Nations and completed in a single war. Each part of that account touches something real. The genocide in Europe did change the politics of the question; the General Assembly did vote in November 1947; the war of 1948 did decide where the lines would run. What the account leaves out is everything that had to exist before any of those moments could produce a state. Laid end to end, the documents of Volume II show a sequence built over five decades and across two continents, each step resting on the one before. The movement did not start cold and turn hot in 1947. This section closes the volume by setting that sequence out in order, and then by showing what the years from 1948 to 1953 added on top of it.

1897–1920: a bank, a land fund, a machine, a promise. In 1897 the Basel Congress set the goal. Two years later, in 1899, the movement founded the Jewish Colonial Trust, a bank chartered in London and capitalized by small shareholders. In 1901 the Jewish National Fund began buying land on a principle that defined it for the next half-century: land it bought was held permanently and never resold. In 1902 came the Anglo-Palestine Bank. These were the movement's first financial and land-holding instruments. Between 1912 and 1918 an American organization took shape: Hadassah; Justice Brandeis with access to President Wilson; and the Zionist Organization of America, which reached 147,000 members by 1918 [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]. In 1917 Britain issued the Balfour Declaration, addressed to Lord Rothschild. In 1920 the movement created Keren Hayesod, its permanent fund, whose mandate before 1948 covered immigration, settlement, defense, water and public works [cite:jvl_kh].

1921–1933: tours, Congress, a currency and a wider money base. In 1921 Weizmann and Einstein toured the United States, and a $5 million delegation tour of 1921–22 met President Harding. Keren Hayesod's worldwide receipts from April 1921 to December 1929 came to $20.1 million, of which $11.0 million came from the United States, followed by South Africa, Germany, Romania, Britain, Poland and Canada [cite:jta_kh_decade]. In 1922 Congress endorsed the Balfour Declaration through the resolution sponsored by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and Representative Hamilton Fish III [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation]. In 1927 came a Palestine pound managed from London. In 1929 non-Zionist American money joined an enlarged Jewish Agency, widening the base beyond the movement's own members. Christian committees were organized from the Zionist political office in 1932, when the American Palestine Committee was formed, and again in 1941–42. The American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine were both organized by the Zionist executive Emanuel Neumann, and one historian has described them as "hardly autonomous" [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. In 1933 came the Haavara transfer agreement, valued at 105 million Reichsmarks, about $40 million at the time, over 1933–39.

1938–1948: a levy, a budget, a demand, a lobby, an arms network. In 1938 the Jewish National Council in Palestine created Kofer HaYishuv, a levy on cigarettes, restaurant bills and luxuries, to fund the Haganah [cite:w_kofer]. In 1939 the Jewish Agency's budget was set at £720,000, of which £80,000 was for "the Jewish security service (defense)," as the American consulate reported [cite:frus_1939_ja_budget]. In 1942 the Biltmore program committed the movement to a Jewish commonwealth. In 1943 the American Zionist Emergency Council, co-chaired by Abba Hillel Silver and Stephen Wise, built a national lobbying machine of local committees, letter and telegram campaigns and a published record of legislators' statements [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]. In 1944 both American party platforms adopted the movement's aims, and US-raised spending that year reached $10.8 million through the Jewish National Fund and $14.9 million through the Jewish Agency and Keren Hayesod [cite:djn_1945_upa]. In 1945 a secret meeting convened by Ben-Gurion, with donors gathered by the United Jewish Appeal's Henry Montor, set up the Sonneborn Institute and its front company, Materials for Palestine, which linked American fundraising to arms [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. In 1946 the Agency sought a $56 million budget [cite:djn_1946_budget]. In 1948 the United Jewish Appeal raised a record $150 million from more than a million contributors [cite:jta_uja_1948]; a June 1948 campaign advertisement called it "the official American agency for aid to Haganah" [cite:djn_1948_haganah]; Golda Meyerson's American tour raised about $50 million, largely for arms; arms were bought in Czechoslovakia; and banknotes for the new state were printed in New York. In the same year Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann raised the $100,000 that, in one historian's account, weighed on a single presidential decision.

Beneath all of this lay older foundations: the Ottoman and British layers of administration, a Christian restorationist lobby dating from 1839, and the Rothschild colonies in Palestine dating from 1882. The documented ties that bound the American part of the sequence together, organization to organization and person to person, are mapped in [scene:nw_s02|the network of the American build-up].

Two maps draw the same sequence from different angles. The first follows the institutions and the votes, from the Basel Congress through the bank and the land fund, the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate, the Jewish Agency and the American political machine, to the UN Special Committee, Resolution 181 and the state. The second follows the money, from the blue-box coins of small donors through the Colonial Trust, the land fund and Keren Hayesod to the Haavara transfer, the Sonneborn network, Golda's tour and the Czech arms.

What the Holocaust changed, and what it did not. The strongest form of the familiar account deserves to be stated plainly, because part of it is right. The Holocaust changed the urgency of the question, the politics around it and the moral case for a state. In 1945–48 the argument made to Americans rested first on refuge for some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in Europe. The money changed in scale: the United Jewish Appeal, formed in 1939, raised more than $124 million across all of 1939–45, then $100 million in 1946 alone [cite:raphael_uja], about $110 million in 1947 [cite:cambridge_solidarity] and $150 million in 1948. Much of that money was for the survivors themselves: $64.9 million of the 1948 campaign went to the Joint Distribution Committee for relief in Europe [cite:jta_uja_1948]. What the record does not support is the step from "the Holocaust changed everything" to "the Holocaust created the state." The bank, the land fund, the recognized agency, the American fundraising and lobbying machinery, the congressional endorsement and the defense levy all existed before 1939. The arms channels were assembled in 1945 by people and networks that were already in place. The catastrophe gave the apparatus its urgency; it did not build the apparatus.

Nor does the record support the opposite overstatement, that the sequence was a single plan executed on schedule. Each step was contingent, and each met opposition. Edmond de Rothschild, who funded the early colonies, opposed Herzl. In Washington the State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense opposed the policy in writing, the CIA warned that American backing would harm American interests, and all were overruled. Britain spent about £100 million on its forces in Palestine in 1945–47 alone, with some 100,000 troops deployed, before giving up the Mandate [cite:hmg_termination_1948][cite:hansard_1947_08]. What the documents show is accumulation, not inevitability: institutions built early, and kept running, so that when the political moment came the instruments to use it already existed.

What 1948–1953 added. The state that was declared on 14 May 1948 inherited this apparatus and, within five years, built a second layer on top of it. This chapter has told each part in its place; seen together, they complete the architecture.

The first addition was territorial. The four armistice agreements of 1949 fixed the lines the war had produced. They were not borders by their own terms: Article V of the Egyptian agreement says the demarcation line "is not to be construed in any sense as a political or territorial boundary" [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text]. But they enclosed what Israel's Foreign Ministry describes as about 78 percent of the Mandate area [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map], against the 56 percent the 1947 plan had allotted. How those lines were drawn and supervised is told in [scene:rd6_armistices|the armistice agreements].

The second addition was legal title to land, and here the pre-state instrument and the new state's law met directly. A Custodian of Abandoned Property was appointed on 15 July 1948; emergency regulations in December 1948 transferred control to a Custodian of Absentee Property; and on 14 March 1950 the Knesset passed the Absentees' Property Law [cite:rs_rd6_fischbach_2003][cite:un_absentees_property_law_text]. A Development Authority established in 1950 became, in the words of a 2016 study, both a major land allocator and the body that set "the rules of the game" [cite:rs_rd6_jih_devauthority]. According to an analysis published by BADIL, the 1950 law let the Custodian pass absentee land to the Development Authority and on to the Jewish National Fund; nearly 2.4 million dunams were transferred, "more than trebling the JNF's holdings as of 1941," and by 1951 the government and the Fund together held 92 percent of the land within the state [cite:rs_rd6_badil_landregime]. Those are BADIL's figures, and the scholarly sources in this chapter do not give a settled total of their own. According to the same analysis, the Land Acquisition Law of 1953 then retroactively validated seizures made during and after the war [cite:rs_rd6_badil_landregime]. The land fund founded in 1901 to buy land and never sell it had held about 936,000 dunams by 1947. After 1948 it also became a receiver, by sale and transfer from the state, of land that its owners had never sold.

The third addition defined who belonged. Section 1 of the Law of Return, dated 5 July 1950, states that "Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh" [cite:rs_rd6_mjl_lawofreturn]. Presenting it, Ben-Gurion said the right "preceded the State of Israel," and the Encyclopaedia Judaica links the law to the Basel Program of 1897, Article 6 of the Mandate of 1922 and the Declaration of Independence [cite:rs_rd6_ej_lawofreturn]. The reference work itself draws the line from the state's immigration statute back to the first step of the sequence above. The Nationality Law, in force from 14 July 1952, made every immigrant under the Law of Return a national "by return," while former Palestinian citizens could become nationals "by residence" only if they met a registration test tied to 1 March 1952 [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_nationality]. In the same years, Arab citizens lived under a military government established in October 1948 and administered through the British Defence (Emergency) Regulations of 1945 [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah].

The fourth addition was money, and its sources shifted. Private American giving remained, but now beside it came a state's own instruments. The United States government gave $100.0 million in 1949, through the Export-Import Bank and other channels, then nothing in 1950, $35.1 million in 1951 and $86.4 million in 1952, all of it economic [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_usaid_table]. Israel Bonds, launched in the United States in 1951, sold more than $52 million in their first year and within six years were financing 35 percent of the state's special development budget [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_bonds]. And the reparations agreement with West Germany, signed in Luxembourg on 10 September 1952, paid in oil and German goods that made up 12 to 14 percent of Israel's annual imports over the following decade [cite:rs_rd6_nli_reparations][cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations]. Here the Holocaust entered the architecture directly: Sharett's claim of $1.5 billion was calculated as the cost of absorbing 500,000 Nazi victims at $3,000 each [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations]. The agreement's total value is given differently in two sources, and the Knesset vote to negotiate, 61 to 50 on 9 January 1952 with protesters outside, shows how divided the new state's own public was [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_reparations]. These are told in full in [scene:rd6_financing|how the new state was paid for]. On the American side, the lobbying council of 1943 was renamed the American Zionist Council in 1949 [cite:azec_powerbase], and in 1953–54 Isaiah Kenen founded a separate American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs, renamed AIPAC in 1959 by one account, while another dates AIPAC's emergence to a restructuring in 1963 [cite:cjh_kenen_papers][cite:aipac_wiki].

The architecture as it stood in 1953 can be drawn in one figure. Its center was a sovereign state with a parliament and a government. Around it were the four instruments the state had built in its first five years, and beyond it the three outside channels that paid for it. Two of the four inside instruments, the land regime and the Law of Return, connected directly back to institutions or principles laid down before 1948, and the diaspora channel ran partly through an organization that had existed since 1943.

The finding of this volume, stated at its documented size, is this. Israel was not improvised in two years. The bank, the land fund, the recognized agency, the American fundraising and lobbying machinery, the congressional endorsement and the arms channels were assembled over five decades, and the state's first laws on land and immigration were built on them. The Holocaust changed the urgency, the politics and the moral case; it did not create the apparatus. None of the steps listed here is an inference. Each is sourced where it is told. What a synthesis adds is the order, and the order is the argument.

Some things the order cannot show. It does not show that any single person or body designed the whole, and the documents point the other way: different founders, different decades, different organizations. It does not give an audited total of the money raised before 1948, because none exists, and the Haganah's full budget is not documented. It cannot establish whether money reached individual members of Congress before 1948, because the disclosure records that would show it in either direction were never required. And it does not settle what the armistice lines would become; in 1953 they were, by their own text, still not borders.
