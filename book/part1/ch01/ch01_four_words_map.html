The Four Words on the Map

The previous section separated the four words by meaning. Each also has a geography, and a quick way to see the difference between them is to put them on a map. A common shorthand map of Zionism shades all of Europe in one colour and draws arrows from it to Palestine. Part of that picture is right: the movement's ideas and its organization were mostly born in Central and Eastern Europe. Most of the rest is wrong. Its earliest British supporters were Christians, one of its first groups of settlers came from Yemen, and when Jews left Eastern Europe in their millions, the great majority went west, not to Palestine.

Where the movement began

The first person on this map is not Jewish. In the late 1830s Lord Shaftesbury, an English evangelical, repeatedly lobbied the Foreign Secretary, Lord Palmerston, to encourage a Jewish return to the land, and in 1838 Britain appointed a consul in Jerusalem. [cite:fwm_wiki_cz_uk] The Jewish forerunners came next: Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai at Semlin, near Belgrade, in 1843, and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer at Thorn, today Toruń in Poland, whose book Derishat Zion appeared in 1862. [cite:fw_jvl_alkalai] [cite:fwm_wiki_kalischer]

The pogroms of 1881 moved the idea from books into organizations. In January 1882 students in Kharkov founded Bilu, and fourteen of its members landed at Jaffa on 6 July 1882. [cite:fwm_jvl_bilu] Leon Pinsker, a physician in Odessa, published Auto-Emancipation the same year. [cite:fw_brit_pinsker] In November 1884 thirty-two delegates of the Lovers of Zion societies, twenty-two of them from Russia, met at Kattowitz, then in Germany, with Pinsker in the chair, and based their central committee in Odessa. [cite:fwm_wiki_katowice] Vienna supplied the name and the program: Nathan Birnbaum coined the word "Zionism" there in 1890, and Theodor Herzl, a Viennese journalist, wrote Der Judenstaat in 1896. [cite:fw_jvl_birnbaum] [cite:fw_wiki_judenstaat] In August 1897 the first Zionist Congress, meeting in Basel, founded the Zionist Organization. [cite:fw_wiki_firstcongress]

Settlers did not come only from Europe. A few hundred Jews left Sanaa and nearby towns in Yemen in 1881 and 1882, arriving about seven months before most of the Eastern Europeans; most settled in Jerusalem. Groups from Romania sailed from the Danube port of Galați and founded Zikhron Ya'akov and Rosh Pinna in 1882, the same year Rishon LeZion was founded near Jaffa. [cite:fwm_wiki_firstaliyah] [cite:fw_jvl_firstaliyah]

Where the emigrants actually went

The shorthand map implies that Europe's Jews, driven by persecution, headed for Palestine. Most did not. Between 1881 and 1914 more than two and a half million Jews left Eastern Europe. [cite:fwm_mjl_emigration] By the Posen Library's estimates, about 1.7 million went to the United States, about 300,000 to England, 100,000 to Argentina, 80,000 to France, 60,000 to Canada and 50,000 to South Africa. About 60,000 went to Palestine. [cite:fwm_posen_migration] Estimates of the first wave of settlers range from 25,000 to 60,000, and half or more of them later left again. [cite:fwm_wiki_firstaliyah] In the years the movement was being organized, Palestine received roughly one Jewish emigrant from Eastern Europe in every thirty or forty.

That is the strongest evidence that "Zionist" and "Jewish" did not mean the same thing even at the movement's birth. Millions of Jews voted with their feet for New York, London and Buenos Aires. Others argued against the movement outright, from American Reform rabbis to the socialist Bund, founded in Vilna weeks after the Basel Congress. [cite:fw_aja_pittsburgh] [cite:fw_wiki_bund]

Where Jews live today

The same separation holds now. On the Jewish Agency's 2024 figures, compiled by the demographer Sergio DellaPergola, about 7.3 million Jews lived in Israel and about 6.3 million in the United States, followed by France, Canada, Britain, Argentina, Germany, Russia and Australia. [cite:fwm_jns_2024] More than half of the world's Jews are not Israelis, and about a fifth of Israel's residents are not Jews but Arabs, as the previous section sets out.

The shorthand against the record

A map that shades one continent and draws one arrow makes a political movement look like the movement of a whole people. The record shows a movement that began among a minority, in specific cities, with specific founders, some of them Christian, and that in its first decades most of the people it claimed to speak for chose another road.
