Occupation, Apartheid, Genocide: What the Words Mean and Who Has Said What

Three words recur in nearly every public argument about Israel and the Palestinians: occupation, apartheid and genocide. Some treat them as slurs with no legal meaning; others treat all three as proven in court. Neither is accurate. Each word has a defined meaning in international law. Each has been applied by different bodies with different authority, and each has been disputed by Israel. The first step to weighing what has actually been found is to keep those bodies apart: a court, a United Nations commission, a human-rights organization and a government.

Occupation. In international law, occupation means a state's forces controlling foreign territory. In July 2024 the International Court of Justice, in an advisory opinion, advised that Israel's continued presence in the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza is unlawful and must end as rapidly as possible.[cite:icj_press_19jul24] This is a court speaking, though in the form of an advisory opinion.

Apartheid. Apartheid is a crime under international law, defined in the 1973 Apartheid Convention and in the Rome Statute. It means an institutionalized regime of systematic domination by one racial group over another. Three human-rights organizations have concluded that Israel commits apartheid: Human Rights Watch in 2021, the Israeli group B'Tselem in 2021, and Amnesty International in February 2022.[cite:fara_amnesty_explainer] The International Court of Justice, in its 2024 opinion, found that Israeli policies breach the prohibition on racial segregation and apartheid.[cite:11kbw_icj_ao] Israel rejects the apartheid label. The wording matters. The organizations concluded that apartheid is being committed; the court found a breach of the prohibition. The findings are related, but they are not the same statement.

Genocide. Under the 1948 Genocide Convention, genocide means specified acts committed with intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part. Several bodies have concluded that genocide, or acts of genocide, have occurred in Gaza. Amnesty International did so in December 2024. Human Rights Watch, also in December 2024, found acts of genocide through the deprivation of water.[cite:ips_amnesty_hrw_genocide] The Israeli organizations B'Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights–Israel reached the same conclusion in July 2025. The International Association of Genocide Scholars passed a resolution to that effect in August 2025, and a United Nations Commission of Inquiry concluded the same in September 2025.[cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] [cite:news9_un_coi]

These findings have been challenged. More than 500 scholars called on the genocide scholars' association to retract its resolution, citing how it was adopted. Israel rejects all of these findings. Most important, no court has decided the question. The case South Africa brought at the International Court of Justice is still pending. In 2024 the court issued provisional measures finding that some rights were plausibly at risk, but it did not decide whether genocide occurred.

Side by side, the record looks like this. On occupation, there is a court opinion. On apartheid, there are conclusions by three human-rights organizations and a court finding of a breach of the prohibition. On genocide, there are conclusions by human-rights organizations, a scholarly association and a United Nations commission; a public challenge from scholars; and a court case not yet decided. In every instance, Israel disputes the finding.

A court opinion, a commission's conclusion, an organization's report and a government's denial are different kinds of statement, and they carry different weight. Throughout this book each is named for what it is, so the reader can judge each on its own terms rather than receive them as one verdict.
