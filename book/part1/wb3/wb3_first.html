The Warning, and the First Settlements

The Meron opinion. In September 1967 the Prime Minister's Office asked the Foreign Ministry's legal counsel, Theodor Meron, whether Israel could settle civilians in the territories it had taken. His answer was unequivocal: "civilian settlement in the administered territories contravenes the explicit provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention." The opinion went to the justice minister. Days later the cabinet approved Prime Minister Levi Eshkol's announcement of an "outpost" at Kfar Etzion, south of Bethlehem, presented as a base of the army's Nahal corps, though it had no connection with the military. [cite:wb_gorenberg_meron]

Israel's legal position. Israel adopted a different reading of the law from Meron's. It argued that the West Bank had belonged to no recognized sovereign when it was taken in 1967, since almost no state had recognized Jordan's annexation; that the Fourth Geneva Convention, which covers the occupation of "the territory of a High Contracting Party," therefore did not apply to it as a matter of law; and that Israel would nonetheless observe its "humanitarian and customary provisions." Critics answered that the Convention protects people, not sovereign claims, and applies whenever a population falls under an army's control. [cite:wb_cow_israelview] Part Ten sets the two positions out in full.

The Allon Plan. On 27 July 1967 the minister Yigal Allon proposed that Israel keep most of the Jordan Valley, East Jerusalem and the Etzion area and offer the populated hill country to Jordan. The cabinet never formally adopted the plan, and King Hussein rejected it, but in the first decade of occupation settlement largely followed its lines: a ring of settlements along the Jordan Valley and around Jerusalem, away from the Palestinian towns. [cite:wb_wiki_allon]

Hebron. Hebron had held a Jewish community until 1929, when 67 Jews were killed by Arab rioters. [cite:wb_wiki_hebron] In 1968 a group led by Rabbi Moshe Levinger rented the city's main hotel and refused to leave, with the tacit support of Eshkol and Allon. The government moved them to a nearby army camp, and from it grew the settlement of Kiryat Arba, beside the city; from 1979 settlers moved into the old Hadassah building in the centre of Hebron itself. [cite:wb_wiki_hebron] The pattern, a settlement begun without authorization and then accepted by the government, would be repeated for decades.

The numbers. The first decade's settlement was small. By the count of the Israeli group Peace Now, 1,500 settlers lived in the West Bank outside East Jerusalem in 1972 and 4,400 in 1977. [cite:wb_peacenow_pop] By 2025 the figure was more than 529,000, with about 246,000 more in East Jerusalem. [cite:wb_wiki_popstats] Part Four follows the change that began in 1977.
