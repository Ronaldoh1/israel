Kadesh and Musketeer: The War, 29 October–22 December 1956

The war ran on two timetables. Israel's campaign in Sinai, which it called Operation Kadesh, lasted about a hundred hours. The Anglo-French operation, Musketeer, began two days after Israel's, put troops ashore only after Israel's fighting was nearly over, and ended with a cease-fire less than two days after its first troops landed; the last of its soldiers left Egypt on 22 December. The secret timetable that bound them, the Sèvres protocol, is told in the previous section. This section follows what the armies actually did, then counts the dead, as far as the sources allow.

Egypt's own long history with Gaza, which it had administered since 1948 as a military protectorate without ever annexing it, is told in Volume II, in [scene:rd6_arab_palestine|who would govern Arab Palestine]. That history is the background to one of the war's first objectives. More broadly, Suez belongs to a decade the region's political record marks as one of overturned governments and foreign intervention: Husni al-Za'im's coup in Syria on 30 March 1949, the first of three that year; the Free Officers' overthrow of King Farouk in July 1952; the removal of Iran's prime minister by the CIA and MI6 in August 1953; the invasion of Egypt in 1956; and the fall of Iraq's monarchy on 14 July 1958, which belongs to Stage Four. [cite:w_1949_syria_coup] [cite:w_1952_egypt] [cite:w_1953_coup] [cite:w_suez] [cite:w_1958_iraq] The familiar claim that the region's wars and dictatorships grew only from local culture and religion does not survive that list. In three of its five events, outside powers were involved: American officials had contacts with Za'im before his coup, the degree of their direction being debated; the CIA and MI6 organized the Iranian coup; and in 1956 two European powers invaded Egypt alongside Israel. [cite:w_1949_syria_coup] [cite:w_1953_coup] [cite:w_suez]

Kadesh: Sinai in a hundred hours. The Israel Defense Forces date the start of Operation Kadesh to 17:00 on 29 October 1956, with the first paratroop landing in Israel's military history, near the eastern approaches of the Mitla Pass. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] The historian Michael Oren writes that the landing came "at dawn"; the IDF's own time is used here. [cite:rs_cw3_oren_hnn] The drop was the "real act of war" Dayan had designed at Sèvres: deep in Sinai, close enough to the Canal to give Britain and France their pretext. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The campaign was commanded by Lieutenant General Moshe Dayan, and the IDF gives its length as 100 hours. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] The IDF and Defense Establishment Archives put it the same way: the peninsula was occupied "within 100 hours," between the war's start on 29 October and 5 November 1956. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_archive]

The fighting was not uniform. According to the US Library of Congress's country study of Egypt, Egyptian forces resisted at Abu Uwayqilah, which Israeli sources call Abu Ageila, and at the Mitla Pass. After the Anglo-French bombing of the air bases, Nasser pulled his troops out of Sinai to protect the Canal. [cite:rs_cw3_loc_egypt] Israel occupied the Gaza Strip on 2 November and Sharm el-Sheikh on 5 November 1956; these dates come from a year chronology and from Benny Morris's account of the occupation of Gaza, which has it beginning on 2–3 November. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_1956israel] [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] Article 4 of the Sèvres protocol had provided for Israel to occupy the western shore of the Gulf of Aqaba and the islands of Tiran and Sanafir, and Ben-Gurion had called the Straits of Tiran "the State of Israel's Suez Canal." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] What the occupation of Gaza meant for its people, including the killings at Khan Yunis on 3 November, is told in [scene:cw3_gaza_killings|Khan Yunis and Rafah].

The balance of forces is given differently by different sources. Lance Janda, in a reference work, puts the forces at 45,000 Israelis against 30,000 Egyptians; Ben-Gurion told the Knesset that Egypt had "over 30,000 men" in Sinai. [cite:rs_cw3_janda_ebsco] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov]

Musketeer: from the air to Port Said. General Sir Charles Keightley's despatch on Operation Musketeer, published in the London Gazette on 12 September 1957, is the British commander's own account of the operation. By his record, Royal Air Force Valiant and Canberra bombers began attacking Egyptian airfields at 16:15 GMT on 31 October. The Sèvres protocol had set the Anglo-French attack for early that day, if Egypt refused the ultimatum; the bombing began in the late afternoon. By the end of 2 November the Egyptian Air Force was, in Keightley's word, "all but" neutralized. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The landings came three days later. At 08:20 GMT on 5 November about 600 men of 3 Parachute Battalion Group jumped onto Gamil airfield, and about 460 French paratroopers dropped at Port Fuad. The seaborne assault followed at 04:50 GMT on 6 November, when Royal Marine commandos landed; 45 Commando was carried in by 22 helicopters. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley]

The ground operation was stopped almost as soon as it began. The cease-fire was ordered from London at 17:00 GMT on 6 November and took effect at 23:59 GMT that night. The force ashore nonetheless kept growing for weeks after the fighting stopped: it peaked on 1 December at 13,500 British and 8,500 French troops, some 22,000 men. The last of them were aboard ship by 17:10 GMT on 22 December 1956. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] Why London stopped, and how Washington's votes, money and oil brought it to stop, is told in [scene:cw3_un_ceasefire|Washington against its allies].

Counting the dead. No single figure for any party's losses can be given without its source, and for Egypt no official figure was found at all. The numbers below are set out with their sources and their ranges.

For Israel, the counts cluster but do not agree. The IDF gives 171 soldiers killed in battle; the Jewish Agency gives 172 IDF soldiers. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen] Ben-Gurion's first public figure, on 7 November 1956, was "about 150 killed," an early count; by 5 March 1957 he gave "170 killed and 1 prisoner." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] Janda's reference work gives 189 killed and 899 wounded. [cite:rs_cw3_janda_ebsco] The official later figures run from 170 to 172; the full range across these sources is about 150 to 189.

Egypt's losses rest entirely on foreign estimates. The Library of Congress study, citing foreign military observers, gives about 1,650 ground forces killed, about 4,900 wounded and more than 6,000 captured or missing. [cite:rs_cw3_loc_egypt] Janda estimates 1,030 killed and 4,000 wounded. [cite:rs_cw3_janda_ebsco] Keightley called Egyptian losses "much more difficult to assess," and naval-history.net, which reproduces the despatch with notes, estimates them at "well in excess of 1,000 military killed." [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] Egypt's military dead therefore lie somewhere between about 1,030 and about 1,650 on these estimates. On 27 December 1956, 5,580 Egyptian prisoners of war were exchanged for four Israelis held by Egypt. [cite:rs_cw3_sahistory_pows]

The dead of Port Said and Port Fuad are the least certain of all. According to Christopher Weeks's summary, the British estimated that 650 Egyptians died in Port Said during the invasion and occupation, and 100 more in Port Fuad; his summary leaves unclear what share were civilians. Keightley's despatch records "some 60 of the enemy" killed at Port Fuad. Egypt's letter to the United Nations of 5 November 1956 (A/3292) alleged attacks on civilians but gave no figures. [cite:rs_cw3_weeks_portsaid] [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] The civilian toll at Port Said is not settled by these sources.

For Britain and France the figures are official but changed with time. Keightley's despatch gives 16 British killed and 96 wounded, and 10 French killed and 33 wounded. On 12 December 1956 the Defence Ministry told the House of Commons that the latest figure was 21 British killed and 112 wounded; the later figure includes deaths during the occupation, among them an officer killed in an ambush in December. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] [cite:rs_cw3_hansard_12dec56]

Beaten, or withdrawn? The two sides have told the Sinai campaign differently from the start. Israel's account is that the IDF "crushed" the Egyptian army and took the peninsula in 100 hours, an account Morris's history of the occupation shares. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] Nasser's account is that Egypt had not been defeated by the Israelis but had withdrawn from Sinai to defend the Canal against the Anglo-French attack. [cite:rs_cw3_loc_egypt] Each version has evidence on its side. The speed of the Israeli advance, the 5,580 prisoners against four, and the loss of Gaza and Sharm el-Sheikh support the first. [cite:rs_cw3_sahistory_pows] The Library of Congress study supports part of the second: it records real resistance at Abu Ageila and the Mitla Pass, and it dates Nasser's pull-back to after the Anglo-French bombing of his air bases, which is to say after the war had become a war on two fronts. [cite:rs_cw3_loc_egypt] The two claims are about different things. One measures what the Israeli army achieved in Sinai; the other explains why the Egyptian army was not there to stop it. The sources used here do not show how the Sinai fighting would have gone had Egypt faced Israel alone.

The wider outcome was clearer. The invasion achieved its immediate military objectives, and it was reversed under pressure from both the United States and the Soviet Union, a pressure that ran, unusually for the Cold War, against Washington's own allies. [cite:wikipedia_suez_crisis] [cite:w_suez] That reversal is the subject of the sections that follow in this stage.

"A war deluxe." Ben-Gurion drew his own lesson within weeks, and it was a warning. At a debriefing of the senior command on 27 December 1956, the day the prisoners were exchanged, he told the officers: "this was a war deluxe." "You cannot learn from it how to fight the next war," he said, and there was "no basis to assume that in the next war, we will have helping forces from abroad." [cite:rs_cw3_idf_archive] [cite:rs_cw3_jpost_dec56] The Sinai campaign had been fought with Egypt's air bases under Anglo-French bombing and its army pulling back to the Canal. His words suggest the point he was making: that Israel's soldiers should not mistake those conditions for their own strength.

What the record leaves open. No Egyptian official count of the dead was found, so every Egyptian figure here is a foreign estimate, and the estimates differ by more than six hundred. The civilian dead of Port Said are known only through a British estimate, at second hand, that does not separate civilians from soldiers, and Egypt's own complaint to the United Nations gave no number. The two figures for Port Fuad, 100 and "some 60," cannot be reconciled from these sources. The Israeli figures differ by up to 39 between the earliest count and the highest. The hour of the Mitla drop is given as 17:00 by the IDF and as dawn by Oren. And the question of whether Egypt was beaten in Sinai or chose to leave it rests on what Egypt's army would have done without the Anglo-French attack, which the documents used here cannot show.
