This stage of the story runs from 1896 to 1917, from the publication of Theodor Herzl's Der Judenstaat to the eve of the Balfour Declaration. It covers roughly two decades in which a political program written by a Viennese journalist became a set of working institutions: a Congress with elected delegates, an executive and a council, national federations in America and Britain, a bank incorporated in London with a branch in Jaffa, and a fund that bought land in Palestine and never resold it.

The question this stage answers is when, and how, the project of a Jewish state was conceived and given structure. A common assumption places its origin in the catastrophe of the 1930s and 1940s. The dates examined here run in the other direction. By 1905 the movement had weighed Argentina, a Sinai buffer at Suez and a British offer in East Africa, and had chosen Palestine through its own contested votes.

The record of these years is also a record of argument. The Bund, Orthodox rabbis, Ahad Ha'am and the official bodies of British Jewry all opposed or contested political Zionism, and the movement split over East Africa. Meanwhile Herzl approached the Kaiser, the Sultan, Britain and the Tsar's minister in turn, seeking a charter from whichever power would grant one. Land was bought, mostly from absentee owners, and tenants who held no title paid the price. What follows traces how these pieces were assembled, and by whom.
