## 1. Getting Your Bearings

**Summary.** Before any history, the ground rules. The part sets out how the book grades evidence (documented, reported, contested, unresolved) and a three-question test for any viral claim. It then draws the map: Israel inside the 1949 Green Line, the West Bank with its Areas A, B and C, Gaza, and East Jerusalem, each under its own legal regime. It explains who "the Palestinians" are in law, about 14.8 million people in several legal situations, and who may become a citizen of Israel under the Law of Return and the Nationality Law. It closes with what "occupation," "apartheid" and "genocide" mean in international law, who has found what, and Israel's rejection of each finding.

**Script.**

Before we talk about Israel and Palestine, I want to give you the ground rules. Not my opinion. A test you can use on anything I say, and on anything anyone else says.

Here's the test. When you see a claim, ask three things. Is the fact true? Is it the whole fact, or a piece of it? And does the conclusion actually follow from it? Most viral claims about this history pass the first question and fail the third. A true fact gets attached to a conclusion it doesn't support. Keep that in your pocket.

Now the map, because most of the confusion starts here. People say "Israel" and "Palestine" as if each were one place. There are four places, and each lives under its own law.

Israel, in this book, means the state inside the 1949 armistice lines, the Green Line. East of that line is the West Bank. Jordan held it from 1948 to 1967, and Israel has occupied it since 1967. Under the Oslo II agreement of 1995 it was cut into three zones. Area A, about 18%, is under the Palestinian Authority. Area B, about 22%, is shared. Area C, about 61%, is under full Israeli control. Then there's Gaza, and then East Jerusalem, which Israel annexed. The UN Security Council called that annexation null and void, by fourteen votes to none, with the United States abstaining. The US moved its embassy there in May 2018. Four places, four legal regimes.

So who are "the Palestinians"? The Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics counted about 14.8 million worldwide in 2024. They're one people in several legal situations. Some are citizens of Israel. At the end of 2023, Israel had about 9.7 million people, and 21.3% were Arabs. Some live in the West Bank under military rule. Some live in Gaza. About 5.9 million are registered refugees. Which law applies to a Palestinian depends on where that person was born and where they live now. That's the fact you need before you can even talk about the word "apartheid."

Next, citizenship, which you can answer straight from the statute books. The Law of Return of 1950 opened the door to every Jew in the world. The Nationality Law of 1952 decided which of the Arabs who stayed in 1948 would count as citizens. Then in 2003 a measure called temporary closed the route for Palestinians from the occupied territories to join a spouse who held Israeli citizenship. It lapsed in 2021 and was passed again in March 2022, 45 votes to 15. Supporters call it security. Critics call it demography. The book gives you both, with the law's own wording.

Last, the three words everyone fights over: occupation, apartheid, genocide. One side says they're slurs with no legal meaning. The other says they've already been proven in court. Neither is right. Each word has a legal definition in a treaty or statute. On 19 July 2024, the International Court of Justice, the UN's highest court, gave an advisory opinion on Israel's occupation, and Israel rejects it. Apartheid is a crime under the 1973 Apartheid Convention and the Rome Statute. Human Rights Watch, the Israeli group B'Tselem and Amnesty International have all concluded Israel commits it, and Israel rejects that too. And genocide? The case is open. It has not been decided. Anyone who tells you it has, either way, is ahead of the court.

That's what this first part is for. Before the story starts, you know where every place is, who lives there under which law, and what each loaded word actually means. Everything that follows builds on that ground.

## 2. The Land Before the State

**Summary.** Three thousand years in brief. The part follows what the stones and ancient writers name, from the Merneptah stele (about 1207 BCE) and the kingdoms of Israel and Judah, through Rome and the name "Palestine," to Muslim, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman rule. It shows Ottoman Palestine as a named, counted and governed province, with the 1871–72 census of 381,954 residents. It describes the Old Yishuv, the Jewish community already there before Zionism (about 27,000 by 1880). It ends by separating three questions people fold into one: who lived in the land, whose connection runs back unbroken, and when a modern national identity took shape.

**Script.**

There are two lines you'll hear in every argument about this land. One says Jews were absent from it for two thousand years. The other says Palestinians only appeared in the twentieth century. Both treat the past like only one people can have one. The record supports neither.

Start with stone. In 1896 the archaeologist Flinders Petrie found a granite slab in a temple at Thebes in Egypt. It's called the Merneptah stele, from around 1207 BCE, and it names "Israel" as a people. Later inscriptions, the Tel Dan and Mesha stelae from around 840 BCE, point to the kingdoms of Israel and Judah. So there were Jewish kingdoms in the first millennium BCE. That's documented.

Then Rome. Rome took the region in 63 BCE, destroyed the Temple in 70 CE, and crushed the Bar Kokhba revolt in the 130s. After that the province was renamed Syria Palaestina. Why? Historians disagree. Louis Feldman argued in 1990 that the emperor Hadrian did it to erase Jewish national life. Other scholars push back. The book shows you both sides and doesn't pretend the debate is settled. But here's the key point: a Jewish presence survived Rome, and the name "Palestine" goes back to Greek writers like Herodotus, long before that.

Then the centuries most people skip. Byzantine rule. Then Muslim rule, with a province called Jund Filastin. Crusader kingdoms. The Mamluks in the thirteenth century. And from 1516 the Ottoman Empire, for four hundred years, until 1917. Over those centuries most of the land's people became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians. Those are the ancestors of today's Palestinians.

Now, the claim that it was empty. Under the Ottomans, Palestine was named, counted and governed. There were no borders between today's Syria, Lebanon, Jordan and Israel, because none of those states existed yet. Almost every border that matters today was drawn by Britain and France between 1916 and 1922. The Ottoman census of 1871 and 1872 counted 381,954 residents of Palestine, not counting the Bedouin. Roughly 85% Muslim, 11% Christian, 4% Jewish. That's not an empty land. That's a populated province.

And here's what people often don't know. Political Zionism didn't bring the first Jews to the land. When the first Zionist settlers arrived from 1881, they found a Jewish community already there. Historians call it the Old Yishuv. It was religious, mostly in four holy cities: Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias. By the National Library of Israel's count, there were about 7,000 Jews in the land in 1800 and about 27,000 in 1880. Many of them lived on charity from abroad and had no interest in founding a state.

So who belongs to this land? The book's answer is that the question is really three questions, and each has its own answer. First, who lived there? Both peoples, at different times and in different numbers, and that's documented. Second, whose culture and connection run back unbroken? Jewish connection runs through scripture, prayer and a presence that never fully ended. Palestinian connection runs through centuries of continuous life on the land. Third, when did a modern national identity form? For both peoples, in the modern era. A newspaper called Filastin, "Palestine," started publishing in Jaffa in 1911. Palestinian Arab congresses met from 1919 and petitioned the Paris Peace Conference. And Zionism as an organized movement started in 1897.

Two real histories. When someone tells you only one people has a past here, they're folding three questions into one to get the answer they want. Keep them apart and the record speaks for itself.

## 3. Words and Names

**Summary.** Four words people swap without noticing (Hebrew, Jewish, Israeli, Zionist) and who each one includes, with maps of where the movement began and where emigrants actually went. The part reads the name "Israel" in its first text, Genesis 32, and offers one symbolic reading of it, clearly labeled as a reading. It traces "Semitic" from a 1781 language family to "antisemitism," a word made by a Jew-hater in 1879, alongside the documented history of the hatred itself. It explains the flag's two symbols, the menorah and the Magen David. It ends with how the word "antisemitism" is used as a shield, why the charge silences people, and where the fight over its definition stands as of November 2026.

**Script.**

Four words get swapped all the time: Hebrew, Jewish, Israeli and Zionist. They don't mean the same thing. Hebrew is a language and an ancient name. Jewish names a people and a religion. Israeli is a citizenship. Zionist is a political movement. When one word gets quietly swapped for another, a statement about a state starts sounding like a statement about a people. That swap is where most of the confusion starts.

Some quick facts that break the usual picture. In Pew's 2020 survey of American Jews, 27% said they were atheist, agnostic or "nothing in particular," and still considered themselves Jewish. So Jewish isn't only a religion. Israeli isn't only Jewish: about one in five Israeli citizens is Arab. And Zionist isn't only Jewish either. Its earliest British supporters were Christians. In the late 1830s, Lord Shaftesbury, an English evangelical, lobbied the Foreign Secretary to encourage a Jewish return, and in 1838 Britain opened a consulate in Jerusalem. One of the first groups of settlers came from Yemen. And when Jews left Eastern Europe by the millions, most went west, to America, not to Palestine.

Now the name itself. Before "Israel" named a land or a state, it named one man. In Genesis 32, Jacob wrestles through the night at the ford of the Jabbok river and is renamed Israel. The scholarly reading takes the name from El, God, and a root meaning "to strive." The book also offers a symbolic reading, IS, RA, EL, and it's labeled as exactly that: a reading, not a finding. It doesn't replace the Hebrew record.

Next, "Semitic." In 1781 a German scholar, August Ludwig von Schlözer, coined the word for a family of languages: Hebrew, Arabic, Aramaic and others. It was about languages, not blood. Then race science in the 1850s turned the language family into a "race." In 1860 a Jewish scholar, Moritz Steinschneider, first wrote "antisemitic" to attack that race theory. And in 1879 a German agitator, Wilhelm Marr, made "antisemitism" the name of his own movement against Jews. So the word is a misnomer. But its meaning is not. It means hatred of Jews.

And that hatred is far older than the word. The book lays out the record plainly: the medieval blood libel, the false charge that Jews kill Christians for their blood; the Damascus affair of 1840; legal exclusion, expulsion and killing; and a forged text that kept circulating into this century. No honest conversation about the word can skip that.

The flag. Most people think the six-pointed star is the ancient sign of the Jewish people. The older Jewish symbol is the menorah, the lampstand from the Temple. The Star of David became a Jewish emblem in the Middle Ages. A blue-and-white banner with the star was made in Boston in 1885. The Zionist Congress chose it in 1897, and the new state confirmed it in 1948.

Now the hard part: the word as a shield. Let's be clear first. Hatred of Jewish people is antisemitism, full stop. A question about what a government does is a question about a government, whether it's Canada's, Mexico's or Israel's. The problem is a claim that's become common: that questioning Israel is itself antisemitic. The most cited definition is the IHRA working definition from 2016. The United States government adopted it, and as of August 2024 so had 35 states and the District of Columbia. In March 2021 more than 200 scholars of Jewish history and the Holocaust signed an alternative, the Jerusalem Declaration. In April 2023, 104 organizations asked the UN not to adopt IHRA.

Why does the charge shut people up? The book cites research showing that being shut out of a group registers in the brain a lot like physical pain. People go quiet to avoid that pain, not because they've been answered. Knowing that is how you keep thinking when it happens.

## 4. Peoples and Traditions

**Summary.** The Jewish lineages (Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi) and what the genetics shows, including a careful test of the Khazar claim and who has told that story and why. A map of some two dozen Jewish communities across three continents. The difference between language and ancestry. A thousand years of Jewish life in the Muslim world, neither utopia nor endless hatred. The land's other communities: Druze, Bedouin, Circassians, Samaritans and Palestinian Christians. The documented line between Judaism, a religion, and Zionism, a political movement founded at Basel in 1897. Dissent from within every lineage. And the Christian argument over one verse, Genesis 12:3.

**Script.**

Here's a claim you've probably seen online. Ashkenazi Jews, the Jews of Europe, aren't really from the Middle East at all. They're descended from the Khazars, a Turkic people in Central Asia who converted to Judaism. So, the argument goes, they have no real connection to the land.

The book tests that claim against the genetic record. The most complete study, by Doron Behar and colleagues, was published in Nature in 2010. It found that Jewish communities are distinct, each with its own history, but that they share a detectable common origin in the Middle East. A 2014 study modelled Ashkenazi Jews as roughly an even mix of European and Middle Eastern ancestry. The Khazar idea was argued in a 2012 paper by Eran Elhaik, and the wider genetic research doesn't support it. So the claim fails.

But the opposite claim fails too: that all Jews are genetically the same. Two thousand years in different countries left real differences. The book also asks who has told the Khazar story and why. The writer Arthur Koestler, himself of Jewish origin, argued it in 1976, hoping to undercut antisemitism. The Tel Aviv historian Shlomo Sand made it part of a wider argument in 2008. And antisemites have used the same story for the opposite reason. One claim, opposite agendas, the same evidence underneath.

Then the map. Not one diaspora moving in one direction, but some two dozen communities. Salonica, today's Thessaloniki, had about 80,000 Jews around 1900; in 1943 the Germans deported more than 45,000 of them to Auschwitz. Baghdad had about 50,000 Jews in 1900, more than a quarter of the city.

That brings up a big myth: that Jews and Arabs have always hated each other. For more than a thousand years, Jewish communities lived across the Muslim world, in Baghdad, Cairo, Damascus, Fez and Casablanca. The record shows real violence and real legal inequality, and also deep intellectual, commercial and civic collaboration. Not a utopia. Not an ancient, permanent war either.

The land also has other communities that don't fit "Jews versus Muslims." The Druze, whose men were included in Israel's military draft in 1956. The Bedouin, whose relationship with the state turns on land. Circassians from the Caucasus. The Samaritans, a few hundred people. And Palestinian Christians, members of some of the oldest Christian communities on earth.

Now the most important line in the whole volume. Judaism is a religion and a people, thousands of years old. Zionism is a political movement with a place and a date of birth: Basel, Switzerland, 29 to 31 August 1897, convened by Theodor Herzl, a secular journalist from Vienna. Jews themselves drew that line. Twelve years before Basel, in 1885, American Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform said Jews were a religious community, not a nation. The Jewish Labour Bund, founded in Vilna in 1897, opposed Zionism from the left. Religious groups opposed it on religious grounds. Mizrahi scholars have critiqued it from their own history. Dissent came from every lineage, not from a fringe.

And Christians? There's a long argument among Christians over one verse, Genesis 12:3, spoken to Abraham: "I will bless those who bless you." Christian Zionists say it requires supporting the state. Other Christians say the promise applies to all who share Abraham's faith. That's a debate inside Christianity, and the book lays out both sides. In 1904, when Herzl asked Pope Pius X for support, the Pope said no.

So when someone says criticizing Zionism means attacking a people, remember: the line between the two was drawn by Jews, on the record, from the very beginning.

## 5. Two Displacements

**Summary.** Two great movements of people, each told on its own terms. About 850,000 Jews left Arab and Muslim countries between 1948 and 1972, country by country, for several documented causes at once: persecution by Arab governments, organized recruitment by Israel and the Jewish Agency, and in one Egyptian case, sabotage by Israeli military intelligence. In the war of 1947–49, roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians were displaced, the Nakba. The part tests the claim that the two were an even population exchange, and shows why the documents don't support it.

**Script.**

You've probably heard this one. In 1948 Arabs left Palestine, and around the same time Jews left Arab countries. So it was a fair swap. A population exchange. Case closed.

Both displacements are real. Both were grievous for the people who lived them. But the "even exchange" doesn't hold up, and this part of the book shows why.

Start with the Jews of the Arab world. Between 1948 and 1972, roughly 850,000 Jews left Arab and Muslim countries, from about two dozen states over about 24 years. The usual story is one sentence: Jews were persecuted in Arab lands until Israel gave them refuge. That sentence holds real history, and it leaves out most of it.

The documents show several causes working at once. One is violence and legal persecution by Arab governments. In Baghdad, on 1 and 2 June 1941, during the festival of Shavuot, crowds turned on the city's Jews. That's the Farhud. Note the date: 1941. It can't have been a reaction to 1948. In Libya, under fascist Italian rule, about 2,584 Jews were deported to a camp at Giado by June 1942, and 562 died there.

A second cause is organized recruitment by Israel and the Jewish Agency, with named operations. And a third, in one specific Egyptian case, is sabotage carried out by Israeli military intelligence. All three are on the record, side by side.

And arriving in Israel wasn't the end of the story. Many were put in transit camps. The book follows that too, country by country: Iraq, Yemen, Morocco, Libya, Egypt and the rest, each with its own dates, laws and violence. There's even an argument over how many left and whether to call them refugees, and the book lays out both sides.

Now the Palestinians. In the war of 1947 to 1949, roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians were displaced. A UN estimate puts it at about 711,000. Palestinians call it the Nakba, the catastrophe. In December 1948 the UN resolved that refugees who wished to return should be allowed to. Most never were.

So why isn't it an even exchange? Look at the shape of each. The Palestinian displacement was concentrated: about 700,000 people within one war of about eighteen months. The Jewish departures were spread across two dozen countries and twenty-four years, with many different causes in each place.

And the people weren't exchanged. The Jews who left Iraq didn't get Palestinian homes as a deal between governments, and Palestinian refugees didn't get Jewish homes in Baghdad. No treaty set up a swap. The phrase "population exchange" came later, as a way to cancel one account against the other.

Here's the real point. Setting one suffering against the other, to make both disappear, is a way of not looking at either. The documents treat them as two separate events, each with its own causes, its own victims and its own unfinished questions: property, compensation, return. You can hold both at once. In fact, you have to, if you want the record and not a slogan.

## 6. Who Holds the Pen

**Summary.** Who decides what a child reads about this history. The part starts with Robert Maxwell, the press baron with a documented relationship to Israel who co-owned a major American school-book operation at the end of the 1980s, and keeps the fact separate from what it proves. It follows who owns the textbook houses now (private-equity deals of $250 million, $4.5 billion and $2.8 billion), how America scatters the decision across state boards and organized reviewers, and how Israel, Germany and the Palestinian territories each hold the pen differently. It shows why the American system hasn't changed in eighty years, the new state laws of 2023–26, and how states, Israel among them, write their founding story into law.

**Script.**

Somebody chose every sentence about Israel and Palestine in your kid's textbook. This part of the book asks who.

Start with one man. Robert Maxwell was born Jan Ludvik Hoch in 1923, in a village then in Czechoslovakia. Most of his family was killed in the Holocaust. He escaped, joined the British Army, won the Military Cross in 1945 and took an English name. He built Pergamon Press into a major publisher, then a media empire. And for about three years at the end of the 1980s, through a 1989 venture with McGraw Hill, he co-owned one of the largest school-book operations in the United States. He also had a long, documented relationship with the State of Israel.

That's the record. What does it prove? On its own, nothing about what any textbook said. The book keeps those two apart, first line to last. Ownership is a fact. Influence over content needs its own evidence.

But the structure that made it possible is still here. The American school-book market is dominated by a few big firms, and they're bought and sold like any other business, with no public review of who's buying. In 2019 Pearson sold its US school business to a private investment firm for $250 million; it's now called Savvas. In 2021 Platinum Equity bought McGraw Hill for $4.5 billion. In 2022 Veritas Capital bought Houghton Mifflin Harcourt for $2.8 billion. Private equity owns a large share of what American kids read.

So who decides the content? Federal law actually forbids Washington from controlling curriculum or picking textbooks. So the decision is scattered across states. Texas matters most, because it's a huge buyer. Its State Board of Education has fifteen elected members. In 2015 it voted against having independent experts check textbooks for factual errors. And outside groups review the books and comment. One is the Institute for Curriculum Services, founded in 2005 by the Jewish Community Relations Council in San Francisco.

Other countries do it differently. In Israel, the education ministry decides directly, so the story of 1948 taught to Arab pupils changes with the minister. In 2007 a minister approved a sixth-grade book for Arab schools that used the word Nakba. In 2009 his successor removed it. Germany decides by consensus across its states. And Palestinian textbooks are paid for, and can be stopped, by foreign donors.

Why doesn't the American system change? In the 1930s the most popular social studies books were by Harold Rugg, who showed America's weaknesses as well as its strengths. In 1939 an advertising federation resolved to act against them, and by 1940 they'd been banned in about half a dozen school systems. Publishers learned fear. The historian Frances FitzGerald traced that lesson in 1979, in her book America Revised.

Since 2023, the pressure is being written into law. Virginia put the IHRA definition of antisemitism into state law in 2023, with its examples about Israel. Other states followed.

And this isn't only an American story. Many states guard their founding story with law. In March 2011 the Knesset passed what's called the Nakba Law, 37 votes to 25. It lets the state cut funding to bodies that mark Israel's independence day as a day of mourning. Turkey prosecutes people who call the killing of Armenians in 1915 a genocide. Israel's version is one case of a common pattern.

So next time you open a textbook, ask the question this part asks: who held the pen, and who paid for it?

## 7. How Jewish Students Are Taught

**Summary.** How American Jewish children meet Israel: about 900 day schools with nearly 300,000 pupils, plus Hebrew schools; summer camps staffed by Israeli emissaries sent by the Jewish Agency (a record 2,050 in 2025); and Birthright, the free ten-day trip offered since 1999. The part shows how the system is designed to build attachment first and complexity later, if at all, and what a generation taught this way now says was missing, from the 2018 "You Never Told Me" campaign to the walk-offs and the shift in surveys.

**Script.**

This book keeps repeating one line: being Jewish and supporting a political movement are not the same thing. So this part asks where, in a Jewish kid's education, those two get joined together.

American Jewish children are taught in two kinds of schools. Day schools are full-time Jewish private schools. The last national count, for the 2018 to 2019 school year, found a little over 900 of them, with nearly 300,000 pupils, in at least 37 states. Hebrew schools are part-time, usually after school or on Sundays. Both have to hold together families with very different views. So the topics that divide the community, like a full account of 1948 or the occupation, are the ones a school has the strongest reason to leave out.

Then there's summer camp, which for many kids is the most powerful Jewish education of all. Every summer the Jewish Agency for Israel, the same body that organized Jewish immigration before 1948 and still works closely with the Israeli government, sends young Israelis to work at North American camps. They're called shlichim, Hebrew for "emissaries." In 2025 it sent a record 2,050 of them to 190 camps. The agency says they reached more than 150,000 campers, staff and community members.

Then, at eighteen, the trip. Birthright Israel has offered a free ten-day trip to young Jewish adults since 1999. The route follows a recognizable sequence: the mystical town of Tzfat, the Golan Heights with a lookout over the 1973 battlefields, a lecture on geopolitics, Jerusalem, the Western Wall. It's designed to build a bond. What it mostly leaves out is the Palestinian side of the same land.

Put it together and you get a design: attachment first, complexity later, if ever. That isn't a secret plot. Schools and camps want kids to love their heritage. The question is what happens when those kids grow up and meet the fuller record.

Many of them say the same thing: they weren't told. In 2018 the group IfNotNow launched a campaign called "You Never Told Me." It gathered former campers and day-school students who said they'd never learned about the Nakba or the occupation. That May, it ran a day-long training in Boston for about a dozen counselors from eight camps, on how to bring Palestinian perspectives into their bunks. The same summer, some Birthright participants walked off the trip to go and see what was missing.

Surveys show the shift. Younger American Jews hold very different views from their parents and grandparents on Israel. A 2021 poll of 800 Jewish voters for the Jewish Electorate Institute found striking numbers among younger voters on how they describe Israel's policies.

This part isn't about blaming parents or teachers. It's about seeing the system clearly. When a child is taught that loving a people means supporting a state, the two get welded together. Later, when the adult questions the state, it can feel like betraying the people, and like betraying their own childhood. Knowing how that weld was made is the first step to being able to hold both: love for your people, and an honest look at a government.

## 8. The Same Event, Nation by Nation

**Summary.** The same history, told differently around the world. A survey of how governments read it, then the Holocaust in the world's classrooms (a 2015 UNESCO-backed study of 272 curricula from 135 countries). Germany, teaching through responsibility, its Staatsräson, and what happened when October 2023 reached its schoolyards. Israel's four school systems. Palestinian classrooms under three authorities. The Arab states, whose curricula followed their peace treaties and the 2020 Abraham Accords. Britain, France, India, Latin America and East Asia, where the fight is often over a single word, a guidance letter or a bestseller.

**Script.**

The version of this history you learned is one of many. Every country reads the same events through its own past. This part of the book goes around the world and asks: what does each country teach?

Start with the Holocaust, because many countries teach this region through it. In 2015 a study for UNESCO by the Georg Eckert Institute looked at 272 school curricula from 135 countries, and 89 textbooks. The finding: the Holocaust is a required topic in some systems, a passing mention in others, and framed differently almost everywhere. Chinese textbooks, for instance, use words like "genocide" and apply the same language to the Nanjing massacre of 1937.

Germany carries this history with the most weight. On 18 March 2008, Angela Merkel told the Knesset that Germany's historic responsibility is part of its Staatsräson, its reason of state. Every one of Germany's sixteen states anchors the Holocaust in its history curriculum. Then came October 2023. After the Hamas attack, state ministries sent guidance to schools. A Palestinian pupil in Berlin could find a family symbol treated as a possible sign of support for the attack. And in August 2025, Chancellor Merz halted some arms exports that could be used in Gaza.

Israel itself runs four school systems: state secular, state religious, ultra-Orthodox and Arab. Each teaches the founding differently. The most cited critical study of Israeli schoolbooks is by the Israeli scholar Nurit Peled-Elhanan, from 2012.

Palestinian children have no single, sovereign school system at all. Depending on where they live, their curriculum is set by the Palestinian Authority, taught by a UN agency, or increasingly replaced by Israel's. The UN agency, UNRWA, educates more than 540,000 children in about 700 schools, according to a January 2026 US Government Accountability Office report. Before 7 October 2023 it ran 288 schools in Gaza with more than 298,000 pupils. Formal schooling there stopped that day. Every layer is fought over, and every layer is paid for or permitted by someone else.

In the Arab world, curricula followed diplomacy. Egypt made peace in 1979. The United Arab Emirates normalized relations under the Abraham Accords in 2020, and in January 2023 its embassy confirmed that Holocaust education would be added to its curriculum. But here's a catch the book points out: almost everything known in detail about Arab textbook changes comes from two Israeli sources. So their findings are reported as theirs.

In Britain, teachers have a legal duty of political impartiality, and 2022 government guidance named the Israeli-Palestinian conflict as an example. In France, a 2026 dispute erupted when a revision guide described the people killed on 7 October as "more than 1,200 Jewish settlers" and didn't call Hamas a terrorist group. In India, a 2022 revision removed a chapter on "Central Islamic Lands" from an eleventh-grade history book.

And then there's the bookshop. In parts of the world far from the region, popular books shape what adults believe long after school. One conspiracy bestseller in China sold more than a million copies by 2011, its sequel more than two million, and senior officials were reported to have read it.

The pattern is the same everywhere: whoever controls the gate decides the story. A ministry, an exam board, a publisher, or a donor. Once you see that, you stop asking "what's the true version I was taught?" and start asking "who chose the version I was taught?"

## 9. How the Story Is Told

## 10. After the Flag: The Ground Since 1948

**Summary.** How the land, the people and the law changed from 1948 to 2026. The land: the 1949 armistice lines, Jordan's rule over the West Bank to 1967, the six days of June 1967, Sinai returned in 1979, East Jerusalem annexed in 1980, the Oslo areas, the 2005 withdrawal from Gaza's settlements. The people: roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians displaced, UN Resolution 194, UNRWA from 1950, Arab citizens under military government until 1966, the Absentees' Property Law, Kafr Qasim. And a single timeline from 1948 to 2026, each line told in full in a later volume and shown in red on the network map.

**Script.**

Everything in this volume so far is the ground before the flag. This last part asks what happened to that ground after 1948: the land, the people and the law.

Start with the land. When the war of 1948 ended, the armistice agreements of 1949 left Israel inside lines that became its de facto border, the Green Line. Iraq, alone among the Arab states that fought, never signed one. Jordan held the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Egypt held Gaza. Then, in June 1967, in six days, Israel took Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights.

Some of that changed hands again. Israel gave Sinai back to Egypt under the peace of 1979. In 1980 it passed a Basic Law declaring all of Jerusalem its capital, and the UN Security Council declared that null and void. In the 1990s the Oslo agreements created the Palestinian Authority and split the West Bank into Areas A, B and C. In 2005 Israel left Gaza's settlements, while keeping control of its borders, its airspace and its coast.

Now the people. In the war of 1947 to 1949, roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians were displaced. In December 1948 the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 194, saying refugees who wished to return and live in peace should be permitted to. They weren't. On 1 May 1950 a new UN agency, UNRWA, began operating and took over the refugee camps. Today about 5.9 million Palestinians are registered refugees.

The Arabs who stayed inside Israel became citizens. But they lived under military government until 1966. The Absentees' Property Law of 1950 took the property even of people who had stayed in the country but had been away from their homes during the war. Some became "present absentees": citizens living a few miles from homes they weren't allowed to reclaim. In 1956, at the village of Kafr Qasim, Israeli border police shot dead 49 villagers returning from their fields after a curfew they hadn't been told about.

And the law kept changing. The citizenship laws of the 1950s. The family-unification ban of 2003, renewed in 2022. The Nation-State Law of 2018, which named Hebrew "the state's language" and gave Arabic only a "special status."

The book puts it all on one timeline, from 1948 to 2026. And 2023 onward: the Hamas attack of 7 October and the war in Gaza.
