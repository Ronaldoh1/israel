A Palestinian Answer at the UN: Ahmad Shukairy, 14 November 1963

Most of the documents of 1963 that this stage has followed were written in Washington, Jerusalem and Cairo, by governments. On 14 November 1963 a Palestinian spoke for himself in a United Nations committee room, and his words survive in the official summary record. Ahmad Shukairy's statement to the Special Political Committee is one of the few places in this year's record where the refugees' case was put in a Palestinian's own terms, and it is set down here in those terms, with his figures and his characterizations marked as his.

Who he was. Shukairy came to the committee with a long diplomatic career behind him. He had been appointed an assistant secretary-general of the Arab League on 2 March 1951. From the autumn of 1957 until 1962 he served Saudi Arabia as its minister of state for United Nations affairs and its permanent representative to the UN. [cite:rs_cw5_palquest_shuqayri] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_shukeiri] In 1963 Ahmad Hilmi Abd al-Baqi, who had represented Palestine at the Arab League, died. The Arab Higher Committee nominated Shukairy to succeed him, and the Council of the Arab League chose him as Palestine's representative at the League. The sources give the year of that choice, not the date. [cite:rs_cw5_palquest_shuqayri] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_shukeiri] He had spent five years speaking at the UN for a sovereign state; by November 1963 he held a mandate from the Arab League as Palestine's representative there.

The meeting. On 14 November 1963 the Special Political Committee of the eighteenth General Assembly took up the annual report of the Commissioner-General of the United Nations Relief and Works Agency, UNRWA. Shukairy spoke at the committee's 407th meeting as spokesman for the group named in document A/SPC/89. [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407] The record is a summary record, not a verbatim transcript, and the words quoted below are the summary's.

The refugees' condition. Shukairy began with the agency rather than against it. He credited the Commissioner-General, Davis, and his predecessors with giving "a true picture of the distressing condition" of the refugees. He said they lived on about six cents a day, and he told the committee that UNRWA's objectives had not been met. [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407] He put the refugee population at 1,120,170 as of June 1963. That is his figure, given in his statement; the summary does not record its source, and it is reported here as he gave it. [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407]

Property, and the word he used. His central demand was the restoration of property. He argued that Arab property should be returned to its owners. To make the case that the refugees had gained nothing from what they had lost, he cited figures published by the Jewish Agency in May 1951 on the revenue from Arab property, and argued that little of it had reached the refugees. He called the seizures "genocide." [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407] The word is his charge, made in a political forum; the summary record reports it as his, and this book does so too. The underlying claim he was making, that property belonging to the refugees was producing revenue in which they did not share, rested in his telling on the Jewish Agency's own figures; the summary reached here does not reproduce those figures, and they cannot be checked from it.

His use of the Fulbright inquiry. Shukairy then turned to Washington. He cited the Senate Foreign Relations Committee's inquiry of August 1963 as showing that the American Zionist Council had received funds from Israel to "combat Arab propaganda," and he urged member states to outlaw such campaigns. [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407] The record does not verify that characterization. The part of the Senate hearings he was referring to concerned the activities of the Jewish Agency for Israel and related lobbying organizations, one of thirteen parts of a wider inquiry into the agents of foreign principals in the United States. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] What those hearings established about the Agency's payments to the Council, and how large they were, was in dispute then and is in dispute now; the evidence is set out in [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the committee].

His reading had a direct counterpart from the other side, given three months earlier. In August 1963 Dr Emanuel Neumann, chairman of the Jewish Agency–American Section, told the Jewish Telegraphic Agency that Senator Fulbright had praised the Agency's achievements and said repeatedly that there was "no wrong doing," and that the only question left open was technical: whether the Agency's periodic reports were detailed enough. Neumann called the outcome a vindication: "a prolonged and meticulous examination has turned up absolutely nothing damaging to the status of the Jewish Agency." [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630816] Each man was describing the same hearings, and each was a party to the question. Neither account is confirmed by the hearing record in the sources reached for this section.

The American reply. The United States representative, recorded as Mr Cook, answered briefly. He said that Shukairy had made many misstatements and had been abusive, that "personal insult did no service to the Palestine refugees," and that he would not respond further. [cite:rs_cw5_un_spc407] The summary does not say which statements he considered misstatements, or what he took to be insulting. His refusal to engage point by point left Shukairy's figures and charges unanswered on the record of that meeting, and it also left the American government's own objections unspecified. Whether Israel's delegation answered at the same meeting is not in the material reached here.

Why the meeting matters. The exchange shows the structure within which a Palestinian claim could be made in 1963. The refugees' case reached the General Assembly through a committee reviewing a relief agency's report; it was made by a man whose standing came from a mandate of the Arab League, not from a Palestinian state or an elected body; and the United States answered it, on the record of that meeting, with a brief dismissal. Shukairy, for his part, drew the American controversy over the foreign-agents law into the UN, and used a domestic Senate inquiry as evidence for a charge against Israel before an international audience. The two readings of that inquiry, his and Neumann's, show how quickly a hearing whose published record was still being compiled in late 1963 had become an argument abroad. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26]

Within weeks his role changed. The Cairo summit of January 1964 authorized him to prepare the First Palestine National Congress, which met in Jerusalem from 28 May to 2 June 1964, created the Palestine Liberation Organization, and elected him chairman of its Executive Committee. [cite:rs_cw5_palquest_shuqayri] That story belongs to the next stage, in [scene:cw6_summits_plo|the summits and the PLO].

The record leaves several things open. The day on which the Arab League chose him as Palestine's representative in 1963 is not established. The source of his figure of 1,120,170 refugees, and the content of the Jewish Agency's 1951 figures that he cited, are not given in the summary record. The summary record condenses his speech; it is not his own text. And his reading of the Fulbright inquiry, like Neumann's opposite one, is a party's characterization; neither is settled by the documents reached for this section.
