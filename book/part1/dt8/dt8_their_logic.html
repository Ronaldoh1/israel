Their Logic, Held to the Same Standard

Thiel, Yarvin and Land do not make one argument against democracy; they make several, in different registers. Thiel's 2009 essay was a statement of lost faith: he no longer believed "that freedom and democracy are compatible," he named the extension of the vote to women in 1920 and the growth of welfare as turning points, and he proposed escape from politics into cyberspace, outer space and the sea. [cite:de3_cato_thiel] On 1 May he added that he did not think "any class of people should be disenfranchised," but had "little hope that voting will make things better." [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] Yarvin, in 2025, said democracy was not bad so much as "very weak," that he did not "believe in actually voting at all," and that voting provides "this almost pornographic stimulus." [cite:de1_nyt_interview] [cite:de1_audit_nyt_voting] [cite:de35b_nyt] Land's serial of 2012 was the bluntest: for the hardcore neoreactionaries, he wrote, democracy "is not merely doomed, it is doom itself," and "winning elections is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying." [cite:de1_land_gwern] [cite:de7_land_de] This section sets out the logic those statements share and tests each step against the record assembled in the previous parts.

The argument in four steps

Stripped to its structure, the case runs as follows. The United States is a democracy. It performs badly. Therefore democracy performs badly. Therefore voting should give way to ownership, a state run by a chief executive answerable to its shareholders, in which, in Land's summary, residents have "no voice" and "free exit." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Each step is a separate claim, and they do not stand or fall together.

The second step is the critics' strongest ground, and [scene:dt1_where_they_are_right|Part One] granted most of it. The break comes at the first and third steps. The candidate with fewer votes nationwide won the presidency in 2000 and 2016; the Supreme Court has held that partisan gerrymanders are beyond the reach of the federal courts; corporations may spend without limit on independent political advocacy; and the First Amendment does not protect users against the platforms that carry most political speech. [cite:dt2_brit_splits] [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt5_lii_halleck] Each is a documented design fact. None proves that the system would perform better without it, but together they mean that the system's failures cannot be charged to democracy without first asking how much of the system is democratic. The argument's conclusion assumes the answer to that question.

What the texts do not address

In the texts this library has examined, Thiel's essay and its follow-up, Land's serial, and Yarvin's interviews of 2025, none discusses ranked-choice voting, the popular-vote compact, proportional representation or public financing. [cite:de3_cato_thiel] [cite:de35b_land_ebook] [cite:de1_nyt_interview] That is a statement about the material reviewed, not about everything they have written. What the texts do address is money, and there they agree with the reformers about the mechanism while proposing the opposite remedy: Land's summary of the neocameral transition has "the entire social landscape of political bribery" mapped and converted into "fungible shares" in the state. [cite:de7_land_de] The reformer proposes to shrink what money can buy; the neocameralist proposes to give those who already buy influence formal title. The comparison is drawn in full in [scene:de7_the_other_diagnosis|The Other Diagnosis].

The mirror problem

This book's title makes a claim that has an exact mirror in the texts it answers. The passage Land quotes on the neocameral state concedes that "the full neocameralist approach has never been tried." [cite:de8_land_de] To say that democracy has not been tried is to make the same kind of claim from the other side, and it carries the same weakness: an argument that the real thing has never been attempted can excuse any failure, because every failure can be blamed on the attempt not being real enough.

The way out is to make the claim checkable, and the record allows that in a way the neocameral design does not. Pieces of a fuller democracy have been built and measured: ranked-choice counting in New York City, Maine and Alaska, small-donor matching in New York, vouchers in Seattle, independent commissions in Arizona and California. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] [cite:dt7_malbin_parrott] [cite:dt7_cascade_seattle] [cite:dt7_bp_airc] The neocameral state's central mechanism, a board holding keys to disarm the state's weapons, has never been built anywhere. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] If this book's thesis is right, the pieces should show measurable gains where they run: more competitive races, a broader base of donors, winners backed by majorities. Part Seven found such gains, and found them small. [cite:dt7_nelson_ps] [cite:dt7_malbin_parrott] [cite:dt6_burnett_kogan] That is a partial confirmation and a warning at once.

What other democracies show

If incomplete democracy were the whole explanation, the more complete democracies would be the more contented ones. The record is mixed. In Pew's survey of 16 high-income countries in February to May 2026, 69 per cent of Americans were dissatisfied with how their democracy was working, against a median of 54 per cent. Dissatisfaction was 24 per cent in Sweden, 40 in Germany, 43 in Canada and 44 in the Netherlands; it was also 62 per cent in Italy, 63 in Israel, 68 in Spain, 69 in France and 77 in Greece. [cite:dt8_pew_intl2026] Israel elects its parliament from nationwide party lists, with a threshold of 3.25 per cent of the vote, a system far more proportional than the American one, and its dissatisfaction matched the American level within six points. [cite:us3_taub_threshold] [cite:dt8_pew_intl2026]

The same survey shows how much satisfaction moves with events and with who holds power. In South Korea it rose from 28 to 61 per cent in a year, after a president was impeached and an early election held. In the United States the fall from 37 to 30 per cent was, in Pew's words, "largely driven by Republicans," whose satisfaction dropped from about six in ten to 48 per cent, while Democrats stayed at 86 per cent dissatisfied. [cite:dt8_pew_intl2026] Two readings are open. One holds that design matters, and that the most satisfied publics live under rules that weigh votes more evenly than America's do. The other holds that satisfaction tracks outcomes, scandals and partisanship, and that rules are a small part of it. The survey does not settle which is right, and it does not support the claim that a fuller design would by itself make Americans content.

The voters themselves

One charge stands apart because no reform in this book is aimed at it: that voters do not know enough to govern. The record gives it partial support. In the Annenberg Public Policy Center's survey of August 2025, 70 per cent of American adults could name all three branches of government, up from 65 per cent a year earlier, and 13 per cent could name none; 21 per cent believed the First Amendment protects the right to bear arms. [cite:dt8_annenberg2025] Ranked-choice ballots ask more of voters, not less, although the studies reviewed by New America found that initial confusion largely faded after a second use. [cite:dt6_na_review] The reforms in this book change how votes are counted and how campaigns are paid for; they do not make voters better informed. Yarvin's answer removes the need for informed voters by removing the vote, and with it the means by which, as [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Who Removes a Bad Sovereign] showed, a ruler who goes wrong is removed.
