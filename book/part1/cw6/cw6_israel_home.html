Israel at Home, 1965–1967: Rafi, the Sixth Knesset and the Recession

The government that faced the crisis of May 1967 was not the one Ben-Gurion had led. It was a coalition built after a party split and a hard-fought election, and it governed an economy that had stopped growing. Both facts shaped the country that entered the last weeks before the war, and neither is visible in the familiar account that moves straight from the arms and border stories of these years to the crisis itself. This section sets out the political arithmetic and the economic record as Israel's own institutions documented them.

Ben-Gurion's break. In 1965 David Ben-Gurion and a group of his associates left Mapai and founded a new list, Rafi. According to the Israel Democracy Institute's party profile, three disputes drove the break: Ben-Gurion's push to reopen the Lavon affair, his opposition to Mapai's joint list with Ahdut HaAvoda, and his doubts about the leadership of his successor as prime minister, Levi Eshkol. Moshe Dayan, Shimon Peres and Yitzhak Navon were among Rafi's members. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_rafi] The Lavon affair itself is told in the earlier stages of this volume. What matters here is that Ben-Gurion now stood outside the party that governed, and that Dayan and Peres went with him.

The election of 2 November 1965. The Sixth Knesset was elected on 2 November 1965. Of 1,499,988 eligible voters, 83.0 per cent voted. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] The results show a field reorganized into blocs. Mapai and Ahdut HaAvoda ran together as the Alignment and won 45 seats with 36.7 per cent of the vote. On the right, Herut and the Liberals had merged as Gahal, which won 26 seats with 21.3 per cent. The National Religious Party took 11 seats, Rafi 10, Mapam 8, the Independent Liberals 5, Agudat Yisrael 4 and Rakah 3. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965]

Neither of the new blocs did as well as its parts had done separately. Measured against its components' previous totals, the Alignment came back five seats fewer and Gahal eight fewer. Rafi's 10 seats, the institute records, disappointed Ben-Gurion. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] Rafi's exact vote is given two ways by the same institute, and the discrepancy should be stated rather than smoothed. Its party profile gives 95,328 votes, or 7.9 per cent. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_rafi] Its election table prints 42,654 votes beside the same 7.9 per cent and the same 10 seats, a figure that cannot be reconciled with that share. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] This book uses the party profile's figure, and flags the table.

Eshkol's coalition. Forming a government took more than two months of negotiation. The coalition Eshkol assembled brought together the Alignment, the National Religious Party, Mapam, the Independent Liberals and Poalei Agudat Yisrael, with the Arab lists affiliated with the Alignment: 75 members in all. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] Both Rafi and Gahal stayed outside it. That arrangement held until the eve of the June 1967 war, when Eshkol brought both into a national unity government. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] [cite:rs_cw6_idi_rafi] The mechanism matters for what followed. In the spring of 1967 the parties that were not in government included Ben-Gurion's own list, with Dayan in it, and the main opposition on the right. When the crisis came, the pressure to widen the cabinet had a ready address: the two lists the 1965 coalition had left out.

The recession. The second fact about Israel in 1966 is economic, and the best source for it is the Bank of Israel's own annual report for that year. Real gross national product grew by 0.6 per cent in 1966, against 8.2 per cent in 1965. Real GNP per head fell by 2.0 per cent. Gross investment fell by 18.4 per cent, and investment in construction by about 20 per cent. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch2] An economy that had grown by more than eight per cent in one year had, in the next, all but stopped.

The Bank attributed the turn mainly to falling demand after the boom of 1961–64. "The recessionary forces operating in 1966," it wrote, "largely emanated from the construction sector." It listed the causes: overinvestment in housing; steep wage increases, with wages per employee up 19 per cent in 1966; lower immigration and lower transfers from abroad; and government restraint. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch2] In the Bank's account these were not separate shocks: the construction boom, the fall in immigration and the fall in transfers from abroad all appear on the same list of causes.

Policy, late. The Bank's account of government policy is critical, and it is the government's own central bank that makes the criticism. At the end of 1964 the government decided to moderate economic activity, but in 1965 it raised demand instead. The curbs came in 1966, a year in which no new development projects were started. Late in 1966 the government reversed course, turning to capital spending, deficit financing and easier credit. The Bank judged that the reversal came too late. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch2] On this record the slowdown was partly the planned result of policy, and the planners, by the Bank's own reckoning, had first delayed the brake and then held it on past the point where it was needed.

Who bore the cost. The cost fell on workers who lost their jobs. The Bank's chapter on population, labour force and employment gives unemployment, by the manpower surveys, at an average of 3.6 per cent in 1965, or 33,200 people. In 1966 the average was 7.4 per cent, or 70,200 people, and in the last quarter of 1966 it reached 10.3 per cent, or 99,100 people. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch9] Registered job seekers rose from 5,463 in December 1965 to 19,376 in December 1966, more than three and a half times as many. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch9] Because the downturn came mainly out of construction, by the Bank's account, it struck hardest where the building boom had employed most. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch2]

Immigration and emigration. The same chapter records a change in what was driving the population's growth. Net immigration accounted for 49 per cent of population growth in 1964, 31 per cent in 1965 and 12 per cent in 1966. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch9] Emigration rose. The Bank's preliminary figures for 1966 showed more people leaving than in 1964 or 1965, though not many more than the 10,900 who had left in 1963. [cite:rs_cw6_boi_1966_ch9] A year in which net immigration supplied about an eighth of the population's growth, against nearly half two years earlier, marked a turn in its own right.

What the record does not settle. The documents fetched for this book stop with the Bank's 1966 report. They do not give the unemployment figures for the first months of 1967, so the peak of the recession, and whether the late-1966 reversal had begun to work by May, cannot be stated from them. Nor does any source here connect the recession to the decisions of May and June 1967. Israel's chief of military intelligence would tell the American defence secretary on 1 June that the mobilization was straining the economy, but that is a statement about the reserves called up in the crisis, not about the recession that preceded it. What the record does establish is the condition of the country on the eve: a prime minister governing with a 75-member coalition, a founder in opposition with a party of 10, and an economy in which roughly one worker in ten was out of work at the end of 1966. How that government widened on 1 June 1967, and who joined it, is told in [scene:cw6_tiran_june4|the last section of this stage].
