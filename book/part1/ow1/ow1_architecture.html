Building the Machinery, 1981–2008

Between 1981 and 2008 the relationship acquired its permanent machinery: a strategic memorandum, a stockpile of American munitions on Israeli soil, a free trade agreement, a legal status shared with only a handful of countries, a special way of paying out aid, three ten-year aid pledges and a statute that made Israel's military edge a matter of American law. Each was created by a named act of a named administration, and most can still be read. This section follows them in order, then shows in one diagram how an aid dollar moves from Congress to a contractor.

The Strategic Cooperation memorandum, 1981. On 30 November 1981 Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger and Defense Minister Ariel Sharon signed a Memorandum of Understanding on Strategic Cooperation. [cite:ow1_wiki_1981isr] It provided for a joint committee to plan exercises, for the Sixth Fleet's use of Israeli ports and for the pre-positioning of American supplies in Israel; as an executive memorandum it did not go to the Senate for ratification. [cite:ow_wiki_sca1981] It lasted eighteen days. On 14 December the Knesset passed the Golan Heights Law, applying Israeli law to the Golan, and on 18 December the Reagan administration suspended the memorandum. [cite:ow1_wiki_1981isr] Prime Minister Menachem Begin said Israel understood the suspension to mean cancellation. [cite:ow1_csm_1981] Strategic cooperation was not revived until late 1983, when Reagan and Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir agreed on 29 November to form a Joint Political-Military Group. [cite:ow1_reagan_1983] [cite:ow1_wiki_1981isr] The episode is the clearest early case of an American administration using the strategic relationship itself as leverage, and it was short.

The stockpile, 1984–1989. An April 1984 bilateral memorandum began the pre-positioning of American weapons in Israel. After a decision of September 1989, the stockpile became War Reserve Stocks for Allies–Israel, known as WRSA-I; in 1989 the United States agreed to place $100 million of equipment there. [cite:ow1_jinsa_wrsa] The 1989 agreement, signed on the Israeli side by Yitzhak Rabin and on the American side by Dick Cheney, also allowed Israel to lease American equipment. [cite:ow1_cie_sdi] The stockpile belongs to the United States. Israel may draw on it only with American approval, as it did with permission from the Bush administration in 2006 and the Obama administration in 2014, when the Pentagon said it was "vital to U.S. national interests to assist Israel." [cite:ow1_jinsa_wrsa] [cite:ow1_npr_wrsa2014] Congress sets how much may be added each year: since FY2025 the cap is $500 million a year through FY2027. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] Part Seven follows the stockpile inside the Pentagon.

Trade, missile defence and a new status, 1985–1988. In 1985 the United States–Israel Free Trade Agreement entered into force; the Office of the US Trade Representative describes it as "the United States' first FTA." In 2024 American goods exports to Israel were $14.8 billion and imports $22.2 billion. [cite:ow1_ustr_fta] On 6 May 1986 the two governments signed a secret agreement for Israeli participation in research under the Strategic Defense Initiative, the framework under which the Arrow missile-defence system was developed. [cite:ow1_cie_sdi] Section 1105 of the National Defense Authorization Act for FY1987 (Public Law 99-661, 4 November 1986) created the category of "major non-NATO ally," and Israel was designated in February 1987. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] On 21 April 1988 Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir and President Reagan signed a Memorandum of Agreement that recorded that designation and created standing bodies: a Joint Political-Military Group for combined planning, exercises and logistics, a Joint Security Assistance Planning Group to review Israel's aid requests each year, and a Joint Economic Development Group. It ran for five years and renews automatically. [cite:ow1_jvl_moa1988]

Early disbursement, 1991. The most unusual feature of the aid is how it is paid. Public Law 101-513, the appropriations act for FY1991, required that Israel's military aid be disbursed as a lump sum early in the fiscal year and held in an interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] Current appropriations require disbursement within 30 days of enactment. Israel used the interest for years to pay down its debts to the United States and, as of September 2024, had no outstanding sovereign debt to the US government; it may not use the interest for procurement inside Israel. [cite:ow1_crs_aid2025] A related authority under Section 23 of the Arms Export Control Act, known as cash-flow financing, lets Israel place multiyear orders and pay for them over time. [cite:ow1_crs_aid2025]

The diagram shows why the aid is often described as both a gift to Israel and a subsidy to American industry. The money is a grant to a foreign government, but almost all of it is spent in the United States, and the share Israel could spend at home has been cut to zero by the 2016 agreement. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] What the arrangement gives Israel that other recipients do not is timing: the full year's money at once, earning interest, and the ability to commit future years' money to large orders.

The ten-year pledges, 1999 and 2007. From 1999 the aid was set by ten-year memoranda negotiated between the two executives and then funded year by year by Congress. The 1999 memorandum, covering FY1999–2008, totalled $26.7 billion, of which $21.3 billion was military; the 2007 memorandum, covering FY2009–2018, totalled $30 billion and allowed up to 26.3 per cent of it to be spent on Israeli-made equipment. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] (Some accounts attach the $30 billion figure to the 1999 memorandum; on the Congressional Research Service's figures it belongs to the 2007 one.) A memorandum is not a law. Congress is not bound by it, but it has appropriated the pledged amounts in every year since.

The edge becomes law, 2008. In 2008 the Naval Vessel Transfer Act, Public Law 110-429, wrote Israel's "qualitative military edge," or QME, over its neighbours into statute. It defined QME, required the executive to assess it every four years, and amended the Arms Export Control Act so that a proposed arms sale to another country in the Middle East must be accompanied by a determination that it will not adversely affect Israel's edge. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] The law constrains what the United States sells to Israel's neighbours; it does not fix how much aid Israel receives, which is set by the memoranda and appropriations. Claims that the QME law makes it legally impossible to cut aid overstate it.

By the end of 2008 every element of the later relationship existed: political and military consultative bodies, a stockpile, a trade agreement, a privileged legal status, a unique payment method, a ten-year funding cycle and a statutory edge.
