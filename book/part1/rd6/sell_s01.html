How Israel Was Sold to Americans

It is often assumed that Americans backed Israel in 1948 because it was a proven strategic ally. The documented case made to the American public at the time rested on other grounds entirely, and the strategic argument came later, after a long interval and in the face of the national-security establishment's earlier opposition.

The first ground was refuge. The central argument of the years 1945 to 1948 was humanitarian: some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in Europe, and the Holocaust. Earlier parts of this book document that crisis in detail.

The second ground was religious. For Christian supporters, a Jewish state in Palestine fulfilled restorationist belief, a current this book traces back to 1839. The third ground was political: the new state was presented to Americans as a democracy.

What the case did not rest on, in 1948, was American strategic interest. The national-security establishment argued the opposite. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary and the CIA all warned that American backing for the new state would harm American interests. They were overruled. The idea of Israel as a strategic ally was not the reason for American support at the founding; at the founding, the strategic professionals regarded support as a liability.

The publicity of the period also carried a hostile framing of Arabs. Pro-Zionist material drew heavily on the wartime collaboration of the Grand Mufti with Hitler, a real and documented episode, and used it as a broad brush. That should be distinguished from what came much later. The organized, well-funded anti-Islam network in the United States is documented mainly from the 2000s, building on currents that emerged after 1967 and after 1979. [cite:cap_fear_inc] Anti-Arab framing was present in 1948; the industry did not yet exist.

The strategic case arrived a generation after the founding. The argument that Israel was a strategic asset grew after its victory in 1967 and was formalized under President Reagan, who called Israel a strategic asset and signed a strategic-cooperation agreement in 1981.

The sequence matters for how the relationship is understood today. The terms on which Israel was first presented to Americans were refuge, scripture and democracy. The terms on which the relationship is often defended now are alliance and security. The second set was not available in 1948, and the people whose job it was to assess American security interests at the time said so in writing. Reading the present framing back into the founding misdescribes both periods. It is one of several founding narratives examined in [scene:pn_s01|seven narratives, checked].
