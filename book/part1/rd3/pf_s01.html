The Passfield White Paper and the "Black Letter"

British policy in Palestine is sometimes described as moving in one steady direction until the White Paper of 1939. The years 1930 and 1931 show otherwise. After the violence of 1929, two British inquiries found that land sales and immigration were creating a landless Arab class. London acted on their findings, and then, within four months, effectively withdrew the policy under political pressure. It is the clearest case before 1939 of the Zionist movement reversing a British policy decision in a matter of months.

The first inquiry was the Shaw Commission, sent to investigate the 1929 violence. Its report of March 1930 concluded that the fundamental cause was Arab fear, economic and political, of being displaced through Jewish immigration and land purchase. It recommended that Britain clarify its policy on both [cite:w_shaw].

The second went further. Sir John Hope Simpson surveyed land and agriculture and reported in October 1930. He found that a large proportion of rural Arab families lacked enough land to live on, that evictions following land sales were producing a landless class, and that, without development, there was no margin for further Jewish agricultural settlement [cite:w_hopesimpson]. It is the most detailed British land study of the Mandate period.

On that basis the Colonial Secretary, Lord Passfield, issued a White Paper in October 1930. It made immigration depend on Arab as well as Jewish unemployment and tightened control over land transfers [cite:w_passfield]. It was the first serious attempt to slow the settlement machinery that the Mandate had set in motion.

Chaim Weizmann resigned as president of the Jewish Agency in protest. Senior Conservative and Liberal politicians attacked the policy in Parliament and in the press, denouncing it as a breach of the Mandate, and the press campaign was intense. In a closely watched by-election in Whitechapel, a constituency with a large Jewish electorate, Labour's vote fell [cite:w_passfield]. The pressure landed on a minority Labour government.

On 13 February 1931 the Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald, sent Weizmann a letter that was read into the record of the House of Commons. It "interpreted" the White Paper in a way that removed its restrictions. Arab leaders called it the "Black Letter" [cite:w_passfield]. A policy grounded in two official inquiries had been effectively withdrawn within four months of its publication.

What followed can be measured. Jewish immigration rose sharply, from about 4,000 in 1931 to roughly 30,000 in 1933 and to over 60,000 in 1935, its peak for the Mandate period. The reversal was not the only factor. The restrictions had been lifted, and from 1933 the Nazi takeover in Germany, described in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse], came on top of it. The Arab Revolt began the following year, in 1936.

The episode is significant less for its outcome than for its mechanism. London's own inquiries had identified a problem and recommended limits, and London's own government had adopted them. Political pressure in London, exerted through resignation, parliamentary attack, the press and an electoral setback, then overrode those findings.
