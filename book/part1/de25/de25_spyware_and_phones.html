Inside the Phone: Spyware, Extraction and Fake Towers

The location trail described in [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|the previous section] shows where a phone has been. A second family of tools goes after what is on it: messages, photographs, contacts, notes, passwords. They come in three kinds, governed by different law. Remote spyware is installed from a distance, without the owner touching anything. Forensic extraction tools copy a phone that investigators already hold, after an arrest, seizure or border inspection. Cell-site simulators are portable fake phone towers that make nearby phones reveal themselves. The Israeli origins of several of these firms, and Israel's own export controls, are set out in [scene:ow10_nso|the library's account of NSO] and [scene:ow10_paragon|of Paragon]; this section concentrates on their use in the United States.

One term needs defining first. End-to-end encryption, used by Signal, WhatsApp and Apple's iMessage, scrambles a message on the sender's phone and unscrambles it only on the recipient's, so a warrant served on the company carrying it yields at most who talked to whom and when, which is called metadata. It does not protect the phone at either end: a tool that runs on the phone itself reads the message after it has been unscrambled for display. That is why spyware and extraction tools matter more as encryption spreads. [cite:ow6_guardian_paragon] [cite:de25_fs_forecast]

How a zero-click attack works

The most closely documented example is FORCEDENTRY, an exploit used by the Israeli company NSO Group to install its Pegasus spyware on iPhones in 2021. Google's Project Zero researchers Ian Beer and Samuel Groß dissected it on 15 December 2021. The attacker needed only the target's phone number or Apple ID and sent a message through iMessage; the owner did not have to open it. A file named as a GIF image was in fact a PDF document containing a compressed image format called JBIG2. A counting error in the phone's JBIG2 decoder let the attacker write beyond the memory set aside for the image, and from there the attacker used more than 70,000 of the format's simple logical operations to assemble a small working computer inside the image decoder, which then ran the next stage of the attack. The researchers called it "one of the most technically sophisticated exploits we've ever seen." Apple fixed the flaw on 13 September 2021, after the University of Toronto's Citizen Lab found a sample. [cite:de25_pz_forcedentry] Each such exploit stops working once its flaw is found and patched.

Pegasus: blacklisted, tested and not deployed

The American record on Pegasus is one of purchase and refusal. The FBI bought it, paying NSO about $5 million, tested it for about two years at a facility in New Jersey, including a version called Phantom designed to work on American phone numbers, and on 22 July 2021 decided to "cease all efforts" to use it; the bureau says it never used it operationally. [cite:ow10_ai_nytmag] [cite:ow10_globe_fbi] On 3 November 2021 the Commerce Department put NSO on its Entity List, which bars American firms from supplying it without a licence, for providing spyware used to "maliciously target" officials, journalists and activists. [cite:ow10_doc_entity] In 2023 the New York Times reported that a contractor, Riva Networks, had used another NSO tool, the geolocation product Landmark, on the FBI's behalf after the ban; the bureau said it had been misled and ended the contract. [cite:ow10_9to5_riva] A California jury on 6 May 2025 ordered NSO to pay WhatsApp $167.25 million in punitive damages for hacking about 1,400 users; the judge cut that to $4 million in October 2025 and barred NSO from targeting WhatsApp, and in June 2026 WhatsApp sought a contempt finding for breach of that order. [cite:ow10_axios_jury] [cite:ow10_cs_inj] [cite:ow10_sw_contempt]

NSO has since changed owners and set out to win American business. It was reported in late 2025 to be controlled by investors led by the film producer Robert Simonds, with David Friedman, ambassador to Israel in Trump's first term, as executive chairman. [cite:ow10_rec_friedman] It issued a "transparency report" in January 2026, and its 2025 attempt to leave the Entity List was reported to have been refused. [cite:de25_wpn_nso] No removal from the list and no American government contract for Pegasus had been reported as of October 2026.

Graphite: bought, paused and revived at ICE

Paragon Solutions' Graphite, which can take over a phone without a click and read encrypted messaging apps, took the opposite path. ICE signed a $2 million contract for it on 27 September 2024. In October 2024 the Biden administration put the contract on hold for review under Executive Order 14093, Biden's March 2023 bar on federal use of commercial spyware that poses counter-intelligence risks or has been misused abroad. [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] [cite:ow10_eo14093] An American private-equity firm, AE Industrial Partners, agreed in December 2024 to buy Paragon. On 30 August 2025 ICE lifted the hold, and on 1 April 2026 its acting director, Todd Lyons, told members of Congress that Homeland Security Investigations was using the tool against foreign terrorist organisations, "particularly those involved in the trafficking of fentanyl," and had certified under the same executive order that it posed no significant counter-intelligence risk. [cite:ow10_reg_paragon] [cite:ow10_tc_lyons] The record after that is less clear than either side's statements. A procurement notice shows the contract closed out on 20 January 2026, before Lyons said he had approved the tool's use, and on 22 May 2026 the department told NPR that "ICE has no relationship with Paragon Solutions, Inc. or with the company that acquired them" and had signed no new contract, while declining to "confirm or deny" whether ICE still had the capability. The two accounts are consistent if ICE kept using what it had already bought; whether it does is not answered. [cite:de25_npr_paragon]

How a government customer used the same tool was tested in Italy. Early in 2025 WhatsApp told about 90 journalists and members of civil society that they had been targeted with Graphite; Citizen Lab confirmed infections among them, including two journalists at the news site Fanpage and activists of Mediterranea Saving Humans, a group that rescues migrants at sea. [cite:ow10_is_italy] In June 2025 the parliamentary committee overseeing Italy's intelligence services found that the targeting of the activists had been lawful and found no evidence that the Fanpage editor Francesco Cancellato had been targeted. In March 2026 prosecutors in Rome and Naples confirmed by forensic analysis that Cancellato's phone had been hacked; they could not reach the same conclusion for his colleague Ciro Pellegrino. Paragon had cancelled its contracts with Italy's two intelligence agencies and, as of April 2026, had not answered the prosecutors' request for information; Giorgia Meloni's government denies hacking journalists. [cite:de25_cl_cancellato] [cite:de25_tc_italy]

The law that governs Graphite's use inside the United States has not been set out in public. Criminal wiretaps of communications need a judge's order, and American judges authorised 1,735 of them in 2025. [cite:us10_usc_wiretap2025] Representative Summer Lee, one of the members who received ICE's April 2026 letter, said the agency had given no substantive answer on who could be targeted or under what legal authority inside the United States; an unnamed homeland security official said the technology supports investigations while respecting civil liberties. [cite:ow10_npr_lyons] The executive order remains the operative federal limit on commercial spyware, and it is a presidential order that a president can amend or revoke. [cite:ow10_eo14093]

Extraction: Cellebrite and GrayKey

Forensic extraction is far more common than spyware. A device made by Cellebrite, an Israeli company with about 7,000 customers, or GrayKey, made by the American firm Magnet Forensics, is connected to a phone that agents hold, exploits flaws in the phone's software to get past the lock screen, and copies its contents. [cite:de25_fs_forecast] [cite:de25_tc_graykey] ICE has bought Cellebrite since at least March 2017, when it spent $2.2 million, and in June 2019 it announced plans for a contract of up to $30–35 million. [cite:ow6_ipt_cellebrite] In 2025 its cyber-crime unit said no other product offered "equivalent capabilities" for "logical, file system, physical, and password data extraction." [cite:ow10_fs_cb] A Homeland Security forecast published in May 2026 planned a five-year contract with Cellebrite worth up to $100 million for ICE and HSI, covering phones and, after Cellebrite's purchase of a drone-forensics firm, drones; it called Cellebrite's tool "the most widely utilized and deployed computer forensic tool" in HSI. [cite:de25_fs_forecast] HSI signed a $3 million licence contract with Magnet Forensics in September 2025, which TechCrunch judged was most likely for GrayKey, after a $5 million GrayKey contract in 2024; ICE declined to say whether it uses the devices on the phones of arrested migrants. [cite:de25_tc_graykey]

The documented misuse came from a foreign customer. Amnesty International's Security Lab found that Serbian authorities had used a chain of previously unknown flaws, linked to Cellebrite's equipment, to unlock a student activist's Android phone while he was held by police in Belgrade on 25 December 2024, before an attempt to install spyware on it. On 25 February 2025 Cellebrite said it had "suspended product usage by certain Serbian customers." [cite:de25_ci_serbia] American critics point to reach rather than documented abuse: the Electronic Frontier Foundation objected to a Cellebrite feature that defeats the automatic restarts relocking seized phones, and Cellebrite answered that its tools require "valid legal authorization." [cite:de25_fs_forecast]

At the border, which [scene:de25b_who_knows_you_travel|Part Twenty-Seven] covers in full, the warrant rule that protects a phone elsewhere does not apply in the same way. Customs and Border Protection searched a record 55,318 electronic devices in the year to September 2025, about 17 per cent more than the year before; 50,922 of them were "basic" searches, a manual look through the phone that CBP may carry out without any suspicion, and 13,590 belonged to American citizens. [cite:de25_dn_cbp] [cite:de25_iptp_cbp]

Fake towers: cell-site simulators

A cell-site simulator, often called by the brand name Stingray, is a box, usually mounted in a vehicle, that imitates a phone tower so that phones nearby connect to it and give up their identifiers and location; some models can also intercept calls and texts. [cite:de25_tc_tosv] On 3 September 2015 the Justice Department required its agents to obtain a warrant on probable cause before using one, with exceptions for "exigent" and "exceptional" circumstances, barred their use to collect the content of communications in criminal cases, and ordered data deleted "as soon as that device is located, and no less than once daily." [cite:de25_doj_css] A 2023 audit by the Homeland Security inspector general found that the Secret Service and ICE "did not always adhere to statute and policies," and recommended controls to separate genuine emergencies from policy exceptions and to record data deletion accurately. [cite:de25_oig_css] ICE used simulators at least 1,885 times between 2013 and 2017 and at least 466 times between 2017 and 2019, and it bought simulator vehicles from TechOps Specialty Vehicles for $818,000 in September 2024, under Biden, and $825,000 in May 2025, under Trump. [cite:de25_tc_tosv]

Put together, the record shows an immigration agency that now holds every class of phone tool: remote spyware, extraction for seized phones, and fake towers for finding a phone in a crowd or a building. Most purchases predate 2025; the decisive change of 2025 was the revival of a spyware contract the previous administration had paused. The worst-case extension is plain from the capabilities. Graphite reads encrypted messages on the phone itself, and a simulator can sweep every phone near a gathering. ICE's stated use of commercial spyware is against drug-trafficking groups, and no document as of October 2026 shows any of these tools used in the United States against a journalist, an activist or a political critic. The Italian case shows that a government customer can turn such a tool on journalists and that discovery depended on a messaging company, an academic laboratory and prosecutors, not on the vendor. [cite:ow10_tc_lyons] [cite:de25_tc_italy] Which of these safeguards would operate in the United States, and which have actually stopped a tool, is examined in [scene:de26_counterweights|Part Twenty-Eight].

If it were connected

A phone tool yields contents: contacts, messages, photographs, the names and numbers of everyone a person deals with. Joined to a platform that links records, those contents become a map of a person's associates. Joined to extraction: documented. Internal ICE documents from 2014 to 2022, obtained by Just Futures Law and reported by the Guardian, show that agents could import phone extractions made with Cellebrite into Palantir's investigative tools and search the names and numbers found on them against the student-visa database SEVIS and airline passenger data. [cite:de25_gdn_palantir] Joined to spyware: possible, with no document showing a feed from Paragon's tool to Palantir's systems; members of Congress asked in April 2026 which datasets sit in DHS's Palantir-built systems and named Paragon and cell-site simulators among the tools of concern, and the department gave no public answer. [cite:de25_goldman_letter] [cite:de25_npr_paragon] Joined to the location purchases of [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|the previous section], so that a device seen in a neighbourhood is tracked, located with a simulator and then searched: possible, since ICE holds all three kinds of tool, but no document shows them used in sequence against one person. Joined to the government's records on work and security vetting in [scene:de24b_work_and_vetting|Part Twenty-Five], so that a seized phone's contents feed a clearance or employment decision: possible, with no document. What stands in the way of a wider join is mostly procedure: a warrant or border authority for each search, the spyware order's certifications, and the inspector general's findings that ICE did not always follow the simulator rules it already had. [cite:de25_doj_css] [cite:ow10_eo14093] [cite:de25_oig_css] The full set of joins is mapped in [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty].
