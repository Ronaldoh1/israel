Israel: Equal Citizens on Paper, Unequal Outcomes in the Record

Israel's founding declaration of May 1948 promised "complete equality of social and political rights" to all inhabitants "irrespective of religion, race or sex". [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] No Basic Law since has restated that promise as a free-standing right; the nearest, the Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty of 1992, passed by 32 votes to 21, protects dignity and liberty without naming equality. [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws] The Nation-State Law of 19 July 2018 then gave constitutional rank to a different principle: that "the right to exercise national self-determination" in the state "is unique to the Jewish people". [cite:gi5_toi_nslaw] An eleven-justice panel upheld it 10–1 on 8 July 2021, the majority holding that it did not license discrimination against non-Jewish citizens; Justice George Karra dissented. [cite:gi5_adalah_hcj] [cite:gi5_aje_hcj2021] The law and the ruling are set out in an earlier volume. [scene:gi5_nationstate|The Nation-State Law] This section measures what the record shows for four groups of citizens whose outcomes the state's own statistics track separately: Arab citizens, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) Jews, Mizrahi Jews and Ethiopian Israelis. The figures describe how laws, budgets and institutions have treated people. They say nothing about the character of any community.

Arab citizens: from military rule to five-year plans

Arab citizens are about a fifth of Israel's population. [cite:us5_cnn_killingaday] For the state's first eighteen years most of them lived under a military government, set up in October 1948 that, once the fighting ended, was "neither cancelled nor was it legally regularized". [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] The Knesset voted to end it on 8 November 1966, and it was abolished on 1 December 1966. At that point it still covered about 150,000 Arab citizens, mainly along the Jordanian border and around Nazareth. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] [cite:rs_cw6_jta_jun1966] Earlier volumes trace that period and how it ended. [scene:rd6_military_government|Military government, 1948–1966] [scene:cw6_military_rule_end|The end of military rule]

The first official admission of unequal treatment came after October 2000, when police killed twelve Arab citizens and one Palestinian during demonstrations. The commission the government appointed to investigate, the Or Commission, reported on 2 September 2003. It found that Arab citizens faced discrimination and that the government had not given their needs fair and equal attention. Arab rights groups Adalah and Mossawa called this perhaps the first such official admission. [cite:us8_wiki_orcomm] The policy response came twelve years later. Government Resolution 922, adopted on 30 December 2015, was announced at NIS 15 billion over five years, NIS 5.8 billion of it for education. An evaluation for the Institute for National Security Studies (INSS) found the real total, excluding education, was about NIS 10 billion. About NIS 9.6 billion was distributed and about NIS 6 billion, some 62 per cent, actually spent. [cite:us8_inss_922] Its successor, Resolution 550 of October 2021, budgeted about NIS 30 billion. After the war began in October 2023 it was cut by 15 per cent. In December 2025 the government proposed moving a further NIS 3 billion of it to the Ministry of National Security; the Israel Democracy Institute (IDI) advised against the move. [cite:us8_idi_550] Part Five follows that money into policing, and the killings in Arab towns that it was meant to address. [scene:us5_arab|The killings in Israel's Arab communities]

The outcomes moved. Arab women's employment, about 26 per cent before Resolution 922, passed 40 per cent before the pandemic. Arab students rose from about a tenth of college and university students to 18 per cent of undergraduates in 2019–20. [cite:us8_inss_922] By Central Bureau of Statistics data, Arab women's employment rose from 33 per cent in 2014 to 45 per cent in mid-2023 and 46.1 per cent early in 2024. [cite:us8_globes_emp2023] [cite:us8_globes_emp2024] Poverty fell too, though it stayed far above the Jewish rate. The National Insurance Institute (NII) put the poverty rate of Arab families at 51.7 per cent in 2013 and 53.3 per cent in 2015, against 13.8 per cent for Jewish families. [cite:us8_nii_2015] For 2024 it reported 37.6 per cent, against 20.0 per cent for all families. Nearly half of Arab households (48.7 per cent) said they could not cover their monthly expenses, against 22.7 per cent of Jewish households. [cite:us8_nii_2024] The two reports come from the same annual series. This book has not established whether changes to the NII's survey method between them account for part of the fall. Health shows the same pattern. In 2022 infant mortality was 5.1 per 1,000 among Arab citizens and 2.1 among Jewish citizens, and the highest life expectancy in any Arab district (80.1) was four years below the highest Jewish one. [cite:us16_toi_moh_gaps] [scene:us16_outcomes|Health outcomes over time]

Haredi Jews: poverty tied to policy choices

Haredi families had a poverty rate of 52.4 per cent in 2014 and 48.6 per cent in 2015, by the NII's definition based on the last school a person attended, and 32.8 per cent in 2024. [cite:us8_nii_2015] [cite:us8_nii_2024] Employment among Haredi men stayed near half for a decade. It was 52 per cent in 2014 and 51 per cent in 2019, rose to a peak of 56.7 per cent at the end of 2023, and was 54.3 per cent early in 2024. Haredi women's employment was 74 per cent in 2019, against 82 per cent for other Jewish women. [cite:us8_taub_labor2019] [cite:us8_globes_emp2023] [cite:us8_globes_emp2024] The state's own arrangements shape these figures: the military exemption for yeshiva students, state funding for Torah study, and a school stream allowed to teach little of the core curriculum. Part Seven sets out that structure and its cost. [scene:us7_haredi|The Haredi exemption and its funding]

Mizrahi Jews: a gap that narrowed slowly

Israel's statistics do not track Jews of Middle Eastern and North African origin (Mizrahim) as a separate group after the second generation. The best long view therefore comes from survey research. Using the European Social Survey for Israel, the sociologists Yinon Cohen, Noah Lewin-Epstein and Amit Lazarus found that 63.9 per cent of second-generation Ashkenazim (Jews of European origin) held at least a bachelor's degree, against 30.5 per cent of second-generation Mizrahim. In the third generation the figures were 56.4 and 36.0 per cent. More than half of that narrowing came from falling attainment among Ashkenazim. [cite:us8_cle_mizrahi] The authors cite separate work showing the net income gap between Ashkenazi and Mizrahi households falling from about 40 per cent in the mid-1990s to 27 per cent in 2011. They suggest that schools in poorer and peripheral areas offered fewer advanced courses and steered more pupils into vocational tracks; their data did not allow them to test this. [cite:us8_cle_mizrahi] No official series continues the comparison to 2026.

Ethiopian Israelis: rescue, then unequal treatment

Israel brought about 6,500 Ethiopian Jews to the country in Operation Moses (November 1984 to January 1985) and 14,325 in 36 hours in Operation Solomon on 24–25 May 1991. [cite:us8_wiki_ethiopian] [cite:us8_wiki_solomon] The community numbered 168,800 in 2023. [cite:us8_mrg_ethiopian] State bodies then treated it differently in documented ways. In January 1996 the newspaper Ma'ariv revealed that the blood service, Magen David Adom, had been secretly discarding donations from Ethiopian immigrants. In January 2013 the Health Ministry's director-general ordered four organisations to stop giving Ethiopian women Depo-Provera contraceptive injections. A 2016 inquiry into the claims of 35 women found no evidence that injections had been forced. [cite:us8_wiki_ethiopian] On 30 June 2019 an off-duty policeman shot dead Solomon Tekah, an Ethiopian Israeli aged 19 (18 in some accounts), and protests followed across the country. At the time Ethiopian Israelis were about 1.7 per cent of the population but 20 per cent of young people in juvenile detention. [cite:us8_csm_tekah] [cite:us8_mrg_ethiopian]

The economic gap has narrowed. A Bank of Israel report found 54 per cent of Ethiopian-Israeli households in poverty in 2003 and 35 per cent in 2013, still nearly twice the national rate. [cite:us8_csm_tekah] In 2019 their average net household income was NIS 14,027 a month, against NIS 17,779 for all Jewish households and NIS 11,810 for Arab households. The gap in the share of pupils earning a matriculation certificate good enough for university fell from 27 percentage points in 2016 to 17 in 2022–23. [cite:us8_wiki_ethiopian]

The record shows two things together. Israel's official bodies have documented unequal treatment of Arab and Ethiopian citizens, from the Or Commission to the Health Ministry's 2013 order, and budgeted programmes to close the gaps. Those programmes moved employment and education measurably. The same record shows the money for Arab towns underspent in 2016–20 and cut after 2023, and the gaps still wide: Arab families' poverty nearly double the national family rate in 2024, Arab infant mortality more than twice the Jewish rate in 2022, and Arab homicide rates many times the Jewish rate since 2023.
