"Under God": The American Home Front, 1951–1953

While Israel's government was choosing a side abroad, the country it was turning toward was arguing about how to describe itself at home. The words "under God" entered the American Pledge of Allegiance in June 1954, and the familiar account treats the change as a single act of Congress in a single anti-communist moment. The record shows a longer campaign with more than one origin. Its documented engine for most of 1951–1953 was a fraternal order, the Knights of Columbus, working through its own rituals, its state councils and finally the mail to every member of Congress. A second, earlier campaign, traced by the historian Kevin Kruse, ran through business-funded clergy and had a domestic target as much as a foreign one. This section tells the campaign to the end of 1953; the law itself, and the question the law left open, belong to Stage Two.

The first documented step was internal. On 22 April 1951, according to an account published by Gene T. Janikowski of the Knights of Columbus South Carolina State Council, the order's Board of Directors adopted a resolution adding "under God" to the Pledge as it was recited at the order's Fourth Degree assemblies. [cite:rs_cw1_kofc_janikowski] The change applied to the Knights' own meetings, not to the country. It was a fraternal order amending its own ritual, with no legal force outside the order. One reading of why the step mattered is that it gave the wording a body of members who already said it, before anyone asked Congress to adopt it.

In 1952 the order turned outward. Knights of Columbus state councils in Florida, South Dakota, New York and Michigan urged Congress to amend the Pledge, and on 21 August 1952 the order's Supreme Council urged that the change be made general. [cite:rs_cw1_crec_2002] [cite:rs_cw1_kofc_janikowski] Both of these steps rest on accounts written later, one in the Congressional Record of 2002 and one by the order's own state council; neither reproduces the resolutions themselves, and the dates should be read as those sources give them.

The same sources disagree on one date in the campaign. The National Fraternal Congress, meeting in Boston, adopted a resolution on 24 September. The Congressional Record account of 2002 places that resolution in 1952. [cite:rs_cw1_crec_2002] Janikowski places it in 1953, though his chronology is not consistent with itself elsewhere. [cite:rs_cw1_kofc_janikowski] Neither text supplied here reproduces the resolution, so the year cannot be settled from these sources. If 1952 is right, a wider body of fraternal societies endorsed the idea within weeks of the Knights' Supreme Council; if 1953, it followed the order's second, larger push.

That second push came on 20 August 1953. According to the Congressional Record account of 2002, the Supreme Council repeated its call and, this time, sent copies to the President, the Vice President, the Speaker of the House and every member of Congress. Seventeen House resolutions followed. [cite:rs_cw1_crec_2002] The mechanism is plain in the sequence: a ritual change inside one order, then resolutions from state councils, then a national resolution, then a mailing that put the proposal on the desk of every legislator.

Kevin Kruse's account does not begin with the Knights at all. He traces an earlier campaign by the Reverend James Fifield, whose organization, Spiritual Mobilization, was funded by business leaders including J. Howard Pew and Alfred Sloan. It had about 17,000 minister representatives and used the slogan "Freedom Under God". Among its events was a ceremony on 4 July 1951 organized by Cecil B. DeMille. [cite:rs_cw1_npr_kruse] The money in this second story is named: it came from business leaders, it moved through a clerical organization, and its product was a phrase, "under God", attached to a political idea of freedom. Kruse also judges the Knights' role more modestly than the order's own historians do. In his telling, their proposal at first "doesn't go anywhere", and it "catches fire" only after Eisenhower's own pastor endorsed it. [cite:rs_cw1_npr_kruse] That endorsement is told in Stage Two.

The two accounts are not simply rival chronologies. They are rival explanations of motive, and both have documentary support of different kinds. The first is the explanation Congress gave for itself. When the change passed in 1954, the House report (H.R. Rep. No. 1693) presented the words as rejecting the communist philosophy "with its attendant subservience of the individual", and the Senate report (S. Rep. No. 1287) said: "The spiritual bankruptcy of the Communists is one of our strongest weapons in the struggle for men's minds." [cite:rs_cw1_doj_newdow_brief] Those reports survive here as quoted by the United States Solicitor General in his 2003–04 brief in Elk Grove Unified School District v. Newdow, and they are the strongest evidence for the Cold War reading: they are the legislators' own stated reasons, written at the time.

The second explanation is Kruse's interpretation, and it should be read as one. He argues that the common story, in which "the Soviet Union discovered the bomb and the United States rediscovered God", papers over a domestic conflict, and that the architects of this language were most worried about the New Deal and Fair Deal administrations. [cite:rs_cw1_npr_kruse] The strongest form of his case is the timing and the money: a business-funded campaign using "Freedom Under God" was already running, with thousands of ministers attached, before the Knights' resolutions reached Congress. Its limit is that the source given here is an interview, not his full documentation, and that it does not show the Knights of Columbus themselves acting for Fifield's sponsors. The two readings can both be partly true: a domestic campaign could have supplied the phrase, and a Cold War consensus could have supplied the votes.

The outcome, briefly, because it is told in full in the next stage. The resolution of Representative Louis C. Rabaut of Michigan passed both houses, and President Eisenhower signed the change on Flag Day, 14 June 1954. On the House floor Rabaut said: "From their earliest childhood our children must know" what "under God" means. [cite:rs_cw1_crec_2002] [cite:rs_cw1_doj_newdow_brief] On 17 August 1954 Eisenhower wrote to the Supreme Knight, Luke Hart, that he was "particularly thankful to you for your part in the movement", and said the words would remind Americans "that despite our great physical strength we must remain humble." [cite:rs_cw1_crec_2002] The letter is the documented acknowledgement of the order's role, from the President who signed the law. Religious language entered the national coinage and motto in the same years: according to Kruse, "In God we trust" went onto a stamp in 1954, onto paper money by act of Congress in 1955, and became the national motto in 1956. [cite:rs_cw1_npr_kruse]

One question a reader of this volume will bring to the subject should be answered directly. Nothing in these sources connects the campaign to Israel, to any Zionist organization, or to the diplomacy told elsewhere in this stage. The 1954 committee reports, Rabaut's floor remark, Eisenhower's letter and Spiritual Mobilization's slogan, as documented here, speak of communism, of the individual, of children, of humility and of freedom; none mentions Israel. Any reading that ties the change to Israel would be interpretation, and it is not made here. What the campaign does show is the domestic side of the alignment this stage describes: in the same years that Washington was asking Middle Eastern states to choose between East and West, the language of American public life was being recast in religious terms, by a fraternal order and by business-funded clergy, terms that the 1954 committee reports would present as a contrast with communism.

Several things the record here does not settle. The year of the National Fraternal Congress resolution remains open between 1952 and 1953. The text of the Knights' 1951 and 1952 resolutions is not reproduced in these sources, so their stated reasons cannot be compared directly with the 1954 reports. Whether the Knights' campaign drew on Fifield's, or ran in parallel without contact, is not shown. And the weight of each motive, the Cold War contrast the legislators named and the domestic quarrel Kruse identifies, is a matter of interpretation that the documents can inform but not decide. What the law said, how the President's own pastor came to endorse it, and what the unnamed "God" in it meant are taken up in [scene:cw2_pledge|Stage Two's account of the Pledge].
