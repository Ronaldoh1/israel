Sufism is the inner path of Islam: mystical belief and practice that seeks, in Britannica's words, "direct personal experience of God". [cite:sf_brit_sufism] It began among ascetics who wore wool, as "a counterweight to the increasing worldliness" of a fast-growing Muslim empire, and it grew into orders whose missionary activity, Britannica notes, still continues worldwide. [cite:sf_brit_sufism] [cite:sf_brit_orders] Its most famous poet, Rumi, is often described as the best-selling poet in the United States. [cite:sf_pf_rumi]

It is also, like every tradition on this shelf, a story of who held sacred knowledge and what power did with those who claimed it. A Sufi who said "I am the Truth" was crucified in Baghdad in 922. A philosopher of light was put to death in Aleppo on a prince's order. A Sufi order became a dynasty and imposed its creed on an empire. A secular republic closed every Sufi lodge in 1925. A desert movement levelled the domes over the graves of the Prophet's family, and a militant group killed more than three hundred worshippers in a Sufi mosque in 2017. This book follows those events, together with two neighbouring traditions, the Nizari Ismailis of Alamut and the anonymous Brethren of Purity, whose stories were distorted by their enemies. It ends with what happened to Rumi in English. The book names states, dynasties, movements and documented people throughout; it treats the conflicts over Sufism as disputes within a civilisation, not as the character of any people. The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads four Sufi teachings as maps of the inner life.
