India, Latin America and East Asia: The Syllabus and the Bookshop

Far from the region, fewer scholars have studied how schools teach this history, but the record is not empty. It has two layers. The first is the syllabus: what exam boards and textbooks put in front of pupils. The second is the bookshop: the popular books that shape what millions of adults believe about Jews, Israel and the Palestinians long after school. In India, Latin America and East Asia the two layers often point in different directions, and the second is frequently the louder.

India: a British textbook, an exam syllabus and a bookshop bestseller

India's most detailed school treatment of the conflict is in the history syllabus of the Indian School Certificate, one of the country's national exam boards. Its twelfth-grade unit, "Middle East: Israeli-Palestine conflict (1916–1993)," begins with the conflicting British promises of the First World War (the Husain-McMahon correspondence, the Sykes-Picot agreement and the Balfour Declaration), then covers the wars from 1948 to the Camp David accord of 1979, the founding of the PLO, the Intifada, and the Oslo accords of 1993 with an assessment of "why it failed to bring peace." [cite:nat_oswal_isc] Popular revision notes for the unit summarize 1948 as an attack by the Arab states on the new state, after which Israel "captured Gaza and West Bank areas" and "nearly one million Arabs" became refugees. [cite:nat_shaalaa_isc]

The textbook many of those schools use is not Indian at all. It is Mastering Modern World History by the British teacher Norman Lowe, published in Britain in a Macmillan series of exam guides. [cite:nat_bookshare_lowe] The American scholar Marcy Jane Knopf-Newman, reading its third edition, found that it frames Israel's creation through Jewish persecution, says that "the Arab states refused to recognize Israel as a legal state and they vowed to destroy it," and does not discuss the expulsion of Palestinians in 1948. [cite:nat_knopf_lowe2014] Her account is a critic's reading of one edition, but it shows how the pen can cross borders: a British publisher's exam guide carrying one version of the story into Indian classrooms.

India's national textbook council has meanwhile been narrowing what pupils study about the wider region. In 2022 it removed the chapter "Central Islamic Lands" from its eleventh-grade world history book, and in 2022–23 it dropped "The Cold War Era" from twelfth-grade civics. [cite:nat_efa_ncert] State boards have gone further in other directions. In 2004 a ninth-grade social studies book issued by the government of Gujarat portrayed Hitler as a strong leader who restored order and national pride, and dealt with the Holocaust in a single sentence; after protests from an Ahmedabad human-rights group and Jewish organizations abroad, the state said positive references to Hitler would be withdrawn. [cite:nat_tes_gujarat2004] [cite:nat_dna_gujarat] Outside school, booksellers reported in 2009 that more than 10,000 copies of Mein Kampf had sold in New Delhi in six months, mostly to business students who read it as a self-help book about one man's vision. [cite:nat_hnn_meinkampf2009] How India's government turned from Palestine toward Israel is told in the survey that opens this part; the classroom and the bookshop show how unevenly that turn reached ordinary readers.

Latin America: the exam that asks about Palestine

In Brazil the clearest evidence comes from the national university entrance exam, ENEM, taken by millions of students a year. Teachers describe the conflict as a recurring subject because it fits so many of the skills the humanities paper tests, from the formation of nation-states to the reading of maps. Questions have included a 2018 text on left-wing Zionist views of the West Bank and Gaza, whose correct answer identified a concern with "pursuing ethnic preeminence over the national space," and a 2019 question setting maps of Palestine and the Jewish state in 1947 beside a map of 2013, whose answer was "securing territorial possession." [cite:nat_folhape_enem2023] Brazilian scholars have also begun studying the books themselves. A 2025 study in Educação e Pesquisa, by Ashjan Sadique Adi of the Arab-Palestinian Federation of Brazil and Fábio Bacila Sahd of the Federal University of Paraná, examined textbooks of the Adventist school network and argued that they reproduce a colonial narrative and create "false symmetries" between Israelis and Palestinians. [cite:nat_usp_adi2025] Both authors write from a stated position, and the study covers one private network, not the national system.

Elsewhere in the region the documented commitments concern the Holocaust rather than 1948: Argentina announced in 2006 that Holocaust education would become a required part of its curriculum. [cite:nat_jta_argentina2006] No study located for this book examines how schools in Chile, whose Palestinian community of 300,000 to 500,000 is the largest outside the Middle East, teach the founding of Israel. [cite:fp_chile_diaspora]

East Asia: memory by analogy, and the conspiracy bestseller

In China the Holocaust entered secondary-school history textbooks in the 2000s, and from 2007 the books reportedly taught it in parallel with the Nanjing Massacre committed by Japanese forces in 1937, framing both through imperialism, fascism and racism. A researcher at the University of Exeter, writing for the National University of Singapore's Middle East Institute, argued that this teaching has built sympathy for Israel among young Chinese, while warning that it could tilt how they see the conflict with the Palestinians. [cite:nat_mei_china2023] On the same shelves sat Currency Wars by Song Hongbing, published in 2007, which claims that the Rothschild family controls about $5 trillion and that the US Federal Reserve is run by private banks loyal to it. It sold more than a million copies by 2011, its sequel more than two million, and senior officials were reported to have read it. Economists dismissed its thesis, and the New York Times criticized its conspiracy content. [cite:nat_wiki_currencywars] The library's Rothschild research sets the documented family against the invented one.

In Japan, a study worksheet built on a standard high-school world history textbook teaches the 1947 partition plan, the founding of the state in 1948, the refugees who left the areas Israel took, the PLO and the Oslo accords, and describes the area enclosed by Israel's separation wall as an "open-air prison." [cite:nat_jp_worksheet] That is one teaching aid, not a national study. Japan's bookshops have told a different story. In 1986 and 1987 two books by Masami Uno, built on the forged Protocols of the Elders of Zion, sold a combined 650,000 copies, prompting protests from American Jewish groups and members of Congress. [cite:nat_jta_uno1987] [cite:nat_wiki_uno]

The books that travelled furthest

The popular book with the widest reach on this subject is not a conspiracy tract but a novel. Leon Uris's Exodus (1958) spent nineteen weeks at the top of the New York Times bestseller list and had sold more than five million hardcover copies by 1965; it became a 1960 film and was copied by hand and duplicating machine among Jews in the Soviet Union. David Ben-Gurion reportedly called it the best propaganda ever written about Israel. Edward Said called it the main narrative model for how Americans think about Israel's founding, and scholars including Rashid Khalidi have criticized its portrayal of Arabs and its silence about the expulsions of 1948. [cite:nat_wiki_exodus]

What these cases show

Where the conflict is far away, schools teach it briefly, often from imported books, and the popular market fills the space. That market carries both of the distortions this volume has tracked: a heroic founding story with the Palestinians left out, as in Exodus, and the old conspiracy about Jews and money, as in Uno's books and Currency Wars. Neither comes from the documentary record. A reader in Delhi, São Paulo, Beijing or Tokyo who wants the record has to go past both. The gaps that remain are specific: no study located for this book measures how Chilean, Mexican, Korean or African classrooms teach 1948, and the text says so rather than guess.
