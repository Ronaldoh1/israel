The Porte Says No, and the Arabs of Palestine Object, 1882–1896

A familiar account holds that the Ottoman Empire welcomed Jewish settlement in Palestine, or even sold the country to the Zionist movement. The documents point the other way. From 1882, within months of the first colonies, the Ottoman government in Constantinople, known to diplomats as the Porte, issued a series of rules meant to keep foreign Jews from settling in Palestine and from buying land there. The rules were reissued, tightened and loosened over the next twelve years. They failed in large part, for reasons the diplomatic record makes plain. In the same years Arab notables in Jerusalem began to petition the government to enforce them. Both stories belong to this period, and both are told here from the documents that survive.

The policy began quickly. According to Büşra Barın's study of Ottoman policy (Middle East Technical University, 2014), in June 1882 the entry of foreign Jews into Ottoman territory, and their purchase of land, were forbidden. In July 1882 the rule was refined: Jews were allowed to settle anywhere in the empire except Palestine, provided they took Ottoman nationality [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014]. A reference summary drawing on Neville Mandel's 1974 study dates the government's recognition of the new wave to November 1882, and records that restrictions were imposed despite a Sultan's permit issued in May of that year. It adds that in 1882 the American minister in Constantinople, Lew Wallace, was told that Jews would be welcome anywhere in the empire except Palestine [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki_bans] [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki_bans]. The two accounts differ on the month the government acted; they agree on the shape of the policy. Jews could become Ottoman subjects and live elsewhere in the empire. On this reading, what the Porte would not accept was a concentration of foreign Jews in one district.

The instrument was the pilgrimage. Barın writes that from 1882 only pilgrims were admitted to Palestine; from 1884 Jewish visitors needed visas from Ottoman consulates in their home countries; and pilgrim stays were limited to one month, later extended to three for Jews under foreign protection, whose passports were held against a red permit card [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014]. The date of the red card is uncertain. Barın's own footnote says the card system was implemented from 1900, and the British and American diplomatic correspondence of 1887–88 documents the one-month and three-month limits without mentioning a card at all [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014] [cite:rs_rd0_cojs_moore1887] [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_may]. The safest reading is that the time limits are documented for the 1880s and the card belongs to a later stage.

The rules can be watched in operation through the consuls who had to deal with them. On 5 March 1887 the British consul in Jerusalem, Noel Temple Moore, reported to his ambassador that foreign Jews were "only allowed to come on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem for one month." The governor of Jerusalem gave three reasons: poverty, the overcrowding of the Jewish quarter, and public health. The ambassador, Sir William White, replied on 19 October 1887 that the rule was "ipso facto null and void" for persons under British protection, citing the Capitulations, the treaty privileges of foreigners in the Ottoman Empire [cite:rs_rd0_cojs_moore1887]. Britain had already protested to the Porte in September 1887 [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_may].

The American record adds the Porte's own explanation. On 28 January 1888 the United States minister, Oscar Straus, reported that the Porte had limited the stay of foreign Jews at Jerusalem to one month and planned to extend it to three. The Grand Vizier gave him two reasons: tensions with Christians at Easter, and a rumor that Jews meant to re-establish a Jewish kingdom around Jerusalem. The American consul reported that a shipload of Russian Jews had been refused landing at Jaffa about two years earlier [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_jan]. An Ottoman note of 2 March 1888 then set the rule in writing: foreign Jews would be admitted only on passports stating a pilgrimage, and for at most three months. On 19 May 1888 Straus told the State Department that the United States could not accept passport rules tied to religion, which he called "utterly repugnant" to the American Constitution [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_may].

The exchange shows the structure of the dispute. The Porte framed its rule as public order and public health, and, to the Grand Vizier, as a guard against a political project. The Western governments did not argue for Jewish settlement in Palestine. They argued against a rule that treated their citizens differently because of their religion. Britain and the United States reached the same position from their own legal commitments; these papers show no sign of a shared plan, only a shared principle.

In the early 1890s the contest moved from entry to land. On 14 June 1893 the Foreign Office instructed its ambassador on petitions from British Jews in Jerusalem against regulations, in the Office's words, "Prohibiting Jews from buying & selling real Estate in Palestine." It held that rules aimed at Jews for their religion were inconsistent with Article 62 of the Treaty of Berlin [cite:rs_rd0_cojs_fo1893]. That article, agreed in 1878, declared that "in no part of the Ottoman Empire shall difference of religion be alleged against any person" [cite:rs_rd0_fo1893_berlin62]; its origins belong to [scene:rd0_tanzimat|the reform era]. On 29 July 1893 the American minister reported the Grand Vizier's assurance that earlier restrictions "had been modified" and that the government's aim was to prevent "the alienation of the land to nonresidents" and to speculators [cite:rs_rd0_frus1893_terrell].

The assurance did not last. An Ottoman note of 27 March 1894, quoted by the American minister in a dispatch of 1898, forbade Jewish immigrants to acquire real property in Palestine "for political reasons." Its stated object was "the prevention of the permanent establishment in Palestine of Jewish immigrants." The rule applied to native and foreign Jews alike, but a purchase could go ahead on a consular certificate stating that the buyer was not in a barred class [cite:rs_rd0_frus1898_angell]. The note is the clearest single statement of Ottoman purpose in this period. It named the concern as political, it named the group, and it built in an exception.

When the land ban began is disputed, and the sources give three answers. A reference summary, citing the historians Ocak (2002) and Porath (1977), dates it to 1892 and says it applied even to Ottoman Jewish subjects [cite:rs_rd0_landpurchase_wiki_bans]. Barın describes a decree of 1893 for Jewish immigrants in Jerusalem, extended the following April to all Jews [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014]. The text that American diplomats treated as operative is the note of 27 March 1894, and in July 1893 the Grand Vizier had said that earlier restrictions were already modified [cite:rs_rd0_frus1898_angell] [cite:rs_rd0_frus1893_terrell]. These are not necessarily contradictory: a regional rule of 1892 or 1893, softened in mid-1893 and then generalized in 1894, would fit all three. The documents in hand do not settle it, and a firm's complaint of March 1893, discussed below, shows that even contemporaries disagreed about whether a ban was in force.

The reason the rules leaked lay in the empire's treaty obligations. Barın finds that enforcement was undermined by the Capitulations and the protégé system, which let foreign Jews live in Palestine under European consular protection; Britain had declared in 1847 that its consulates would protect Jews whose own states did not. Despite the bans, she concludes, more than 50,000 Jews settled [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014]. Corruption is also named, in one account, alongside foreign consular protection as a reason the rules were enforced unevenly. The mechanism, then, ran like this. The Porte could issue notes. Local officials could refuse a landing at Jaffa or block a sale. But a Jew who held a British, American or other foreign passport could appeal to a consul, the consul could appeal to an ambassador, and the ambassador could invoke treaties the Porte had signed. On this record, a Jew without a foreign protector was the most exposed to the rules and a protected foreigner the least, and the settlements grew through the gaps.

The same years saw the first organized response from the Arab population of Palestine. Emanuel Beška, in a 2007 study of Arab responses to Zionism before 1908, dates modern Jewish immigration and colonization to the 1880s and finds that Arab opposition took time to form. The first reactions came from peasants, then from artisans and merchants whose livelihoods were affected [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007].

The first political act on record came in 1891. According to a reference timeline citing Neville Mandel's The Arabs and Zionism before World War I, on 24 June 1891 Arab notables in Jerusalem petitioned the Grand Vizier to prohibit Russian Jews from entering Palestine and from buying land there [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki]. A 1978 review of Mandel's book in The Detroit Jewish News describes the same action differently, as a protest telegram from Arab leaders in Jerusalem and Jaffa calling for an end to Jewish immigration and land purchase, sent "barely a decade after the arrival of the Bilu'im," the BILU pioneers [cite:rs_rd0_djn_1978]. A Jewish Agency historical overview agrees that Jerusalem notables made the request in 1891 and says it was "repeated time and again" [cite:rs_rd0_jafi_opposition]. Whether it was a petition or a telegram, and whether Jaffa's leaders joined it, the sources leave open. Its text, its signatories and their number are not available in the material this book could reach.

The most persistent opponent in the record was the mufti of Jerusalem, Muhammad Tahir al-Husayni. Beška finds that he followed Jewish immigration "since the 1880s" and took an active stance against it [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007]. He should not be confused with an earlier mufti of the city, Tahir Afandi al-Husayni, who acted as a mediator in the revolt of 1834 and was later exiled [cite:rs_rd0_rood_1834]; the sources name them separately. On 15 March 1893 the firm J. Frutiger & Co. complained to the German consul that the governor of Jerusalem and a "Commission for Sale" chaired by al-Husayni were blocking land sales to Jews. The firm said the ban had been "abolished some time ago"; the mufti held that it remained in force under new regulations. On 12 May 1893 the consul complained to his ambassador of a campaign to prompt Muslim petitions for a comprehensive ban [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007]. Here the Ottoman rules and the Arab response meet in a single office: a local religious official sat on a commission that applied the state's land policy, read that policy strictly, while a European consul complained of a campaign to prompt petitions that would make it stricter still.

Opposition also began to appear in print. In 1895 Najib al-Hajj, editor of the Cairo paper Abu al-Hol, accused Jewish colonists of taking the means of livelihood of Arabs, according to a related paper excerpted with Beška's article [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007]. Just after the period of this chapter, in 1897, a committee chaired by al-Husayni put land sales to Jews in the Jerusalem district on hold for several years [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007].

Whether this opposition was political is a real dispute among the sources, and each side has evidence. The Jewish Agency overview describes the earlier local opposition as sporadic attacks and disputes over land and says it is "doubtful whether this opposition had any political connotations" [cite:rs_rd0_jafi_opposition]. The strongest form of that case is that peasants and merchants who clashed with the colonies were defending their own livelihoods, case by case, rather than pursuing a political program. The other side, drawing on Mandel as cited and on Beška, holds that the opposition was directed at the state from the start: the 1891 approach to the Grand Vizier and the mufti's campaign of 1893 over land sales were acts aimed at changing Ottoman policy, made by notables through the government's own channels [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_djn_1978] [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007]. The two positions are closer than they look. The Jewish Agency overview itself records the 1891 request and its repetition; the dispute is over whether the earlier peasant clashes carried political meaning, not over whether the notables acted. On the evidence here, by 1891 at the latest Jerusalem notables were making a demand of their government about immigration and land; whether to call it political is the point in dispute.

Herzl entered this setting in 1896. He sought a charter from the Sultan from that year, offering to help consolidate the Ottoman public debt. His diary records a message relayed from Abdul Hamid II that the Sultan could not sell even a foot of the land, because it belonged not to him but to his people [cite:w_herzl]. Herzl met the Sultan only in May 1901, and came away without a charter [cite:w_abdulhamid]; that story belongs to [scene:tk_s02|the Sultan's refusal]. The legal form through which land was later bought in large tracts, the Ottoman Land Code of 1858, is the subject of [scene:ot_s01|the section on that law].

Several matters remain open. The exact start of the land-sale ban is not settled by the documents in hand. The red permit card may belong to 1900 rather than the 1880s. The text, signatories and authors of the 1891 protest have not been reached in this research, so its form and whether Jaffa joined remain uncertain. There is no count here of how many Jews were turned away under the rules, as against Barın's estimate of more than 50,000 who settled regardless. Her study covers 1882 to 1920, but the passage that gives the figure does not say which years it counts, so it cannot be read as a total for this chapter's period alone. And whether the peasant clashes of the 1880s carried political meaning is a question on which the sources disagree and the material here cannot decide.
