Warwick, the CCRU and the Idea of Acceleration

Nick Land is the writer who gave the Dark Enlightenment its name, and he arrived at it from a direction no one would predict from the name alone. He was not a libertarian economist or a blogger in Silicon Valley. He was an English academic philosopher who spent the 1990s at the University of Warwick writing about Kant, Nietzsche and the French philosopher Georges Bataille, and who built, with a circle of students, a body of experimental writing about technology, capital and the future that still has admirers on the far left. This section follows that path: the Warwick years, the research group known as the CCRU, the idea that came to be called accelerationism, the split of that idea into left and right versions, and Land's move to Shanghai, where he has written ever since.

Warwick and the CCRU

Land, born in 1962, took his doctorate at the University of Essex and taught philosophy at Warwick from 1987 until he resigned in 1998. [cite:de4_wiki_land] The journalist Park MacDougald, writing in 2015, described his reputation there for unorthodox teaching and odd personal habits, and the mythology that gathered around him; the critic Mark Fisher later wrote that "Land was our Nietzsche." [cite:de4_awl] [cite:de4_wiki_land]

The Cybernetic Culture Research Unit, the CCRU, was founded at Warwick in 1995 by the cultural theorist Sadie Plant; after she resigned in 1997, Land took it over. [cite:de4_haider] It was less a department than a collective of graduate students and fellow travellers who mixed philosophy, cybernetics, cyberpunk fiction and the dance music of the period into what they called "theory-fiction." Robin Mackay, one of Land's students, recalled him living in his office and rarely sleeping. Land later claimed to be inhabited by named "entities" and, looking back on that writing, told Mackay, "I don't even remember writing half of those things." [cite:de4_haider] The group continued to meet under Land after he left Warwick, and dissolved in the early 2000s. [cite:de4_wiki_land] [cite:de4_wiki_accel] Its texts were later collected as CCRU Writings 1997–2003 (2015), and the small press Urbanomic published Land's collected essays, Fanged Noumena, in 2011; in 2014 Mackay and Armen Avanessian placed Land in the "Cyberculture" section of their anthology #Accelerate, beside Plant and Iain Hamilton Grant. [cite:de4_wiki_land] [cite:de4_frieze]

What accelerationism meant

Accelerationism, in its original philosophical sense, is the idea that the way beyond capitalism runs through it, by speeding up the processes it sets loose rather than resisting them. Its usual starting point is a passage in Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari's Anti-Oedipus (1972) calling, after Nietzsche, to "accelerate the process." [cite:de4_wiki_accel] The word itself came later. The British philosopher Benjamin Noys named the tendency in his 2010 book The Persistence of the Negative, as a description of a line of thinkers he criticised. [cite:de4_haider] [cite:de4_wiki_accel]

Land's version was the most radical. In his writing of the 1990s, capital and technology form a loop that feeds itself, gathering speed until it no longer needs, or tolerates, human direction; he wrote of "an invasion from the future by an artificial intelligent space." [cite:de4_haider] The reader of this book should hold on to one feature of that vision, because it separates Land from Yarvin. Land's engine does not want a king. It wants a process that escapes control, and the human institutions that try to steer it, democratic or otherwise, appear as brakes.

Left and right: the 2013 split

In May 2013 two young British theorists, Alex Williams and Nick Srnicek, published "#Accelerate: Manifesto for an Accelerationist Politics" on the website Critical Legal Thinking. [cite:de4_clt_date] It claimed the idea for the left. The manifesto credited Land with capturing the dynamic "most acutely," with "a myopic yet hypnotising belief" that "capitalist speed alone could generate a global transition towards unparalleled technological singularity." [cite:de4_accel_manifesto] Their own programme was the reverse of Land's: "the material platform of neoliberalism does not need to be destroyed. It needs to be repurposed towards common ends." [cite:de4_accel_manifesto] Where Land saw a machine that would carry humanity past politics, they saw machinery that a democratic left could seize and steer.

Land rejected the left version. Haider records his verdict, "Neoreaction is Accelerationism with a flat tire," and his casting of the left as a "decelerator." [cite:de4_haider] Williams, for his part, described Land's late work as a "sick perversity." [cite:de4_haider] The philosopher Ray Brassier, whose own work draws on Land's, said in 2017 that Land had gone from arguing "Politics is dead" twenty years earlier to "completely old-fashioned, standard reactionary stuff." [cite:de4_wiki_land] From 2013, then, there were two accelerationisms, each claiming the same ancestry and each treating the other as a betrayal of it.

Three things that share the name and are not the same

Two later uses of the word must be kept apart from Land's. The first is "effective accelerationism," or e/acc, a slogan that spread among technology investors in the 2020s for pursuing artificial intelligence and other technologies with as few restraints as possible. [cite:de4_wiki_accel] It borrows the word and some of the mood. It is a distinct current, and this book treats it in [scene:de16_neighbours_not_the_same|Part Sixteen].

The second is a terror usage. Neo-fascist and white-supremacist networks use "accelerationism" to mean hastening racial conflict and social collapse through violence, assassination and sabotage, in the hope of building a white ethnostate on the ruins. [cite:de4_wiki_accel] The writer Zack Beauchamp has suggested that the label reached those networks through the overlap between Land's later politics and the online far right, while noting that Land's technocapitalist ideas are otherwise unrelated to that programme. [cite:de4_wiki_accel] No text by Land discussed in this book calls for violence of that kind, and the terror usage is never meant in this book when the word appears without qualification.

Shanghai

After leaving Warwick, Land dropped from view. He resurfaced in the 2000s as a journalist in Shanghai, writing opinion pieces and travel guides, and has lived there since. [cite:de4_haider] [cite:de4_spectator] From Shanghai he wrote his best-known political text, the serial "The Dark Enlightenment," published in ten instalments between 2 March and 20 July 2012 and gathered on its own website. [cite:de4_land_de] [cite:de4_awl] Its argument is summarised in the next section.

His writing from 2013 to 2017 was later collected as Xenosystems and published by Passage Press. [cite:de4_spectator] In October 2025 The Spectator described him as in his sixties, still in Shanghai, and noted that the investor Marc Andreessen lists him as a "patron saint" of his thinking. [cite:de4_spectator] In that interview Land called China's combination of communism and capitalism "the greatest political engine of social and economic development the world has ever known." [cite:de4_spectator] The citation by Andreessen is a documented tie of reading, not of policy; it shows that an investor counts Land among his influences, which is Andreessen's own statement, and it shows nothing about what Land has done, which on the record is to write.

The path matters for what follows. Land came to the Dark Enlightenment as a philosopher of runaway change who had already broken with the academic left, and he adopted Yarvin's corporate state as a political form for an engine he had been describing for twenty years. [cite:de4_awl] [cite:de4_land_de] His version of the ideas is darker in its metaphysics and harder in its writing about race than the program he borrowed, and it is less interested in who sits at the top.
