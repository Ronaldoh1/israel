Kennedy and Ben-Gurion at the Waldorf-Astoria, 30 May 1961

Four days after the White House received its scientists' report on Dimona, Kennedy met Ben-Gurion for the first time. The meeting had been sought by Israel through the spring, and in the National Security Archive editors' reading the visit to the reactor was its tacit condition, as told in [scene:cw4_kennedy_dimona|Kennedy's first months]. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] It took place in New York, not at the White House, and it was fitted into Kennedy's journey to Vienna for his summit with Khrushchev. Three records of it survive, and they do not say quite the same thing about the subject that mattered most.

Who was in the room. The two men met in the President's suite at the Waldorf-Astoria on 30 May 1961, from 4:45 to 6:15 in the afternoon. With them were Israel's ambassador, Avraham Harman; Myer Feldman; and Assistant Secretary of State Phillips Talbot. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_doc9b] The meeting covered three subjects: the reactor, weapons, and the Palestinian refugees.

Dimona. In the official American memorandum of the conversation, Kennedy described Dimona as "a large reactor, with plutonium producing capability." Ben-Gurion replied that Israel's "main—and for the time being, only—purpose is this," by which he meant economic development, including "the economic desalinization of sea water." He added that "in three or four years we might have need for a plant to process plutonium." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] Kennedy said that two American scientists "had given him a good report of it," and he asked whether neutral scientists could observe it as well. Ben-Gurion asked, "who are really neutrals these days?" Kennedy suggested "the Scandinavians and the Swiss," and Ben-Gurion said he had no objection. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon]

The exchange is short, and almost every phrase in it carries weight. "For the time being" left the future open in the Prime Minister's own words. A plant to process plutonium, in three or four years, was the facility that the Joint Atomic Energy Intelligence Committee had reported on 2 December 1960 and that the May visitors had not mentioned; here Ben-Gurion named it as a possible future need rather than an existing structure. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2024] And the answer to Kennedy's request for neutral observers was an agreement in principle with no date attached.

Three records of one exchange. The official memorandum is not the only version. The National Security Archive has published a draft of the same memorandum, written before it was edited, and it differs from the official versions. According to the archive, the draft includes Ben-Gurion's tacit acknowledgment of a weapons potential and a statement suggesting that Israel kept its freedom of action on eventual reprocessing. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_doc9b] The Israeli minutes of the meeting record Ben-Gurion as saying that "for the time being, the only purposes" were "for peace," and then: "we will see what happens in the Middle East." [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_doc9b]

Two readings follow from the three texts. On the first, which the official memorandum and the Israeli minutes both support, Ben-Gurion gave Kennedy a peaceful purpose qualified only by "for the time being," and a possible reprocessing plant years off. On the second, which rests on the unedited draft as the National Security Archive presents it, he went further: he tacitly acknowledged that the reactor gave Israel a weapons potential and reserved Israel's freedom to reprocess. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_doc9b] The Israeli minutes' "we will see what happens in the Middle East" can be read as closer to the draft than to the edited text, since it made Israel's purpose conditional on the region. What the record does not show is who edited the American draft, or why the passages were changed.

Weapons in the region. The conversation then turned to Egypt and to weapons. Ben-Gurion said that "in ten or fifteen years the Egyptians presumably could achieve it themselves," and that "If they should defeat us they would do to the Jews what Hitler did." Kennedy answered that "the UAR would not permit Israel to go ahead in this field without getting into it itself." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] The two statements frame the dilemma from both sides. Ben-Gurion's was the case for Israel's security put at its starkest, in terms of Hitler's destruction of the Jews, against a neighbour whose capacity he expected to grow. Kennedy's was the case against proliferation: an Israeli nuclear program would not stay Israel's alone, because the United Arab Republic would follow.

The Hawk. Israel had asked for missiles. Kennedy replied that "while the Hawk is a defensive weapon it is also a missile," that "we are reluctant to introduce missiles into the Middle East," and that "we do not want to see Israel at a disadvantage." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] In May 1961 that was a refusal with a promise inside it. How the refusal became an offer fifteen months later is told in [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision].

The refugees. On the Palestinian refugees of 1948, Ben-Gurion said the Arab states "regard the refugees as the best weapon at hand" and "don't care what happens to people." Kennedy referred to the United States paying 70% of UNRWA's costs. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] Each man spoke from his own position. Ben-Gurion's charge put responsibility for the refugees' condition on the Arab governments; it is his statement, and the memorandum records no reply to it from any Arab government, nor any voice of the refugees themselves. Kennedy's figure put on the table what the refugees cost the United States, and so why Washington had an interest in a settlement. Neither statement settles who was responsible for the refugees' plight, a question this library examines in its account of 1948 and after.

How much weight did Dimona carry? The National Security Archive's editors judge that the reactor took "no more than ten to fifteen minutes" of a meeting of about an hour and a half, and that Kennedy "exerted no new pressure." They quote a biographer: "Ben Gurion felt relieved. The reactor was saved, at least for the time being." On their reading, the confrontation over Dimona was postponed for two years. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] This is the editors' assessment. The documents support its core: Kennedy cited his scientists' "good report," asked for neutral observers, and accepted Ben-Gurion's agreement in principle without terms or dates. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon]

Briefing the Arab states. Washington also had to explain the meeting to Arab governments. Talbot briefed the representatives of ten Arab states afterwards; the memorandum of the briefing was drafted on 7 June 1961. He told them that Ben-Gurion's assurance that the program was "for peaceful purposes only" was firm, and that Kennedy had expressed concern about proliferation and satisfaction with the assurances. The Sudanese ambassador asked about "Israel's second reactor." The Iraqi ambassador asked about American arms aid to Israel; according to the memorandum, Talbot said that question was not pursued at the meeting. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d59] On the refugees, the Department's public statement said the United States "will fully support moves made by the Commission," meaning the Palestine Conciliation Commission, and Ben-Gurion's reported comment on the Commission's effort was "It's worth a try." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d59]

Set beside the record of 30 May, the briefing was a simplification. Talbot told the Arab representatives of a firm assurance "for peaceful purposes only"; the official memorandum had Ben-Gurion speaking of a purpose that was peaceful "for the time being," and a reprocessing plant possibly to come. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d59] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] The briefing memorandum, as summarized in the sources used here, also leaves unclear whether "the meeting" at which the Iraqi question was not pursued was the Waldorf meeting or the briefing itself; the Waldorf memorandum does record Kennedy discussing the Hawk. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d59] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon]

The second visit, September 1962. On 17 June 1961 a circular telegram reported the results of the May inspection to American posts abroad. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d87] The next visit came more than a year later and on Israel's timing. On 26 September 1962 Israeli officials took two visiting scientists of the Atomic Energy Commission to Dimona without warning, for a tour of 40 minutes. A State Department circular drafted on 24 October 1962 reported "no evidence of preparation for nuclear weapons production" and "Work on reactor progressing normally." [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d87] The same circular instructed the embassy in Cairo, in connection with the Cuban crisis, to renew to Nasser the assurances about Dimona given in June 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d87] The assurance Washington had passed to Arab governments after the Waldorf meeting was thus being renewed to Egypt during the Cuban crisis, on the strength of an unannounced 40-minute tour.

How many visits? The two visits of 1961 and 1962 were the start of a series whose size is itself counted differently. Cohen and Burr give eight American visits between 1961 and 1969, seven of them after Kennedy forced regular visits in 1963. The Federation of American Scientists gives seven during the 1960s and says they were tightly controlled by Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] The sources used here do not reconcile the difference of one.

The gaps in the record are specific. It does not show who edited the draft memorandum of the Waldorf meeting, or why. It does not record any follow-up on the Scandinavian and Swiss observers to whom Ben-Gurion agreed in principle. It does not say whether the 40-minute tour of September 1962 included the place where the December 1960 intelligence report had located a separation plant. And the Arab briefing records the Sudanese ambassador's question about a "second reactor" but, in the sources used here, not the answer. By the end of 1962 the arrangement that began at the Waldorf was in place: Israeli assurances, occasional American visits on Israeli terms, and American assurances passed on to Arab capitals. It held until Kennedy tried to change its terms in 1963, as told in [scene:s11b|Kennedy and Dimona, 1963].
