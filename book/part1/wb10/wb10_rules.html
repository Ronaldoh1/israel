The Rule

Article 49. The Fourth Geneva Convention, adopted in 1949 after the Second World War, governs how an occupying power treats an occupied population. Its Article 49 forbids "individual or mass forcible transfers" of the occupied population, "regardless of their motive," and its last paragraph adds: "The Occupying Power shall not deport or transfer parts of its own civilian population into the territory it occupies." [cite:wb_fmep_art49] Part Three showed that in September 1967 Israel's own Foreign Ministry legal adviser, Theodor Meron, warned that civilian settlement in the territories would breach this provision. [cite:wb_gorenberg_meron]

Israel's Position

Israel's official position is that the convention does not apply to the West Bank as a matter of law, because the territory was not the recognized sovereign territory of another state when Israel took it in 1967; Jordan's annexation, as Part Two showed, was recognized by almost no one. Israel has pledged to comply with its "humanitarian and customary provisions." [cite:wb_cow_israelview] In 2012 a committee appointed by Prime Minister Netanyahu and chaired by a former Supreme Court justice, Edmund Levy, went further: it concluded that Israel's presence in the West Bank is not an occupation in the classic sense, that the settlements are legal, and that unauthorized outposts should be legalized. The government never formally adopted the report; the US State Department answered that it did not accept "the legitimacy of continued Israeli settlement activity." [cite:wb_wiki_levy]

The United States, 1978–2026

American positions have changed with administrations. In April 1978 the State Department's legal adviser, Herbert Hansell, wrote to Congress that "the establishment of the civilian settlements in those territories is inconsistent with international law," reading Article 49 to cover any transfer the occupying power assists, whatever its purpose. [cite:wb_jvl_hansell] In 1981 President Reagan said the settlements were "not illegal." In 1991 Secretary of State James Baker called them "an obstacle to peace," and when asked whether the administration considered them illegal, said: "That is not our policy." [cite:wb_tablet_forty] In November 2019 Secretary of State Mike Pompeo announced that the United States no longer viewed the settlements as inconsistent with international law; in February 2024 Secretary of State Antony Blinken restored the earlier position, calling them "inconsistent with international law." [cite:wb_axios_blinken] In its second term, the Trump administration revoked the sanctions on violent settlers, [cite:wb_ofac_20250124] and in February 2025 imposed sanctions on officials of the International Criminal Court.
