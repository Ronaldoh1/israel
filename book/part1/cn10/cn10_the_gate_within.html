As Above, So Below: the Gate Outside and the Gate Within

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The phrase "as above, so below" is a modern paraphrase, popularised by Helena Blavatsky, of a line in the Emerald Tablet, a Hermetic text whose oldest version is Arabic, from the late eighth or early ninth century; Isaac Newton translated the line as "That which is below is like that which is above". [cite:de39_wiki_emerald] The inner traditions use it as a rule of reading: the structure of the world mirrors the structure of the self, and the same pattern can be found at every scale.

Applied to this book, the rule turns its history into a mirror. Parts One to Nine described an outer gate. From Irenaeus onward, the authority to say which books were scripture was held by an office, and that office drew money, law, land and finally a list of banned books that lasted until 1966. [cite:cn_iren_3_3] [cite:cn_va_index1966] The reading proposes that the same gate stands inside every reader. It is the part of the self that would rather be told than know, that hands its judgement to an authority, and that treats the inner sayings as statements about someone else.

The history also shows how such a gate falls, and here the reading draws on a pattern of systems. Every hierarchy rests on its bottom layer. The structure described in this book rested on readers who could not read the book for themselves: the Council of Toulouse in 1229 ordered vernacular translations destroyed, England's Constitutions of Oxford required permission for any new translation, and William Tyndale was strangled and burned in 1536 for printing an English New Testament. [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] [cite:cn_wiki_oxford] [cite:cn_brit_tyndale] When the bottom layer gained the book, through printing and translation, the structure above it could not hold in the same form: Tyndale's English became the model for English Bibles for nearly four hundred years. [cite:cn_brit_tyndale] The office then made a concession that changed less than it seemed. When the Index of Prohibited Books was abolished as church law in 1966, the Vatican declared that it remained morally binding, though without the force of law. [cite:cn_va_index1966]

Read through the rule of correspondence, the inner lesson is the same as the outer one. A gate held by others falls when the person beneath it reads for themselves; a gate held within falls the same way. The Gospel of Thomas puts the condition in one phrase: the kingdom is found "When you come to know yourselves". [cite:gn_ut_thomas] The canon's history, from the first lists to the end of the Index, is in this reading a picture at the scale of a civilisation of what each reader does at the scale of one life.

The record does not show that anyone in this history thought of it this way. The bishops, the translators and the censors acted for their own documented reasons, set out in the parts before this one. Correspondence is a way of reading patterns, and it finds them wherever it looks; that is exactly why it cannot be evidence. It is offered as a lens, held up to a history that stands without it.
