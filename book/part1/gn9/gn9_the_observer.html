A Note on the Observer

Gnosis means knowing, and modern readers often reach for physics to explain it. A common claim runs: quantum physics has proved that the observer creates reality, so consciousness is the ground of the world, as the Gnostics taught. This note sets out what physics actually shows, because the reading in this part is stronger if it does not lean on a claim the science does not make. The book The Observer, later on this shelf, takes the subject further.

The puzzle is real. In quantum mechanics a system is described as a combination of possible states until it is measured, when one definite result appears. Why and how a definite result appears is called the measurement problem, and it is unsolved. [cite:ob_sep_decoherence] But "observer" in physics does not mean a mind. When a quantum system interacts with its surroundings, with stray particles, air or a detector, the interference between its possible states is suppressed, a process called decoherence. The environment, in the words of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, interacts with the system "in such a way that it could in principle be used as a measuring apparatus." [cite:ob_sep_decoherence] No conscious being is needed for this. Decoherence does not by itself solve the measurement problem either; it explains why we do not see mixtures of results, not why we see one. [cite:ob_sep_decoherence]

The idea that consciousness causes the result does have a history in physics. John von Neumann in 1932 showed that the point of "collapse" could be placed anywhere along the chain from apparatus to observer; Fritz London and Edmond Bauer in 1939 gave the observer's mind a central role; and Eugene Wigner proposed in the 1960s that consciousness is what collapses the wave function. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc] Wigner himself abandoned the idea in the late 1970s and later called it "solipsism", crediting work on decoherence for his change of mind. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc] The view is described as "largely discarded by modern physicists"; in a 2011 poll of 33 researchers in the foundations of quantum theory, two said the observer plays a distinguished physical role such as consciousness causing collapse. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc]

Two famous experiments are often cited. John Wheeler proposed in 1978 a "delayed-choice" test in which the decision whether to look for a wave or a particle is made only after a photon is already on its way; a team led by Vincent Jacques carried out a version of it in 2007, and the results match what quantum mechanics predicts. [cite:ob_wiki_delayed] Physicists draw from them the conclusion that no simple picture of a photon as definitely a wave or a particle in flight can be right, not that minds send signals into the past. [cite:ob_wiki_delayed] Entanglement is also real: the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger for experiments with entangled photons showing the violation of Bell inequalities. [cite:ob_nobel_2022] And the theory that consciousness arises from quantum processes in the brain's microtubules, proposed by Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff in the 1990s, remains contested; Max Tegmark argued in 2000 that the brain is too warm and wet for such states to last. [cite:ob_wiki_orchor]

The honest conclusion is that physics does not confirm gnosis, and does not refute it either. It describes how measurements behave; it does not say that knowing creates the world. The Gnostic texts spoke of a different kind of knowing, the recognition of one's own origin, and their claim stands or falls on its own ground. The reading in this part may use the observer as an image, the watcher who changes what is watched by watching, but it borrows the image, not the authority.
