Rome's First Attempt: The Jesuit Patriarch, 1622–1634

For a thousand years after Chalcedon, the Ethiopian church stood outside the reach of Rome. Contact resumed in the fifteenth century: in 1439 Pope Eugene IV sent a message to the Ethiopian emperor Zara Yaqob during the Council of Florence, which sought the union of the Eastern churches with Rome. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] Union did not come from councils. It came, briefly, from the conversion of an emperor, and its collapse shaped Ethiopia's relations with the West for two centuries.

In the early seventeenth century Jesuit missionaries gained influence at the court of the emperor Susenyos. The Jesuit Afonso Mendes was appointed Latin Patriarch of Ethiopia in 1622; he reached the country in June 1625. On 11 February 1626 Susenyos and Mendes publicly acknowledged the primacy of the Roman see, and Catholicism became the state religion. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] For the first time since Frumentius, the head of the Ethiopian church would not be a bishop sent from Alexandria but one sent from Rome.

What Mendes did next is the core of the record, and it shows what Roman authority meant in practice. He condemned the Ethiopian observance of the Saturday Sabbath, kept alongside Sunday, and the church's frequent fasts. He ordained Catholic priests and had Latin texts translated. According to his Wikipedia biography he told Ethiopian women that their children were in hell because they had been baptised in the Orthodox church. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] Many Ethiopians were forced to convert by threats, imprisonment and battle, and rebellion followed. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] [scene:vt27_how_the_west_lost_enoch|The Vatican Architecture, Part Twenty-Seven]

The emperor gave way. On 14 June 1632 Susenyos rescinded his edict, and Fasilides, who succeeded him, exiled Mendes in 1634. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] It was the same Fasilides who rebuilt and enlarged the church of Mary of Zion at Aksum, the church that claims the Ark. [cite:et_wiki_zion] The record of Mendes's mission does not mention the canon or the Book of Enoch, and there is no evidence that Rome tried to remove books from the Ethiopian Bible. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] What Rome tried to change was the practice, the calendar and, above all, the authority: who would consecrate the church's bishops and whose primacy its emperor would acknowledge.

The episode fits the pattern this shelf has traced, with one difference. In Europe, the Roman office that defined the faith was backed by states that enforced it. In Ethiopia, the same office was imposed through one emperor's conversion, and when the emperor's own people rose against it, there was no state willing to enforce it any longer. The Ethiopian church kept its books, its Sabbath and its Alexandrian bishop because the throne, in the end, chose its people over Rome. A small Ethiopian Catholic Church survives today, in communion with Rome and using the Ge'ez rite. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic]
