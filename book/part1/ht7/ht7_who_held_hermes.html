Who Held Hermes

The Hermetic tradition was held by a different kind of keeper from the others on this shelf: not chiefly by churches or orders, but by patrons, princes and kings, because its arts touched what they cared most about, prestige, health and money.

Three findings stand out. The first is that power valued the Hermetic arts for what they promised power: a family's prestige as the restorer of ancient wisdom, an emperor's hope of gold, a queen's need for learned counsel. [cite:ht_sep_ficino] [cite:ht_quil_rudolf] [cite:ht_brit_dee] The second is that the same power policed the arts when they threatened it: the crown banned unlicensed gold to protect the coin, and the Inquisition burned a magus who would not submit. [cite:ht_loc_multipliers] [cite:ht_brit_bruno] The third is that the tradition's claim to authority, its great age, was false, and was shown to be false by a scholar's reading of the texts. [cite:ht_enc_hermeticism]

The pattern of the shelf holds, with one turn. In The Canon the gate was an office that held texts; here the gate was the treasury and the court, and the texts were sought because they promised what a treasury and a court want. The inner traditions that Part Eight reads promise something else, a change in the one who works, and that is the part of Hermes that no crown could license or ban.
