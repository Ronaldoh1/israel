The Leading Question

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The Malleus Maleficarum recommended leading questions and deception to obtain confessions, and the descriptions of sabbaths that fill the trial records came from courts that followed such manuals. [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus] [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] In Friuli the benandanti described night battles for the harvest, and over decades of questioning their testimony was reshaped into the sabbath their judges expected. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles] In the Basque country the inquisitor Salazar collected nearly 2,000 confessions, most of them later retracted, and found no objective proof of anything confessed. [cite:vt30_wiki_basque]

The inner reading sees in this a lesson about the mind as much as about courts. A question asked often enough, by someone with power, produces the answer it expects; the person questioned comes to describe the world in the questioner's words. Every inner tradition on this shelf has a name for that danger: the voice that is not one's own taking the place of one's own experience. The benandanti's first account, of fennel stalks and the caul, was their own. The account of the Devil was the inquisitors'.

Salazar's rule is the reading's other half. He would sentence no one "unless the case can be proven with external and objective evidence". [cite:vt30_wiki_basque] Turned inward, that is discernment: the practice of asking, of any conviction one holds, whether it came from experience or was put there by a question. The inquisitor who stopped the burning in Spain did it by doubting the confessions his own office had produced.

The record limits the reading. Ginzburg's account of the benandanti is contested in its wider claims, and the reshaping of testimony is his reconstruction from the records. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles] The application to inner life is the reader's.
