Moscow Said Yes First

The popular memory of 1948 has two superpowers in fixed places: the United States as Israel's sponsor, and the Soviet bloc hostile from the start. For the years in which the state was actually made, the record runs the other way on the second point. Between May 1947 and May 1948 the Soviet Union backed a Jewish state earlier, and held to it more steadily, than Washington did. Its satellites voted for partition, it stood firm at the Security Council when the Americans retreated, it was the first government in the world to recognize Israel in law, and arms from a Soviet-bloc country reached the new state while an American embargo was in force. In December 1948 the bloc voted against the UN resolution on refugee return, and only in the 1950s did Moscow turn toward the Arab states.

The first move came at the special session of the General Assembly that Britain had requested. On 14 May 1947, the day before the Assembly created the Special Committee on Palestine [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop], the Soviet representative Andrei Gromyko addressed the session. He said the Soviet Union preferred a single Arab–Jewish state in which both peoples would have equal rights. But if that proved impossible to achieve, he said, partition into two states was a justifiable alternative [cite:israeled_gromyko_speech] [cite:israeled_gromyko_timeline]. Coming from Moscow, that was a reversal. For decades the Soviet state had opposed Zionism [cite:israeled_gromyko_speech]. Gromyko's speech did not endorse partition outright; it ranked it second. But it put the Soviet Union on record, before Washington had committed, as willing to accept a Jewish state.

Why Moscow changed course is not stated in any Soviet document among the sources used here. Analysts at the time and since have read the motive as a wish to remove British influence from the region [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel]. It remains an interpretation of Soviet interest, drawn from what Moscow did, rather than a finding from Moscow's own records. What it does explain is why the Soviet Union and the Zionist movement could find themselves on the same side without sharing any larger aim. This was convergence of interest, not alliance.

When the Assembly voted in November 1947, the Soviet Union and its satellites voted for partition [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel]. The test came four months later. In March 1948 the United States moved to retreat from partition toward a temporary United Nations trusteeship, a shift told in [scene:rd5_trusteeship|Washington's retreat, March 1948]. The Soviet Union did not follow. At the Security Council it stood behind partition, and Gromyko told the Council that the rapid creation of two states was the only way to reduce the bloodshed [cite:yated_stalin_israel] [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel].

On 14 May 1948 Israel declared its independence, and Truman's recognition followed the same day, but it was de facto recognition. On 17 May 1948, three days after independence, the Soviet Union became the first state to grant Israel full legal, or de jure, recognition [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel]. The Soviet recognition was thus the fuller of the two. The wider story of who recognized Israel, when and on what legal basis, is told in this chapter's section on recognition.

Recognition was not the only support that came from the Soviet side. Arms reached the new state from Czechoslovakia, a Soviet-bloc country, at a time when an American embargo was in force [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel]. The contracts, the airlift and their price are documented in [scene:cz_s01|the Czechoslovak pipeline]; the point here is that the first great power to recognize Israel in law and the government behind its Czechoslovak arms both stood on the same side of the Cold War line.

The first sign that the alignment had limits came seven months after independence. In December 1948 the Soviet bloc voted against United Nations Resolution 194, the resolution on the return of refugees [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli]. The sources used here do not explain the bloc's reasons for that vote, and they should not be supplied by guesswork. The resolution itself, and what followed from it, belong to the next stage of this book and are told in [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194]. From the 1950s, Moscow turned toward the Arab states [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli].

That later turn largely erased the earlier record from popular memory. The common account holds that Israel was created by American support while the Soviet bloc was hostile from the start. The documents support only half of it. American support mattered, but it wavered in March 1948. Soviet support came first, held through that wavering, and produced the first full recognition. In 1947 and 1948 both superpowers backed the new state, for different reasons, Moscow earlier and more firmly, apparently to push Britain out.

What the record does not settle is the question of motive. Gromyko's speech, the votes, the Security Council statement and the recognition are all documented acts. The reasoning behind them inside the Soviet leadership is not documented in the sources used here; the "remove Britain" explanation is the reading analysts have drawn from those acts. Nor do these sources show how far Soviet diplomacy and the Czechoslovak arms sales were coordinated as a single policy, rather than running in parallel. The first-to-recognize fact, by contrast, is not in doubt.
