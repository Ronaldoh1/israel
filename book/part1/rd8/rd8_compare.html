What the Other Homelands Show

Not one of the eight was an offer of a state, and on the record none could have become one by 1948. They fail in different ways, and the ways are the finding. Where land was plentiful, as in Argentina, Alaska and the Dominican Republic, the ruler offered refuge or citizenship, never self-rule, and refuge could be capped, as Roosevelt capped Alaska, or withdrawn, as Suriname's governor withdrew his offer in 1948. Where a ruler did grant autonomy, as Britain proposed for El Arish and East Africa, a neighbour's water or a settler population stood in the way, and in East Africa the land was the Maasai's. Where a government actually handed over a territory, in Birobidzhan, few Jews chose to live there, and most who came left. Madagascar was never an alternative at all: it was a plan by others to remove Jews from Europe, and in 1940 a plan for their deaths. [cite:a_wp_madagascar]

Palestine was the only land that passed the third test, a mass movement willing to go, and after 1917 it also had a great power willing to back it. It failed the second. In 1945 it had about 1,061,000 Muslims, 136,000 Christians and 554,000 Jews [cite:a_wp_mandpal], and in February 1947 Bevin told the Commons that the two sides' positions were an "irreconcilable conflict of principles." [cite:a_cojs_47] The conflict that followed came from that test, not from a lack of alternatives. Of the eight, the closest to a peaceful refuge was Suriname in 1947: approved by a government, aimed at undeveloped land, and planned for 30,000 people on leased land, not a state. Who lived in the area it targeted is not documented here, and would have had to be established before calling it empty.

A last question sits underneath the choice of Palestine: whose land it was to return to. The record holds two facts that are often set against each other, though both are true. A Jewish community never left. About 24,000 Jews lived in Palestine in 1882, among them Arabic-speaking Jews, the Musta'arabim, whose communities predated the coming of Islam, alongside Sephardic communities from the late Mamluk and early Ottoman periods and Ashkenazi immigrants of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [cite:m_wp_oldyishuv] Most of those who built the state, though, came from Europe, and genetic studies describe the largest group among them, the Ashkenazim, as a mixture of Middle Eastern and European ancestry. The Middle Eastern share is most likely Levantine and is estimated at anywhere from about a fifth to over half, depending on the method. The same studies find Ashkenazi, Sephardi and Mizrahi Jews closer to one another than to the peoples they lived among, and count Palestinians, Bedouin and Druze among their closest genetic neighbours. [cite:m_wp_genetics] Ancestry does not decide a claim to land, and this book does not use it to. What the record shows is narrower: neither the community that stayed nor the diaspora that returned was a stranger to the region, and the people already living there in 1947 had their own ties to it.
