IS-RA-EL: A Symbolic Reading of the Name

The previous section traced the name Israel to its documented roots: El, the Semitic word for God, joined to a root meaning to strive or prevail. This section offers something of a different kind. It sets out one symbolic reading of the name, drawn from esoteric traditions, the inner schools of religious thought. It is an interpretation laid over a documented text, not a finding of linguistics or of the archives, and it should be read that way. Critics dismiss it as mere wordplay with no connection to any religious tradition. They are partly right and partly wrong, and the line between the two is worth drawing carefully.

The reading splits the English spelling of the name into three parts. IS is taken to evoke Isis, the Egyptian goddess whom Hermetic and other inner traditions associate with a receptive, magnetic, lunar principle, what later traditions would call the divine feminine.[cite:wiki_isis_egyptian] RA is taken to evoke the Egyptian sun god, a radiant, generative principle that later traditions would call the divine masculine.[cite:wiki_ra_egyptian] EL is the one part that is genuine etymology: the actual ancient Semitic word for God.[cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] In this reading, El stands for the unified field above both poles. "Israel," so understood, names neither a place nor a people but a state of being, in which feminine and masculine are brought together under the divine. Its proponents connect this to the transformation that Genesis 32 describes in Jacob.

The limits of this reading need to be stated plainly. It works on the English spelling, not the Hebrew original. It does not claim that ancient Hebrew scribes hid the names of Egyptian gods in the word. It does not displace the documented derivation, which remains the scholarly linguistic record. What it proposes is a symbolic resonance. On this view, transformation traditions across the ancient Near East and Egypt shared a common structural language of polarity, integration and ascent, and the Jacob story reads coherently within that language, whether or not the etymology is literal.

Part of the reading, however, is not new at all. Mainstream Jewish commentary supports the idea that Jacob's story describes an inner transformation open to any person, not only the origin of a family line. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, a widely respected Orthodox authority, wrote of Jacob and Israel as two aspects of one person. He asked readers whether they are Jacob, embarrassed by who they are, or Israel, with the courage to stand upright.[cite:sacks_jacob_israel] What this book adds, and marks as its own interpretive layer, is the specifically Hermetic vocabulary of IS-RA-EL. That vocabulary is not part of mainstream Jewish or Christian commentary.

The traditions themselves also build non-literal reading into their methods. Judaism has PaRDeS, a named four-level method of interpretation: Peshat, the plain sense; Remez, the hinted or allegorical; Derash, the comparative and homiletical; and Sod, the secret or mystical, the level from which Kabbalah grows. One traditional image has the text descending from above while the reader climbs through the four levels from below.[cite:wiki_pardes_exegesis] Islam developed a parallel distinction between tafsir, conventional commentary, and ta'wil, esoteric interpretation, practiced in Shia, Sufi and Sunni scholarship since at least the eighth century CE. The Quran's own account of Khidr refers to an inner meaning behind outward acts.[cite:wiki_esoteric_quran_interpretation] [cite:britannica_tawil] In Christianity, the third-century Church Father Origen helped establish allegorical interpretation.[cite:wiki_aggadah] Scholarship on medieval Catholic exegesis records that in the High Middle Ages a commentary on scripture was thought perfect only if it contained both a literal and an allegorical interpretation.[cite:hnet_tawil_medieval_catholic] In each tradition, the plain sense is named alongside the non-literal one.

The symbolic reading also points to a narrower argument about the modern state's name. Herzl's 1896 pamphlet weighed Argentina alongside Palestine, and in 1903 the Zionist Congress voted on British East Africa. Whichever territory had been chosen, a state called "Israel" would have carried the same scriptural weight, because that weight travels with the word, not with a particular piece of land.[cite:genesis_15_18] On this view, a largely secular political movement attached its project to a name already heavy with religious meaning. This book has found no primary source showing that Herzl, Ben-Gurion or the founders of 1948 consciously intended any such symbolism, and it does not claim they did. The name carries the weight regardless of intent.

The reading, then, is a key for thinking about the name's layered resonance. It is not a documented finding about the name's origin or about anyone's intention, and it should be weighed accordingly.
