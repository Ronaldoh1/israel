Church and Empire: Privilege, Law and the First Heresy Edicts, 313–395

The Milan letter of 313 made the church legal. What Constantine and his successors did over the next eighty years made it something else: a privileged corporation inside the Roman state, funded and protected by the emperor, with its bishops sitting as judges, and with imperial law standing behind its definition of right belief. The same decades produced the first imperial laws that closed the meeting houses of Christian groups the bishops called heretical, ordered their books searched out, and, by the end of the century, banned the public and private worship of the old gods. This section sets those acts out in order. It is the institutional ground on which the later history of suppression in Book Two stands.

Money, immunity and courts

Constantine's first privileges came almost at once. In a letter of 313 to Anullinus, the governor of Africa, he ordered that the clergy of the catholic church be "entirely exempted from all public duties", the costly civic offices that wealthy Romans were obliged to carry, so that they would not be "drawn away from the service due to the Deity". The stated reason was that their worship benefited the state. [cite:vt21_hist_x7]

He also made bishops judges. Two enactments, in 318 and 333, allowed litigants to take civil cases to a bishop's court, a practice later called episcopalis audientia. Constantine had already used bishops as judges in the Donatist dispute in North Africa, and in 316 he himself sat with bishop-judges to hear the appeal. C. G. Bateman argues that he trusted bishops as honest judges at a time when he was legislating against corruption in the ordinary courts. [cite:vt21_bateman] Whatever the motive, by the 330s a bishop was not only a pastor but a public magistrate.

Then came property. From 312 Constantine built the Lateran basilica in Rome on the site of a demolished barracks of the imperial horse guard, then Old St Peter's over the shrine on the Vatican hill, and in Jerusalem the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] Eusebius describes the Jerusalem works in detail: a shrine of Venus over the site was torn down, the ground dug out, and a basilica raised at imperial expense, with further churches at Bethlehem and on the Mount of Olives dedicated by his mother Helena. [cite:vt21_vc3] After 320 the emperor endowed churches with land and other wealth. [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol] These are the first imperial endowments behind the church's later landed wealth. A later forgery, the "Donation of Constantine", claimed that he had handed Pope Sylvester primacy over the great sees and control of Rome and the western regions; it was composed in the eighth century, as Part Two of Book One sets out. [cite:vt2_britannica_constantine] What the church holds today is examined in [scene:vt9_true_vs_public_wealth|Part Nine].

The edict against the heretics

The last step in that list is the one most relevant to Book Two, and it is rarely mentioned in popular accounts. Eusebius's Life of Constantine preserves an undated edict addressed to "Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites, Paulians, ye who are called Cataphrygians": that is, rigorist Novatian Christians, followers of the Gnostic teacher Valentinus, followers of Marcion, followers of Paul of Samosata, and the Montanists of Phrygia. [cite:vt21_vc3_64] It ordered that "none of you henceforth presume to assemble yourselves together", in public or "in any private house or place whatsoever", and that their meeting houses "be made over without delay to the catholic Church", with other property confiscated to the state. [cite:vt21_vc3_65] Eusebius adds that the law "directed that search should be made for their books", and that many members, deprived of their meeting places, returned to the church. [cite:vt21_vc3_66]

This is the documented point at which imperial power first acted against a Gnostic group by name. It confiscated buildings, which shows that Valentinians and others had meeting houses of their own, and it ordered their books sought. Eusebius does not say that the books were burned, and no surviving text records what happened to them. How strictly it was enforced is not recorded. The same emperor, in a letter after Nicaea, ordered that any treatise by Arius be "consigned to the flames", with death for anyone caught concealing one, an order discussed in the next part. [cite:vt21_socrates19] [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided]

Constantine also ordered Christian books made. In a letter preserved by Eusebius he asked for fifty copies of the scriptures, written on prepared parchment in a portable form, for the churches of his new capital. [cite:vt21_vc4] The letter does not list which books they contained, and claims that surviving fourth-century codices are among them are speculation.

Reversal and restoration

The partnership did not move in a straight line. Constantine's son Constantius II banned sacrifice in Italy in 341 and in 356 made it a capital crime, though there is no evidence that anyone was executed under the law. His cousin Julian (361–363), the last pagan emperor, reopened temples, withdrew Christian privileges and in 362 effectively barred Christians from teaching the classics. [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] Julian died within two years, and his successors restored the church's position.

Theodosius: one faith by law

The decisive legal step came under Theodosius I. On 27 February 380, with his co-emperors Gratian and Valentinian II, he issued the edict known from its opening words as Cunctos populos. It required all peoples under their rule to follow the faith "delivered to the Romans by the divine Apostle Peter", as professed by Damasus, bishop of Rome, and Peter, bishop of Alexandria: belief in one deity in the Holy Trinity. Those who did not were "foolish madmen" and heretics, could not call their meeting places churches, and faced divine condemnation first and then imperial punishment. [cite:vt21_wiki_thessalonica] The edict was directed at Christian dissent, chiefly the non-Nicene parties; it named two bishops, of Rome and Alexandria, as the measure of orthodoxy. The Council of Constantinople in 381 then gave the Nicene creed its final, expanded form. [cite:vt21_wiki_thessalonica]

The laws against the old religion followed a decade later. Gratian had already, around 382, cut the public subsidies of the pagan priesthoods and the Vestal Virgins and removed the Altar of Victory from the Senate house. [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] A law of 391 ordered that "no one is to go to the sanctuaries, walk through the temples, or raise his eyes to statues"; another closed temples; and a sweeping law of 392 banned all sacrifice and worship, down to honouring the household gods with fire, wine or incense in a private home. Historians including Malcolm Errington question how far these laws were enforced, and archaeologists Luke Lavan and Michael Mulryan note that only four of 43 temple destructions reported in written sources are confirmed on the ground. [cite:vt21_wiki_theodpagan] [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] By Theodosius's death in 395, Christianity in its Nicene form was the only lawful public religion of the empire, at least on paper.
