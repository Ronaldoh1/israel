First Riots, First Militia, and Two British Reports

Less than three years after the Balfour Declaration, violence broke out under British rule. The bloodshed of the early 1920s is sometimes described as if it came from nowhere, or as if it had a single cause. Britain's own investigators, writing at the time, saw it differently. They recorded attacks by Arab crowds on Jews, and they recorded the political fear that lay beneath them. One of their reports was published. The other was not.

The first outbreak came in Jerusalem between 4 and 7 April 1920, during the Nebi Musa festival. Speeches by Arab religious leaders were followed by attacks on the city's Jews. The sources disagree on the toll: the figures reported are five to six Jews and four Arabs killed, and some 200 injured. [cite:jvl_arab_riots_1920s] Ze'ev Jabotinsky, who had organized Jewish self-defense in the city, was sentenced to 15 years' hard labor. A general amnesty covering both Jews and Arabs later lifted the sentence. These days also left a counterweight in the record: the sheikhs of 82 villages issued a document protesting the violence against Jews. [cite:jewishpress_nebimusa_palin]

Two months later, in June 1920, the Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine, formed the Haganah to defend Jewish settlements. [cite:wiki_israel_timeline]

Britain's first inquiry into the violence, the Palin Court of Inquiry, reached a pointed conclusion. It blamed the Zionists, "whose impatience to achieve their ultimate goal and indiscretion are largely responsible for this unhappy state of feeling." The report was never published. [cite:jewishpress_nebimusa_palin]

The second outbreak came in May 1921. It began in Jaffa, after a clash between rival Jewish May Day marches, and spread to Petah Tikva, Hadera and Rehovot. On 2 May, members of the Haganah launched the first reprisal attacks on Arabs. [cite:wiki_jaffa_riots_1921]

A second British inquiry, the Haycraft Commission, examined the 1921 violence. Its findings had two parts, and both belong in the record. It identified the "fundamental cause" as Arab "discontent with, and hostility to, the Jews, due to political and economic causes, and connected with Jewish immigration," and with Zionist policy as its exponents had stated it. It also found that "the Arab majority, who were generally the aggressors, inflicted most of the casualties." Most of the Arab casualties, for their part, were caused by British forces restoring order. [cite:jvl_haycraft_report]

Read together, the two reports give a fuller picture than either side's later shorthand. Arab crowds attacked Jews, and British investigators said so plainly. The same investigators traced the hostility to fear of what Jewish immigration and the Zionist program meant for the country's future. Neither finding cancels the other. The violence had perpetrators. It also had political causes, which the mandatory power's own officials identified within months of the events.

What Britain chose to make public is part of the story too. The report that laid the weight of blame on Zionist impatience stayed unpublished. In the same period, as [scene:pc_s01|the account of the Paris conference] shows, the American King–Crane report, which found the population opposed to the Zionist program, was also being held back. The first years of British rule thus set a pattern. Officials investigated, and their findings were sometimes candid. How much of that candor reached the public depended on decisions made after the reports were written. [cite:jewishpress_nebimusa_palin]
