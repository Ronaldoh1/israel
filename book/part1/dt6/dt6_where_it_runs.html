Where It Runs, and Where It Is Banned

Ranked-choice voting reached American state politics only in 2016, and within a decade it had become one of the most contested election rules in the country. Two states use it, eighteen have banned it, and voters asked directly have rejected it more often than they have adopted it. This section sets out that record: the three places where it has been tested hardest, New York City, Maine and Alaska; the bans; and the ballot-box results, which do not match the polls that supporters cite. Every figure is given as of October 2026, a week before Alaska votes for the second time on whether to repeal it. [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] [cite:dt6_fv_2026use] [cite:dt6_bp_bans2026] [cite:dt6_bp_2024results]

New York City

On 5 November 2019 New York City voters amended the city charter to use ranked-choice voting in primary and special elections for mayor, public advocate, comptroller, borough president and City Council, from 2021, with up to five rankings. The measure passed with 510,153 votes to 182,900, or 73.6 per cent. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] Its first test, the 2021 Democratic mayoral primary, took eight rounds and was decided by 7,197 votes; its second, in 2025, took three. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_nyc_2025] After the 2025 primary a SurveyUSA poll published by FairVote, an organisation that campaigns for ranked-choice voting, found that 96 per cent of voters called the ballot simple, 81 per cent said they understood the system extremely or very well and 76 per cent wanted to keep or expand it. The city's ballot records show that 78 per cent of voters in the Democratic mayoral primary ranked at least two candidates. [cite:dt6_fv_nyc2025] Because the survey's publisher is an advocate, its numbers are best read alongside the independent research in the next section.

Maine

Maine voters adopted ranked-choice voting by citizen initiative, Question 5, on 8 November 2016, by 52.1 to 47.9 per cent. On 23 May 2017 the state's Supreme Judicial Court advised, unanimously, that the method conflicted with the Maine Constitution's requirement that governors and state legislators be elected by plurality in general elections. The legislature then passed a law delaying the system, and on 12 June 2018 voters overturned that law by a people's veto. [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] The result is a split system: Maine uses ranked-choice voting in primaries and in general elections for Congress, but not for governor or the legislature in the general election. That autumn the 2nd District count, described in the previous section, gave the seat to the candidate who trailed in the first round. A federal court rejected the losing candidate's challenge under Article I, the Fourteenth Amendment and the Voting Rights Act on 13 December 2018. [cite:dt6_baber] [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018]

Alaska

Alaska's version arrived as a package. Ballot Measure 2, passed on 3 November 2020 by 174,032 votes to 170,251 (50.55 per cent), replaced party primaries with a single "top-four" primary open to all candidates, used ranked-choice voting among the four in the general election, and added a "true source" rule requiring disclosure of the original donors behind large political contributions. The Alaska Supreme Court upheld it on 19 January 2022. A repeal measure failed in November 2024 by 160,973 votes to 160,230, the narrowest margin for any ballot measure in the state's history. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026]

A second repeal, also numbered Measure 2, is on the ballot on 3 November 2026. It would restore partisan primaries and plurality general elections and repeal the 2020 disclosure rules. The lieutenant governor verified 42,837 signatures on 31 December 2025, and on 27 August 2026 the Alaska Supreme Court ordered added wording to the ballot summary. Supporters of repeal include President Trump, Representative Nick Begich, Governor Mike Dunleavy, the Alaska Republican Party and Elon Musk. Opponents include the Alaska AFL-CIO and other unions, the Libertarian Party of Alaska and the national reform group Unite America. Through 2 October 2026 the repeal side had raised about $2.5 million and the opposing side about $8.5 million, of which $4.9 million came from Unite America's political committee. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] The result is not yet known.

The bans

Florida and Tennessee banned ranked-choice voting in 2022; Idaho, Montana and South Dakota in 2023; Alabama, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi and Oklahoma in 2024, when Missouri voters also approved a ban in the state constitution. [cite:dt6_bp_bans2024] [cite:dt6_bp_2024results] Arkansas, Iowa, Kansas, North Dakota, West Virginia and Wyoming followed in 2025, and Indiana on 24 February 2026, bringing the total to eighteen states. [cite:dt6_bp_bans2025] [cite:dt6_bp_bans2026] All but two were enacted where Republicans controlled the legislature and the governorship; in Kansas and Kentucky, Republican legislatures acted under Democratic governors. [cite:dt6_bp_bans2025] The bans are a design choice made by elected legislatures and, in Missouri, by voters, and they foreclose the city-charter route by which New York adopted the system.

What voters said when asked

Surveys in places that use the system, such as New York's, report high satisfaction. The ballot box in 2024 told a different story. Voters in the District of Columbia adopted ranked-choice voting with 73 per cent, and the Alaska repeal failed narrowly. Every other statewide measure to adopt ranked-choice voting or open, top-several primaries failed: Montana's two measures with 49 and 40 per cent, Nevada's with 47, Colorado's with 46, Oregon's with 42, Arizona's with 41, South Dakota's with 34 and Idaho's with 30. Missouri's ban passed with 68 per cent. [cite:dt6_bp_2024results] In the one state that voted on keeping a system already in use, Alaska, voters kept it by 743 votes; in the states voting on whether to adopt one, they mostly said no.

In 2026 the system will be used in Alaska and Maine, in the District of Columbia, in eleven cities from Albany and Oakland in California to Portland, Maine, and Takoma Park, Maryland, and in Multnomah County, Oregon, and Arlington County, Virginia. Voters in Honolulu, Irvine and Longmont, Colorado, will decide on 3 November whether to adopt it for local elections. [cite:dt6_fv_2026use] The pattern of the decade is clear enough: the method has spread where cities and voters chose it and been halted where state legislatures chose otherwise. Whether it works as promised where it runs is the question for the critics. [scene:dt6_the_critics|The critics]
