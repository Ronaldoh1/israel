Alpha: The Anglo-American Search for a Settlement, 1954–1956

While Israeli and Arab forces traded raids across the armistice lines, the governments in Washington and London were working, in secret, on a plan to end the conflict altogether. The effort had a public prelude in 1954, when Assistant Secretary Henry Byroade delivered the speeches that so angered Israel's government, told in [scene:cw2_border_1954|the year after Qibya]. It had a private start at the end of that year. "Alpha" was the State Department's code word for a secret American–British effort to devise an Arab–Israeli settlement, and on 20 December 1954 Secretary of State John Foster Dulles assigned Francis Russell to coordinate it with his British counterpart, Evelyn Shuckburgh. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_alpha]

The familiar account of these years treats the conflict as a contest between local parties, with the great powers looking on or taking sides. The record of Alpha shows something else: for fifteen months, two Western governments drafted the terms of a peace on the parties' behalf, put them to the world in a speech, carried them to Cairo and Jerusalem by a secret envoy, and found that neither side would accept them. What follows is the record of that effort, so far as the documents fetched for this book show it, and of the reasons each side gave for saying no.

The mechanism mattered from the start. Alpha was not a negotiation between Israel and Egypt. It was a planning exercise run by two foreign ministries, coordinated by two named officials, whose product would then be offered to governments that had not asked for it. Neither Israel nor Egypt was bound by anything Washington and London agreed between themselves. What the two Western governments could offer was inducement: money, guarantees, and their own diplomatic weight. What they could not do was make either party sign.

The terms the two governments reached in 1955, according to the Jewish Virtual Library and the Economic Cooperation Foundation's database, had three main parts. Israel would cede parts of the Negev to Egypt and Jordan, creating a land link between the two Arab states. Israel would resettle 75,000 Palestinian refugees inside its territory. And the parties would agree to non-belligerence "in lieu of peace", a lesser state than a full treaty. [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_alpha] [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_alpha] Each term asked something specific of Israel: land in the south and the admission of a fixed number of refugees. What the Arab states would give in return was an end to the state of war, short of recognition in a peace treaty.

The public face of the plan came on 26 August 1955, when Dulles addressed the Council on Foreign Relations in New York under the title "American Policy Toward the Middle East". [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55] Read closely, the address is a careful document. Dulles named three problems. The first was "the tragic plight of the 900,000 refugees". The second was "the pall of fear that hangs over the Arab and Israel people alike". The third was the lack of fixed permanent boundaries between Israel and its neighbours. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55]

On the refugees, he proposed that they should be enabled to live in dignity "through resettlement and, to such an extent as may be feasible, repatriation". The order of the words carried the policy. Resettlement came first and without qualification; return to their former homes came second, and only as far as it proved feasible. He then turned to money. "Compensation is due from Israel to the refugees," he said, and he suggested an international loan to help pay it, to which President Eisenhower would recommend "substantial participation by the United States". [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55] The money chain he sketched ran from an international lender, with American participation, through Israel, to the refugees: the obligation lay with Israel, but the means would be partly borrowed abroad, with Washington underwriting a share.

On borders and security he offered what Israel had long sought in another form. Eisenhower, he said, would recommend formal American treaty engagements "to prevent or thwart any effort by either side to alter by force" the boundaries the parties agreed. He did not treat the existing lines as final: the armistice lines, he said, were not "permanent frontiers in every respect". He backed a United Nations review of the status of Jerusalem, and shared use of the waters of the Jordan Valley rivers, the subject of the Johnston mission then under way. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55]

Set beside the secret terms, the speech is notable for what it left general. In the passages of the address quoted in the sources used here, Dulles named no figure for refugees to be admitted to Israel and named no territory to be ceded; he spoke of agreed boundaries and of armistice lines that were not permanent in every respect. Whether the full text was more specific, the excerpts available here do not show. The specifics of Alpha, on this evidence, stayed in the private channel. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_dulles55] [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_alpha]

The address had to be explained in Cairo almost at once. On 1 September 1955 the State Department sent Telegram 421 to its embassy in Egypt with instructions to reassure Nasser on three points. The speech did not exclude some refugees returning to Israel. The proposed guarantee treaties concerned only the borders between Israel and the Arab states, not the wider defence of the Middle East. And the proposals were "only a beginning". The same record reports that Fawzi, for Egypt, had said there was "no plan" to discuss the speech seriously. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tel421] The second reassurance is revealing. Washington evidently expected that Cairo might read a treaty guarantee as a step toward a Western regional defence system, the kind of system taking shape that year in [scene:cw2_baghdad_bandung|the Baghdad Pact].

Within a month the ground had shifted. On 27 September 1955 Nasser announced the arms deal with Czechoslovakia, and the question in every capital became the balance of arms rather than the terms of peace. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_czech] London made the next public move. On 9 November 1955 Prime Minister Eden addressed the subject in a speech that the American Embassy in London called his "Mansion House" speech; the Economic Cooperation Foundation's database calls it the Guildhall speech. The sources used here do not settle the venue's name; the date is not in doubt. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_uk] [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_eden]

Eden set out the two positions as he saw them. Israel preferred the status quo, the armistice lines as they stood. The Arab states demanded the 1947 partition plan and the other United Nations resolutions. The two sides, he said, "must make some compromise between these two positions". He also called the Soviet arming of Egypt "a new element of danger". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_uk] [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_eden] Any compromise between the armistice lines and the 1947 plan could only mean that Israel would give up some of the territory it held beyond the partition boundaries. That was why the speech was received as it was in Jerusalem.

Washington kept its distance. Dulles, replying from Geneva on 12 November, said the "US is in no sense committed" to the speech. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_uk] The two governments that had built Alpha together were now speaking separately in public, and the American Secretary of State would not stand behind the British Prime Minister's formula.

Israel's response came at two levels. Eytan, Director-General of the Foreign Ministry, told the American Embassy that the speech "focused entirely on territorial question" and that the "armistice lines had to be starting point", while adding that Israel was "quite prepared to discuss it". Ben-Gurion went further, in public. On 15 November he told the Knesset that the proposals would "truncate Israel for benefit of neighbors". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_il] The official's reply left a door open for talks on Israel's own terms; Ben-Gurion's closed it on Eden's.

Israel's own priorities by then lay elsewhere. In February 1955 Dulles had offered Israel a mutual defence pact on conditions that its leaders would accept only in part, an episode told in [scene:cw2_sharett_ben_gurion|the leadership split]. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] Avi Shlaim records that after the Gaza raid of 28 February 1955, Israeli diplomats shifted from seeking a defence pact to seeking arms. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_alpha] Alpha offered a guarantee of borders in return for concessions of land and refugees; on the record here, Israel wanted weapons rather than a guarantee paid for in territory. The arms requests, and the final American refusal of them in April 1956, are told in the section on the arms race that follows. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_alpha]

With the speeches failing, Eisenhower turned to a personal envoy. Facing opposition from both Egypt and Israel, he sent Robert Anderson, a Texas lawyer and a personal friend, on a secret mission supported by the CIA between January and March 1956. Anderson travelled between Ben-Gurion, Sharett and Nasser. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_alpha] The President followed the mission closely. On 27 February 1956 he approved letters to Ben-Gurion and to Nasser for Anderson to carry on his return trip; the record notes that "The President went over these drafts and approved them". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_anderson] The documentary record itself places the mission beside a second question: the State Department's published volume files the Anderson papers in one chapter with its "Continued U.S. Consideration of Funding the Aswan High Dam", the dam whose fate closes this chapter. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_anderson] Peace and the dam were being weighed in the same months, by the same officials, with the same Egyptian leader. Both sides, in the end, rejected the plan Anderson carried. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_alpha]

Side by side, the American proposals and the parties' documented positions show how little common ground the record holds. On several of the questions Dulles raised, the sources fetched here record no position at all from one side or the other, and the table says so rather than filling the gap.

How each side received the effort can be stated from the record, and it should be stated in each side's own terms. Egypt's government, on the evidence of Telegram 421, declined to engage seriously with the public proposals at all; Washington's anxiety to reassure Nasser that the guarantees were not a back door into a Western defence pact suggests what Cairo feared. Israel's leaders refused territorial cession in plain words, and wanted arms and a guarantee of their own kind rather than concessions in exchange for a promise. From Israel's side, a plan whose central terms were a slice of the Negev and the admission of 75,000 refugees asked it to give up tangible assets for an undertaking from a power that was, in the same months, declining to sell it weapons. From the Arab side, as Eden summarized it, the starting demand was the 1947 plan, and the Western proposals offered far less than that. [cite:rs_cw2_ecf_alpha] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_uk] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eden_il]

The strongest case for Alpha's designers is that they addressed every one of the questions left open by the armistices, refugees, borders, security, Jerusalem and water, and offered American money and American treaty commitments to make an agreement possible. The strongest case against it is that it was built without the parties, kept its most demanding terms secret, and asked each side to accept what its leaders had publicly ruled out. Both cases are consistent with the documents. Neither is proven by them.

What the record fetched for this book does not settle is the question every account of Alpha eventually reaches: whether either leader could have accepted it. The sources show refusals; they do not show the internal deliberations behind them, or what Nasser or Ben-Gurion might have accepted on different terms, at a different moment, or without the arms race that began in September 1955. The Anderson papers are cited here only for the President's approval of his letters; their contents, and the envoy's own reports of what each leader told him, are not in the record used for this section. Nor do these sources show what Shuckburgh and Russell argued between themselves, or how the 75,000 figure was reached. The plan failed. Why, beyond the reasons each government stated, remains open.
