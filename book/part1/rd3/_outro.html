By the mid-1930s Palestine held the institutions of a state, but no state to hold them. The Histadrut, the Jews-only labor federation, ran its own construction firm, bank and health fund, and it was training the men and women who would govern. The Jewish Agency, enlarged in 1929, had brought wealthy American non-Zionists into its governance. The Anglo-Palestine Bank carried the movement's money. A seventy-year electricity monopoly had produced a grid that followed Jewish settlement. In the United States, a Zionist executive had founded the American Palestine Committee in 1932 to organize prominent, mainly non-Jewish Americans. In the 1940s it and a parallel council for clergy, both organized from within the Zionist political office, would claim thousands of members.

Nor was the movement unified. Jabotinsky's Revisionists had seceded in 1925 and left entirely in 1935. The Irgun had broken with the Haganah's policy of restraint in 1931. And Jewish dissent from Zionism had been met, at least once, with a killing.

Britain, for its part, had counted a country that remained largely Arab and had investigated the violence of 1929. It had then seen its attempt to limit immigration and land transfer reversed within four months. Jewish immigration rose sharply, to over 60,000 in 1935. In Germany, democracy had been voted away and the Nuremberg Laws had made exclusion a matter of statute. The movement's own Congress, meanwhile, had chosen to continue the transfer agreement with the Nazi state. The pieces of the state-in-waiting were in place, and so were the pressures that would test them.
