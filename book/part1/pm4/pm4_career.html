The Current Congress: Who Has Received the Most

The question most readers ask first is the simplest: which members of Congress have received the most pro-Israel money? The most complete answer available for the Congress sitting in 2026 comes from Track AIPAC, an advocacy group critical of the lobby that compiles Federal Election Commission data for every member. Its figures, read as of 5 July 2026, are used in this section with three cautions stated at the start. They are career totals, covering every race a member has run, not a single election. They count all pro-Israel PACs, including the dovish J Street, and the large individual donors to those PACs. And they add outside spending made on a member's behalf, which is money the member did not receive. [cite:trackaipac_congress] [cite:wiki_trackaipac] Each figure below gives the direct and outside parts separately where they matter.

The Senate. Among Senate Democrats the largest total is Adam Schiff's, about $5.6 million, almost all of it, about $5.0 million, spent by outside groups on his behalf in his 2024 Senate race. Jacky Rosen of Nevada follows with about $2.3 million, mostly given directly, then Jon Ossoff and Raphael Warnock of Georgia and Cory Booker of New Jersey, each with between $850,000 and $960,000. The top twenty-five Senate Democrats together account for about $22.1 million. Four members of the Democratic caucus, Bernie Sanders, Chris Van Hollen, Jeff Merkley and Peter Welch, are listed at nothing. [cite:trackaipac_congress] Among Senate Republicans, Susan Collins of Maine leads every senator of either party with about $4.1 million, nearly all of it, $3.6 million, given directly over a long career. Ted Cruz of Texas follows with about $1.4 million, then Tom Cotton, John Cornyn and Mitch McConnell, each near $1 million. The top twenty-five Republicans total about $18.9 million; at the other end, Rand Paul of Kentucky is listed at $2,000. [cite:trackaipac_congress]

The House. The House lists show the difference between money given and money spent more sharply than any other figures in this volume. The top three House Democrats, Haley Stevens of Michigan with about $36.3 million, George Latimer of New York with about $19.2 million and Wesley Bell of Missouri with about $18.1 million, owe almost all of their totals to outside spending in contested primaries: about $34.9 million, $16.2 million and $13.7 million respectively. [cite:trackaipac_congress] Latimer and Bell are the challengers who defeated Jamaal Bowman and Cori Bush in 2024, and most of Stevens's total was spent in her 2026 Senate primary, with some in her 2022 primary against Andy Levin, told in Part Five. The top twenty-five House Democrats total about $128.8 million. The House Democratic leader, Hakeem Jeffries, is listed at about $1.5 million, all of it direct. Seven Democrats, among them Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Rashida Tlaib, Ilhan Omar and Summer Lee, are listed at nothing. [cite:trackaipac_congress]

House Republicans' totals are an order of magnitude smaller and mostly given directly. Mark Messmer of Indiana leads with about $2.9 million, $2.5 million of it outside spending in his 2024 primary, followed by Don Bacon of Nebraska, Juan Ciscomani of Arizona, Mike Lawler of New York and Speaker Mike Johnson of Louisiana, each with between $1.1 million and $1.3 million given directly. The top twenty-five total about $21.8 million. Thomas Massie of Kentucky is listed at nothing. [cite:trackaipac_congress]

What the lists show, and what they do not. Read with their cautions, the lists show three things. The money reaches the leadership of both parties, the Speaker and the Democratic leader among them. The largest totals belong not to the members who have taken the most money but to those for whom the most was spent, in primaries against candidates the lobby opposed. And the members at zero are a small and identifiable group: a handful of progressive Democrats and one libertarian Republican. What the lists do not show is why any member votes as he or she does. A member's total reflects the length of a career, the cost of a state's races and the competitiveness of a seat as much as any position on Israel, and the records cannot say whether the money followed the votes or the votes the money.
