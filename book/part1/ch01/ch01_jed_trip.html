The Journey

The money behind Birthright Israel, the free ten-day trip offered to young Jewish adults since 1999, is set out in the section on how the story is taught. This section looks at the trip itself: the route, what it is designed to do, what it leaves out and what happened to participants who went looking for the missing part.

The route is the lesson

Birthright's own sample itinerary moves through a recognizable sequence: the mystical town of Tzfat; the Golan Heights, with a lookout over the 1973 battlefields and a required lecture on geopolitics; the Western Wall and the Old City of Jerusalem; Yad Vashem, Israel's Holocaust memorial, with survivor testimony, followed directly by Mount Herzl, the national military cemetery; a sunrise climb of Masada and a night in a Bedouin village; and Independence Hall in Tel Aviv. Groups may be joined by young Israeli soldiers and professionals as peers, an encounter the programme calls the mifgash. [cite:jed_br_itinerary]

The order carries meaning. The Holocaust memorial comes before the military cemetery, catastrophe before sacrifice, and the trip ends in the hall where the state was declared. The journalist Kiera Feldman, who took the trip in 2010 and reported on it for The Nation, found that every itinerary had to include the Western Wall and Masada and be approved by Birthright's office in Jerusalem. She quoted the programme's educational architect, Barry Chazan, describing a design meant to "bombard" participants with information and produce an "emotionally overwhelming experience," and its chief executive, Gidi Mark, saying participants should be able to tell anti-Zionists on campus: "Don't tell me what you saw on CNN—I was there." Co-founder Charles Bronfman described the moment of arrival in a phrase: "Israel does its magic." [cite:jed_nation_feldman2011]

What the route leaves out

The published route does not include a Palestinian town in the West Bank, a Palestinian family or a refugee camp. Feldman reported that the programme's educational architect told her no trip provider had taken participants into a Palestinian town in the West Bank, and that Palestinian citizens of Israel were barely present on the itineraries she could see. [cite:jed_nation_feldman2011] She also reported that the trip took her group to a cosmetics factory in a West Bank settlement without discussing the occupation. The programme's defenders answer that Birthright is a heritage trip, not a political seminar, and that ten days cannot cover everything. Both statements can be true. The choice of what fills the ten days is still a choice.

The walk-offs

In the summer of 2018 some participants went looking for what was missing. On 28 June five young women, organized with IfNotNow, left their trip on its last day, filmed the moment, and joined Breaking the Silence, a group of Israeli army veterans, on a tour of Hebron in the West Bank. A Birthright spokesperson called their video misleading and "the opposite of respectable debate and consideration of divergent views." [cite:jed_fwd_walkoff2018] By the end of the summer thirteen participants had walked off. Birthright cancelled their return flights, under its rule for anyone who leaves a trip early, and said it "will not tolerate any attempts to use this experience to promote ideological agendas." They raised money to fly home. [cite:jed_alma_walkoffs]

Read one way, the incident shows the structure in miniature. A trip whose route includes a geopolitics lecture and an encounter with soldiers treats one view as education, while a visit to Hebron with Israeli veterans counts as an "ideological agenda." Who draws that line, and who pays for the flights home, is the same question this volume has asked of textbooks: who holds the pen.
