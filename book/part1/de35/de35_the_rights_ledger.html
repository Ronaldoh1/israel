The Rights Ledger

A democratic republic is defined less by its elections than by the rights it guarantees against its own government. This section sets thirteen of those rights, as the American Constitution states them, beside what the Dark Enlightenment texts say about each, and reads from the texts' own words what would become of it under a sovereign run as a company. It does not infer intent beyond the words. Where the texts say nothing about a right, the ledger says so, because silence is a finding too: a design that does not mention a right offers it no protection, but it does not announce its abolition either. The texts used are Land's 2012 serial, which summarises Yarvin's design, Yarvin's own posts and interviews, and, for one right, Peter Thiel's 2009 essay.

The vote, the removal of rulers and the petition

The texts are clearest on the rights that concern power. In the design Land summarises, only shareholders vote, by share, and they elect a board that "hires and fires managers"; residents are customers, and for a customer to take an interest in politics would "exhibit semi-criminal proclivities." [cite:de35_land_ebook] Yarvin drew the distinction himself in January 2025: "free speech is a freedom. The right to vote is a form of power." He called voting "this almost pornographic stimulus," and said he does not vote. [cite:de35_nyt_interview] [cite:de8_audit_nyt] Thiel's 2009 essay declared, "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible," but his reply of 1 May 2009 added that he did not "think any class of people should be disenfranchised" and that "voting is not under siege in America, but many other rights are." [cite:de10_cato] The two men should not be merged: Thiel's text doubts the vote's usefulness; Yarvin's design removes it from residents.

On removing a bad ruler, the design gives the power to the board, and The New Yorker's account of Yarvin's plan has board members holding cryptographic keys able to disarm the state's weapons, to prevent a coup. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] The residents have no such power. Asked in 2025 about rulers like Nero, Yarvin called the question "the most important question of all" and offered no mechanism; his idea of selection was to take a Fortune 500 chief executive, "pick one at random and put him or her in charge of Washington." [cite:de35_nyt_interview] The Constitution, by contrast, lets Congress remove a president by impeachment and, since 1951, limits him to two elected terms. [cite:de8_lii_art2s4] [cite:de8_annenberg_22] The question of who would choose and remove the chief executive is the subject of [scene:de35b_what_the_texts_say|Part Thirty-Eight]. The right of petition survives in the design only as a consumer complaint: a dissatisfied resident may "notify its customer service function." [cite:de35_land_ebook]

Courts, due process and the jury

The Constitution gives federal judges office "during good Behaviour" and pay that "shall not be diminished," so that a president cannot remove or starve them. [cite:de35_ncc_art3] The texts treat any such independence as illusory. In Land's summary of Yarvin's reasoning, sovereignty "cannot be eliminated, caged, or controlled," and constitutions have "exactly as much real authority as a sovereign interpretative power allows them to have." [cite:de35_land_ebook] Yarvin in 2025 described rule by "people in the courts and lawyers and professors" as aristocracy rather than democracy. [cite:de35_nyt_interview] The design names no court above the sovereign corporation; its courts would be its own departments, as [scene:de8_tests_it_fails|Part Eight] showed.

Due process, the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments' bar on taking "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law," fares worse in the texts. [cite:de35_na_bor] [cite:de35_bp_14th] The 2008 Patchwork posts describe residents' slums sold "out from under them" and the unwanted held in "permanent solitary confinement," with no hearing mentioned. [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de6_tnr_duran] CNN reported that in 2022 Yarvin described agents who would act "without respect for paper protections." [cite:de6_cnn_2025] Against that stands his 2025 statement that a new regime "can't be" about bayoneting professors and should treat the old regime's officials as ordinary people. [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] The jury is the one right a text praises: Land quotes, with approval, the economist Steve Hanke's line that "the only thing that the citizens can demand from the State, under the Bill of Rights, is for a trial by a jury." [cite:de35_land_ebook] The praise belongs to Land's account of the old American republic, though; the neocameral design itself says nothing about juries.

Speech, press, assembly and religion

The First Amendment protects speech, the press, assembly, petition and religion in one sentence. [cite:de35_na_bor] The texts separate them. The design promises personal freedom without political freedom: the model states have "high levels of personal and economic freedom" and are "weak only in political freedom," which is "unimportant by definition when government is stable and effective." [cite:de35_land_ebook] Speech that is merely personal would on this reading be tolerated; speech that seeks power is exactly the "voice" the design abolishes. The press fares worse, because Yarvin counts it, with the universities and the civil service, as part of "the Cathedral," the unelected establishment he set out to dismantle ([scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]). [cite:de6_pein_2014] In 2025 he called the New York Times "a fifth generation hereditary absolute monarchy." [cite:de35_nyt_interview] The New Yorker summarised his regime as one that would "destroy universities, abolish the press"; the Harvard Crimson quoted a 2021 post in which he wrote that "regime change" would require "all accredited universities be both physically and economically liquidated," though at a Harvard debate in May 2025 he said the university should be remade, not destroyed. [cite:de35_ny_autocracy] [cite:de35_crimson_univ]

On assembly the texts examined say nothing directly; it falls under the general abolition of "voice." On religion they say nothing about freedom of worship. What they say concerns the established creed: Land writes that democracy is asserted "as a religious creed," and Yarvin, quoted by Land, calls the Cathedral's faith "Universalism," "a nontheistic Christian sect." [cite:de35_land_ebook] The design would disestablish that creed; whether it would protect anyone's worship is a question the texts leave open.

Privacy, equality and the right to leave

The Fourth Amendment protects "persons, houses, papers, and effects" against unreasonable searches. [cite:de35_na_bor] The texts examined say nothing about privacy. On equal protection they say something worse than nothing. The Fourteenth Amendment forbids a state to deny anyone "the equal protection of the laws." [cite:de35_bp_14th] Yarvin's 2009 essay on Carlyle holds that for some people character and intelligence are "more suited to mastery than slavery," and that this is "most parsimoniously explained by genetic differences." [cite:de6_mm_2021] That is a doctrine of natural inequality; the design builds no guarantee against it.

The right to leave is the texts' own answer to every other loss. "No voice, free exit," Land writes. [cite:de35_land_ebook] The American right to travel is recognised as part of the liberty the Fifth Amendment protects, since Kent v. Dulles in 1958. [cite:de25b_kent] But the exit the texts promise depends on someone else. Yarvin's surplus residents may emigrate, he wrote, only if another realm "is willing to accept them." [cite:de6_audit_le] Exit also needs money, a passport and a place to go, which [scene:de35b_what_citizens_become|the next part] and [scene:de25b_who_can_stop_you|Part Twenty-Seven] examine. [cite:de25b_crs_childsupport]

The strongest counter-reading

The texts' defenders would say the ledger counts the wrong things. Yarvin's point is that freedom and power are different, and that a stable sovereign could leave people freer in daily life than a democracy does, as the model cities show: "minimal crime and high levels of personal and economic freedom." [cite:de35_land_ebook] [cite:de35_nyt_interview] The record gives that claim a test, and it does not pass cleanly. Singapore, the model most often cited, scored 48 of 100 in Freedom House's 2026 report, and only 29 of 60 on civil liberties, the personal freedoms the design says it would keep. [cite:de8_fh_sg2026] The ledger's finding is narrower than the fear and firmer than the defence: the texts remove, in their own words, the rights by which people hold rulers to account, and promise nothing enforceable for the rest.
