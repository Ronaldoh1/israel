Every mechanism in this book runs on rules: tax provisions, bankruptcy law, lending regulations, court doctrines on contracts. The taught version says those rules are written by representatives answering to voters in open markets. This part asks who else takes part in writing them and what the record shows about the effect. The first section measures federal lobbying by industry, campaign spending after the Citizens United decision of 2010 and the revolving door between office and lobbying, sets the leading study of whose preferences shape policy beside its critics, and follows one documented trail from lobbying to a rule change to revenue. The second measures the share of corporate profits earned by finance and traces how arbitration clauses moved consumer and workplace disputes out of the courts. The third tests the promise of competitive prices: market concentration and markups, shared rent-pricing software, prices tailored to individual shoppers, hidden fees, and the laws that now give owners a right to repair what they buy.
