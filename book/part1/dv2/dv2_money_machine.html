The Money Machine: From the Bank of England to the Offshore Network

The Corporation governs one square mile. The financial market that grew up around it is a different thing, much larger and much less tidy, and it is this market, rather than the Corporation's council chambers, that gives "the City" its meaning in most sentences. To understand what London could do for a movement or a state in the twentieth century, and what it can do now, the reader needs a sense of how that market was built and how large it has become. This section follows four stages: the state's bank, the chartered companies of empire, the deregulation of 1986, and the offshore network that now surrounds the market.

The state's bank. The founding of the Bank of England in 1694 is told in full in [scene:rd0_bank1694|an earlier volume]. The essentials bear repeating because so much else rests on them. After Charles II's government stopped repaying its debts to London's goldsmith-bankers in 1672, the Crown could not easily borrow. When William III needed money for war with France, a scheme proposed by the Scottish merchant William Paterson raised £1.2 million from 1,268 subscribers in eleven days. The subscribers lent through a chartered company, the Governor and Company of the Bank of England, which received its Royal Charter on 27 July 1694 and was paid 8 percent interest by the government. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970] [cite:dv2_dmo_boe] The arrangement turned a private loan into a permanent institution through which the state borrowed, and it made the City's lenders partners in the state's wars.

The partnership lasted two and a half centuries before the state took the Bank over. It was nationalised in 1946, a change that the government's own debt office says "made little immediate practical difference": the Bank remained the Treasury's adviser, agent and debt manager. In 1997 it was given its independence as a central bank, and in April 1998 the management of government debt passed to a new Debt Management Office. [cite:dv2_dmo_boe] For most of the period this volume covers, then, the institution at the center of British public finance was a privately owned corporation with a royal charter.

The chartered companies. The City's merchants did not only lend to the state. They also carried the state's flag abroad under charters of their own. On 31 December 1600 Elizabeth I chartered "the Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East-Indies," the East India Company, whose headquarters, East India House, stood on Leadenhall Street. [cite:dv2_wiki_eic] After the Battle of Plassey in 1757 the Company expanded from trade into rule, and at its height its three presidency armies numbered about 260,000 soldiers. A reference summary repeats a common estimate that the Company handled half of the world's trade in the mid-1700s and early 1800s; that figure is an estimate and should not be read as a measured share. After the rebellion of 1857 the Government of India Act 1858 transferred its territories, administration and armies to the Crown, and the Company was dissolved on 1 June 1874. [cite:dv2_wiki_eic] A private corporation registered in the City had governed a subcontinent for a century.

The City's past also includes men whose fortunes were tied to slavery, and the Corporation has had to decide how to remember them. In January 2021 it voted to remove the statues of William Beckford and Sir John Cass from Guildhall over their links to slavery. In October 2021 it reversed the decision and kept the statues with plaques explaining those links, citing a poll in which 71 percent of respondents wanted them kept and the difficulty of altering a Grade I listed building. [cite:dv2_cityam_statues_2021] The episode is small, but it shows the Corporation acting as the keeper of a long institutional memory, as it has since its first charter.

The deregulation of 1986. By the 1980s London's stock market still worked under rules a Victorian broker would have recognized. That changed on 27 October 1986, the day the City called the Big Bang. The old separation between brokers and jobbers was abolished, overseas firms were allowed to compete in London, and electronic trading was introduced, moving business onto the telephone and the screen. [cite:dv2_moneyweek_bigbang] Foreign banks rushed in, mergers followed, and the market's center of gravity began to move east to Canary Wharf. Some critics argue that the looser rules of that era contributed to the crisis of 2008; the claim is debated, and the sources used here record it as an argument rather than a finding. [cite:dv2_moneyweek_bigbang]

The scale today. The measurable result is a financial center with few equals. Every three years the world's central banks survey trading in currencies and derivatives through the Bank for International Settlements. In April 2025, the most recent survey, average daily foreign-exchange trading in the United Kingdom was $4,745 billion, 37.8 percent of all the currency trading in the world, and the United Kingdom remained the single largest center. It accounted for 49.6 percent of global turnover in over-the-counter interest-rate derivatives, an average of $4,320 billion a day. [cite:dv2_boe_triennial_2025] Three years earlier the shares had been 38.1 percent and 45.5 percent: London's lead in currencies held steady, and its lead in derivatives grew. [cite:dv2_boe_triennial_2022] Within the Square Mile itself, the Corporation counts 614,500 workers, reports that the City holds one in every five financial-services jobs in Great Britain, and puts the City's annual economic output at nearly £85 billion. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts]

The offshore network. London's market does not end at the coast. Around it sit the Crown Dependencies, Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, each with its own legislature, and the British Overseas Territories, among them the British Virgin Islands, the Cayman Islands and Bermuda. [cite:metabunk_city_sovereign_debunk] [cite:dv2_ferret_cthi_2024] The independent campaign group Tax Justice Network publishes two indices that measure what it calls tax-haven risk. Its Corporate Tax Haven Index, released on 1 October 2024, ranked the British Virgin Islands, the Cayman Islands and Bermuda first, second and third in the world, attributed about a third of global corporate tax-abuse risk to the United Kingdom and its network, and estimated that countries lose £63 billion a year in corporate tax through that network, or £126 billion including evasion by wealthy individuals. [cite:dv2_ferret_cthi_2024] Its 2020 Financial Secrecy Index moved the United Kingdom from 23rd to 12th place, a change it attributed mainly to the territories and dependencies. [cite:dv2_taxjournal_fsi_2020]

Those figures come from an advocacy organization with a stated position, and they are estimates built on its own methods. The government's tax authority, HMRC, answered the 2024 index by pointing out that Jersey and Guernsey have committed to recording the true owners of companies and to sharing that information on request. [cite:dv2_ferret_cthi_2024] The disagreement is real and should be presented as one. What is not disputed is the structure: a very large financial market in London, linked by law, language and long habit to a ring of smaller jurisdictions with their own rules.

The power this section describes is power of a particular kind. It is not the power to command, which London's market has never had. It is the power to lend, to clear, to price and to hold: to raise money for governments and companies on terms that other centers cannot match, to keep the reserves of currencies that are not its own, and to give legal form to institutions that operate elsewhere. Those were exactly the services a political movement without a state, and later a state without much capital, would need. The sections that follow trace how the Zionist movement and Mandate Palestine used them.
