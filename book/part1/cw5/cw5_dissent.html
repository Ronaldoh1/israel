Dissent Among American Jews: The American Council for Judaism and "Peoplehood"

The Justice Department's order of November 1962 and the Senate inquiry of 1963 are often told as a contest between the American government and a pro-Israel lobby. The record of these years shows a third party to the argument, and it was not outside the Jewish community. The American Council for Judaism, an organization of American Jews opposed to Zionism, welcomed the order, argued in public over what the relationship between American Jews and the State of Israel meant in law, and drew answers from the American Zionist Council and from the head of the Jewish Agency's American Section. The questions raised by the foreign-agents inquiry, in other words, were also being argued among American Jewish organizations themselves, by name and on paper.

Origins, 1942. The Council was founded in 1942 by 36 Reform rabbis led by Louis Wolsey. They acted in protest at a resolution of the Central Conference of American Rabbis supporting a Jewish army in Palestine. Within a year the leadership had passed to the layman Lessing J. Rosenwald and to Rabbi Elmer Berger. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_acj]

Before and after 1948. The Council's position was more specific than a general hostility to Jewish settlement in Palestine. Before 1948 it accepted the recommendation to admit 100,000 Jews to Palestine, while opposing the creation of a Jewish state. After the state was founded, its aim shifted: it sought to limit Israel's influence on the Jews of the Diaspora. It also built an institutional alternative. By the end of 1955 it had set up ten schools teaching classical Reform Judaism. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_acj]

In an unsigned article of 1997, the Council gave its own account of its work in these years. It says it sponsored speakers who were concerned that Judaism was being subsumed by Israeli nationalism, and that Senator J. William Fulbright, chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, whose committee conducted the 1963 inquiry, appeared on one of its platforms. The article gives no date for that appearance. [cite:rs_cw5_acj_1997] These are the Council's statements about itself, written more than three decades later, and the sources reached here do not confirm them independently.

The order of 1962. When the Justice Department ordered the American Zionist Council to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act in November 1962, the American Council for Judaism welcomed it, according to the researcher Grant F. Smith. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] The order itself, and the American Zionist Council's resistance to it, are told in [scene:s11a|the FARA order]. The welcome was consistent with the Council's long-standing aim of limiting Israel's influence on the Jews of the Diaspora. The sources reached do not quote a statement the Council issued in November 1962, so its stated reasons at the time are not on the record here.

Of the Senate committee's examination of the Jewish Agency in the summer of 1963, the Council's 1997 account gives the same reading as Smith: that the evidence showed Agency money funding American lobbying and public relations. That account adds claims about closed sessions and White House intervention that the contemporary reports found do not confirm. Those claims, and the opposing reading of the Agency's chairman, are set out in [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the committee]. [cite:rs_cw5_acj_1997]

The "peoplehood" exchange, spring 1964. The sharpest public argument came the following spring. On 14 March 1964 the Council wrote to the State Department about the legal status of "Jewish peoplehood." The Department replied on 20 April 1964, and the Council publicized the reply. The American Zionist Council answered that "the concept of Jewish people-hood is purely a spiritual and cultural one." [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19640515] The Jewish Telegraphic Agency's dispatch, printed in the Detroit Jewish News on 15 May 1964, is the source for the exchange; the summary reached here does not quote the State Department letter's own wording, and this book does not supply it.

The American Zionist Council's sentence is the one document of the exchange quoted in full here, and its timing gives it weight. It came from the organization whose relationship to the Jewish Agency the Senate had just examined, and which the Justice Department had ordered to register as an agent of a foreign principal. In it, that organization placed the bond of Jewish "people-hood" in the spiritual and cultural sphere, with the word "purely." [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19640515] The sources reached do not record how either side characterized the outcome. As a matter of reading only, the sentence can be taken two ways: as the Zionist Council's answer to the suggestion that its work made American Jews part of a foreign political body, or as the agreement on a narrow definition that the Council for Judaism had been seeking. Both readings rest on the same sentence.

The Jewish Agency answers. The argument reached the State Department again through the Jewish Agency itself. Rose L. Halprin, chairman of the Jewish Agency's American Section, corresponded with Phillips Talbot, the Assistant Secretary of State, in May and June 1964 about the Council's statements. In October 1964 she sought advice on how to rebut an article by W. T. Mallison Jr. on Jewish peoplehood and on the Council's views. [cite:rs_cw5_cornell_konvitz] The record of this correspondence survives in a file headed "Jewish Agency, 1963–1970" in the papers of Milton R. Konvitz at Cornell University; the description reached here is the archive's finding aid, not the letters themselves. [cite:rs_cw5_cornell_konvitz] It shows that the Jewish Agency's American Section treated the Council's arguments as serious enough to take up with an Assistant Secretary of State and to seek a rebuttal.

What the dissent shows. The Council's place in this story is a corrective to two simpler accounts. One treats the foreign-agents inquiry as an attack on American Jews from outside; the record shows that an organization of American Jews, founded by Reform rabbis and led by Rosenwald and Rabbi Elmer Berger, supported the order and argued the case for separating religion from the Israeli state. The other treats the Council's position as the hidden view of American Jews generally; the sources reached here make no such claim, and the Council lost several prominent lay supporters after the 1967 war. Both the Council and the bodies that answered it, the American Zionist Council and the Jewish Agency's American Section, were named organizations with named leaders, and the argument between them is documented as an argument between organizations, not as the opinion of a community.

After 1967. The June 1967 war led several prominent lay supporters to leave the Council; Elmer Berger was ousted as executive vice president in 1968, and the Council's journal Issues ceased publication in November 1969. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_acj]

Several things remain unsettled by the sources reached. The State Department's letter of 20 April 1964 is known here only through the Council's publicity and the JTA's report of it, and its exact terms are not quoted. Talbot's replies to Halprin, and whatever rebuttal of Mallison was finally written, are described only in an archival finding aid. The date of Fulbright's appearance on a Council platform, and the Council's own claims about the 1963 hearing, rest on its unsigned 1997 account. The size of the Council's membership in these years is not given in this material. What is documented is the argument itself: in 1962–64 the legal meaning of the tie between American Jews and Israel was contested between American Jewish organizations, in letters to the State Department, by name.
