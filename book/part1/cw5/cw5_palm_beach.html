Palm Beach, December 1962: The Terms of the Relationship

Stage Four ended with two decisions of the summer and autumn of 1962: Kennedy's offer of the Hawk anti-aircraft missile to Israel, and a second, brief American visit to the reactor at Dimona in late September, both told in [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision]. This section takes up what those two decisions left behind. By the turn of 1962–63 Washington had begun to arm Israel, had looked at Dimona twice and found nothing it was prepared to call alarming, and had not yet decided what the relationship between the two states actually was. The best record of how Kennedy himself described it in that winter comes from a private conversation with Israel's foreign minister at Palm Beach, two days after Christmas.

"No evidence": the circular of October 1962. A month after the September visit, the State Department told its own diplomats what that visit had found. A circular airgram, CA-4726, titled "Israel's Dimona Reactor" and drafted on 24 October 1962, informed American posts that a recent visit by American scientists had again found Israel's statements of peaceful intent consistent with what they had seen, and that there was "no evidence" of preparation for the production of weapons. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d87] The word "again" referred back to the first American visit of 1961. Two details of the circular say as much about the politics of the reactor as its finding does. The embassy in Tel Aviv was excluded from the distribution, and no publicity was planned. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d87] The reassurance was meant for American missions in other capitals, where questions about Dimona were being asked; it was not meant to become a public American certificate of the reactor's innocence, and it was not to be shared with the government whose reactor it described.

The visit on which the circular rested was not one its own later editors admired. William Burr and Avner Cohen, who edited the declassified record of the following year's correspondence for the National Security Archive, call the improvised second visit of late September 1962 a "fiasco." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That is the editors' judgment, made with the benefit of documents that came later; it is not the judgment the State Department recorded in October 1962. The two readings sit some distance apart, and the distance is itself part of the story of 1963. The circular represented what Washington was prepared to say in the autumn of 1962. Within three months, as the next section shows, its own intelligence community was saying something rather different.

Palm Beach, 27 December 1962. Golda Meir, Israel's foreign minister, met Kennedy at Palm Beach on 27 December 1962. The record of the meeting, reproduced by the National Security Archive, shows Kennedy acknowledging a United States "special relationship" with Israel. It also records, for the first time explicitly, an American commitment to support Israel "in case of an invasion." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Both phrases were Kennedy's own, and the record marks the second as the first explicit commitment of its kind.

He attached a limit in the same conversation. The relationship, Kennedy said, could not be allowed to damage American ties with the other states of the region. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The two halves belong together. The phrase "special relationship" is often quoted on its own, as if it described an alliance; in the record of the meeting it arrived with a condition that described a balance. Kennedy was at that moment pursuing the opening to Nasser's government in Cairo described at the start of this stage, and the Palm Beach formula was compatible with it. Israel was to have a promise of support against invasion; the Arab states were not to have reason to conclude that the United States had chosen a side.

On the reactor, the record of Palm Beach is short. Kennedy spoke of nonproliferation only vaguely, and Meir "reassured the President" that there would not be "any difficulty" over Dimona. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Nothing in the record shows Kennedy asking for a further visit, a schedule of visits, or any assurance more specific than the one Meir volunteered. That restraint did not last. Three months later the State Department was instructing its ambassador in Tel Aviv to seek "semi-annual" visits to the site, the opening of the exchange told in [scene:s11b|the battle of the letters]. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The money and the missiles. The commitments of Palm Beach had a material counterpart in the Hawk. The terms of the sale are not given in the declassified documents collected for this stage. One contemporary account gives figures. I. L. Kenen, the founding director of the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, told an off-the-record session of the United Jewish Appeal's Third Annual Young Leadership Conference in New York on 13 September 1963 that the Hawk missiles were financed by a loan of about $25 million, repayable over ten years. [cite:factually_aipac_fara] [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] The interest rate in the surviving text of his address is illegible. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963]

In the same address Kenen put total American aid to Israel at about $85 million in 1963, up from about $60 million a year earlier in the decade. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] These figures should be read for what they are. They are Kenen's, given in a speech, not a government accounting. They survive as an optical-character-recognition text of a document held in the Herbert A. Friedman Collection at the American Jewish Archives, and they have not yet been checked against the scanned page; they are given here as approximate, as he gave them, and not as exact amounts. [cite:rs_cw5_kenen_uja_1963] What they show with more confidence is the direction he described to his audience: more American money in 1963 than in the years just before, and a missile sale paid for on credit rather than given.

The aid panel below sets that year in the longer sequence. It marks 1948 to 1961 as a period of economic aid and loans only, and 1962 as the year of the first American military aid to Israel, extended as loans. Kenen's description of a ten-year loan for the Hawk is consistent with that row: on his account, the missiles were bought on credit, not granted. How far that dating holds is examined in Stage Four; the panel records it as the dashboard gives it.

A promise, not a guarantee. The Palm Beach language was a spoken assurance recorded in a memorandum of conversation. It was not a treaty, a joint declaration or a public commitment, and Kennedy declined over the following months to turn it into any of these. When Ben-Gurion proposed in April 1963 a joint American–Soviet guarantee of the territorial integrity of the region's states, Kennedy refused it, and through the summer of 1963 he remained "unwilling to make 'explicit' guarantees." An American official named Grant advised that a unilateral American guarantee "should not be considered." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The commitment of December 1962 was therefore the outer edge of what Kennedy was prepared to say, not the first step toward something more formal. How Ben-Gurion pressed on that edge, and how Kennedy held it, is the subject of the battle of the letters.

Two policies at once. The documents of these months show the same administration moving in two directions, and neither cancels the other. The United States was selling Israel a missile system on credit, raising its aid, and, in the President's own words, acknowledging a special relationship and a commitment against invasion. The same government had already begun, in its intelligence agencies, to treat Dimona as a potential weapons programme, and within months would press Israel harder on the reactor than on any other question. Kennedy armed Israel and pressed it on Dimona in the same year; the record does not support a reading of 1962–63 that keeps only one of the two.

The longer argument over what the alliance that began in these years has given the United States, and at what cost, is set out at its strongest in [scene:wa_s07|the case for the alliance], which carries that argument forward from the Hawk sale to the present. That argument belongs to later decades. In the winter of 1962–63, the relationship had a name, a missile loan and a spoken promise, and its two governments had not yet tested how much weight any of them would bear.

The record leaves several things open. The text of the Hawk loan agreement, its interest rate and its final value are not in the sources collected for this stage; the only figures are Kenen's, given in a speech and surviving in an unchecked transcription. The Palm Beach memorandum records Kennedy's words but not what, if anything, he intended "in case of an invasion" to require in practice, and no later document in this stage tests it. Nor does the record explain why the State Department kept its October circular from the embassy in Tel Aviv, beyond the fact that it did.
