Two Real Histories: Presence, Continuity and National Identity

Two claims about this land circulate constantly, and each comes in two mirror-image versions. The first holds that one people is indigenous and the other a recent colonizer: Jews native and Arabs latecomers, or the reverse. The second holds that there is no distinct Palestinian people at all, only Arabs in general, and that a Palestinian national identity was invented recently. Both claims rest on the same error. They fold three questions into one: who lived in the land, whose culture and connection to it run back unbroken, and when a modern national identity took shape. The documents treat these as separate questions, and they give separate answers. Kept apart, the answers do not produce a single winner.

The familiar version runs that only one population has a real and ancient claim to the land, and that the other's national identity was invented recently and has no deep roots. Taken one question at a time, the record supports neither half of that sentence as it is usually meant.

The first question: ancient Jewish presence. Ancient Jewish kingdoms existed in parts of the land, and Jewish religious, cultural and historical connections to the region are documented in archaeological, textual and religious sources over more than three thousand years.[cite:substack_historical_record_balfour] This is not in dispute among mainstream historians of any political orientation. It is one of the points in this history where the record is simply settled, and the section on the ancient record in this chapter sets it out in detail. What that continuity does not do, by itself, is settle a modern question of sovereignty or statehood. Ancient presence and modern political legitimacy are related, but they are not the same question.

The second question: a counted Arab population. The land was not empty. The Ottoman census of 1871–72 counted 381,954 residents of Palestine, excluding the Bedouin, of whom roughly 85 percent were Muslim, 11 percent Christian and 4 percent Jewish.[cite:cjpme_historical_demography] This Arabic-speaking population's presence under Ottoman rule is documented continuously for centuries, and modern genetic and archaeological studies find strong Levantine biogeographical continuity in it, consistent with long-term local ancestry.[cite:factually_archaeological_evidence] Two cautions belong beside those figures. The census left out the Bedouin, so the true population was larger than 381,954. And a counted population, however long its roots, is not the same thing as a formal national identity. That is the third question, and it has a later date.

The third question: when national identity formed. The most authoritative statement that Palestinian national identity is modern comes not from a hostile source but from the field's leading Palestinian historian. Rashid Khalidi, Edward Said Professor of Arab Studies at Columbia University and a past president of the Middle East Studies Association, set out the finding in his foundational study Palestinian Identity: The Construction of Modern National Consciousness. Formal Palestinian national identity, he documents, has its roots in the nationalist discourses that emerged among the Ottoman Empire's peoples in the late nineteenth century, and it sharpened through what he identifies as the critical years of 1917 to 1923.[cite:cup_khalidi_palestinian_identity] [cite:wiki_palestinian_nationalism] Khalidi is explicit that this modern political consciousness drew on a much older regional history and a real population presence. A late date for the political identity, in his account, does not mean the people or its culture was invented. He does not treat his own finding as delegitimizing, and it should not be quoted as if he did.

The same pattern describes the other side. Formal Zionist national identity was founded at the First Zionist Congress in Basel in 1897, as a modern political movement built on older religious and cultural material rather than as an ancient ethnogenesis; the movement's founding is told in [scene:s00c|the section on Zionism and Judaism]. The structural parallel is exact on this one point: in both cases a modern political consciousness took formal shape in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, drawing on a far older attachment. To treat "the national identity is recent, therefore illegitimate" as a test for one people and not the other is an inconsistent standard, not a historical finding. The parallel goes no further than that. It describes how formal national consciousness emerged in each case; it does not say that the two movements' histories run alike in every other respect.

"Invented in the 1960s." The sharpest popular form of the second claim says there was no Palestinian people before the Palestine Liberation Organization. The PLO was founded in 1964. The people it claimed to represent were already on the record decades earlier, and the markers are dated and public. A newspaper named Filastin, "Palestine," began publishing in Jaffa in 1911 and campaigned as a Palestinian voice; its story is told in [scene:fil_s01|the section on the newspaper Filastin]. Palestinian Arab congresses met from 1919 and petitioned the Paris Peace Conference. Britain's census of 1922 counted 757,182 people in Palestine. Historians date the emergence of Palestinian national identity to the late Ottoman and British Mandate periods, the same century in which the region's other national identities formed, Jordanian, Syrian and Israeli among them. Measured against that record, the claim is false. Its kernel of truth is the one Khalidi himself documents: the national identity is modern. Its error is to treat a modern identity as a fake one, a standard that would disqualify most of the region's nations, Israel included.

"A land without a people." The best-known slogan of the first claim has a documented origin, and it is not Zionist. Its earliest published version appeared in 1843 in a book by Alexander Keith, a Church of Scotland clergyman and an advocate of Jewish "restoration" who had traveled to Palestine in 1839 and had seen that it was inhabited. Lord Shaftesbury used similar wording in 1853. The first use by a Zionist came in 1901, when Israel Zangwill wrote that Palestine was "a country without a people."[cite:meq_land_without_people] [cite:aijac_slogan_origin] Historians debate what the phrase meant. One reading takes it literally, as a claim that the land was empty. The other holds that it meant a land "without a nation," a population that had not organized itself as a people in the national sense. The second reading is the stronger defense of the phrase, and it turns on the third of the questions above rather than the first. Whatever its users intended, every Ottoman and British census shows a populated country.

"A two-thousand-year war." A companion claim holds that Jews and Muslims have been fighting over this land for two millennia. Jews, Muslims and Christians lived together under Ottoman rule, as [scene:s00e|the section on coexistence] sets out. The national conflict is roughly a century old: it began with modern political Zionism in 1897 and with the British Mandate, and the first Palestinian national movement was organized jointly by Muslims and Christians: a national alignment, not a religious one. A third slogan of the same family, that the Arabs "rejected every offer," compresses a record that runs in both directions. Arab leaders rejected the Peel plan of 1937 and the partition plan of 1947; Israel's refugee offer at Lausanne in 1949 was made only within a full peace and later withdrawn; and the offer at Camp David in 2000 is disputed by participants on the American side, who say it was never written down. Those episodes belong to later chapters of this library. What the three slogans share is that each is short enough to travel, and the documented history behind each is longer and, in each case, less flattering to the side that repeats it.

The population, counted and growing. The panel below gathers the figures the British authorities and the United Nations recorded in the decades after the Ottoman census. Britain's 1922 census counted 757,182 people. Britain recorded 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946. The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP) estimated the population at about 1.85 million in late 1946. These figures bear on both of the claims this section examines. They show a substantial population on the land when the Mandate began, which disposes of "a land without a people" for the twentieth century as the 1871–72 census does for the nineteenth. They also show that immigration was a substantial part of the change that followed. The counts come from different authorities using different methods, and the Ottoman figure excluded the Bedouin, so the growth from 381,954 to about 1.85 million cannot be read off as a single like-for-like series.

The strongest case on each side. Put at its strongest, the Jewish claim to indigeneity rests on what is not in dispute: ancient kingdoms in the land, and a religious, cultural and historical connection to it documented across more than three thousand years in archaeology and text.[cite:substack_historical_record_balfour] Put at its strongest, the Palestinian claim rests on what is equally documented: a substantial Arabic-speaking population, counted by the Ottoman state itself, present continuously for centuries, with genetic evidence of long-term local ancestry, and forming the great majority of the land's people when the modern contest began.[cite:cjpme_historical_demography] [cite:factually_archaeological_evidence] Neither case requires the other to be false. Each popular version of the argument becomes false only at the point where it denies the other.

What the record does not settle. It settles the facts: an ancient Jewish connection, a counted and continuous Arab population, and a modern national identity on both sides that took shape through comparable nineteenth- and twentieth-century political processes. None of those three facts cancels either of the others. The record does not settle what follows from them politically: who should hold sovereignty, in what borders, under what arrangement. Those are questions of law and politics, and the historical record can inform them without deciding them. What it does rule out is the framing of "who is truly indigenous" as a contest with one legitimate winner. The documents do not support that framing for either side.
