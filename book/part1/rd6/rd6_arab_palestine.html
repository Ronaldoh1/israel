Who Would Govern Arab Palestine, 1948–1951

By the late summer of 1948 the question of who would govern the parts of Palestine that Israel did not hold had become a contest among Arab governments as much as a matter for Palestinians themselves. Three answers were offered within a few months of one another: an Arab League administration, a Palestinian government proclaimed in Gaza, and union with the Hashemite kingdom across the Jordan. By April 1950 the third had prevailed on the West Bank, Egypt held the Gaza Strip under military administration, and the Gaza government had become, in one historian's word, a phantom. This section follows that contest from September 1948 to the assassination of King Abdullah in July 1951.

A common account holds that the Arab states went to war in 1948 for one purpose only, to destroy the new Jewish state, with no territorial aims of their own. The record of these three years does not bear that out. Jordan's army took and kept territory, and its parliament annexed it. Egypt took and governed another strip of land for nineteen years. Their rivalry over what would become of Arab Palestine was itself one of the forces that shaped the outcome.

The armies came first. Egypt joined Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq in invading the newly declared State of Israel in May 1948, after the end of the British Mandate. [cite:wikipedia_1948_war] Jordan's Arab Legion, trained and commanded by British officers, took the Old City of Jerusalem and the West Bank. [cite:wiki_jordan_annexation] It was Jordan's most consequential and successful action of the war, and the territory it held afterward would stay under Jordanian administration for the next nineteen years. Egypt's forces ended the war holding the Gaza Strip. Where the armies stood when the fighting stopped decided, in practice, which Arab government would have the power to act in each part of the country.

The Arab League tried to set the terms first. On 8 July 1948 it created a temporary civil administration for Palestine, and in September it agreed to an Arab government seated in Gaza, announced on 20 September. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] Two days later, on 22 September, a communiqué of the Arab Higher Committee declared Palestine independent under the All-Palestine Government, headed by Ahmad Hilmi Abdul Baqi. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] On 30 September a Palestinian National Council met in Gaza under the presidency of Hajj Amin al-Husayni. It adopted a provisional constitution and declared independence over the whole of Palestine. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990]

These were acts of Palestinian political leaders, taken in their own name, and they claimed the whole country, not a partition share of it. Five Arab states recognized it. Egypt recognized the new government first, then Iraq, then Syria, Lebanon and Saudi Arabia in the middle of October. King Abdullah of Transjordan refused. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990]

His refusal was not a passing objection. On the same day the National Council met in Gaza, 30 September 1948, a rival congress in Amman pledged loyalty to Abdullah. On 1 December 1948 the Jericho Congress went further: it proclaimed the union of Palestine and Transjordan and acknowledged Abdullah as king. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] Palestinians were thus present on both sides of the contest. One set of notables proclaimed independence in Gaza; another, in Amman and Jericho, asked to be joined to the Hashemite crown.

Why did the Arab states back a government in Gaza at all? The historian Avi Shlaim, in a study of the All-Palestine Government published in 1990, argues that Egypt sponsored it as a "focal point of opposition to Abdullah," to frustrate the king's plan to join Arab Palestine to Transjordan. His verdict is severe: the government was "a phantom deliberately created by the Arab states, with Egypt at their head." [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] The events bear out how little ground it had to stand on. After Israel's October 1948 offensive its ministers were moved to Cairo, and it governed nothing from there. Gamal Abdel Nasser closed its offices in 1959. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990]

Shlaim's judgment and the Palestinian initiative are both part of the record, and they do not cancel each other. The declaration of 22 September and the Council of 30 September were real political acts by Palestinian leaders who claimed a state in the whole of their country. What they lacked was the means to make that claim good: an army, a territory under their own control, and patrons whose interest lay in their success rather than in blocking a rival. The government in Gaza depended on Egypt's sponsorship, and its fate followed Egypt's purposes.

Egypt's own position in Gaza took a form that set it apart from Jordan's. From 1948 to 1967 Egypt administered the Gaza Strip as a military protectorate. It governed the territory directly but never annexed it as Egyptian territory, and Egyptian officials kept to that distinction throughout. The record shows the fact of non-annexation more clearly than the reasons for it. Some accounts stress the Arab League's consensus against any formal annexation of Arab Palestine; others stress Egyptian strategic caution. The sources used here do not settle which weighed more. After the revolution of 1952, Nasser's rise made Egypt the leading voice of pan-Arabism through the 1950s, and in 1956 his nationalization of the Suez Canal brought an invasion by Israel, Britain and France that was reversed under American and Soviet pressure. [cite:wikipedia_suez_crisis] That war belongs to a later volume. The common belief that Egypt never had any administrative role in Gaza is mistaken: for nineteen years it was the governing power there, a history that still shapes Egypt's relationship with the territory's border.

On the West Bank, Abdullah moved step by step. He took the title of king in December 1948, and in April 1949 he ordered the country renamed the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] The scale of what he was absorbing was large. About 500,000 Palestinian Arabs had taken refuge in Transjordan or the West Bank. The West Bank itself had about 500,000 indigenous residents. Transjordan's prewar population had been about 340,000. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] By these estimates the Palestinians brought under the crown, residents and refugees together, outnumbered the kingdom's original subjects by roughly three to one. Elections were held on both banks in April 1950, and after them Abdullah formally annexed the West Bank. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] The ceasefire lines on which this rested were fixed in the Jordanian–Israeli armistice of April 1949, which is told in [scene:rd6_armistices|the Rhodes armistice agreements].

On 24 April 1950 Jordan's parliament passed the resolution of union. The sources render its central phrase in two slightly different translations. One gives it as support for "complete unity between the two sides of the Jordan and their union into one State," grounded in what the resolution called the "national, physical and geographical unity" of both banks. [cite:wiki_jordan_annexation] Martin Kramer, writing in 2020, quotes it as "complete unity between the two banks of the Jordan, the eastern and western, and their union in one single state." [cite:rs_rd6_kramer_annexation] The meaning is the same in both.

The language is worth reading slowly. The idea that the two banks of the Jordan belong together as one political unit had appeared earlier in this history in Ze'ev Jabotinsky's Revisionist Zionist program, which claimed both banks for a Jewish state. In 1950 the Hashemite Kingdom used the same territorial reasoning in the opposite direction, to bring the West Bank under the crown in Amman. The parallel is one of structure. The sources do not show that either movement borrowed the idea from the other or was answering it.

The next day, 25 April 1950, the British cabinet agreed to recognize the union of Arab Palestine and Jordan together with the State of Israel. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_cab128] The decision paired two recognitions: Britain accepted Jordan's annexation and Israel in the same act.

How many other states recognized the annexation is disputed, and the sources consulted for this book do not settle it. The Library of Congress country study of Jordan says it was recognized only by Britain and Pakistan. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] Kramer notes that Israelis customarily say either that only Britain recognized it or that Britain and Pakistan did. [cite:rs_rd6_kramer_annexation] Another account, used elsewhere in this library, names three states, Britain, Iraq and Pakistan, and adds that even Britain's recognition excluded Jerusalem. [cite:sixdaywar_jerusalem] All agree that the list was very short and that Britain was on it.

The strongest opposition to the annexation came not from Israel or the Western powers but from Jordan's partners in the Arab League. The Library of Congress study records that the annexation was condemned within the League as advancing Hashemite dynastic ambitions. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] Weeks after the vote, the League's Political Committee met in Cairo, with Jordan absent, and ruled that the annexation violated the League's own earlier resolution, which had declared that annexation of Arab Palestine by any single Arab state would be treated as a breach of the Charter and subject to sanctions. The committee went so far as to consider expelling Jordan from the League. [cite:sixdaywar_jerusalem] Later scholarship links this rejection partly to competing Arab state ambitions over Palestine's future, a thread the sources here do not fully unpack. The same rivalry that produced the Gaza government in September 1948 shaped the reaction to Jordan's union in 1950.

Jordan's rule over the Old City had another side, which the record documents plainly. During the nineteen years of Jordanian administration, Jewish access to the Western Wall and the other Jewish religious sites of the Old City was denied, contrary to the terms of the 1949 armistice agreement, and dozens of historic synagogues were damaged or destroyed. These facts are corroborated across independent historical accounts.

The contest ended in violence. On 20 July 1951 Abdullah was assassinated as he entered al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem for Friday prayers, with his grandson Hussein at his side. The assassin was a Palestinian, reportedly hired by relatives of Hajj Amin al-Husayni, [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] the man who had presided over the National Council in Gaza less than three years before. The account of who hired him is reported, not established in the sources used here.

By the summer of 1951 the shape that would last until 1967 was in place. Jordan held and had annexed the West Bank and East Jerusalem. Egypt administered Gaza without annexing it. The All-Palestine Government survived only as offices in Cairo. The refugees who had crossed into both territories, and how the host governments treated them, are the subject of [scene:dsp_s01|where the refugees went].

Several questions remain open in the record used here. It does not establish how much support each side commanded among Palestinians in 1948–1950, whether the Gaza government or the Jericho union. The April 1950 elections are recorded, but the sources give no figures on how they were conducted. The reasons Egypt chose administration over annexation in Gaza are disputed. The list of states that recognized Jordan's annexation is given three ways, and the assassin's sponsors are named only on the strength of report. What the record does settle is that no independent Arab government came to rule any part of Palestine in these years, and that the rivalry between Egypt and Jordan, as well as the war with Israel, had much to do with it.
