Part Eighteen established that Israel builds its family support into national insurance, as rights that follow residence and work: 15 weeks of paid maternity leave since 2017, a universal child allowance of NIS 173–219 a month per child in 2026, publicly funded IVF and, in practice, publicly funded abortion through committees that approved 99.6 per cent of requests in 2020. [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] [cite:us18_nii_childrates] [cite:us18_toi_abortion2022] The United States guarantees unpaid leave, gives most child support through the tax code, spent 0.60 per cent of GDP on family benefits in 2023 against Israel's 1.89 per cent on the same OECD measure, and since June 2022 has let each state set abortion law, with 13 total bans in force in January 2026. [cite:us18_crs_leave] [cite:us18_socx_usa] [cite:us18_socx_isr] [cite:us18_kff_abortioncov] Each record has its own gaps: Israel's allowances were cut in 2002–04 and 2013, raising child poverty, and its child poverty rate was 28 per cent in 2024 on the national measure, and 23.4 per cent in 2023 on the OECD's comparable scale; America's Social Security trust funds are projected to be exhausted in 2034, and its child poverty rate more than doubled on the SPM when the expanded tax credit lapsed in 2022. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007] [cite:us18_nii_pov2024] [cite:us18_crs_ss2026] [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] The headline poverty rates, 10.2 per cent in the United States in 2025 and 20.7 per cent in Israel in 2024, use different definitions and cannot be set side by side as a measure of the gap. [cite:us18_census_p60290] [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] All figures are as of October 2026; Israel votes on 27 October and the United States on 3 November, and benefit levels and budgets should be rechecked after both elections.
