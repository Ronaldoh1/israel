A City Made by Bargain: The Federal District, 1790–1973

Every ordinary American city is a creature of a state. The Supreme Court said so in 1907: municipal corporations are "political subdivisions of the state, created by it," and "wholly under its legislative control." [cite:dv1_hunter_1907] Chicago's broad powers, for example, come from Illinois's constitution, and the Illinois legislature can limit them by a three-fifths vote. [cite:dv1_il_const_art7] Washington is the one city that belongs to no state at all. Its legal basis is a single clause of the Constitution, Article I, section 8, clause 17, which gives Congress power "to exercise exclusive Legislation in all Cases whatsoever" over a district "not exceeding ten Miles square" that particular states would cede to become the seat of the national government. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] Over that district Congress acts as both the national and the local legislature. It is not required to give the district an elected government, and it is not forbidden to delegate its powers to one. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] Everything that is unusual about Washington follows from that clause.

The bargain of 1790. Where the district would be was settled by a political trade. Over dinner at Thomas Jefferson's house in 1790, Alexander Hamilton, Jefferson and James Madison agreed that the new national government would assume the states' war debts, which Hamilton wanted, and that the capital would sit on the Potomac River, which Southern members wanted; an earlier bill that had placed it on the Susquehanna in Pennsylvania had to be repealed to make room for the deal. [cite:dv4_wtop_residence] The Residence Act was signed on 16 July 1790. Philadelphia served as the capital during the ten years the Act allowed for building, and when the government moved in the autumn of 1800 only the President's House, the north wing of the Capitol and the stretch of Pennsylvania Avenue between them were ready. [cite:dv4_wtop_residence] The part of the district on the Virginia side, around Alexandria, was handed back to Virginia in 1846. [cite:dv4_wtop_residence] The capital of the United States was thus born of a debt settlement, which is worth remembering in a volume that also traces London's power to a debt machine.

Self-government given and taken away. For most of its history the district's local government has existed at Congress's pleasure, and Congress has changed its mind several times. An elected mayor governed the city of Washington from 1820. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] In 1871 an Organic Act ended the separate charters of the cities of Washington and Georgetown and merged the district's government into one territorial structure. [cite:dv4_politifact_1871] Elected government ended in 1874 after financial scandals, and from 1878 the district was ruled by commissioners appointed by the President; from 1967 it had an appointed mayor-commissioner and council. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] District residents gained a vote for President under the Twenty-third Amendment, and a delegate in the House who cannot vote on the floor; an amendment to give them full representation in Congress failed to win ratification. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] The present arrangement dates from the Home Rule Act, signed by President Nixon on 24 December 1973. [cite:dv4_wiki_homerule] [cite:dv1_crs_dc_homerule]

Two myths, cleared away. Two claims about the district circulate widely, and both bear on this volume's question, so they are dealt with here. The first is that the Organic Act of 1871 turned the United States into a private corporation run from Washington. The Act did no such thing. It reorganized the district's local government, and its final section's description of that government as a corporation is the ordinary legal form for a city; as the University of Pennsylvania law professor Kermit Roosevelt put it to fact-checkers, it is "common to constitute cities or municipalities as corporations." The Act concerns the government of the District, not the government of the United States. The claim was popularized by the anti-government "sovereign citizens" movement. [cite:dv4_politifact_1871] The second is that the three red stars on the district's flag stand for a "three-city empire" of Washington, London and the Vatican. The flag is based on the coat of arms of George Washington's family, three red stars above two red bars on a white field, and the fact-check that examined the claim found no evidence that the stars represent London or the Vatican. [cite:dv4_politifact_flag] Neither myth is needed to establish what is genuinely unusual about Washington. The constitutional text is enough.

What the anomaly is, and what it is not. The other two cities in this volume are anomalies of excess autonomy: the City of London keeps privileges that no other English local authority has, and Vatican City is a fully sovereign state. Washington is an anomaly in the opposite direction. It is the one major American city whose residents have less control over their own government than the residents of any state, because the body with the last word, Congress, is elected by everyone except them. Its people send no voting member to the legislature that can overrule their council, rewrite their criminal code and approve their budget. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] [cite:dv1_crs_dc_homerule] It is not sovereign in any sense: it has no foreign relations, no army of its own and no power that Congress cannot take back. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] What makes it matter to this volume is not its own power but the power it was built to house. The district is the seat of the government that this part goes on to examine, and the next section shows how tightly that government holds the city around it.
