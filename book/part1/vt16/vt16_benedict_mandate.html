Benedict XV, Balfour and the Mandate, 1917–1939

Between 1917 and 1922 the future of Palestine was decided by the war, by British policy and by the League of Nations, and the Holy See tried to influence each stage. This is the period that supplies both the strongest exhibit for the view that the refusal was a front, a friendly papal audience given to a Zionist envoy in May 1917, and the densest run of written opposition: a cardinal's warnings after the Balfour Declaration, two papal allocutions, and notes to the League against the draft Mandate. Each document is set out below with its date, author, content, where it is held or published, and how strong it is.

Sokolow's audience, May 1917

Nahum Sokolow, a journalist and a leader of the World Zionist Organization, came to Rome in the spring of 1917 to win support for Zionist aims in Palestine. [cite:vt16_wiki_sokolow] According to Minerbi, he met Monsignor Eugenio Pacelli, the future Pius XII, on 29 April, Cardinal Secretary of State Pietro Gasparri on 1 May, and Benedict XV on 4 May. [cite:vt16_ej_minerbi] Other accounts date the papal audience to 6 May and give it forty-five minutes; they describe Pacelli as receiving Sokolow "in a friendly mood" and offering to put his case to the pope. [cite:vt16_wiki_bxvj] The dates differ by two days, and this volume gives both.

What the pope said is known from the Zionist side. Sokolow wrote a report on his reception dated 12 May 1917; the Pave the Way Foundation, which published extracts in 2012, cites it from file A 18/25 of the Yad Vashem archive, and the accounts that describe Pacelli's warmth rest on Sokolow's report to the Zionist Executive. [cite:vt16_ptwf_zenit] [cite:vt16_wiki_bxvj] In the version most often quoted, the pope called the return to Palestine "providential; God has willed it", and Sokolow reported an "expression of favour" to Chaim Weizmann. [cite:vt16_wiki_sokolow] Minerbi, working from the diplomatic archives, gives the pope's words differently: "The problem of the holy places is of extraordinary importance for us. The sacred rights must be protected", and "We will settle this between the Church and the Great Powers. You must respect those rights to their full extent." In Minerbi's account Gasparri had asked for a "reserved zone" covering Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth, Tiberias and Jericho. [cite:vt16_ej_minerbi]

The audience was real and it was cordial; both readings agree on that. What it meant is disputed. Weizmann told a Zionist conference in London on 20 May 1917 that the Church would "favor the establishment of a Jewish National Home in Palestine"; Minerbi calls that claim far from the truth. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] On the record available, the 1917 audience is one party's report of private warmth, and the Holy See's own conditions as Minerbi reports them were territorial: a protected zone for the shrines and a settlement made with the Great Powers rather than with the movement. Grade: documented that the audience took place; the warmth is reported by one party; the pope's words are disputed.

After the Balfour Declaration

Arthur Balfour's letter of 2 November 1917 to Lord Rothschild committed the British government to a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. [cite:wb_ra_balfour] [scene:rd2_addressee|Road to 1948] explains why the letter went to that address. The Holy See's response, as Minerbi reconstructs it from diplomatic reports, came within weeks. In December 1917 Gasparri told a Belgian diplomat that a Jewish state "would not only endanger the Holy Places and injure the feelings of all Christians". On 28 December Benedict XV told the British representative that he feared Palestine might be handed to "the Jews to the detriment of the Christian interests." [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] The Holy See was excluded from the Paris Peace Conference, and in 1919 Gasparri said that "The danger that we most fear is the establishment of a Jewish state in Palestine." [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] Grade: reported by a named historian from foreign diplomats' records; the texts were not seen by this volume.

The allocutions of 1919 and 1921

The first public statement is a primary document published by the Holy See. On 10 March 1919 Benedict XV addressed the cardinals in a secret consistory in the allocution Antequam ordinem. He said that the Holy Land had returned to Christian hands, that he was anxious about what the Peace Conference would decide, and that it would be a grave sorrow to him and to all Christians "se gl'infedeli venissero a trovarsi in Palestina in una posizione di privilegio e di preponderanza" ("if unbelievers were to find themselves in Palestine in a position of privilege and preponderance"), all the more if the shrines were entrusted to non-Christians. [cite:vt16_va_1919] The text says "unbelievers", not Zionists or Jews; Minerbi reads it, in its context, as aimed at Jewish rule over the holy places. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican]

The second allocution, of 13 June 1921, followed the violence in Jaffa on 1 May 1921, which the Vatican newspaper L'Osservatore Romano blamed on Zionism and Bolshevism. In it, according to Minerbi, Benedict XV said that Jews had been given a "position of preponderance and privilege in Palestine." [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] The Holy See's own summary of that allocution, written in 1943, adds the other half: the pope "did not want to interfere with any rights of the Jewish people." [cite:vt16_cicognani1943b] That is the Holy See's stated position for the whole period: no objection to Jewish life in Palestine, and objection to a political order in which a Zionist body held privileges over the Catholic and other Christian communities. Grade: 1919 primary text; 1921 reported by Minerbi and summarized by the Holy See itself.

The Mandate, 1922

The draft Mandate for Palestine gave Britain responsibility for putting the Balfour Declaration into effect and provided that "An appropriate Jewish agency shall be recognised as a public body for the purpose of advising and co-operating" with the administration; the final text kept both provisions. [cite:vt16_un_mandate] The Holy See sent written objections to the League. Minerbi dates a note from Gasparri to 15 May 1922; it opposed "the Jews being given a privileged and preponderant position in Palestine vis-à-vis the Catholics" and opposed the recognition of the Jewish Agency. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] The Holy See's own later account names two documents with different dates: "a letter from Cardinal Gasparri under date of March 6, 1922" and "an 'Aide Memoire' to the Council of the League of Nations on June 4, 1922." [cite:vt16_cicognani1943b] The three dates may describe three documents or may reflect different datings of the same exchange; the texts themselves were not available to this volume, and the discrepancy is recorded as open.

The objections did not change the outcome. The League Council approved the Mandate on 24 July 1922; its Article 13 gave the Mandatory "All responsibility in connection with the Holy Places", and Article 14 provided for a special commission on "the rights and claims in connection with the Holy Places." [cite:vt16_un_mandate] Minerbi records that the Holy See then accepted the Mandate as the lesser evil. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] For the front test this is the clearest early case of opposition that spent influence: written notes to an international body against a text the Zionist Organization wanted, at the moment it was being drafted.

The Latin Patriarch and the 1920s–1930s

In Jerusalem the Latin Patriarch, Luigi Barlassina, went further than Rome. His pastoral letter of 20 July 1920 said: "Let Palestine be internationalized rather than someday be the servant of Zionism." [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] Zanini describes Barlassina and most of the patriarchal clergy as showing "radical opposition to Zionism and their open support for the Arab cause", and his relations with the British authorities as strained from the start. Urban Catholic elites helped promote the Muslim-Christian Associations of the early 1920s, which opposed a Jewish national home. [cite:vt16_zanini_jq] The first of those associations was founded in Jaffa on 8 May 1918; its statement of November 1918 declared that "Palestine was an Arab country." [cite:vt16_wiki_mca] Those are the acts of a patriarch and of local Arab Christian and Muslim notables, not of the Holy See.

Rome restrained its patriarch. A deputy acceptable to the British was appointed in 1924, and in February 1929 Valerio Valeri became Apostolic Delegate with instructions to act as the link to the British and to rein Barlassina in. Zanini notes that Valeri did not share antisemitic stereotypes but held that Catholic and Zionist aims were opposed; in the 1930s the Holy See kept away from Arab nationalist initiatives and worked to improve relations with the Mandate government. [cite:vt16_zanini_jq] Its public line stayed critical. After the violence of August 1929 L'Osservatore Romano blamed "the politics of Zionism"; on 6 August 1937 a Vatican verbal note objected to the Peel Commission's partition plan; in October 1938 the paper wrote that "only one of the two races" could live in Palestine; and it welcomed the British White Paper of May 1939. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] Zanini adds that the Holy See's answer to Peel was to try to keep as many sanctuaries as possible under the Mandate. [cite:vt16_zanini_jq]

Read against the front test, the period between the wars gives one exhibit for private warmth, the 1917 audience as Sokolow reported it, and a long run of written opposition that cost the Holy See something: it lobbied the League, lost, and accepted the result. The same years show that the Holy See and its own patriarch did not always want the same thing, a reminder that "Rome" is not a single voice even inside its own hierarchy.
