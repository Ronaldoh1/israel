Who Can Become a Citizen

The two states answer the question of who may join them by different rules. The United States grants citizenship to almost everyone born on its soil and lets lawful permanent residents apply after a waiting period, whatever their religion or origin. Israel grants citizenship by birth only to the children of citizens, gives every Jew, and certain relatives of Jews, an immediate right to immigrate and become a citizen, and offers everyone else a discretionary naturalisation route.

Israel: return, birth to a citizen, and discretion

The Knesset passed the Law of Return on 5 July 1950. Its first section reads: "Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh", an immigrant. The minister may refuse only a person acting against the Jewish people or likely to endanger public health or state security; a 1954 amendment added people with a criminal past likely to endanger public welfare. [cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] In 1970 the law was extended to the child and grandchild of a Jew and their spouses, and a Jew was defined as a person born of a Jewish mother or converted to Judaism who is not a member of another religion. [cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] The Nationality Law of 1952 then made every oleh a citizen "by return". [cite:rs_ch01_nationality_1952]

For everyone else the 1952 law gives two routes. A child born in Israel is a citizen at birth only if "his father or mother was an Israel national"; a child born abroad to an Israeli parent is also a citizen. There is no citizenship from birth on Israeli soil alone. An adult who is not covered by the Law of Return may be naturalised, at the Interior Minister's discretion, after a period of residence in the preceding five years, with a right to permanent residence, some Hebrew, an intention to settle, renunciation of any prior nationality and a declaration of loyalty. [cite:us6_refworld_nationality] Palestinian Arabs who stayed in Israel after 1948 became citizens "by residence" only if they had been registered as inhabitants on 1 March 1952 and had stayed continuously or entered legally. [cite:rs_ch01_nationality_1952]

Defenders of the Law of Return describe it as a refuge for Jews after centuries in which other states did not reliably protect them; critics, including the Arab-Israeli legal centre Adalah, note that Palestinian refugees and their descendants cannot return to the same land while any Jew may immigrate. [cite:rs_ch01_dailybeast_lor] [cite:rs_ch01_lor_text]

United States: soil and naturalisation

Under the Fourteenth Amendment, people born in the United States and "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" are citizens. On 20 January 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14160, which sought to deny citizenship to children of parents present unlawfully or temporarily. Lower courts blocked it, and on 30 June 2026, in Trump v. Barbara, the Supreme Court held in an opinion by Chief Justice John Roberts that such children are citizens at birth and rejected any test based on the parents' domicile or status; the order remains blocked. Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito wrote dissents, each joined by Justice Neil Gorsuch, arguing that the clause requires domicile. [cite:us6_fd_barbara] [cite:us6_nlr_barbara]

An adult immigrant becomes a citizen by naturalisation. The applicant must be at least 18, have held lawful permanent residence (a green card) for five years, or three if married to an American citizen, speak and read basic English, pass a civics test and show good moral character. [cite:us6_mpi_nat] The conditions are the same whatever the applicant's religion or descent. The narrower gate is the green card itself, which an applicant must already hold; how green cards are allocated is outside the scope of this part.

The numbers over time

The two series measure different things. Israel's counts immigrants, almost all of whom arrive under the Law of Return and become citizens at once; America's counts naturalisations of people who are already permanent residents. Israel's arrivals came in waves. About 240,000 people arrived in 1949 alone. Arrivals fell to 20,428 in 1980 and 10,642 in 1985, then rose to 199,516 in 1990 and 176,100 in 1991 as emigration from the Soviet Union opened, before falling to 60,201 in 2000 and 16,635 in 2010. [cite:us6_jvl_aliyah] The war in Ukraine produced another wave: about 70,000 arrivals in 2022, the most in 23 years, led by 37,364 from Russia and 14,680 from Ukraine in the first eleven months. [cite:us6_ynet_aliyah2022] The final 2022 figure was 74,400; it fell to 46,033 in 2023, 32,161 in 2024 and 22,522 in 2025, though the share from Western countries rose from 21 to 38 per cent, including 3,781 from the United States. [cite:us6_jvl_aliyah] [cite:us6_ynet_aliyah2025] As the 1970 grandchild clause took effect, the share of immigrants who were not Jewish under religious law rose from nearly 20 per cent in 1990–94 to 53 per cent in 1998. [cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek]

American naturalisations rose over the same period. The Department of Homeland Security puts the annual average at about 210,000 in the 1980s, 500,000 in the 1990s, 680,000 in the 2000s and 730,100 in 2010–19. The pandemic cut the number to 628,250 in fiscal year 2020; it reached 969,380 in 2022 and 878,460 in 2023, the latest year in the department's report. Mexico was the leading country of birth, and the median new citizen had been a permanent resident for seven years. [cite:us6_ohss_nat2023] By 2023, 46 per cent of the country's immigrants were naturalised citizens. [cite:us6_pew_immig2025]

Scaled to population the two are closer than the raw numbers suggest. On this book's own rough arithmetic, 22,522 arrivals in 2025 against a population of about 10.3 million is about 2.2 per thousand, and 878,460 naturalisations in 2023 against about 342 million is about 2.6 per thousand; in 2022, Israel's figure was several times higher. [cite:us1_jpost_ilpop] [cite:us1_wa_uspop]

Who cannot become a citizen

In Israel the clearest exclusion is by marriage. The Citizenship and Entry into Israel Law, passed in 2003 during the Second Intifada as a one-year temporary measure, barred residence permits for Palestinians from the West Bank and Gaza married to Israelis, and later for nationals of Iran, Lebanon, Syria and Iraq. It was extended at least 17 times and upheld by the Supreme Court by one vote in 2006 and again in 2012. [cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] [cite:us6_adalah_cit2019] In 2020 Interior Ministry data showed 800–1,000 applications a year, and 45 people who had gained status through family unification were linked to terror offences between 2001 and 2020, none in 2018–2020. [cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] The law lapsed in July 2021 and was re-enacted on 10 March 2022 by 45 votes to 15, with a stated aim that includes protecting the Jewish majority. [cite:rs_ch01_toi_citizenship2022] In December 2022 the Supreme Court asked the state whether it would amend the new law; the Knesset extended it for a year on 5 March 2023 without amendments, with nine petitions pending and humanitarian permits capped at 58. [cite:us6_adalah_cit2023] This book did not find a primary record of each later renewal, so the law's exact status as of October 2026 is stated here as unverified. Part Eight takes up the effect on Arab citizens. [scene:us8_israel|Part Eight: equality among citizens]

A second exclusion concerns Palestinians in East Jerusalem, which Israel annexed in 1967. Most hold permanent residency, not citizenship. Reuters reported in 2015 that applications rose from 114 in 2003 to 800–1,000 a year and that in 2012–13, of 1,434 applications, 189 were approved and 169 rejected; a former Palestinian negotiator put the number naturalised since 1967 at about 24,000, about 10 per cent. [cite:us6_reuters_ej] Al Jazeera, citing Haaretz in 2022, gives 5 per cent; the two estimates differ in source and date. [cite:us6_aje_revocation] Palestinians in the rest of the West Bank and in Gaza have no route at all; Part Nine sets out their status. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank]

Citizenship can also be taken away. The 1952 law already allowed the minister to revoke it for "a breach of allegiance". [cite:us6_refworld_nationality] A February 2023 law made revocation easier for people convicted of terrorism who receive payments from the Palestinian Authority, and in February 2026 Prime Minister Netanyahu signed the first orders under it, revoking the citizenship of two Palestinian citizens and ordering their deportation, subject to court review. [cite:us6_aje_revocation]

In the United States the largest group without a route is the unauthorised population, which Pew estimated at a record 14 million in 2023. They cannot naturalise, because naturalisation requires lawful permanent residence first. [cite:us6_pew_immig2025] [cite:us6_mpi_nat] Their American-born children, however, are citizens, and the Supreme Court's June 2026 ruling kept it so. [cite:us6_nlr_barbara]
