Resolution 181: The Vote, the Pause and the Numbers

By the autumn of 1947 the question of Palestine had passed from Britain to the United Nations. A special committee had visited the country and recommended partition (its work is told in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP]). It now fell to the General Assembly, the body that had replaced the League of Nations as the forum of the world's states, to decide what to do with that recommendation. [cite:un_unispal_186560] The Assembly did so in two stages: first in an Ad Hoc Committee on Palestine, where the plan was debated and alternatives were voted down, and then in plenary session, where a two-thirds majority was required. [cite:avalon_res181] [cite:britannica_res181]

The familiar account compresses all of this into one image: the nations of the world, gathered peacefully, created Israel by democratic consensus. The documents do not support that picture, and they do not support its mirror image either. What they show is a recorded international act with real weight, adopted after a contested count, a pause, and pressure applied by one great power to small states, while the other great power voted the same way for reasons of its own, and while the majority population of the country in question had no seat in the room.

The first count came in the Ad Hoc Committee on 25 November 1947. There the partition plan was adopted by 25 votes to 13, with 17 abstentions. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] On the same occasion the committee rejected the alternative: a draft for a single, unitary state, known as Draft Resolution III, failed by 29 votes to 12, with 14 abstentions. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] A Pakistani amendment that would have capped land ownership at 10% for each community was also rejected, by 22 votes to 8. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] So by 25 November the Assembly had, in committee, turned down both a unitary state and a limit on land holding, and had approved partition. What it had not yet shown was that partition could clear two-thirds in plenary. The accounts this book draws on describe the initial tally of 25 in favor as short of that threshold, and record that the Assembly's president, Aranha, arranged a three-day postponement before the decisive vote. [cite:un_181_records] [cite:un_181_text] The Yearbook excerpt dates the committee vote to 25 November and the plenary vote to 29 November; the material does not record the exact day on which the postponement was called.

The three days are the hinge of the story. A resolution that lacked the votes and then passed after a pause had a specific window in which something changed. The record of that window is uneven. It does not explain every delegation's reasoning, and it was never meant to; what it does document is several of the forces at work inside it, and the plainest of them was the government of the United States. [cite:un_unispal_186560]

The Truman administration's executive branch pushed for a yes vote. Its own foreign-policy and military establishment pushed the other way: the State Department and the Pentagon opposed the push, citing Arab anger and the Cold War stakes of Middle Eastern oil. [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] The most senior internal objection on record came from James Forrestal. Forrestal had been a Wall Street financier before entering government, had served as Secretary of the Navy, and had become the first Secretary of Defense. He approached Palestine through strategy and petroleum, and believed that alienating the Arab world would endanger American access to Middle Eastern oil and hand an advantage to the Soviet Union. [cite:un_unispal_186560] His diaries, published after his death as The Forrestal Diaries (1951), record his distaste for what he called the "bullying" and "coercion" of small, dependent states to secure their votes, and his conviction that American policy in the Middle East was being driven by domestic political calculation rather than national interest. [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] [cite:un_unispal_186560]

The weight of that evidence comes from its form. A diary written for oneself, and not for an audience, is a first-person and contemporaneous source, stronger than a later recollection or a historian's characterization. It records a sitting cabinet officer objecting to the pressure campaign his own government was running. Its limits are just as clear. Forrestal's diary records his characterization and his objection; it is not an operational record of which tactics were used on which country by which official. His warnings were overruled. He left office early in 1949 and, weeks later, died in a fall from a high window at Bethesda Naval Hospital, where he was being treated for depression and exhaustion. The death was ruled a suicide; a minority of historians have noted contested details, and it remains one of the unsettled anomalies of the period. [cite:un_unispal_186560] His opposition is documented. Nothing in the record ties the manner of his death to it, and this book builds no theory on the coincidence of timing.

The pressure Forrestal deplored had named targets. Three states moved from a no vote or an abstention to a yes during the postponement: Haiti, Liberia and the Philippines. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] The historian Michael Cohen, in Palestine and the Great Powers (1982), documents the shift and connects it to a broader pattern of pressure on smaller, economically dependent states in exactly this window. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition]

Liberia is the best-documented case, because its dependency was so large and so visible. The Firestone Tire and Rubber Company held a rubber concession in Liberia that the sources put at about 1.1 million acres; the company was the country's dominant employer, its largest source of foreign exchange, and, in effect, its economy. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] One account in this book's research dates the concession from 1926 and describes it as roughly one million acres on a 99-year lease. The research also attributes the Firestone material to two works by Cohen: Palestine and the Great Powers (1982) in most places, and Truman and Israel (University of California Press, 1990) in one. Liberia's own ambassador complained that the American delegation had threatened aid cuts to several countries in the same period. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] Firestone had no stake in Palestine. The point of the case is the transmission mechanism: a great power's commercial hold over a small client could be turned into that client's vote on a question a hemisphere away. The economic relationship is well documented; the precise way the leverage was exercised is less fully documented than the relationship itself. The corporate pressure and the diplomatic pressure the ambassador described were two separate channels converging on one small state, not one orchestrated scheme.

The Philippines, a recent American possession bound to Washington by aid and security ties, came under American pressure through those economic ties. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] According to the account this book draws on, the Philippine delegation reversed a speech critical of partition and voted yes after American intervention. General Carlos Romulo, the Philippines' own representative, later wrote of being recalled and overruled by his own president: "This particular chapter in history had been exceedingly unpleasant to me." [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] Haiti changed its position after direct communication from the American executive. [cite:militaryhistory_partition_plan] Reports at the time and since describe private approaches to these delegations in the crucial days by influential Americans inside and outside the government; those reports do not name the individuals in the material available here. The evidence is not equal across the three cases. The Liberian link is documented by a serious historian; the exact mechanism behind the Haitian and Philippine shifts is less fully documented, and should be held at that lower confidence.

One delegation said publicly what others did privately. Cuba's delegate announced that Cuba would vote against partition "in spite of the pressure that has been brought to bear upon us." [cite:sagepub_price_resisting_partition] The statement confirms that the same campaign reached states that resisted it as well as those that gave way. Cuba appears in the final list of those voting against. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181]

The other great power needed no persuading. The Soviet Union voted yes, and its motive, as the sources describe it, was not sympathy for Zionism, which Soviet doctrine regarded as bourgeois nationalism. Partition would end British rule in Palestine and weaken Britain's position in the Middle East; many of the Jewish community's leaders were socialists who had grown up in the Russian Empire; and a new state looked, in 1947, like a way to reduce a rival empire and gain influence. [cite:un_unispal_186560] The same logic would bring arms to the new state through Czechoslovakia in 1948. Soviet-Israeli relations cooled within a few years, which fits a vote driven by strategy rather than lasting alignment. (The Soviet record is told in [scene:so_s01|the Soviet layer].) Washington and Moscow, already rivals, pushed the same way for opposite reasons. That is convergence, not consensus.

On 29 November 1947 the Assembly adopted the plan by 33 votes to 13, with 10 abstentions. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] The thirteen against were Afghanistan, Cuba, Egypt, Greece, India, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Syria, Turkey and Yemen. The ten abstaining were Argentina, Chile, China, Colombia, El Salvador, Ethiopia, Honduras, Mexico, the United Kingdom and Yugoslavia. Those in favour included Australia, Canada, France, Poland, Sweden, the Soviet Union, the United States and Uruguay. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] One name in the list carries its own story. Britain, the Mandatory power that would have to hand over the country, abstained.

The resolution was more than a line on a map. It set a timetable. The Mandate was to end "as soon as possible but in any case not later than 1 August 1948." An area with a seaport in the Jewish state was to be evacuated "not later than 1 February 1948." Provisional councils of government were to be in place by 1 April 1948, and the two states were to come into existence two months after the evacuation and "not later than 1 October 1948." A Palestine Commission of five states, Bolivia, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Panama and the Philippines, was to carry it out. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] The plan, in short, depended on an orderly handover by a power that had abstained, carried out by a commission of five states, against fixed dates.

The two cases presented to the Assembly deserve to be heard in their strongest form. The Jewish Agency's argument, as the UN Yearbook records it, set the plan against the map of the region. The Arab states held about 17,000,000 Arabs in roughly 1,290,000 square miles. Palestine, after the separation of Transjordan, was about 10,000 square miles. On that reckoning the plan gave the Jewish National Home less than one-eighth of the territory originally set aside for it. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] The Agency accepted the plan, though it disliked the borders and the separation of Jerusalem. The Jewish Virtual Library, a pro-Israel reference source, states that the Jews of Palestine were dissatisfied on both counts but "welcomed the compromise." It also reports, citing the writers Avneri and Horowitz, that Arab League Secretary-General Azzam Pasha told David Horowitz and Abba Eban on 16 September 1947: "The Arab world is not in a compromising mood." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] That exchange is reported second-hand and should be read as such.

The Arab delegations argued on law as well as justice. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Iraq, Syria and Yemen denounced the plan as contrary to the Charter, illegal and immoral, said they regarded it as a recommendation, and declared that they would not feel bound by it. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] The historian Muhsin Yusuf, of Birzeit University, sets out the reasons for rejection as he reads them: fear of later expulsion and of land confiscation; the injustice of granting statehood to a largely immigrant minority while the majority was denied self-determination; and a view of Zionism as a European imperial project. He recalls that the Arab case to the Anglo-American Committee in 1946 had promised that Jews "will be full citizens" of a Palestinian state. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002]

Behind both arguments lay the same contested facts: how many people lived where, and who owned the land. The Arab majority of Mandate Palestine had no vote in the General Assembly and no direct representation in the decision that partitioned the territory. [cite:britannica_res181] Arab member states voted against; the people most affected were an object of the resolution, not a participant in it. The 1947 British Mandatory census, as summarized in this book's research, counted about 1.3 million Arabs and 630,000 Jews in Palestine, which is the reason the familiar claim that the land was largely empty before 1948 does not survive contact with the record. On the overall shares, the sources agree in outline: Jews were roughly a third of the population (one account gives about 30%), Arabs roughly two-thirds (about 70%). [cite:britannica_res181]

The sources do not agree on the precise allocation, and the disagreement matters, because each figure is used to argue a different case. One account in this book's research gives the Jewish state 55% of the territory and the Arab state 45%, with Jerusalem as an international zone. [cite:britannica_res181] Another, and Yusuf, give the Jewish state 56%; Yusuf gives the Arab state 43%, the other account 44%. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] On population inside the proposed Jewish state, the gap is wider. Yusuf counts about 498,000 Jews and about 494,000 Arabs, a near-even split; the Arab state about 725,000 Arabs and 11,000 Jews; and the Jerusalem district about 105,000 Arabs and 100,000 Jews. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] The Jewish Virtual Library, citing the historian Howard Sachar, gives the Jewish state about 5,500 square miles with 538,000 Jews and 397,000 Arabs, and the Arab state 4,500 square miles with 804,000 Arabs and 10,000 Jews; citing Aharon Cohen, it adds that about 60% of the Jewish state was Negev desert. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] Yusuf's figure supports the argument that the Jewish state would have had barely a Jewish majority; Sachar's supports the argument that it would have had a clear one, on land much of which was desert.

Ownership is a separate question from allocation. One account in this book's research says the Jewish population legally owned under a tenth of the land. [cite:britannica_res181] The summary of the pre-1948 record puts Jewish ownership at approximately 7% of the country's land in 1947. Yusuf gives about 6% of Palestine overall, and about 10% of the land inside the proposed Jewish state. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] These figures are close enough to agree on the essential point: the plan assigned a majority of the territory to a community that owned a small fraction of it. They differ enough that any single number should be quoted with its source.

The map that follows shows where the vote sits in the larger structure. Resolution 181 stands at the point where two lines meet, the special committee's recommendation of partition and the votes lobbied by the American Zionist political machine, and from it runs the line marked "legal basis claimed" to the state proclaimed in May 1948. This section has added a second American line, as it appears in the record of the three days: an executive pressing for votes, over the objection of its own professionals.

What, then, did the vote decide? It recorded a two-thirds majority for partition, and it created a timetable and a commission. It also left decisive questions open. Those voting against, and several of those abstaining, regarded the resolution as a recommendation; Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Iraq, Syria and Yemen said so in terms. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] Within days the country was at war with itself, and the question of who accepted the plan and who fought over it became a separate story, told in [scene:s09b|the two escalations]. The territorial and demographic shares would soon be overtaken: the war of 1948 produced roughly 700,000 Palestinian Arab refugees, a figure documented by Israeli, Palestinian and international historians, including Israeli scholars working in declassified military archives from the 1980s, and Israel's lines in 1949 lay far beyond those of the plan.

Several things remain unsettled. The record establishes the three-day window, the three changed votes and the leverage on Liberia; it does not establish the full mechanism behind the Haitian and Philippine changes, or which American officials made which approaches. The population of the proposed Jewish state is given differently by serious sources, from a near-even split to a clear Jewish majority, and the land shares vary by a point or two depending on the account. And Forrestal's death, ruled a suicide, remains questioned at the margins without any documented link to his politics. What the record does settle is the shape of the decision: not a serene consensus and not a secret plot, but a contested vote, carried over the line by great-power pressure on small states during a three-day scramble, about a people who were not in the room.
