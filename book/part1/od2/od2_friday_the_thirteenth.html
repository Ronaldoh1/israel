Friday 13 October 1307

In the early hours of Friday 13 October 1307, officers of King Philip IV of France arrested the Templars throughout his kingdom and seized their property, acting formally on behalf of the papal inquisitors in France. The charges were that new brothers were made at secret receptions to deny Christ and spit on the crucifix, that they exchanged indecent kisses, that sodomy was permitted, and that they worshipped an idol. In October and November almost all the prisoners, including the Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, confessed. Malcolm Barber, the leading historian of the trial, writes that on many of them "torture was freely used". [cite:vt29_barber_cup2]

Pope Clement V had not authorised the arrests and at first treated them as an affront to his own authority. On 22 November 1307 he changed course and issued Pastoralis praeeminentiae, ordering every Christian ruler to arrest the Templars in his lands. [cite:vt29_barber_cup2] Why Philip acted is a question of motive, and the documents give no single answer. What is documented is that he was short of money for his war with England and owed the order large sums, and that the arrests freed him of those debts and gave him its treasuries. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]

By early May 1310 almost six hundred Templars in Paris had come forward to defend the order before a papal commission. The king's answer was swift. On 12 May 1310 fifty-four of them were burned outside Paris as relapsed heretics: men who had confessed and then withdrawn their confessions. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

Between 17 and 20 August 1308, three cardinals sent by the pope questioned Molay and four other leaders at the castle of Chinon. The record of that inquiry, the Chinon parchment, was found misfiled in the Vatican archive by the palaeographer Barbara Frale in September 2001. It records that the leaders confessed to some charges, chiefly the denial of Christ at reception, which several said was done "in words only, not in spirit", asked for pardon and were absolved from excommunication. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon] It is not a verdict of innocence: an absolution is given to those who confess and repent. Frale reads it as evidence that Clement thought the order could be reformed; other historians warn that testimony given after torture cannot be read at face value in either direction. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon] The full account of the parchment is in [scene:vt29_templars|The Vatican Architecture: the Templars].

At the Council of Vienne, on 22 March 1312, Clement issued Vox in excelso. Its wording matters. He suppressed the order "not by definitive sentence, but by apostolic provision": the council had not convicted it of heresy. The reason given was scandal, which could not be allayed "as long as the order continues to exist". [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne] On 2 May 1312 Ad providam gave the order's property to the Knights Hospitaller, outside the Iberian kingdoms; in Portugal King Dinis refounded the Templar houses as the Order of Christ. [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne] [cite:vt29_nwe_templar] Molay and Geoffroi de Charney were sentenced on 18 March 1314, withdrew their confessions in public, and were burned that evening on an island in the Seine. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

The popular legends go further. The claim that the arrests are the origin of the Friday-the-13th superstition has no documentary basis; the combined date is hard to find as a fixed superstition before the twentieth century, and the Templar link was spread by the 1980s bestseller Holy Blood, Holy Grail and by Dan Brown's novel in 2003. [cite:vt29_natgeo_fri13] What the record supports is narrower and still serious: an order under papal protection was arrested by a king who owed it money, its members were tortured into confessions, men who defended it were burned, and it was dissolved without a verdict.
