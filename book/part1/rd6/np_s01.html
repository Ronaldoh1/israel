How the Flight Was Told: "They Left on Orders" Until the Archives Opened

For more than a decade after 1948, the dominant Western account held that the Palestinians had left voluntarily, on the orders of Arab leaders, and that this had been established at the time. Parts of what happened were indeed reported promptly. But the explanation that took hold for the mass flight was not supported by the documents, and it took some forty years for the documents to displace it.

Some events were known almost at once. The killings at Deir Yassin in April 1948 were reported in Western newspapers within days. They were documented by the Red Cross delegate who entered the village. News of the massacre spread through Arab Palestine, and the historian Benny Morris records it as a major accelerant of flight. [cite:w_morris_birth]

Objection was public, and some of it came from prominent Jewish figures. On 4 December 1948 a group that included Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and Sidney Hook published a letter in The New York Times condemning Menachem Begin's new Herut party. The letter cited Deir Yassin and called the party fascist in its methods. [cite:w_arendt_letter] It stands as a documented Jewish objection at the time, printed in America's paper of record.

Yet the explanation that became standard in the West ran the other way. Arab leaders, the story went, had broadcast orders by radio for civilians to leave. In the 1950s this became the usual explanation in Western and Israeli official accounts. Its effect was to place responsibility for the refugee problem on the Arab states.

The familiar version stopped there. The record did not: the broadcasts had been monitored, so the claim could be tested. In May 1961 the Irish writer Erskine Childers published "The Other Exodus" in The Spectator. He reported that he had examined the British and American monitoring records of Arab broadcasts in 1948, including the BBC's. He found no evacuation orders. He found appeals to stay. His article set off a long public dispute, with claims and counterclaims running for weeks in the letters pages. The Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi had made the same case, from Arab sources, in 1959. [cite:w_childers]

The decisive correction came from Israel's own records. In 1988 Benny Morris published The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem, drawing on newly opened Israeli military and state archives. He concluded that the main causes of flight were military attack, expulsion and fear. Some local Arab commanders, he found, had ordered women and children evacuated, but there was no general Arab order to leave. Later editions of his work found more expulsion orders. [cite:w_morris_birth]

How the account changed matters as much as where it ended. The early version was a political narrative the documents did not support, and the documents that corrected it were Israeli state archives, not an adversary's claims. The correction has limits. Historians still disagree about intent and planning, including the role of the military plan known as Plan Dalet. But mainstream scholarship no longer defends the account that the refugees simply left on orders.

The forty-year gap is itself part of the history. The explanation that took hold in the 1950s was checked against the monitoring records only in 1961, and against Israel's own archives only in 1988.
