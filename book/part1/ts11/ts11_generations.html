Evangelical Dissent and the Generational Shift

The strongest test of the theology shield is not what Catholics or Palestinian Christians think of it but whether it holds among American evangelicals, the constituency it was built on. Here the record shows two things at once. Evangelical scholars and leaders have argued against Christian Zionism from inside the movement for decades, and white evangelical Protestants remain, as of 2026, the American religious group most favourable to the Israeli government. Yet the surveys also show support falling, and falling fastest among the young. This section sets out the named dissenters first and then the dated polls, each with its population and method, because the shift is easily overstated in both directions.

Dissent from inside

The dissent is older than the polls. Gary Burge, who taught New Testament at Wheaton College for about 25 years and then at Calvin Theological Seminary from 2017 to 2023, published Whose Land? Whose Promise? in 2003 (revised in 2013) and Jesus and the Land: The New Testament Challenge to Holy Land Theology in 2010, which examines how the New Testament interprets the Old Testament promises of land. [cite:ts11_wiki_burge] In July 2007, 34 evangelical leaders signed a letter in The New York Times supporting a two-state solution. [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] After John Hagee's webcast of January 2011, Richard Mouw, president of Fuller Theological Seminary, argued that God's promise to bless Ishmael in Genesis 17:20 still binds Christians and that Israel's blessing depends partly on justice towards its neighbours, and Joel Hunter of the National Association of Evangelicals' board said Hagee's view reflects "a limited portion of Christians who are millennial dispensationalists in their theology." [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] Hunter later spoke at Christ at the Checkpoint in Bethlehem, and the World Evangelical Alliance's secretary-general, Geoff Tunnicliffe, was announced among its 2014 speakers. [cite:ts11_jta_catc2014] [scene:ts11_palestinian|Palestinian Christians]

The CUFI side has stated its position in the same debate. Hagee has said that "Israelis – and they alone – have the right to make existential decisions about land and peace," and CUFI describes its own aim as asking Washington not to press Israel into concessions. [cite:gi11_jpost_hagee] In 2014 CUFI's David Brog said he was "worried" that a generational shift might follow, and that "This generation is in play." [cite:ts11_jta_catc2014] The question the surveys answer is whether that worry was borne out.

What the surveys show

The first detailed baseline is LifeWay Research's survey of 2,002 Americans with evangelical beliefs, conducted from 20 to 28 September 2017 and sponsored by Chosen People Ministries and the author Joel C. Rosenberg. It found that 80 per cent said Israel's founding in 1948 fulfilled biblical prophecy, and that support for Israel stood at 58 per cent among those aged 18 to 34 and 77 per cent among those 65 and over. Only 23 per cent favoured a treaty creating a Palestinian state, 31 per cent opposed one and 46 per cent were unsure. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] The age gap was already there; so was a large share of undecided respondents, which is not the picture of a uniformly committed bloc.

The sharpest measured change comes from the political scientists Motti Inbari of the University of North Carolina at Pembroke and Kirill Bumin, whose surveys from 2018 to 2021 appear in their book Christian Zionism in the Twenty-First Century. Among evangelicals under 30, support for Israel fell from 68.9 per cent in 2018 to 33.6 per cent in 2021; support for the Palestinians rose from "a little over 5%" to 24.3 per cent; and those supporting neither side rose from 25 to 42.2 per cent. [cite:ts11_tau_inbari] The authors offered three explanations: an information environment shaped by social media in which Israel is portrayed as the "aggressor"; "a sort of youthful rebellion" against the evangelical establishment; and theology. Bumin reported that almost seven in ten young evangelicals surveyed in spring 2021 held postmillennial or amillennial views, which give Israel little role in end-times prophecy, and that the most pro-Israel, premillennial pastors were older. [cite:ts11_tau_inbari] Tel Aviv University's release of these findings does not give sample sizes, which limits how far a single drop over three years can be generalised.

The most recent large survey is the Pew Research Center's, fielded from 4 to 17 May 2026 among 12,574 American adults, with a margin of error of plus or minus 1.3 points. White evangelical Protestants were "the only religious group in which a majority (57%) have a favorable view of the Israeli government"; 74 per cent viewed the Israeli people favourably and 33 per cent the Palestinian people. Among Catholics the favourable share for the Israeli government was 34 per cent, among white non-evangelical Protestants 33, among Black Protestants 30 and among Jewish Americans 47. [cite:ts11_pew_2026] According to a report of the same data, white evangelicals' favourable view of the Israeli government had fallen from 71 per cent in 2024, and of the Israeli people from 84 per cent. [cite:ts11_crosswalk_pew] Pew's release does not break white evangelicals down by age; its age figures are for all adults and by party. Among Republicans and Republican-leaning adults, 28 per cent of those aged 18 to 29 viewed the Israeli government favourably, against 75 per cent of those 65 and over. [cite:ts11_pew_2026]

What the numbers do and do not show

Set side by side, the surveys measure different things. LifeWay counted people by belief, Inbari and Bumin asked young evangelicals which side they supported, and Pew measured favourable views of a government and a people among white evangelicals and among age groups by party. As of October 2026, then, the documented position is a split rather than a collapse. The constituency on which the shield was built still holds the most favourable view of the Israeli government of any American religious group, while within it, and within the Republican Party, younger cohorts hold markedly cooler views, and evangelical scholars and institutions have argued against the theology for two decades.
