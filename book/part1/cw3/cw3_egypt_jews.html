The Jews of Egypt, 1956–1958

Volume II followed Egypt's Jewish community from about 75,000 people in 1948 through the bombings of that year and the emigration of the early 1950s, in [scene:rd6_exodus|the Jews of the Arab world], and left the decisive rupture to this volume. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] This section tells that rupture. In the months after Israel, Britain and France invaded Egypt in October and November 1956, the Egyptian government detained Jews in their hundreds, issued a nationality law that excluded "Zionists" without defining the word, published a proclamation declaring that "all Jews are Zionists and enemies of the state," and placed Jewish property under state administration. Within about two years, between 23,000 and 25,000 Jews had left. These were the acts of a government, its ministries and its military authorities; they are documented as such here, and the record of them does not license any statement about Egyptians or Muslims as a people.

The sources. The documentation for these events comes from three kinds of source, and the reader should know which is which. The first is reporting by the Jewish Telegraphic Agency: an article by Brenda Gazzar published on 21 December 2006, reported from Ramat Gan, which draws on historians. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] The second is advocacy: an essay by Lyn Julius in the journal Fathom in autumn 2017 (Julius directs the advocacy group Harif), and an undated report by the advocacy organization Justice for Jews from Arab Countries (JJAC), which credits the Historical Society of Jews from Egypt. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] The third, used in Volume II, is the Jewish Virtual Library's summary of contemporary wire reports by the Associated Press and the New York World-Telegram. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] The legal details below, the numbers of laws and articles and the published sequestration lists, come from the advocacy sources. The texts of the nationality law and of the military proclamation themselves were not fetched. The overall numbers of those detained and of those who left are corroborated by the JTA and by the contemporary reports.

Arrest and expulsion, November 1956. The arrests began with the war. According to the JTA, citing historians, about 1,000 Jews in Egypt, many of them Egyptian citizens, were detained or imprisoned during the Suez crisis. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] The contemporary reports cited by the Jewish Virtual Library give the same order: about 1,000 detained. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] Julius gives a sequence. A first wave of about 500 Jews was given 24 hours to leave; about 900 more were arrested by 7 November 1956; and detainees were held at the Abbasiya school, where there were 500, half of them stateless, and at Les Barrages prison, where there were 300. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] The JTA's account of the expulsions distinguishes two groups: Egypt expelled British and French nationals, among whom were many Jews, and it also expelled about 500 Jews who held no British or French passport. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] The first group was expelled as nationals of states then at war with Egypt; the second held no such nationality, and the JTA counts them separately, as Jews.

The nationality law, 22 November. On 22 November 1956 Egypt enacted Nationality Law No. 391 of 1956. According to the JJAC report, the law barred "Zionists" from Egyptian nationality, without ever defining the term, and allowed the Interior Ministry to declare a person's nationality forfeited (Article 18). [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] The mechanism lay in the missing definition. A law that withholds nationality from a political category it does not define leaves the ministry free to decide who falls inside it.

The proclamation, 23 November. The next day supplied a definition of its own. On 23 November 1956 a proclamation by Egypt's Minister of Religious Affairs, read in mosques, declared that "all Jews are Zionists and enemies of the state," and promised their expulsion. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] The contemporary Associated Press and New York World-Telegram reports cited by the Jewish Virtual Library record the same proclamation and the same words. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] This is antisemitism in its plainest form, and it was a state act: a government minister declared every Jew, by virtue of being a Jew, an enemy of the state. Read beside the law of the previous day, it erased the line between a political allegiance and a people.

Sequestration. Property followed persons. Under Military Proclamation No. 4, according to the JJAC report, orders issued between 1 and 20 November 1956 placed the property of hundreds of Jews under Egyptian administrators; the published lists named 486 persons and firms, of which at least 95 per cent were Jewish. [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] The JTA and Julius both give about 500 Jewish companies sequestered between November 1956 and March 1957. Julius adds that about 800 more enterprises were blacklisted, and that the custodians charged a fee of 10 per cent. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] Those who left could not take their property with them. According to Julius, departing Jews received exit visas stamped "ONE WAY-NO RETURN," signed declarations "donating" their property to the state, and were allowed one suitcase and a small sum of money. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius]

Departure. Between 23,000 and 25,000 Jews left Egypt from November 1956, by expulsion or under heavy pressure. The JTA gives that range for the period through 1957; Julius gives it through 1958. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] The contemporary reports cited by the Jewish Virtual Library describe almost 25,000 as expelled and their property confiscated. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_egypt] More than 6,000 left by ship with the Red Cross by 1957, and by that year, according to the JJAC report, the community numbered about 15,000. [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] Roughly half of Egypt's Jews, Julius writes, eventually went to Israel. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] The Jewish Agency's own historical account describes the emigration of many Egyptian Jews to Israel as "an indirect outcome of the war." [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen]

The limits. Julius, whose account is among the strongest statements of the case that this was persecution, records its limits herself. Jews of Egyptian nationality were at first spared; some expulsion orders were rescinded; Greeks, Armenians and Italians were not targeted in 1956; and the wider nationalizations of the 1960s also hit non-Jewish families. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] Her first point sits in some tension with the JTA's report that many of the roughly 1,000 detained were Egyptian citizens. [cite:rs_cw3_jta_2006_egypt] The two may describe different measures, detention on one hand and expulsion on the other, but the sources fetched here do not reconcile them. Her third point cuts in a particular direction: if communities of foreign origin in general had been the target, Greeks, Armenians and Italians would have been treated alike in 1956, and by her account they were not.

Why it happened: the dispute. Two explanations are offered, and the evidence for each should be stated at its strongest.

The first is that this was state persecution of Jews as Jews. Its evidence is the documents' own words and numbers: a minister's proclamation that "all Jews are Zionists and enemies of the state"; a nationality law aimed at an undefined category of "Zionists"; sequestration lists at least 95 per cent Jewish; and, on Julius's account, the sparing of Greeks, Armenians and Italians in the same months. Julius and the JJAC report make this case, and Julius reports Krämer's view that Jews alone were targeted because of the conflict with Israel. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] [cite:rs_cw3_jjac_egypt] The strongest form of the argument is that the measures did not stop at enemy nationals: they reached stateless Jews and Egyptian-citizen Jews, and the proclamation addressed every Jew.

The second explanation places the events in the context of war and decolonization. Egypt had been invaded by Britain, France and Israel; it expelled British and French nationals as enemy nationals, and many of the Jews who left were among them. Julius reports that Joel Beinin holds this view and blames Zionism for what befell the community, and she notes that British and French nationals were expelled too. [cite:rs_cw3_fathom_julius] Those who take this view also point to Operation Susannah: in July 1954 Israeli military intelligence activated a cell of Egyptian Jews, recruited for the purpose, to plant incendiary bombs in Egypt, an affair told in [scene:lv_s01|Operation Susannah and the Lavon affair]. On this reading, Israel's war and that earlier operation sharpened measures that were, at root, war measures. Philip Mendes, whose work Volume II used, offers a wider caution: many Egyptian Jews before 1956 appear to have left for economic reasons rather than because of specific persecution, and the departure of Jews from Arab countries as a whole is best explained as "a complex combination of push and pull factors." [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010] Mendes describes the two partisan versions of the larger story, one attributing the exodus almost solely to Arab violence and the other to "a malicious Zionist conspiracy," and rejects both. [cite:rs_rd6_mendes_2010]

The two explanations are not wholly exclusive. The record supports the second as far as British and French nationals are concerned: they were expelled as nationals of enemy states. It does not support it as an account of the proclamation of 23 November, which named no state, no nationality and no act, only Jews. Whatever Israel's conduct in 1954 or 1956, a government's declaration that all Jews are enemies of the state is a statement about Jews, and the documents record it as made.

Egypt's expulsions are often set against the Palestinian displacement of 1948 as two halves of a single exchange of populations. Whether that framing holds is examined in [scene:s00e3|the two displacements], which finds the two processes different in mechanism, duration and documentation.

The gaps here are substantial and should be stated. The texts of Nationality Law No. 391 and of Military Proclamation No. 4 were not fetched, so their provisions are known only through an advocacy report and an advocacy author; their numbers and dates are not in doubt from the corroborating reporting, but their exact wording is not established here. The number of British and French nationals expelled in 1956, and how many of them were Jews, is not given by any source fetched. The relation between the 1,000 detained (JTA) and the 900 arrested by 7 November (Julius) is not explained. And the views of Krämer and Beinin are known here only as Julius reports them; their own writings were not consulted.
