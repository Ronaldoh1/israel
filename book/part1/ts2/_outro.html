Part Two established that Christian belief in a Jewish return to the land is three centuries older than the Scofield Bible: it ran from Brightman's commentaries, first printed in 1609, and Finch's book of 1621 through Increase Mather in Boston to the London Society of 1809, and in most of its forms it expected the Jews to convert. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] [cite:ts2_wiki_finch] It became policy only when it met a strategic interest. Shaftesbury helped secure a British consul in Jerusalem in 1838, and Palmerston's dispatch of 11 August 1840 asked the sultan to admit Jewish settlers as "a check" on Muhammad Ali, without a word of scripture; the sultan declined. [cite:ts2_wiki_young] [cite:ts2_cojs_palmerston1840] [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] The Anglo-Prussian bishopric of 1841 gave the mission a bishop, a convert from Judaism, and had little practical effect. [cite:ts2_wiki_bishopric] The phrase "a land without a people" was coined by Christian clergy before any Zionist used it; how widely Zionists later used it, and what it meant, remain contested among named historians. [cite:ts2_muir_meq] [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] By the 1890s a restorationist chaplain stood beside Herzl, and the centre of gravity had moved to the United States, where Blackstone presented his memorial in 1891. [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] What restorationism lacked was a system that told believers where they stood in a timetable of the end. Part Three turns to the man who supplied one, John Nelson Darby, and to the conferences that carried his system across the Atlantic.
