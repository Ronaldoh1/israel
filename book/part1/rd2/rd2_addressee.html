Why the Letter Went to Walter Rothschild

The Balfour Declaration is one sentence, but it is written as a letter, and a letter needs an address. On 2 November 1917 Arthur Balfour sent his to "Lord Rothschild" at 148 Piccadilly, and asked him to pass the declaration on to the Zionist Federation. [cite:wb_bl_addms41178] [cite:wb_ra_balfour] The choice looks small. It is not. The government could have written to the man who had done most of the Zionist movement's political work in London, or to the elected body that represented British Jews, or to no one in particular and simply published a statement. It wrote instead to a peer who had left banking nine years earlier to study birds and butterflies. This section asks who he was, what he was worth, what kind of power he held, and why the letter went to him rather than to anyone who could claim to speak for a community. [scene:s06|The Balfour Declaration] tells the story of the letter itself; this section stays with its address line.

Who Walter Rothschild was. Lionel Walter Rothschild was born in London on 8 February 1868 and died at Tring, in Hertfordshire, on 27 August 1937. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_ra_family_walter] He was the eldest son of Nathan, called Natty, the first Baron Rothschild, who headed the London bank, N M Rothschild & Sons. Walter worked at the bank from 1889 to 1908 and then left it. His niece and biographer, the scientist Miriam Rothschild, wrote that he plainly had neither the interest nor the gift for finance. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_jvl_walter] He sat in the House of Commons for Aylesbury from 1899 until he stood down in 1910. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] His real life was elsewhere. On land his father gave him at Tring he built a zoological museum, open to the public from 1892, employed two leading taxonomists and, over the years, some four hundred collectors around the world, and founded a scientific journal. He left the museum and its collections to the British Museum; it is now the Natural History Museum at Tring. [cite:wb_ra_family_walter] [cite:wb_wiki_walter]

When Natty died in 1915, Walter inherited the title and became the second Baron Rothschild. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_wiki_natty] He also inherited something no document conferred. The family's archive calls him the "unofficial leader of the British Jewish community," and his relative Jacob, the fourth Lord Rothschild, wrote in 2017 that Walter took over the family's leadership of British Jewry from his father in 1915. [cite:wb_ra_balfour] [cite:wb_jc_rothschild2017] That standing came mainly with the name, the peerage and the house on Piccadilly. One former president of the Board of Deputies writes that Walter was also the Board's Senior Vice-President in 1917; this book has not confirmed that from the Board's records. [cite:wb_toi_wineman]

What he was worth. Here the record is thin, and it is better to say so than to guess. No figure for Walter's estate at his death in 1937, or for his father's in 1915, was found in the sources reached for this book. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_wiki_natty] He was the heir to one of the largest private fortunes in Europe, a fortune whose private structure the Prelude describes, but he was not its manager, and he had left the bank in 1908. The one sum attached to him in popular accounts points the other way. In the early 1930s he sold most of his bird collection, about 280,000 specimens, to the American Museum of Natural History in New York, for a price reported as $225,000. Miriam Rothschild wrote in 1983 that a former mistress had blackmailed him for decades, and that this lay behind the sale. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_mentalfloss] [cite:wb_wiki_sanford] The price and the date, 1931 or 1932, come from secondary accounts and are not confirmed here. The safe reading is limited: Walter lived on inherited wealth, spent much of it on science, and held his place in 1917 because of whose son he was, not because of money he controlled.

Who did speak for British Jews in 1917. The community had elected institutions, and in May 1917 their leaders publicly opposed the Zionist program. The Board of Deputies of British Jews, the representative body of the synagogues, was led by its president, David Lindo Alexander. The Anglo-Jewish Association was led by Claude Montefiore. Together they ran the Conjoint Foreign Committee, which spoke for the community on foreign affairs. [cite:wb_wiki_alexander] [cite:wb_jewishlink_grobman] On 24 May 1917 The Times published a statement from the committee, signed by Alexander and Montefiore. It objected to treating the Jewish settlements in Palestine as a nation in the political sense, to special rights for Jewish settlers, and to a Jewish chartered company. [cite:wb_wiki_alexander] [cite:wb_jewishlink_grobman] Accounts differ on the details: one former Board president dates the statement to 25 May and names Lucien Wolf among its authors. [cite:wb_toi_wineman] [cite:wb_algemeiner_dunner]

Walter Rothschild answered in The Times, calling the opponents a mere fraction of Jewish opinion. [cite:wb_algemeiner_dunner] [cite:wb_wiki_alexander] The Board itself then turned. On 17 June 1917 it passed a motion censuring the statement, by 56 votes to 51. Alexander had treated the vote as one of confidence, and he resigned, with other officers of the Board. He and Montefiore later helped to found the anti-Zionist League of British Jews. [cite:wb_wiki_alexander] [cite:wb_toi_wineman] Two days later, on 19 June, Balfour asked Rothschild and Weizmann to submit a formula for a declaration. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] [cite:wb_toi_wineman]

Inside the Cabinet the strongest objection came from its only Jewish member. In a memorandum of 23 August 1917, which the National Archives catalogues under the title "Anti-Semitism in the Present Government," Edwin Montagu called Zionism "a mischievous political creed." He wrote, "I assert that there is not a Jewish nation," and he warned that Palestine would become "the world's Ghetto." He asked the government to go no further. [cite:wb_tna_cab21_58_218] [cite:wb_jvl_montagu] The government went further.

The men who did the work. The Zionist case in London was made chiefly by two men who were not British-born. Chaim Weizmann had come to Manchester in 1904 as a chemistry lecturer and became the movement's leading spokesman in Britain. His wartime work on acetone brought him to senior officials, and in September 1915 he was made a scientific adviser to the Ministry of Munitions. [cite:wb_ra_balfour] [cite:wb_liquisearch_weizmann] Historians reject Lloyd George's later claim, made in 1932, that his meetings with Weizmann were the "fount and origin" of the declaration. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] [cite:wb_aje_behind_balfour] Nahum Sokolow, a journalist and an executive of the World Zionist Organization, had come to Britain in 1914. In the spring of 1917 he was received by Pope Benedict XV and obtained a letter of support from the French government. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] Weizmann had met Walter Rothschild in late 1914, through Rothschild women who supported the cause. [cite:wb_jc_rothschild2017] [cite:wb_hnn_livingstone]

Jacob Rothschild later recalled Sokolow's own explanation of the address: the Zionists, he said, had no address, while Walter had a very fine one at 148 Piccadilly. The historian Cecil Roth called the choice "incongruous." [cite:wb_jc_rothschild2017] A Jewish newspaper's account gives the same reason in plainer terms: Weizmann was not the most senior figure in the Zionist hierarchy, and Rothschild, as the community's recognized head, could pass the letter on. [cite:wb_jewishnews_10things] Whether Walter also held office in the English Zionist Federation is itself disputed. Some accounts make him its president; others give that office to Weizmann from 1917, and the Rothschild Archive names no Zionist post for him. [cite:wb_aje_behind_balfour] [cite:wb_toi_wineman] [cite:wb_liquisearch_weizmann] [cite:wb_ra_balfour]

What Walter's draft asked for, and what the letter gave. Walter did more than receive the letter. The Zionist draft dated 12 July 1917, which he sent to Balfour on 18 July (one account says Weizmann presented it), asked the government to accept "the principle that Palestine should be reconstituted" as the national home of the Jewish people, and to work out the methods with the Zionist Organisation. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] [cite:wb_nli_drafts] Ministers cut it back over the following months, and added two safeguards in October. Walter objected to the second of them, the one protecting Jews in other countries, because it implied that Zionism endangered them, which he denied. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] [cite:wb_nli_drafts]

Was the letter bought? A claim that circulates widely holds that the declaration was a payment for Rothschild money: a loan to Britain at war, perhaps, given in exchange. No source reached for this book records any payment, loan or financial undertaking by Walter Rothschild or by the bank in return for the declaration. Walter had left the bank in 1908, and his father, who had advised Lloyd George on steadying the currency in 1914, had died two and a half years before the letter. [cite:wb_wiki_walter] [cite:wb_ra_ww1_business] [cite:wb_wiki_natty] The Rothschild Archive notes that from 1916 the British hoped Jews would help to pay for the war, but it names no loan or sum tied to the declaration. [cite:wb_ra_balfour]

What historians have documented is a belief, not a bargain. Jonathan Schneer argues that British leaders courted an "international Jewry" that they wrongly thought powerful, relying on antisemitic notions: that Jews controlled American finance and could help bring the United States into the war, and that Jews stood behind Russia's wish to leave it. [cite:wb_hnn_schneer] [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] Robert Cecil, a Foreign Office minister, wrote that he did not think it possible to exaggerate the international power of the Jews. [cite:wb_wiki_balfour] The historians Yehuda Bauer and Moshe Fox put the address line in the same light: the letter went to Walter because British officials saw the Rothschilds as the head of a Jewish financial power that existed mainly in their own assumptions. [cite:wb_jc_bauer_fox] On this reading the address was chosen for what officials imagined the name could do, not for anything the family had paid.

Why not a community leader? Asked plainly, the question has a plain answer in the record. The letter was not sent to the community's elected president. The elected leadership of British Jews had opposed the policy in public and lost its president over it. The movement's own leaders, the men who had argued for it, were immigrants without standing in the community's institutions, in Sokolow's words without an address. And the people whose land the letter concerned were not addressed at all. In 1917 Palestine's Muslim and Christian Arabs numbered by one estimate about 700,000, some nine-tenths of the population; the letter calls them "existing non-Jewish communities" and says only that nothing shall be done to harm their civil and religious rights; it says nothing of political rights. [cite:balfour_letter] The British government wrote to the person it regarded as the head of British Jewry by rank and name. That choice tells us how the government saw power in 1917: as something held by titled families, not by those who organized or represented a community.

What the record shows, and what it does not. The documented finding is this. The letter went to a peer whose standing came from inheritance; the elected Jewish leadership of the day opposed the policy; Walter Rothschild sent the Zionist draft and objected to one of the safeguards; and British officials believed, wrongly, in a Jewish financial power they hoped to win over. The record does not show the declaration being bought, and it does not support the old claim of a hidden Jewish hand behind British policy. That claim is antisemitic, and the documents contradict it: the drafters are known, and the sharpest objection inside the Cabinet came from its one Jewish member. The record also leaves questions open. It does not show who in government chose the addressee, or when. It does not settle whether Walter held office in the Zionist Federation, or how much his rank weighed against any such office. And no probate figure has been found that would let anyone say what he was worth.
