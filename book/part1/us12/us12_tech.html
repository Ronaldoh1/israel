Tech and Venture Capital

No industry is cited more often in comparisons of the two countries than technology. American companies built the global platforms, and Israel, a country of about ten million people, became one of the places those companies buy from, invest in and recruit. [cite:us11_wb_pop] This section measures each country's research effort over time, sets the weight of the tech sector in each economy, follows venture money through its boom and slump, and then looks at the traffic between them: American firms in Israel and Israeli founders sold to American buyers. Its figures come from the World Bank, the OECD, Israel's Innovation Authority and the venture-data compilers in each country, whose definitions differ and are named as they appear.

Research spending over time

The clearest long series is research and development (R&D) spending as a share of GDP, which the World Bank publishes from UNESCO data. In 1996 the two countries spent almost the same share: 2.59 per cent in Israel and 2.45 per cent in the United States. Israel's share then pulled away, to 3.93 per cent in 2000, 3.94 per cent in 2010 and 4.94 per cent in 2018, while America's moved slowly from 2.63 per cent in 2000 to 2.74 per cent in 2010 and 2.83 per cent in 2018. [cite:us12_im_rd_isr] [cite:us12_im_rd_usa] The latest figures widen the gap. The World Bank puts Israel at 6.35 per cent of GDP in 2023 and the United States at 3.45 per cent; the OECD, measuring gross domestic spending on R&D, gives 6.76 and 3.45 per cent for 2024. [cite:us12_wiki_rd] The two sources use slightly different definitions, so the latest year should be read beside the World Bank series rather than spliced onto it. In money the comparison runs the other way: because the American economy is so much larger, 3.45 per cent of its GDP is many times Israel's total. The share measures how much of each economy is devoted to research, not who does more of it.

How much of the economy it is

Israel's Innovation Authority, a government agency, measures the sector each year. In its December 2025 report it put high-tech at about 17 per cent of Israel's GDP in 2024, about 57 per cent of exports in the first half of 2025 and about 403,000 employees, 11.5 per cent of the workforce. High-tech exports reached $78 billion in 2024. [cite:us12_iia_2025] The same report recorded a slowdown. Output was "virtually unchanged in 2024 for a second consecutive year"; employment, which had grown by more than 5 per cent a year for about a decade, grew by less than 2 per cent a year from 2023; and the number of R&D employees fell 6.5 per cent in the year to the first half of 2025. [cite:us12_iia_2025] No equivalent single figure for "high-tech" exists in American official statistics, so this book does not set one beside Israel's. What the Israeli numbers show is dependence: more than half of the country's exports come from a sector that employs about one worker in nine.

That dependence is also the sector's limit as a measure of how the system serves people. Nine in ten Israeli workers are outside it, and its slowdown since 2023 coincided with the war. The Innovation Authority's own figures show that three of every five shekels raised in 2025 went to cyber and organisational software, a narrow base for so large a share of exports. [cite:us12_iia_2025] Who works in high-tech, by gender, community and region, is examined in the parts on equality and labour rather than here. [scene:us8_israel|Equality among citizens] [scene:us13_wages|Wages]

Venture money: boom, slump and AI

Venture capital shows the cycle most sharply. By the count of the IVC research centre, Israeli tech companies raised a record $25.6 billion in 2021, $18.64 billion of it from foreign investors. [cite:us12_toi_ivc2021] Funding fell through 2022 and 2023; in the second quarter of 2023 it was about $1.78 billion, 65 per cent below the same quarter a year earlier. [cite:us12_toi_ivc2021] IVC and LeumiTech counted $11.1 billion in 418 rounds in 2025, up 15 per cent on 2024, which puts 2024 at roughly $9.7 billion by this book's arithmetic. [cite:us12_jn_ivc2025] The Innovation Authority summarised the recovery as a return to "2019-2020 levels". [cite:us12_iia_2025]

American venture investment, counted by PitchBook and the National Venture Capital Association, followed the same curve at a far larger scale: $156.1 billion in 2019, $358.2 billion in 2021, $168.8 billion in 2023 and $339.4 billion in 2025. The recovery of 2025 was concentrated. Artificial intelligence and machine learning took 65.4 per cent of deal value, and half of all the money went to 0.05 per cent of completed deals; the largest round of the final quarter was Anthropic's $15 billion. [cite:us12_nvca_q4_2025] The two data sets are compiled differently, so the comparison is of direction and concentration, not of exact size. Both show money returning to the sector in 2025 while it narrowed to fewer companies and fewer fields.

The military pipeline

Part of Israel's tech sector grows out of its army. The founders of Check Point, set up in 1993, included two veterans of Unit 8200, the signals-intelligence unit; another 8200 alumnus who worked there, Nir Zuk, founded Palo Alto Networks; and the founders of CyberArk and Cybereason are described as 8200 alumni too. [cite:ow4_sw_alumni] The founders of Wiz met in the army's Talpiot programme; Google completed its $32 billion cash purchase of the company on 11 March 2026, the largest sale of an Israeli tech firm. [cite:ow4_sequoia_wiz] [cite:ow4_globes_wiz] The One-Way Mirror follows that unit and its ties to American agencies. [scene:ow4_gaza|Unit 8200] The United States has no equivalent conscript pipeline, but its own tech sector also drew on defence money: Palantir took early seed money from In-Q-Tel, the CIA's venture arm. [cite:ow9_ap_s1]

American firms in Israel

The largest American industrial presence in Israel is Intel, there since 1974. By the company's own figures it exported about $86 billion from Israel between 1974 and 2023, consistently 3.5 to 5.5 per cent of Israel's total exports, and invested about $27 billion in land, plant and equipment to 2021. [cite:us12_intel_israel] On 18 June 2023 Prime Minister Netanyahu announced a $25 billion expansion of its chip plant at Kiryat Gat, calling it "the largest investment by an international company ever in Israel". The state's grant was 12.8 per cent of the investment and the company's tax rate was set at 7.5 per cent. [cite:us12_reg_intel2023] Globes reported the grant as $3.2 billion, the largest the Israeli government had ever given a private company, paid against milestones. [cite:us12_globes_intel_grant] The record since then is of retrenchment. Intel's Israeli workforce fell from a peak of about 12,000 in 2021 to about 9,350 in mid-2025, and the future of the new fab was in doubt as the company cut costs worldwide. [cite:us12_calcalist_intel2025]

Nvidia was reported in February 2026 to be on course to become Israel's largest private tech employer. It bought the Israeli chip firm Mellanox in 2020 for a reported $6.9 billion and in February 2026 was planning a campus at Kiryat Tivon for up to 10,000 employees, its largest site outside the United States, with first occupancy planned for 2031. [cite:us12_jn_nvidia] These are the terms on which the two economies meet in tech: Israeli research and engineering, American ownership and markets, and Israeli state subsidy to keep the plants in Israel. The told-versus-record contrast is narrow but real. The 2023 announcement was presented as a vote of confidence in Israel's economy; the record two years later was of the same company shrinking its Israeli workforce by about a fifth. Both the grant and the jobs it was meant to buy are matters of public record, and the outcome depended on Intel's global fortunes more than on Israeli policy.
