Part Two goes back before John Nelson Darby and Cyrus Scofield to the British and American Protestants who first read the Hebrew prophets as promising the Jewish people a return to their land. It follows that belief, which historians call restorationism, from Thomas Brightman's commentaries and Henry Finch's suppressed book of 1621, through Cromwell's readmission of the Jews and the Boston sermons of Increase Mather, to the London Society for Promoting Christianity amongst the Jews, founded in 1809 to convert them. It then shows how one evangelical peer, Lord Shaftesbury, brought the idea to his wife's stepfather, the Foreign Secretary Lord Palmerston, and what Palmerston did with it in 1840 for reasons of his own; how a Protestant bishopric was set up in Jerusalem in 1841; and how a restorationist chaplain, William Hechler, became Theodor Herzl's ally in 1896. A third section traces the phrase "a land without a people" from a Methodist sermon to Israel Zangwill and tests the claims made about it. Throughout, the prophetic motive and the imperial one are kept apart, and the aim most restorationists held, the conversion of the Jews, is stated plainly.
