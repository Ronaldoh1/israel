Jews in the Muslim World: Coexistence Without Utopia

For more than a thousand years, Jewish communities lived across the Muslim-majority Middle East and North Africa: in Baghdad, Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Sana'a, Fez, Tunis and Casablanca. In some of these cities they formed a plurality or even a majority. A common claim holds that Jews and Arabs never lived together in peace, and that an ancient, irreconcilable hatred is the natural and permanent state between them. The documented record does not fit that frame. Neither does the opposite picture of an unbroken harmony. What the record shows is a long, uneven coexistence: real violence, and real legal inequality, alongside intellectual, commercial and civic collaboration on a scale that, in the judgment of the historians cited below, Christian Europe of the same period did not match. How that coexistence ended, between 1941 and 1972, is a separate and grave story, told in [scene:s00e2|the section on the exodus from the Arab world].

The oldest of these communities is older than Islam, older than Arab rule in the region and older than either modern state that now claims part of its story. In 586 BCE Babylon destroyed the First Temple in Jerusalem, conquered Judah and deported its population to Mesopotamia, the land that is now Iraq.[cite:huc_farhud] The exiles took root and stayed. Over the following centuries the community became one of the great centers of Jewish religious and intellectual life, and between roughly the third and sixth centuries CE its scholars compiled the Babylonian Talmud, a foundational work of Jewish law that is still the primary text of rabbinic study, nearly 1,500 years after it was compiled in what is now Iraq.[cite:huc_farhud] [cite:ushmm_farhud] One of the two foundational texts of rabbinic Judaism was produced there, not in the land of Israel. Popular accounts often begin this relationship in 1948, as if it started with modern statehood. Historical and religious sources agree, without dispute, on continuous Jewish presence in Mesopotamia from the sixth century BCE, more than a thousand years before the Muslim conquest of the region, and long before the Iraqi state of 1932 or the Israeli state of 1948.[cite:huc_farhud] [cite:ushmm_farhud]

That community was still large in the twentieth century. Sasson Somekh, born a Jew in Baghdad and later a professor of Arabic literature at Tel Aviv University, documents its scale in his memoir Baghdad, Yesterday (2007).[cite:somekh_baghdad_yesterday] The figures vary by year and by source. One account gives 140,000 Jews in 1917, about 40 per cent of Baghdad's civilian population; another gives roughly 140,000 in 1945, about a third of the city; a more cautious reckoning puts the mid-century community at 90,000 to 140,000, depending on the year and the source, but in every version close to a third of the city. Jews formed much of the commercial, banking and professional life of modern Iraq. Trading families such as the Sassoons, the Kadoories and the Basris built networks that reached across the region, into British India and across the British Empire. Iraq's first finance minister, Sassoon Heskel, was Jewish, and the community's chief rabbis served as advisers to the government. In 1917 the community welcomed the arriving British forces.[cite:somekh_baghdad_yesterday] This was not a marginal sect. It was a city community as old as Jewish life in Europe, integrated into the economy and the state. Its place in the new kingdom under Faisal is told in [scene:irq_e02|Faisal's Iraq].

The best-known chapter of this history is al-Andalus, Muslim Iberia, home to what is called the Jewish Golden Age of the tenth to twelfth centuries. Its figures include Maimonides (born in Córdoba, 1135–1204), Judah Halevi (of Toledo and Córdoba), Solomon ibn Gabirol and Samuel HaNagid. Jewish scholars wrote philosophy in Arabic, held the office of vizier and produced foundational works of rabbinic literature.[cite:menocal_ornament] The term usually attached to this period, convivencia, "living together," is contested precisely because the period was not utopian. In 1066 a pogrom struck the Jews of Granada. The historians on whom this account relies, María Rosa Menocal, David Nirenberg and Mark Cohen, do not deny such violence. Their work nonetheless documents a substantial period of Jewish and Muslim intellectual and civic collaboration that, they argue, had no parallel in Christian Europe of the same period.[cite:menocal_ornament]

The Iberian story ended in Christian hands. In 1492 the Catholic monarchs, Ferdinand and Isabella, ordered Spain's Jews to convert or leave; historians estimate that at least 200,000 people were affected. The Ottoman sultan, Bayezid II, opened his territories to receive the exiles, and Salonika became a majority-Sephardic city for centuries.[cite:stillman_jews_arab_lands] The expulsion's long afterlife reached into the present. Spain established diplomatic relations with Israel only on 17 January 1986, the last Western European state to do so, as a condition of joining the European Community, while pledging continued "friendship and solidarity" with the Arab world; in 1948, according to reports, Franco had sought relations and Ben-Gurion had refused because of Franco's ties to Nazi Germany.[cite:israeled_spain_1986] Spain hosted the Madrid peace conference of 1991. In 2015 the Spanish parliament passed a law offering citizenship to descendants of the expelled Sephardim, to repair what it called a "historical mistake." By the 2019 deadline 132,226 people had applied, most of them from Latin America: about 20,000 from Mexico, the largest single group, followed by applicants from Venezuela and Colombia.[cite:euronews_sephardic] [cite:yahoo_sephardic_close] Less than a decade later, on 28 May 2024, Spain, with Ireland and Norway, recognized the State of Palestine on the pre-1967 lines with East Jerusalem as its capital. Israel recalled its ambassador, and its foreign minister said the three states were rewarding Hamas.[cite:catalannews_spain_recognition] [cite:wiki_palestine_spain] Pro-Israel commentators read Spain's position as hostility with historical echoes; the Spanish government presents it as support for a two-state solution and for international law. Both acts, the atonement of 2015 and the recognition of 2024, are documented, and reading either as cancelling the other misses the record.

The Ottoman Empire, which lasted from 1299 to 1922, a span of 623 years, gave this coexistence its most durable legal form. It organized its non-Muslim subjects through the millet system, under which each religious community governed itself under its own leaders in matters of law, education and religion. The Chief Rabbi of Istanbul was responsible for all Ottoman Jews. Jewish communities lived under this arrangement in Istanbul, İzmir, Salonika, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Cairo and Jerusalem. The system was not one of equality: Jews held the status of dhimmi, protected non-Muslims, paid the jizya tax and lived under legal restrictions that did not apply to Muslims. It was also not the persecution of the Nazi regime or of imperial Russia. For four centuries after 1492 it framed daily civic life, and it produced Sephardi and Mizrahi literature and the mystical school of Safed, the Kabbalah associated with Isaac Luria and Joseph Caro. Norman Stillman's The Jews of Arab Lands is the standard documentary sourcebook for this history.[cite:stillman_jews_arab_lands]

The mechanism matters for reading this history fairly. Under the millet, a Jewish community could run its own courts, schools and religious life; it could not stand as an equal of Muslims before the state, and it paid for its protection through a special tax. That is why the same arrangement can be described, accurately, as both tolerant and unequal.

Historians divide over how to weigh this record, and both readings rest on evidence. The first, sometimes called the "golden age" reading, stresses the length and depth of the collaboration: Jewish philosophers writing in Arabic, Jewish viziers in Muslim courts, the Talmud compiled in Mesopotamia, the Ottoman welcome of 1492, and Baghdad's community still a third of the city in the twentieth century. In its strongest form it argues, with Menocal, Nirenberg and Cohen, that nothing comparable existed in Christian Europe at the same time, and that a hatred described as ancient and permanent cannot explain a millennium of shared life. The second reading stresses the limits and the ruptures: dhimmi status as a permanent legal subordination, the jizya, the restrictions, and violence such as the Granada pogrom of 1066. In its strongest form it warns that the golden-age image can idealize what was, in law, a hierarchy. The record supports both sets of facts. It does not support either extreme: not "harmonious coexistence, full stop," and not a story of unbroken persecution.

The twentieth century shows both forces at work in a single community. In Baghdad in June 1941, seven years before Israel's founding, a pogrom known as the Farhud killed an estimated 180 or more Jewish residents. Nazi-inspired, it came during the political turmoil of a short-lived pro-Axis coup, during the Anglo-Iraqi War, and was encouraged by pro-Axis Iraqi elements.[cite:ushmm_farhud] [cite:huc_farhud] After 1948 came further legal restrictions, confiscations of property and violence; at the same time, the Israeli state and the Jewish Agency actively encouraged and organized emigration. Both forces are documented, and neither explains the exodus without the other. The same holds across the region. The departure of some 850,000 Jews from Arab lands between 1948 and the 1970s had several documented causes, not one: rising tension after 1948 with the Arab-Israeli conflict; the criminalization of Zionism in many Arab states, a political rather than a religious prohibition; active expropriation and expulsion in some places, Iraq in 1950–51, Egypt after 1956 and Libya after 1969; and active Zionist encouragement of emigration in others, as in the airlifts from Yemen in 1949–50 and from Iraq in 1951. The Israeli historian Tom Segev and the Palestinian historian Rashid Khalidi both document that complexity.[cite:segev_seventh_million] [cite:khalidi_hundred_years] Neither "all expelled at once" nor "all left of their own accord" survives it. The mass departure of 1950–51 followed. The community had numbered about 140,000; in the end it numbered none, and a presence of some twenty-five centuries had ended within a single decade. The full account, including its disputed episodes, belongs to the section on the exodus. Its sequel in Israel belongs there too, though one point should be recorded here: the scholar Ella Shohat has documented the second-class treatment of Iraqi Jews after their arrival, a displacement upon a displacement.[cite:shohat_sephardim_israel]

Several things remain unsettled. The figures for Baghdad's Jewish population differ by source and by year, and the record here cannot reconcile 40 per cent of the city in 1917 with a third in 1945 beyond noting that the counts and their bases differ. The toll of the Granada pogrom of 1066 is not given in the sources used here. The comparison with Christian Europe is a judgment by named historians, well supported, but a judgment all the same. And the balance between persecution and recruitment in the Iraqi exodus remains a matter of genuine dispute. What the record does establish is narrower and firmer: Jewish life in the Muslim world was long, large and productive; it was never free of inequality or violence; and the frame of an ancient, irreconcilable hatred does not describe it. The hostility of the twentieth century had dates and causes, and it is in those dates and causes, not in a supposed permanent enmity, that the history of the break has to be sought.
