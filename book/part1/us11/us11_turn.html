The Turn: Israel 1985, America 1979–86

Both economies changed direction in the first half of the 1980s, and both turns began with inflation. The American turn is often dated from the Federal Reserve's change of course in October 1979 through the tax laws of 1981 and 1986; the Israeli one from the Economic Stabilization Plan of July 1985. They were different in scale and in method. America brought inflation down from about 13.5 per cent a year; Israel brought it down from several hundred per cent. Setting them side by side shows what each state did with its central bank, its budget and its labour bargain, and how the institutions created or reshaped then still work in 2026.

Israel before the plan

By the World Bank's annual-average series, Israel's consumer prices rose 131 per cent in 1980, 120 per cent in 1982, 146 per cent in 1983 and 373 per cent in 1984. [cite:us11_wb_cpi] Measured from December to December, the Bank of Israel records a peak of 445 per cent in 1984, when prices rose about five and a half times in a year. [cite:us14_boi_hyper] The two figures measure different things and are both correct; this book gives the annual average when comparing with the United States. Brookings economist Dany Bahar describes annualised inflation of about 1,000 per cent in the second half of 1984 and a budget deficit of about 17 per cent of GDP, while capital flight drained the foreign reserves Israel needed to service its external debt. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] A crisis in the banking system in October 1983 had already brought the economy close to financial collapse. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] By the economists Rafi Melnick and Yosef Mealem's figures, public debt stood at about 163 per cent of GDP in the mid-1980s, public spending at about 60 per cent of GDP and defence spending at more than 20 per cent. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick]

The plan of 1 July 1985

The July 1984 election produced a national unity government in which Shimon Peres of Labour served first as prime minister and Likud's Yitzhak Moda'i as finance minister. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] [cite:us11_wiki_esp] The plan, drawn up by a team led by the economist Michael Bruno, took effect on 1 July 1985 after a night-long cabinet meeting. [cite:us11_wiki_esp] [cite:us11_brookings_peres] It had five parts. The budget deficit was cut by about 7.5 per cent of GDP, mainly by cutting food subsidies, with tax increases beside the spending cuts. The shekel was devalued by 20 per cent and then held at a fixed rate. Wages, prices and the exchange rate were frozen, with pre-set adjustments. Peres negotiated the wage freeze with the Histadrut, the labour federation, in a package with employers. And the Bank of Israel was barred from printing money to finance the government's deficit, the measure Melnick and Mealem call the "Non-Printing" law. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] [cite:us11_wiki_esp] Interest rates were raised sharply. [cite:us11_brookings_peres]

The United States took part. An American advisory group of economists included Stanley Fischer, later governor of the Bank of Israel, and Washington provided an emergency grant of $1.5 billion over two years, about 3.5 per cent of Israel's GDP a year, tied to the reforms. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] Part One sets that grant in the longer record of American aid. [scene:us1_aid|Part One: the aid] The results were quick. Monthly inflation fell from 11 per cent in the third quarter of 1985 to about 1 per cent a year later, and unemployment rose by only about two percentage points. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] Annual inflation fell to 47.6 per cent in 1986 and 19.1 per cent in 1987, stayed between about 10 and 20 per cent until the mid-1990s, and reached 1.0 per cent in 2000. [cite:us11_wb_cpi] The public sector, in deficit by about 14 per cent of GDP in 1984, ran surpluses in the following three years. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] Melnick and Mealem's verdict is that the plan "succeeded in achieving its two objectives", stopping inflation and stabilising the balance of payments. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick]

The plan was a fiscal and monetary rescue, but it also began a change in how Israel's economy was organised. The later reforms included "the privatization of many government-owned businesses", and public spending fell from about 60 per cent of GDP in the mid-1980s to about 43 per cent by the end of 2008. [cite:us11_wiki_esp] [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] The Histadrut, which had signed the 1985 wage pact as a central party, lost influence over the labour market during the 1990s. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] Part Thirteen follows the union side of that change and the American one. [scene:us13_unions|Part Thirteen: unions]

America, 1979–86

The American turn ran through the central bank first. On 6 October 1979 the Federal Reserve chair, Paul Volcker, announced after an unscheduled meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee that the Fed would target the volume of bank reserves rather than the daily interest rate, accepting that rates would "fluctuate over a wider range". The federal funds rate reached a record 20 per cent in late 1980; the economy went into a severe recession; unemployment peaked at 10.8 per cent in late 1982; and inflation fell to 3.7 per cent in 1983, by the Federal Reserve's own history. [cite:us11_frh_volcker] The World Bank's annual series shows consumer-price inflation of 13.5 per cent in 1980, 6.1 per cent in 1982 and 1.9 per cent in 1986. [cite:us11_wb_cpi]

Tax law changed next. The top federal income tax rate was 70 per cent in 1980. It fell to 50 per cent in 1982 after the 1981 tax act, to 38.5 per cent in 1987 and to 28 per cent in 1988 after the Tax Reform Act of 1986. It has since moved between 31 and 39.6 per cent and has been 37 per cent since 2018. [cite:us11_tpc_toprate] The Budget Office's long series shows the result for who pays: from 1979 to 2022 the average federal tax rate fell for every income group, and the share of income going to the top 1 per cent doubled. [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] The next section takes up taxes and debt in both countries. [scene:us11_fiscal|Taxes, debt and the budget]

The two central banks now

Both central banks emerged from the 1980s with more independence from the treasury, but their legal mandates differ. The Federal Reserve works under a dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices, with a 2 per cent inflation goal; its September 2026 statement referred to both. [cite:us11_fed_sep2026] The Bank of Israel Law of 2010 makes price stability the "primary goal". Supporting economic activity and financial stability are secondary aims, to be pursued only if the bank's Monetary Committee judges that price stability will not be harmed. The inflation target of 1 to 3 per cent was set by the government in 2000 and has applied since 2003; a review in November 2024 kept it. [cite:us11_boi_target] Israel's 2010 law also moved rate decisions from the governor alone to a committee. [cite:us11_boi_target]

In September 2026 the two banks moved in opposite directions. On 1 September the Bank of Israel cut its rate by a quarter point to 3.25 per cent, its fifth cut since November 2025, saying inflation had moderated. [cite:us11_globes_boi_sep2026] On 16 September the Federal Open Market Committee raised the federal funds target range by a quarter point to 3.75–4 per cent, by 12 votes to none, stating that "Inflation remains elevated." [cite:us11_fed_sep2026] These are the latest decisions this book found as of October 2026.

The stabilisation plan holds lessons that each side of the American debate has claimed. It combined orthodox measures, deep spending cuts and tight money, with heterodox ones, a negotiated wage and price freeze in which the labour federation was a signatory, and Fischer himself credited the mix. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] The American turn relied on the central bank alone to break inflation, at the cost of the deepest recession since the 1930s by the Fed's own account of its severity, and was followed by tax cuts concentrated at the top. [cite:us11_frh_volcker] [cite:us11_tpc_toprate] Which approach a reader prefers is a judgment; the record is that both ended high inflation and that Israel, starting from a far worse position, did so with a smaller rise in unemployment. [cite:us11_brookings_peres] [cite:us11_frh_volcker]
