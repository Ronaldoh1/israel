Part Twenty-Two goes into the room at Nicaea in the summer of 325, the council without which there would be no imperial Church and, in time, no Vatican. The first section reconstructs who called the council, who paid for the bishops' travel, who came from where and who was missing, and where the emperor Constantine sat. The second sets out what the council actually decided, from its own creed and canons and the letters of men who were present: a definition of the Son's relation to the Father built around the word homoousios, which one eyewitness says the emperor urged; the exile of those who refused to sign; the burning of Arius's books; a common rule for Easter, announced in language about Jews that the Catholic Church formally repudiated in 1965; and twenty canons, one of which first ranked the great sees. The third tests Ron's summary of the founding, "Constantine an emperor, Paul who never met Yeshua, and the council decided what Christianity was going to be", alongside the most repeated claims about Nicaea: that it chose the Bible, voted Jesus divine, burned rival gospels and removed reincarnation. In this Part "Yeshua" refers to the historical figure and "Jesus Christ" or "the Son" to the subject of the councils' doctrine.
