Birobidzhan: A Territory Granted, 1928–1959

One government did give Jews a territory of their own, larger than Mandatory Palestine. It was the Soviet Union, and the territory was in its Far East.

A Soviet decree of 28 March 1928 set aside the territory; on 7 May 1934 it became the Jewish Autonomous Region, with Yiddish as its official language. It covers about 36,271 square kilometres, more than Mandatory Palestine. [cite:a_wp_jao] [cite:a_wp_jao_hist]

It was created to show that Soviet Jews, like other Soviet nationalities, could have a territory for cultural autonomy within the socialist state. [cite:a_wp_jao_hist]

Settlement went badly from the start: by October 1928, 49.7 percent of the first settlers had left because of floods, anthrax and harsh conditions. Jews numbered about 20,000 in 1937, 17,695 in 1939 (16 percent of the population), and peaked at about 46,000 to 50,000 in the late 1940s, about a quarter. [cite:a_wp_jao]

By 1959 the Jewish population had fallen to 14,269; the 2021 census counted 837. [cite:a_wp_jao]

The likely path. What happened: a Jewish region inside the Soviet Union that never held more than about a quarter Jews and emptied after 1959. The only place where a government actually granted a Jewish territory, and few chose to live there.

The cost. No war over it; the cost was borne by the settlers in a hard climate under Soviet rule.
