The Arabs Who Stayed, and the Military Government

Not every Palestinian Arab inside the lines Israel held in 1949 had left. According to Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics, as compiled by the Jewish Virtual Library, the state's population at the end of 1948 was 716,700 Jews and 156,000 non-Jews. By 1950 it was 1,203,000 Jews and 167,100 non-Jews. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_population] Those Arabs who remained became citizens of the new state, and for the next eighteen years most of them lived under a regime that applied to them and not to the Jewish majority.

It is often said that the Arab citizens of Israel have enjoyed equal civil rights since 1948. Part of that is true. They voted from the first election. Yet from the war's last months until December 1966, most of them needed a permit from a military officer to leave their own town or village, lived under curfews, and could be barred from whole districts by a commander's order. Both facts belong in the account.

The vote came first in the public record. In the first Knesset election, in 1949, the Democratic List of Nazareth, an Arab party described as a satellite list of Mapai, won 7,387 votes, 1.7 percent of the total, and 2 seats. [cite:rs_rd6_idi_nazareth] Arab citizens could vote and stand for parliament. The terms on which former Palestinian citizens became Israeli nationals, including the residence test of the 1952 Nationality Law, are told in [scene:rd6_law_of_return|the Law of Return and the Nationality Law].

The military government had begun before that election. The sources date its start differently. The account of the period followed elsewhere in this library dates it from 1949. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The historian Alina Korn dates its official establishment to October 1948, with five military governors, and notes that when the fighting ended the regime was "neither cancelled nor was it legally regularized." [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] The difference is between the formal creation of the governorates in wartime and the regime's settled form after the armistices. Either way, it outlived the war by seventeen years.

Its legal tools were mostly British. The Defence (Emergency) Regulations of 1945, issued by the Mandate government, were absorbed into Israeli law by Article 11 of the Law and Administration Ordinance of 1948. The most important of them was Regulation 125, which allowed the military commander to declare "any area or location a closed area." To these were added the Emergency Regulations (Security Zones) of 1949. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] The mechanism was simple. A commander closed an area. No one could enter or leave it without his written permit. Anyone found outside without one had committed an offence and could be tried for it.

The power to act sat with the commander. The Arab citizen could apply for a permit but could not compel one. According to Korn, the travel-permit requirement had no legal foundation at all for its first 18 months, until January 1950. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] Permits were not easily come by. In 1958, Korn reports, only one in three Arabs in the areas under military government held a travel permit. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah]

The courts carried the regime's weight. Korn reports that between 1948 and 1967 about a third of all convictions of Arab citizens were for violations of the Defence Regulations, and that about 95 percent of these were administrative offences, such as lacking a permit. In 1955, 2,714 of the 2,888 people convicted under the regulations were Arab. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] The regulations were written in general terms and could in law be applied to anyone. In practice, more than nine in ten of those convicted under them that year were Arab citizens.

What was the regime for? The government justified it on grounds of security. Israel had signed armistices with its neighbours, not peace treaties. The Rattner Commission, which reviewed the regime in February 1956, concluded that it should not end until "true peace" existed. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] That is the strongest form of the case made at the time: a population with ties across an armistice line, in a state still formally at war, required controls that would be unnecessary in peace.

Korn argues that the regime's main purposes were different: to control the movement of Arab residents and to prevent their access to land that had been confiscated. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] Her evidence is the pattern of its use: on her figures, the great majority of convictions were for administrative offences such as lacking a permit, not for acts of violence. The two instruments worked together. Military rule restricted the movement of internally displaced citizens and, combined with the absentee property law, kept them from returning to reclaim their homes. [cite:wiki_present_absentee] That law is told in [scene:ap_s01|the Absentees' Property Law]. Both readings rest on documented material: the security rationale on the government's own statements and the Rattner Commission's finding, the control reading on the conviction figures and the regime's link to the land. The sources used here do not show which consideration weighed more with those who kept the regime in force year after year.

The regime's worst single event came on 29 October 1956, the eve of the Sinai War. Brigadier Issachar Shadmi moved up a dusk curfew on villages near the Jordanian border, without adequate notice to the villagers. When Major Shmuel Malinki asked how to treat villagers returning from work who did not know of it, Shadmi's reply, according to the court record, was that anyone who left his house would be shot. Two of the three units given the order disregarded it. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The Border Police unit at Kafr Qasim, about a kilometre from the Green Line, did not. It killed 49 villagers returning home after a curfew they had not been told of: 19 men, 6 women and 23 children, the count including an unborn child. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60]

The aftermath documented both accountability and its limits. Eight officers and men were convicted in 1958, and all were released within a few years. Shadmi, tried separately, was fined one-hundredth of an Israeli lira, a sum Palestinian citizens remember as "Shadmi's penny." The same trial established the doctrine that a soldier must refuse a "manifestly illegal" order, one over which "a black flag flies," a standard the Israeli army still teaches. [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The Sinai War that began that week, and Egypt's part in it, belong to a later volume.

The regime ended slowly. The Knesset eased it in 1962 and rejected bills to abolish it in 1963. It voted on 8 November 1966 to end the military government, which was abolished on 1 December 1966. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] Six months later, on 4 April 1967, the Prime Minister's adviser on Arab affairs, Shmuel Toledano, wrote to Levi Eshkol that "in practice, hardly any restrictions have been loosened," since closed areas and travel passes remained. [cite:rs_rd6_akevot_toledano] An official close to the policy thus recorded that the formal end of the regime had changed less than its abolition suggested.

Some accounts draw a further conclusion: that before Israel governed a single Palestinian in the West Bank or Gaza, it had already run an eighteen-year separate administration for Palestinian citizens inside its own borders, and that the occupation after 1967 inherited a model rather than inventing one. That is an argument about continuity, and it rests on the resemblance of the instruments: permits, closed areas and military courts. The later regime is the subject of a later volume.

The record leaves several things unsettled. The sources used here give conviction figures but not the number of permit applications refused, or how permits were allocated among villages and individuals. They date the regime's start two ways. They do not give a source-backed count of Israeli casualties from cross-border attacks in these years, a figure that would bear directly on the weight of the security case. And they do not settle the central dispute, whether security or control of movement and land was the regime's main purpose. What they do establish is the structure: a population with the vote, held for eighteen years under British emergency law, through a permit system that for its first eighteen months had no legal basis at all.
