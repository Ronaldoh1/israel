Local Police: The Multiplier

The United States has thousands of separate police and sheriff's agencies and no national police force. Any federal government that wants enforcement on a national scale has to borrow local officers, and the law already provides a way to do it. This section follows that route, the 287(g) programme, as it grew from about 135 agreements to well over a thousand in a single year; the state laws that pushed it forward in some places and blocked it in others; and the intelligence hubs, called fusion centres, that link local and federal information. The pattern that emerges is geographic: the borrowed force is large where state governments want it and small where they do not.

What 287(g) is

Section 287(g) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, added by the immigration law of 1996, lets ICE sign agreements that deputise state and local officers to perform some immigration functions. ICE runs three models. Under the jail enforcement model, officers in a local jail identify and process people held there who may be removable. Under the warrant service officer model, jail officers are trained to serve ICE's administrative warrants on people already in custody. Under the task force model, officers can question and arrest people for immigration violations in the course of ordinary policing, on the street. [cite:de32_ice_287g] [cite:de32_tpr_sb8] The task force model is the one that turns a local patrol officer into an immigration agent. The Obama administration ended it at the end of 2012, after documented abuses including racial profiling by the sheriff's office in Maricopa County, Arizona; in 2025 it became the programme's largest model. [cite:de32_fwd_287g]

From 135 to 1,372

ICE's own page counted 137 agreements on 15 February 2025: 60 jail, 75 warrant service and just 2 task force. [cite:de32_ice_287g] By 15 August 2025 an academic tally of ICE's published spreadsheets counted 896 agreements with about 690 agencies, 443 of them on the task force model. Florida alone had 318, more than a third of the national total, and Texas 137. Eight states had none: California, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, New Jersey, Oregon and Washington. [cite:de32_kocher_287g] By 26 January 2026 the count stood at 1,372 agreements with 1,169 agencies, 764 of them task force agreements, according to FWD.us, an immigration-reform advocacy group whose counts follow ICE's data. ICE reported 8,501 local officers trained for the task force model by September 2025. [cite:de32_fwd_287g]

Money changed the terms. Until September 2025 ICE paid for training and computer links. From then, by FWD.us's reading of ICE's materials, ICE offered to pay trained officers' full salary and benefits, start-up costs, overtime and "performance" bonuses tied to immigration arrests. FWD.us estimates that this could transfer $1.4 billion to $2 billion to local police in 2026, against $665 million for the main federal community-policing grant programme in fiscal 2024; that is the group's projection, not a record of payments. Not every department accepted: Dallas's police chief and city council declined an offer reported at $25 million, conditioned on every officer being trained and making at least one immigration arrest. [cite:de32_fwd_287g]

States that require it, cities that refuse it

State legislatures decided much of the outcome. Texas's SB 8, in force from 1 January 2026, requires every sheriff who runs a jail to enter a 287(g) agreement by December 2026, lets the state attorney general sue those who refuse, and funds the cost for counties under a million people. Supporters described it as necessary funding for counties; opponents argued it would push victims and witnesses away from the police. [cite:de32_tpr_sb8] Florida accounted for the largest share of agreements nationally. [cite:de32_kocher_287g]

Other places used their own law to say no, and the courts have so far upheld their right to. The legal basis is a 1997 Supreme Court ruling, Printz v. United States, which held 5–4 that Congress could not order local sheriffs to run federal gun background checks; Justice Scalia wrote that "such commands are fundamentally incompatible with our constitutional system of dual sovereignty." [cite:de32_ncc_printz] The principle, known as anti-commandeering, means the federal government can invite local police into immigration work and pay them for it, but cannot compel them. In July 2025 the Justice Department sued New York City over laws limiting its cooperation with ICE; in October 2026 Judge Ramon Reyes dismissed the suit, holding that federal law does not require local governments to share release dates or custody information and that the federal government cannot force them to help enforce immigration law. [cite:de32_dr_nyc]

The fusion centres

Alongside officers, there is shared information. Fusion centres are state and local intelligence hubs, supported by DHS since 2003, where police, federal agencies and sometimes private partners pool reports. A bipartisan investigation by the Senate's Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, published on 2 October 2012, found their counter-terrorism reporting "oftentimes shoddy, rarely timely" and could find no case in which they had uniquely helped disrupt a terrorist plot; DHS's own spending estimates on them ranged from $289 million to $1.4 billion. [cite:de24_psi_fusion] They have also watched protest. In 2018 a DHS contractor, LookingGlass, compiled a spreadsheet of more than 600 planned "Family Separation Day" protests against the first Trump administration's border policy and circulated it to fusion centres; a DHS official called the list "unsolicited." The emails came out through a records request. [cite:de32_ow_lookingglass] No comparable record has been published for the protests of 2025 and 2026. The network was built under both parties, and its data links to licence-plate readers and brokers are described in [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four].

If it were connected

Local jail bookings joined to ICE: documented, by design, under the jail and warrant service models, and in mandate states by law. [cite:de32_ice_287g] [cite:de32_tpr_sb8] Local patrol officers joined to immigration arrests: documented under the task force model, with ICE offering pay linked to arrests. [cite:de32_fwd_287g] Local surveillance cameras joined to federal agencies: documented once and then cut back, when an Illinois audit found that Customs and Border Protection had searched Illinois licence-plate data in breach of state law and the camera company paused its federal pilots on 25 August 2025. [cite:de24_ap_flock] Fusion centres joined to monitoring of protest against immigration enforcement: documented in 2018, possible for 2025 and 2026, with no published record. Deputised local officers joined to political rather than immigration cases: not supported; their authority under 287(g) is limited to immigration functions, and widening it would need a new agreement or law. What stands in the way is mostly state law and the anti-commandeering rule, which means the multiplier is as strong as each state government allows. In Texas and Florida it is very strong; in the eight states with no agreements, it barely exists. The armed forces, which no state can veto in the same way, are the next section's subject.
