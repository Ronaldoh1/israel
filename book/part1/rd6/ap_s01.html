The Absentees' Property Law: Citizens Declared "Absent"

The war of 1948 uprooted hundreds of thousands of people. The fighting did not, by itself, make their loss of property permanent. A body of law passed after the war did that. The familiar account holds that Palestinians who stayed inside Israel in 1948 kept their homes and land as full citizens. For a large number of them, the law said otherwise.

In March 1950 the Knesset, Israel's parliament, passed the Absentees' Property Law, 5710-1950. It built on an emergency regulation of 1948 on absentee property. The text is blunt. All absentees' property is vested in a government-appointed Custodian of Absentees' Property. Every right an absentee held passes automatically to the Custodian. Anyone holding absentee property must hand it over. And the property is vested even if the absentee's identity is unknown. [cite:un_absentees_property_law_text] Anyone found living on such property, including its original owner, was breaking the law. [cite:wiki_present_absentee]

Everything turned on one word. An "absentee" was anyone who had left his usual home during the war, whether for areas outside Israeli control or for anywhere else. That covered the refugees beyond the new borders. It also covered tens of thousands of Arabs who had simply moved from one village to another inside Israel. Afterward they were registered as citizens. They were issued identity numbers. They could vote and stand for the Knesset. In law they were absent; in fact they were citizens living in the country. They came to be called "present absentees," and they could not reclaim their property. [cite:wiki_present_absentee]

Their numbers were not small. By 2001 the present absentees were estimated at about 200,000 people, nearly 17 percent of Israel's Arab citizens. Half the populations of the two largest Arab towns in Israel are internally displaced. [cite:wiki_present_absentee] [cite:wiki_land_day] [cite:cornell_bilsky_absentee_law]

The objection from abroad came early. In April 1949, before the permanent law existed, the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine, the body charged with brokering a settlement, proposed a preliminary step: "suspension of the application of the Absentee law until the final peace settlement." Arab delegations argued that the law treated nationals of Palestine as enemy aliens. Israel did not suspend it. The 1950 law made the arrangement permanent. [cite:un_ccp_absentee_1949]

The law did not work alone. From 1948 to 1966 Israel's Arab citizens lived under military rule, which restricted where they could go. Together the two kept internally displaced citizens from physically returning to reclaim their homes, a subject the next part of this book takes up in its own right. Salman Abu-Sitta estimates that more than 1,000 square kilometres were expropriated from Arab citizens between 1948 and 2003; the figure is his own estimate. [cite:wiki_present_absentee] [cite:wiki_land_day]

The law remains in force and has been applied in East Jerusalem. Its method is simple to state. In everyday speech, "absentee" kept its ordinary meaning. In law, it was stretched to include people who had never left the country. The flight of 1948 is told elsewhere in this book. This law shows how that flight became permanent state ownership, and how it reached even those who stayed.
