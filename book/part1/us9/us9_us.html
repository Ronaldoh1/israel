The American Counterpart, and Why the Comparison Is Unequal

The United States also governs people who cannot vote for its government. The cases are real and documented, and three stand out: the residents of the five inhabited territories, people held by the US military outside the country, and non-citizens held under immigration law. None of them matches what the two previous sections describe, and this section says where each comparison holds and where it fails.

The territories: citizens without a vote

Five inhabited territories belong to the United States without being part of it. At the 2020 census Puerto Rico had 3,285,874 residents, Guam 153,836, the US Virgin Islands 87,146, American Samoa 49,710 and the Northern Mariana Islands 47,329: together more than 3.6 million people, who are US citizens or US nationals. [cite:us9_crs_territories] [cite:us9_census_island] They cannot vote in presidential general elections, though they take part in party primaries. Each territory sends one member to the House of Representatives who may sit on committees but cannot vote on legislation, including bills the member introduced. [cite:us9_cb_territories] Congress holds broad authority over them under the Constitution, and the Supreme Court's Insular Cases, decided from 1901 after the Spanish-American War, distinguished "incorporated" territories on the way to statehood from "unincorporated" ones to which the Constitution applies only in part; all five are unincorporated. [cite:us9_crs_territories] [cite:us9_cb_territories]

The Insular Cases remain law. Justice Neil Gorsuch wrote in 2022 that they "have no foundation in the Constitution" and rest on "racial stereotypes", and the Justice Department has criticised their "racist rhetoric and reasoning", but on 17 October 2022 the Supreme Court declined to reconsider them. [cite:us9_ap_samoa] [cite:us9_cb_territories] The same day it declined to hear a claim by people born in American Samoa that they are citizens by birth. They remain US nationals, not citizens: they cannot vote for president even when living in a state, and their only federal vote is for a non-voting House delegate. The appeals court left the question to Congress, and American Samoa's own government, like the Biden administration, opposed the suit, the territory's government saying citizenship by court ruling could disrupt the islands' cultural traditions. [cite:us9_ap_samoa]

This is the American case closest in kind to the Israeli one: people under the full authority of a government in which they have no vote. The differences are large. The territories' residents are, with the American Samoa exception, citizens, who gain the vote by moving to any state. They are governed by civil law and civilian courts, elect their own local governments, and are not under military rule, and American Samoa's own leaders defended the present arrangement in court. [cite:us9_ap_samoa] [cite:us9_cb_territories]

Military control abroad: Iraq and Guantanamo

The closest American parallel to military rule over a foreign population is the occupation of Iraq. On 22 May 2003 the UN Security Council, in Resolution 1483, recognised the United States and the United Kingdom as "occupying powers under unified command" and called on them to comply with the Geneva Conventions and the Hague Regulations. [cite:us9_icrc_1483] [cite:us9_wiki_1483] US forces detained Iraqis for years afterwards: detention peaked at 26,000 people in November 2007, 22,000 of them at Camp Bucca, some held for three years without charge. A security agreement that took effect in January 2009 required the United States to hand detainees to Iraqi courts or release them, and Camp Bucca closed that September. [cite:us9_ap_bucca]

Guantanamo, which Part Four sets out in detail, is the smaller and longer case. Nearly 800 men have been held there since 2002; by 1 July 2026, 15 remained, six of whom had never been charged. [cite:us4_amnesty_gtmo] [scene:us4_detention|Part Four: detention without trial] The legal principle, detention on the executive's security assessment without criminal trial, is the one critics of Israel's administrative detention object to; the scale is not comparable. In October 2026, by Israel Prison Service data compiled by HaMoked, Israel held 3,101 people classed as security inmates in administrative detention and 1,419 as "unlawful combatants". [cite:us4_hamoked_charts]

Immigration detention

Inside the United States, the largest group held without a criminal conviction is non-citizens in civil immigration detention. Immigration and Customs Enforcement held 68,289 people at the February 2026 peak, 73.6 per cent with no criminal conviction. [cite:us4_trac_ice] Their detention is civil, not criminal: they are held pending a decision on whether they may stay, not as punishment, and the group changes as cases are decided, unlike a population governed for decades. Part Six takes up the law, the asylum system and the politics. [scene:us6_asylum|Part Six: asylum and detention]

The comparison stated plainly

Both states govern people who have no vote in the government that governs them, and both have used detention without trial on security grounds. Both have courts that have accepted limits on the rights of those people: the Insular Cases in the United States, the Al-Bassiouni ruling and the dual legal system in Israel. [cite:us9_cb_territories] [cite:gz_ahmed_hcj] [cite:wb_acri_twosys] There the parallel ends. The United States governs no population of comparable size under military law today; its one recognised occupation of recent decades, in Iraq, ran under a UN resolution, and its mass detention there ended under an agreement with an Iraqi government. [cite:us9_icrc_1483] [cite:us9_ap_bucca] Israel has governed the West Bank under military law since 1967, roughly a third of the people under its rule hold no Israeli citizenship, and the people of the West Bank and Gaza have no route to it. [cite:us9_pcbs_2025] This difference governs every other comparison in this book. When Israel's figures for health, income or democracy are set beside America's, they describe Israel's citizens and residents, not the millions it controls without citizenship; the indices that rank Israel as a democracy say so in their own notes.
