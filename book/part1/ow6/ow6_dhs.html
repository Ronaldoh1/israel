The Department and the Ministry

The Department of Homeland Security, of which Immigration and Customs Enforcement is a component, was set up in 2002–03 after the attacks of 11 September 2001. Its ties to Israel were built afterwards, in four layers: a memorandum between ministers, a research authority written into a 2014 law, a grant programme run with a binational foundation, and a cybersecurity grant programme added by Congress in 2021. Each is small by the standards of the Pentagon programmes described in Part Seven, and each is documented in a public text. [cite:ow6_jvl_dhsmou] [cite:ow6_lii_8606]

The 2007 memorandum. The founding instrument is often dated to 2008; the text and the contemporary reporting date it to February 2007. That month the Secretary of Homeland Security, Michael Chertoff, and Israel's Minister of Public Security, Avi Dichter, signed a memorandum of understanding in Washington "setting the terms for security cooperation between the two nations". [cite:ow6_da_dhspact] Its subjects were counterterrorism and related serious crime; aviation security, including passenger and cargo screening; the training and exchange of analytical personnel; research and testing of explosives-detection technology; and the sharing of emergency planning and response information. Information was to pass on request or on either side's initiative, chiefly through the two embassies. [cite:ow6_jvl_dhsmou]

The memorandum is also notable for what it does not do. It states that it "does not independently authorize disclosure of classified information", which continued to move only under existing security agreements, and that it "does not impose any legal commitments on the Participants". Either side must obtain the other's consent before passing information it received to a third country. [cite:ow6_jvl_dhsmou] In other words, the 2007 text is a framework for cooperation, not a treaty: it creates no rights that a court could enforce and no obligation that Congress voted on. The public record does not show that it has been amended or replaced.

The 2014 research authority. Congress gave the department a statutory mandate seven years later. Section 7 of the United States–Israel Strategic Partnership Act of 2014 (Public Law 113-296, signed on 19 December 2014) authorized the Secretary of Homeland Security, acting through the department's advanced research agency and with the Secretary of State's agreement, to set up "pilot" cooperative research programmes with Israel. Their stated aim was to enhance Israel's capabilities in border, maritime and aviation security, explosives detection and emergency services. [cite:ow6_lii_8606] The Act passed the Senate unanimously on 18 September 2014 and the House by voice vote on 3 December 2014. [cite:ow_jvl_2014act] A 2016 law (Public Law 114-304) dropped the word "pilot", making the programmes permanent, and added cybersecurity as a fourth field; a 2021 law authorized $2 million for fiscal year 2021. [cite:ow6_lii_8606]

BIRD HLS. The money for joint projects has largely run through an older binational body, the Israel–United States Binational Industrial Research and Development (BIRD) Foundation. Since 2016 the department's Science and Technology Directorate and Israel's Ministry of Public Security have selected, with the Israel Innovation Authority, pairs of American and Israeli companies for "BIRD HLS" grants of up to $1 million each, covering up to half of a project's budget. In the 2020 round, BIRD committed $1.5 million to two pairings, with total project value of about $3 million once private money was counted. Israel's chief scientist for public security described the programme as one that "enables us to enhance public safety in Israel and in the United States". [cite:ow6_bird_hls2021]

The 2021 cybersecurity grants. The most recent layer came from the annual defence bill. In April 2021 Senators Susan Collins, Jacky Rosen, Sheldon Whitehouse and Todd Young introduced the US–Israel Cybersecurity Cooperation Enhancement Act, proposing a homeland-security grant programme of $6 million a year for five years. [cite:ow6_collins_cyber] Representative Andrew Garbarino offered the same measure as an amendment to the National Defense Authorization Act for fiscal 2022, and the House adopted it on 23 September 2021. [cite:ow6_garbarino_ndaa] As enacted (section 1551 of Public Law 117-81, 27 December 2021), the programme funds joint ventures between American and Israeli businesses, universities, laboratories or governments; requires at least half of research costs to come from non-federal money; is limited to projects both countries deem unclassified; excludes applicants the Director of National Intelligence finds to be a counterintelligence threat; and authorizes "not less than $6,000,000" a year for fiscal years 2022 to 2026. [cite:ow6_lii_8606] An authorization is not an appropriation, and this book did not find a public accounting of how much has actually been awarded under it; as of October 2026 the authorization runs out with fiscal year 2026.

What each side gets. The texts present the relationship as mutual, and in its research layer it is. BIRD HLS and the 2021 grants fund only joint ventures, so an American company is a party to every project; Israel's ministry describes the gains as public safety in both countries. [cite:ow6_bird_hls2021] [cite:ow6_lii_8606] The 2014 statute is more one-sided in its wording: it states its purpose as enhancing Israel's capabilities, not those of the United States. [cite:ow6_lii_8606] The 2007 memorandum binds neither government to share anything, and its third-party consent clause means that information Israel gives the department cannot travel onwards without Israel's permission, and the reverse. [cite:ow6_jvl_dhsmou] What the public record does not contain is any audit of how much information or technology has flowed in each direction under these instruments. Unlike the arms programmes in Parts Seven and Eight, the homeland-security cooperation has no annual reporting requirement that would let Congress, or a reader, measure it.
