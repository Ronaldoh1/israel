Why No One Else Has Been Charged

The question readers ask most often after reading the files is the simplest one. If so many powerful people appear in them, why has no one besides Epstein and Maxwell been arrested? There are two honest answers, and both are true at once.

The first answer: a name is not a charge

A prosecution needs evidence that a particular person committed a particular crime, provable to a jury. Most of what the files hold about famous names is emails, meetings, flights, photographs and money for ordinary services, and none of that is a crime in itself. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche made exactly this point in February 2026: the photographs and emails, he said, do not "necessarily" allow the department to prosecute anyone, and it cannot "create evidence" for a case that "isn't there". [cite:ep15_ap_blanche2026] The department's July 2025 review had already concluded that there was no client list. [cite:ep5_npr_memo2025] This book holds itself to the same rule. Every person on the companion map is shown by a documented tie of a named kind, and a tie is not an accusation.

The second answer: the system decided who would be looked at

The first answer assumes the evidence has been looked for. The record shows how much of the looking was stopped, narrowed or never done, and who decided.

In 2008 a federal non-prosecution agreement gave immunity to "any potential co-conspirators" in the Southern District of Florida and closed the federal case for a state plea and about thirteen months in custody with work release. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] Under the federal sex-trafficking law, a conviction involving a minor carries a minimum of ten years, or fifteen if the child is under fourteen or force was used. [cite:ep15_ussc_1591] The Justice Department's own review in 2020 found the decision reflected "poor judgment" but no professional misconduct, and no one was disciplined. [cite:ep15_ap_opr2020] The FBI logged a written complaint about Epstein in September 1996 and opened no case for a decade. [cite:guardian_farmer] The banks that moved his money paid civil settlements and a regulator's penalty. [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023] [cite:ep3_cnbc_dfs_db2020] The release of the files in 2026 left the names of 43 of 47 victims checked by the Wall Street Journal readable, while the names of men in the files stayed blacked out until two members of Congress read six of them aloud on the House floor; the department said some redactions had been made "inadvertently". [cite:ep9_wsj_43] [cite:ep15_cbs_sixnames2026]

None of these was one man's choice. Each was an ordinary decision made under ordinary rules by people with discretion: whether to charge, what to trade, which accounts to keep, what to redact. The comparison cases in the previous chapter show the same rules working the other way when the evidence was gathered and someone chose to use it: Raniere and García Luna were convicted, and Nygård was charged by the same federal office in Manhattan that charged Epstein in 2019. [cite:ep15_doj_raniere2020] [cite:ep15_aj_garcialuna2024] [cite:ep15_doj_nygard2020] What separates those outcomes from this one is not that the evidence was impossible to find. It is who was asked to find it, and when.

What can and cannot be said

It cannot be said, from the public record, that any named person committed a crime and escaped charges because of influence. That would need evidence of the crime, and of the influence, and neither is in the released files. What can be said is narrower and, for that reason, harder to wave away. The decisions that limited the case were taken inside the institutions responsible for it, under rules that leave those decisions to a small number of officials, and the people protected by those decisions were the ones with power, while the people exposed were the girls and young women who had none. That is what "the system" means in this book: not a hidden committee, but a set of documented choices, each one defensible on its own, that together decided whose names would be read and whose would be kept.
