Sabotage, Code and Killings: The Campaign Against the Programme

From the second half of the 2000s Israel, at times together with the United States, ran a covert campaign against Iran's nuclear programme. It used computer code, bombs, gunmen, a robotic machine gun, a burglary and drones. Almost none of it has been officially acknowledged. What follows rests on named reporting, chiefly by the New York Times and the Washington Post, on the statements of the governments involved, and on what Iran itself reported. Where the only source is anonymous officials, the text says so.

The best-documented operation is the computer worm later called Stuxnet. David Sanger of the New York Times reported on 1 June 2012 that it was the product of a programme code-named Olympic Games, begun under President George W. Bush in 2006 and continued by President Barack Obama, and that Israel's signals intelligence unit, Unit 8200, supplied technical expertise and intelligence on the enrichment plant at Natanz. It escaped onto the internet through a programming error and was discovered in 2010. Obama, briefed with Vice President Joe Biden and CIA Director Leon Panetta, decided to continue. According to Sanger's sources, the last attack took out nearly 1,000 of the roughly 5,000 centrifuges then running. [cite:ow3_sw_sanger] Sanger later said that a first piece of code had mapped the plant and "phoned home, back to the National Security Agency and the Unit 8200," and that Bush's motive was that he "didn't want to see the Israelis bomb the Iran facilities": in Sanger's words, "the Israelis needed to be convinced that there was an alternative to a military attack." The official estimate he reported was a delay of 18 months to two years. [cite:ow3_npr_sanger] Neither government has confirmed the operation. A related spy program, Flame, was reported by the Washington Post in June 2012 to have been developed jointly by the NSA, the CIA and Israel to map Iran's computer networks. The same report said Israel had, on its own, directed a disruptive attack on Iran's oil-industry computers that caught its American partners off guard. [cite:ir8_wapo_flame]

In the same years a series of Iranian nuclear scientists were attacked in Tehran, most of them by men on motorcycles. Masoud Alimohammadi was killed in January 2010 by a bomb on a motorcycle that appeared to be detonated remotely. In November 2010 Majid Shahriari was killed when a motorcyclist attached a bomb to his car; his wife and driver were wounded. Fereydoon Abbasi survived a similar attack the same morning. Darioush Rezaeinejad was shot dead by two gunmen in July 2011; Israeli reports linked him to nuclear work, which Iran rejected. Mostafa Ahmadi Roshan was killed with his driver by a magnetic bomb in January 2012. [cite:ir8_mee_scientists] The United States and Israel denied involvement in the Shahriari killing, and Washington condemned the Roshan killing and denied a role. Iran's president, Mahmoud Ahmadinejad, blamed "the hand of the Zionist regime" and Western governments for the Shahriari killing. After the Roshan killing Israel's military spokesman, Brigadier General Yoav Mordechai, wrote: "I don't know who took revenge on the Iranian scientist, but I am definitely not shedding a tear." [cite:ir8_mee_scientists] No court has ruled on who carried out these attacks, and no Israeli official has claimed them. The attribution to Israel is the consistent view of named reporting and of the Iranian government. It is a strong inference, not a documented finding.

The campaign also had a conventional military side. From 2017 Israel struck Iranian infrastructure in Syria. In January 2019 the departing chief of staff, Gadi Eisenkot, said Israel had dropped about 2,000 bombs on Iranian targets in 2018 alone and had carried out "thousands of attacks without taking responsibility." [cite:hz10s_toi_eisenkot] Netanyahu told his cabinet on 13 January 2019 that the military had "attacked hundreds of times Iranian and Hezbollah targets." [cite:hz10s_jc_netanyahu] The Hezbollah volume covers that campaign. [scene:hz10_syria|The Syria campaign]

The archive operation is the one Israel itself made public. On 30 April 2018 Netanyahu displayed what he said were Iran's nuclear files; Part Seven describes the presentation and the IAEA's response. [scene:ir7_withdrawal|The archive] Israeli officials later told the New York Times how the files had been taken. A Mossad team entered a warehouse in Tehran at 10:30 p.m. on 31 January 2018, cut into most of 32 Iranian-made safes, and left before 5 a.m. with about 50,000 pages and 163 compact discs. Iran called the material fabricated. American and British officials said it was genuine. [cite:ir8_alarabiya_archive]

The most consequential killing came on 27 November 2020. Mohsen Fakhrizadeh had been named in the IAEA's 2011 annex as the head of the AMAD plan, Iran's pre-2003 weapons-related effort. [cite:ir4_sipri_annex2011] He was shot while driving with his wife near Absard, east of Tehran. On 18 September 2021 the New York Times reported, from interviews with American, Israeli and Iranian officials, that the weapon was a Belgian-made FN MAG machine gun on a robotic mount assisted by artificial intelligence. The device weighed about a ton, had been smuggled into Iran in parts and reassembled, and was fired by an operator "thousands of miles away." It fired 15 rounds in under a minute, and a truck carrying explosives was blown up afterwards. The paper reported that Israel had tracked Fakhrizadeh since at least 2007 and had begun preparing the operation in late 2019 and early 2020. On motive it reported that, in Netanyahu's "best-case scenario," the killing "would derail any chance of resurrecting the nuclear agreement even if Mr. Biden won." [cite:ir8_newarab_fakh] That last point is the newspaper's account of his reasoning from unnamed sources. Netanyahu has not stated it, and Israel has not acknowledged the killing. Iran blamed Israel at once and vowed revenge. [cite:ir8_newarab_fakh]

Natanz itself was hit twice more. In July 2020 an explosion tore through its advanced centrifuge assembly plant, which Iranian authorities later called sabotage. On 11 April 2021 the plant lost power. Iran's nuclear spokesman, Behrouz Kamalvandi, first said there was no casualty or damage, and Iran then called the incident "nuclear terrorism." Israeli broadcasters reported a cyberattack and said Israel was probably responsible, without citing sources. Israel did not claim either attack. The army chief, Aviv Kochavi, said only that Israel's operations "are not hidden from the eyes of the enemy." [cite:ir8_ap_natanz] [cite:ir8_ap_nucterror] Five days later Iran began enriching uranium to 60 per cent at Natanz, the highest level in its history. [cite:ir7_aje_60pc] On 29 January 2023 three drones struck a defence ministry workshop near Isfahan. Iran reported "minor damage" and called the attack "cowardly." The Wall Street Journal, citing US officials, reported that Israel was behind it, and other US officials said Washington had no part in it. [cite:ir8_aj_isfahan23]

What the campaign achieved can be measured only in part. Sanger's sources estimated that Stuxnet cost Iran 18 months to two years. [cite:ow3_npr_sanger] In January 2011 the departing Mossad chief, Meir Dagan, said Iran could not build a bomb before 2015, in part because of unspecified "measures." [cite:ir5_ap_dagan2011] Delay is therefore documented. Prevention is a different matter. Each blow was followed by reconstruction, and the April 2021 sabotage was followed by Iran's step to 60 per cent. Part Five tests Israel's dated predictions against this record.
