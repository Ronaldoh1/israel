Israel: The Security Service at Home

Israel's law protects privacy at the highest level it has. Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty, passed in 1992, says in section 7 that "All persons have the right to privacy and to intimacy", which gives the right quasi-constitutional standing. Below it sit the Protection of Privacy Law of 1981 and the Wiretap Law of 1979, which requires a warrant from the president of a district court to tap a line, for up to three months at a time and renewable; it was extended in 1995 to mobile phones, computers and email. A 2007 criminal-procedure law lets the police obtain telephone metadata, meaning who called whom, when and from where, with a court order, and without one for up to 24 hours in some circumstances. [cite:us10_coda_israel] The civilian privacy regulator gained real teeth only recently: Amendment 13 to the 1981 law, in force from 14 August 2025, gave the Privacy Protection Authority powers to fine, to order processing stopped and to open criminal investigations, and added rules for data brokers. [cite:us10_iapp_amend13]

The Shin Bet's standing access

Beside that civilian structure stands a second one. The General Security Service Law of 2002, which governs the Shin Bet, the domestic security service, allows the prime minister in section 11 to require licensed telecommunications companies to hand the service "data, including communications data and excluding the contents of any conversation": in practice traffic, location, identification and web-use metadata. No judge approves this in advance. The Shin Bet's director authorises use of the data for up to six months, renewable without limit; the rules for its use and retention are set by the prime minister and are classified; and oversight runs after the fact, through quarterly reports to the attorney general and the prime minister and an annual report to a Knesset subcommittee. A petition by the Association for Civil Rights in Israel (ACRI) challenging section 11 was pending before the Supreme Court in March 2024, and in December 2023 the Justice Ministry published a draft bill that would add powers to acquire whole databases and to search computers remotely, while keeping authorisation within the executive. [cite:us10_lawfare_isalaw] The data system built under section 11 is known in Israel as "the Tool". According to Yedioth Ahronoth, the Shin Bet had used it for about two decades by 2021, mainly to find wanted Palestinians. [cite:us10_toi_tool] Because Israeli law sets no mandatory retention period for commercial telecoms, one legal summary describes the Tool as working in effect as a state-run retention system covering every telephone user. [cite:us10_coda_israel]

The pandemic test

The Tool was turned on Israel's own population in March 2020. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's government authorised the Shin Bet to track the phones of people with COVID-19, and those near them, by emergency regulation, without a Knesset vote. [cite:us10_newarab_hcj2020] Adalah petitioned on 18 March on behalf of the Joint List, joined by ACRI, the Journalists' Union and others. On 26 April 2020 the Supreme Court ruled that the Shin Bet Law did not allow the government to use the service for anything beyond national security, so that tracking citizens for public health required a statute; it was the first time the court had set a limit on the service's powers under that law. It let the tracking continue while the Knesset legislated and required separate permission before tracking journalists. [cite:us10_adalah_hcj2020] [cite:us10_newarab_hcj2020] The Knesset passed the law in July 2020. It allowed government orders of 21 days at a time, only while more than 200 new cases a day were recorded, each approved by a Knesset committee, with an appeal to the Health Ministry to be decided within 24 hours. The Privacy Protection Authority opposed it, saying the tool "egregiously violates" privacy. [cite:us10_pc_law2020]

The record on whether it worked is disputed. By the Health Ministry's figures, from July to the end of 2020 about 950,000 people were sent into quarantine on the Tool's say-so and 46,000 of them were found to be infected, against about 900,000 quarantined and 63,000 found infected through ordinary contact tracing. Tehilla Shwartz Altshuler of the Israel Democracy Institute estimated that at least 100,000 people had been quarantined wrongly, and the State Comptroller's interim report of October 2020 found ordinary tracing significantly more effective and the Shin Bet not always deleting data as the law required. [cite:us10_toi_tool] The law expired on 20 January 2021. In a ruling reported on 3 March 2021, a seven-justice panel presided over by Chief Justice Esther Hayut held that from 14 March the tracking could be used only for people who refused to cooperate with human contact tracers, and strongly recommended ending it altogether; Justice Anat Baron would have stopped it at once. [cite:us10_nif_hcj2021] In late November 2021 Prime Minister Naftali Bennett's government brought the tracking back for carriers of the Omicron variant, and four rights groups petitioned again. [cite:us10_ih_omicron]

The police and Pegasus

The second episode concerned the police, and its record shows how a strong allegation and a narrower official finding can both stand. On 18 January 2022 the business daily Calcalist reported that the police had used NSO Group's Pegasus spyware without warrants against mayors, former officials and leaders of the protests against Netanyahu. Public Security Minister Omer Barlev said there was "no practice" of police hacking devices without a judge's approval; the attorney general, the State Comptroller and the police opened inquiries. [cite:us10_jurist_calcalist] A team under Deputy Attorney General Amit Marari, including two former Shin Bet officials, reported on 21 February 2022 that it had found "no indication" that the police had infected the phone of anyone on Calcalist's published list without a court order. It also established that the police did hold a Pegasus licence, which a former police commissioner had denied, and a second, unnamed tool. Justice Minister Gideon Sa'ar called the newspaper's report "incorrect" and said no state commission of inquiry was needed; Calcalist said it would re-examine its findings. [cite:us10_toi_saar] The team's further findings, published in August 2022, again found no use without a judicial order, but found that when the technology was used the police collected information beyond what the warrants covered, which the team called a "violation of authority". ACRI said the findings showed "major failures" and asked for police use of such tools to be barred until specific legislation existed. [cite:us10_ih_marari] The firm and its exports are covered in The One-Way Mirror. [scene:ow10_nso|NSO and Pegasus]

Biometrics and the wartime powers

Two other laws widened what the state holds. The biometric database law of 2009 began as a voluntary pilot; in 2017 the Knesset made it permanent, with a facial image compulsory for every identity card and passport and fingerprints stored unless the applicant opts out, at the price of a document valid for five years rather than ten. The police were barred from the database until the Knesset issues regulations. [cite:us10_lawcoil_biometric] After 7 October 2023, emergency regulations of 14 and 17 October let the army and the Shin Bet hack the computers that run privately owned stationary cameras, without the owner's knowledge and without a court order, reporting monthly and confidentially to a Knesset committee and the attorney general, with no role for the courts or the Privacy Protection Authority. [cite:us10_ctech_cameras] The Knesset kept extending the power; on 25 June 2025 it voted 13–4 to extend it to 31 December 2025. [cite:us10_toi_cameras2025] In July 2026 the government also gave the Shin Bet a role against illegal weapons in Arab communities, a move rights groups opposed on the ground that crime among Jewish citizens is left to the police. [cite:us5_toi_shinbet] [scene:us5_arab|Part Five: the Arab-community killings]

All of this concerns Israel's own citizens and residents. Palestinians in the West Bank live under a different regime: the army has run the Red Wolf facial-recognition system at Hebron checkpoints since 2022 on databases made up only of Palestinians' data, and Unit 8200 stored intercepted Palestinian calls on Microsoft's cloud until Microsoft cut off part of that service in September 2025. [cite:wb_toi_redwolf] [cite:ow4_tc_msft] [scene:wb7_watchers|The watchers] No series measures Israeli domestic surveillance over time the way the American transparency reports do; what exists is a record of laws, court rulings and official inquiries, and this section is built from those.
