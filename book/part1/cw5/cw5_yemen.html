Yemen, 1962–1963: The War That Tested the Wager

Kennedy's approach to Nasser, told in [scene:cw5_nasser_outreach|the previous section], had barely been sealed with wheat when a revolution in Yemen put it to its hardest test. From the autumn of 1962 Egypt and Saudi Arabia backed opposite sides of a civil war in Yemen. Washington was tied to both: to Cairo by the policy it had just signed, to Riyadh by its commitment to Saudi security. Israel, on one scholar's account, entered the war too, covertly and on the royalist side. This section follows the war from September 1962 to November 1963, as far as it touched those four capitals.

The revolution, September 1962. Imam Ahmed, one of the six Arab rulers to whom Kennedy had written in May 1961, died on 19 September 1962. Muhammad al-Badr succeeded him. A week later an army-led rebellion overthrew al-Badr and proclaimed the Yemen Arab Republic. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom] [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] Recognition from the new republic's patrons came within days, though the sources date it slightly differently. The United Nations' own history records that the UAR recognized the new government on 29 September and the Soviet Union the next day, 30 September. Bruce Riedel, in a 2021 essay for the Brookings Institution, dates Soviet recognition to 1 October, and dates the first Soviet airlift flight to Sanaa to 30 September. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom] [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The difference is a single day, but it changes the order of events: read against Riedel's date for the first airlift flight, the UN's dates put Soviet recognition on the same day as that flight; on Riedel's own dates, the airlift preceded recognition. The sources reached here do not settle which is right.

Washington's first reading. The CIA saw quickly what kind of war this would be. A brief of 2 October 1962, quoted by Riedel, warned that "a civil war is shaping up with direct backing from the UAR for one faction and from Saudi Arabia for the other." [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] Three days later, on 5 October, Crown Prince Faisal of Saudi Arabia met Kennedy. Kennedy declined to intervene in Yemen, but he reaffirmed American support for Saudi security, increased the Navy's port visits to the kingdom and expanded training for the Saudi air force. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The meeting fell on the same day that Kennedy approved the multi-year food agreement with the UAR, after Komer had argued that the Nasser policy mattered more than the Yemen policy. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70] Washington was reassuring Riyadh and feeding Cairo in the same week, while the two were arming opposite sides.

The scale of Egypt's commitment. Egypt's role grew fast. The CIA estimated 4,000 Egyptian troops in Yemen on 31 October 1962. Riedel gives about 30,000 by March 1963, and about 70,000 by 1966. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] Within five months, on these figures, the deployment had grown more than sevenfold. The later figure belongs to the next stage, but it shows where the commitment that began in the autumn of 1962 would lead.

Recognition, 18–19 December 1962. Kennedy's answer to the war was to try to end it by diplomacy, and his instrument was recognition. The State Department proposed a statement that both the new republic and the UAR would make. On 18 December 1962 the Yemen Arab Republic issued a communiqué embodying the American text, and on 19 December the UAR issued a nearly identical one. The United States recognized the Yemen Arab Republic on 19 December 1962, saying that the two statements provided a basis for ending the conflict. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d116] [cite:rs_cw5_oh_yemen] The mechanism was a bargain in sequence: public commitments first from Sanaa and Cairo, American recognition on the same day as the second. According to Riedel, Kennedy did so despite pressure from Saudi Arabia, Jordan and Britain, and the UN General Assembly recognized the republic the next day. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen]

On this book's reading, the decision fits the policy Washington had argued all year. To recognize the republic was to accept the outcome Nasser was fighting for, in exchange for public statements that pointed toward disengagement. Among those who objected, on Riedel's account, was the kingdom whose security Kennedy had reaffirmed to Faisal ten weeks earlier. The administration acted as though it could hold both lines at once; the sources reached here do not record its reasoning.

Bunker's mission, March–April 1963. The statements did not end the fighting. In the spring came mediation, through Ellsworth Bunker. Bunker arrived in Riyadh on 6 March 1963. According to Riedel, Faisal reluctantly agreed to end Saudi support for the royalists if Egypt withdrew, and Nasser promised to withdraw "gradually" if Saudi aid stopped. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The terms were reported by the UN Secretary-General on 29 April 1963. Saudi Arabia would end its aid to the royalists; Egypt would withdraw its forces in phases; and a demilitarized zone would run 20 kilometres deep on each side of the Saudi–Yemeni border. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom]

The observers, June–July 1963. On 11 June 1963 the Security Council, by resolution 179, authorized an observer mission to watch the disengagement. The United Nations Yemen Observation Mission began on 4 July 1963. Egypt and Saudi Arabia shared its costs, estimated at under $1 million. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom] The arrangement shows who paid: the two outside patrons of the war, not the United States or the UN's general budget, bore the cost of verifying their own withdrawal.

Did the disengagement work? The record supports two readings, and each rests on its own evidence. On paper it did. The UN's history records that Saudi Arabia, the UAR and the Yemen Arab Republic accepted identical terms in April 1963, and that the observer mission deployed in July as agreed. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom] That is the strongest case for the policy: the war's two foreign sponsors had accepted identical terms, under international supervision, within seven months of the revolution. In practice it did not. Riedel reports that by April 1963, the month the terms were reported, Egypt told the UN mediator it would not withdraw, and Saudi Arabia that it would not stop aiding the royalists. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The mission itself, which began in July 1963, ended on 4 September 1964, and on Riedel's figures Egyptian forces in Yemen went on growing to about 70,000 by 1966. [cite:rs_cw5_unyom] [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The two readings are not contradictory. One describes what the parties signed; the other, on Riedel's account, what they told the mediator and what they then did.

Hard Surface, July–November 1963. Kennedy's other instrument was force, shown rather than used. Under Operation Hard Surface the United States sent F-100 fighters to Saudi Arabia from July 1963, the first American combat deployment to the Arabian Peninsula, according to Riedel; it ended in November 1963. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] The deployment ran alongside the observer mission. While UN observers watched the border to verify a withdrawal, American aircraft stood in the kingdom, a visible form of the commitment to Saudi security that Kennedy had reaffirmed to Faisal in October 1962. That was the shape of the policy by the end of 1963: diplomacy with Cairo, protection for Riyadh, and no intervention in Yemen itself.

Israel's part, on Riedel's account. Israel appears in this war in one source reached for this book. Riedel writes that Israel joined the covert campaign on the royalist side: the Mossad ran an operation called Rotev, and the Israeli Air Force dropped weapons to the royalists, flying over Saudi Arabia with Saudi approval. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] His essay gives no dates for these operations, so this book cannot say whether they fell within 1962–63 or later, and no second source reached here confirms them. The claim is Riedel's, and it is recorded as his. If it is accurate, it would mean that Israel and Saudi Arabia were acting on the same side of an Arab civil war against Egypt, while the United States was trying to end that war on terms Egypt had accepted.

What the record leaves open, and what came after. Several points are not settled by the sources reached here. The date of Soviet recognition differs by a day between the United Nations and Riedel. The troop figures after October 1962 are Riedel's, not documents quoted in full. Israel's covert role rests on Riedel alone, without dates. And the question whether the disengagement failed because neither patron meant to honour it, or because events overtook it, is not answered by the documents here: the record shows what each side signed and, on Riedel's account, what each told the mediator, not what their governments privately intended. The war outlasted the observers and outlasted Kennedy. Its course from 1964, the growth of Egypt's force and what that commitment meant for Egypt's position by the spring of 1967, belongs to [scene:cw6_yemen|Stage Six]. The Yemen Arab Republic broke relations with the United States on 7 June 1967. [cite:rs_cw5_oh_yemen]
