What Stops It

The previous sections described forces that grew: an immigration agency doubled in size, local police deputised by the thousand, Guard units trained for crowd control, a detention industry with record revenue. This section asks the opposite question, using only what happened: when the force was pointed somewhere, what actually stopped it, and what did not. The answer is a set of brakes that are real but uneven. Some held, some slipped on appeal, and one, the refusal of soldiers to obey, has not been tested at all.

Courts

Courts were the brake used most often, and they ruled both ways. Against the government: Judge Breyer found a Posse Comitatus violation in California; Judge Immergut permanently barred the Portland deployment; the Supreme Court refused the Illinois deployment, after which the federalised Guard left Los Angeles, Chicago and Portland on 31 December 2025. [cite:de32_breyer_judgment] [cite:de32_nbc_immergut] [cite:de32_lawdork_il] [cite:us3_audit_lawdork_il] Judge Reyes dismissed the Justice Department's suit against New York City's sanctuary laws, and the Supreme Court ruled against GEO's bid for an early appeal on contractor immunity in a forced-labour case. [cite:de32_dr_nyc] [cite:de32_lii_menocal] Judge Richard Leon blocked the defence secretary's attempt to punish Senator Mark Kelly for a video telling service members they could refuse illegal orders, finding that the secretary had "trampled on Senator Kelly's First Amendment freedoms." [cite:de32_npr_kelly]

For the government: the Supreme Court allowed immigration stops based on factors including apparent ethnicity in Los Angeles; the D.C. Circuit kept the Guard in Washington; the Seventh Circuit vacated Judge Ellis's limits on force in Chicago; the Eleventh Circuit let Florida's Everglades camp stay open; and Judge Snyder struck California's mask ban for federal agents while keeping its badge rule. [cite:de32_scotusblog_perdomo] [cite:de32_nbcw_dc] [cite:de32_yahoo_7th] [cite:de32_wflx_11th] [cite:de32_ktvu_masks] Several of the main questions are still open as of October 2026: the appeals on Posse Comitatus, the D.C. Circuit's ruling on the merits, and GEO's petition on detainee wages, which the Supreme Court was due to consider on 6 November 2026. [cite:de32_scotus_25828]

Governors and states

The Constitution divides the force between two levels of government, and in 2025–26 that division did real work. Because Title 32 deployments need a willing governor, Vermont's Republican governor could refuse to send his Guard to support ICE. [cite:de32_csm_guard] Because federalising the Guard against a governor's wishes now requires showing that the regular military could not execute the laws, the Supreme Court's Illinois order has, in one analysis, functionally limited Guard deployments to states whose governors consent. [cite:de32_votebeat_polls] The anti-commandeering rule means no state can be forced to lend its police, and eight states had signed no 287(g) agreement by August 2025. [cite:de32_ncc_printz] [cite:de32_kocher_287g] The same division also works the other way: Texas requires its sheriffs to cooperate, and Florida signed more agreements than any other state. [cite:de32_tpr_sb8] [cite:de32_kocher_287g]

Prosecutors and juries

A magistrate judge in New Mexico dismissed trespass charges against migrants arrested in a new military zone because the government could not show they knew they had entered it. [cite:de32_aj_nda] In Minnesota the Hennepin County Attorney charged an ICE agent with assault and with lying about a shooting, and the state sued to obtain federal evidence in the two Minneapolis killings; it received the evidence in July 2026 and had made no charging decision by then. [cite:de32_fox9_mn] Juries cut the other way as well: a federal jury in Fort Worth convicted eight people of providing material support to terrorists after a police officer was shot outside an ICE centre on 4 July 2025. [cite:de30_kera_prairieland]

Inside the forces

Every service member swears an oath to the Constitution. Military law presumes orders lawful, and only a "manifestly illegal" order, one a person "of ordinary sense and understanding" would recognise as a crime, may be refused; a debatable order must be obeyed, and the Manual for Courts-Martial warns that an order "is disobeyed at the peril of the subordinate." [cite:de32_stripes_orders] That bar is high by design. Individual refusals of conscience exist: Colonel Doug Krugman of the Marine Corps resigned after 24 years in September 2025, writing that service members "should be confident in questioning possibly immoral or illegal orders." [cite:de32_aol_krugman] At the top, the precedent is mixed. In June 2020 Defense Secretary Mark Esper publicly opposed invoking the Insurrection Act. [cite:de32_mil_esper] In February 2025 the Army's and Air Force's top lawyers were removed as not "well-suited" to advise on lawful orders. [cite:de32_mt_jags] No refusal of a domestic order by a unit or service member in 2025 or 2026 has been documented, which may mean that no clearly unlawful order was given, or that the brake was never pulled; the record does not say which.

Exposure and its effects

Several operations ended after the record about them became public. Judge Ellis's finding that video contradicted agents' accounts was followed by Border Patrol's departure from Chicago. [cite:de32_cbs_ellis] [cite:de32_yahoo_7th] After agents killed two US citizens in Minneapolis and protests followed, the border czar announced the end of the surge there on 12 February 2026, and its commander was reassigned. [cite:de32_kaxe_surge] [cite:de32_yahoo_7th] Independent counts filled gaps the government left: ProPublica's tally of 506 citizens held, human-rights investigators' report on Fort Bliss, the Inspector General's report on the Everglades camp. [cite:de32_dn_propublica] [cite:de32_hrw_bliss] [cite:de32_aic_oig2622] Elections have their own statutory brake: federal law bars troops at polling places, and the White House chief of staff said troops at the polls "will not happen." [cite:de32_votebeat_polls]

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, is that immigration enforcement is the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto the destination, and that such an executive would repurpose existing forces rather than build new ones. Documented: the forces were enlarged and pointed at one priority with remarkable speed. ICE doubled its workforce on its own count and shortened training; 28,390 federal law-enforcement personnel were diverted to its work; local deputisation grew tenfold; Guard quick reaction forces of more than 23,000 were stood up; and the detention business recorded its best year, with the jailer also running the tracker. [cite:de32_kwtx_hiring] [cite:de32_cato_diverted] [cite:de32_fwd_287g] [cite:de32_mt_qrf] [cite:de32_geo_fy25] Documented too are the costs: record deaths in custody, two citizens killed in Minneapolis, more than 500 citizens held, and a court finding of false use-of-force reports. [cite:de32_npr_deaths] [cite:de32_fox9_mn] [cite:de32_dn_propublica] [cite:de32_cbs_ellis]

Probable: that the enlarged force, once built, will be available to whatever priority a future order sets, because its funding runs to 2029 and its legal mandates are broad; that is a reading of capacity, not of any stated plan. Not supported: that these forces have been turned on political opponents as such, that troops have been used at the polls, or that the Insurrection Act has been invoked; it had not been as of 28 October 2026, though the president said in October that he "could use it." [cite:de32_hill_time] [cite:de32_meaww_time] Not supported either: any document linking the build-up to the theorists of [scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two]; the resemblance to their design of an all-powerful chief executive whose check is control of the state's weapons is rhyme, not provenance. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] The record runs against the worst case in the courts' Illinois ruling, the governors' veto over the Guard, the anti-commandeering rule, the Guard withdrawals from Los Angeles, Chicago and Portland, and the end of the Minneapolis surge. The same standard applies to both parties: the private detention structure, the guaranteed bed payments and the fusion centres were built and kept under Democratic and Republican administrations alike. [cite:de32_brennan_14006] [cite:de32_pln_gao] Whether the brakes that held would hold against the Insurrection Act is the open question this part leaves.
