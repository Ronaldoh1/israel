The Second Aliyah, 1904–1914

While the Zionist Organization argued in Basel and Hamburg, a second wave of immigrants was arriving in Jaffa. The Second Aliyah, the immigration of 1904 to the outbreak of war in 1914, was small beside the more than 2.5 million Jews who left Eastern Europe between 1881 and 1914, about 2 million of them for the United States.[cite:rs_rd1_ettinger] And about half of those who came to Palestine did not stay.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] But the years of the wave produced the first collective farm, the first Jewish guards' commune, the first Hebrew high school, and the neighborhood that became Tel Aviv. They also built, in the same years and through the same institutions, a principle of "Hebrew labor" that set Jewish and Arab workers apart. How historians weigh those two achievements against each other is one of the central disputes of the period.

How many came, and how many stayed. The sources do not agree on the size of the wave. The National Library of Israel puts it at about 35,000 immigrants, mostly from Eastern Europe, with a minority from Yemen; the Jewish Virtual Library gives 40,000.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_second] Both agree that departures were heavy. At least half, by the National Library's estimate, and nearly half, by the Jewish Virtual Library's, left within a few years because of economic hardship. By the end of the period the Jewish community numbered about 80,000.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_second] That end-of-period total is itself one of several competing counts of the Jewish population in 1914, which range from under 40,000 Ottoman subjects to over 90,000; the reasons the counts differ are set out in [scene:rd1_ottoman|the opening section].

The country they found: Rothschild's colonies under the ICA. The newcomers did not arrive in empty institutional space. The farming colonies of the First Aliyah, financed by Edmond de Rothschild, were by then under new management. The Jewish Colonization Association, founded by Baron de Hirsch in September 1891 with a capital of £2,000,000, had taken over the administration of Rothschild's Palestine colonies. The 1906 Jewish Encyclopedia dates that arrangement "since 1899."[cite:rs_rd1_je_ica] The year-by-year ledger compiled for this library places it in 1900; the sources differ by a year. On Gershon Shafir's account, discussed below, the First Aliyah colonies relied on cheap Arab labor.[cite:rs_rd1_beinin_shafir]

One arrival: David Ben-Gurion. The best-known immigrant of the wave shows its pattern. David Ben-Gurion, born David Green in Płońsk on 16 October 1886, landed at Jaffa on 9 September 1906, according to Ben-Gurion House, and went that same evening to work in Petah Tikva. Another educational source, the Center for Israel Education, gives his arrival as 7 September 1907; the two dates cannot both be right, and this book does not choose between them. He joined the leadership of Poalei Zion; from 1912 he studied law in Istanbul with Yitzhak Ben-Zvi; and in 1915 the Ottoman authorities expelled him, and he went to Alexandria and then to New York.[cite:rs_rd1_bghouse] [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_bg]

Hebrew labor, in principle and in practice. The immigrants of the Second Aliyah came with an ideal of "Hebrew labor." The National Library of Israel notes that the ideal met friction in practice, because many Jewish farmers went on employing Arab workers.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] What the workers built next, guards and farms of their own, can be read as an answer to that friction; how far it was an answer, and how far the expression of an ideal held in advance, is the dispute taken up below.

The guards: Bar-Giora and Hashomer. The first answer was armed. In 1907 Bar-Giora was founded at Yitzhak Ben-Zvi's home in Neve Tzedek and set up the first Jewish guards' commune at Sejera. In 1909 Hashomer grew out of it under the leadership of Yisrael Shohat. It existed until 1920.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_defense] Hashomer took over the guarding of Jewish settlements from Arab night watchmen, whom the Jewish Virtual Library's account describes as colluding with thieves; that characterization is the account's own, and the material here contains no Arab account of the same events. Hashomer also made it a condition of guarding a settlement that only Jewish laborers be employed there.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_defense] One reading takes that condition as the mechanism in miniature: a security service the settlements needed was tied to the labor policy the workers wanted, so that guarding became a lever for Hebrew labor.

The farms: Kinneret, Degania, Merhavia. The second answer was settlement on national land. Kinneret Farm, set up in 1908, was the first Zionist instructional farm.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] At Umm Juni, on land owned by the Jewish National Fund, the first collective took root, though the sources date its founding differently. According to the Center for Israel Education, Degania, counted as the first kibbutz, was founded on 28 October 1910, when the "Hadera Commune," ten men and two women, leased the land from the Jewish National Fund; an earlier group that Ruppin had placed there in 1909 had dispersed. The Jewish Virtual Library dates Degania to 1909.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_degania] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_second] The difference appears to be not only one of dates: it may turn on whether the dispersed group of 1909 or the commune of 1910 counts as the founding. Ruppin's role came through the Zionist Organization's Palestine Office, whose work is told in [scene:s04g|the section on the Palestine Office]. Two other experiments followed in 1911. The Merhavia cooperative disbanded in 1918, and a women's training farm founded that year trained about 70 young women before it closed in 1917.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] The arrangement at Degania shows the money's path: the land belonged to the Jewish National Fund and was leased to the workers who farmed it rather than sold to them.

The city and the school. The same years produced the first Hebrew urban institutions. The Hebrew Gymnasium, the first Hebrew high school, was founded in Jaffa in 1905; it later became the Herzliya Gymnasium. The idea of a new neighborhood, Ahuzat Bayit, arose in summer 1906. Its building plots were raffled at Passover 1909 among 60 founding families, and the neighborhood took the name Tel Aviv by majority vote.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] The loan that helped finance it came from the Anglo-Palestine Company, as told in [scene:s04e|the section on the Anglo-Palestine Company].

The Yemenites. The Second Aliyah is usually told as a story of young Eastern European socialists. A significant part of it came from Yemen, and that migration had begun long before. From 1881 to 1882 a few hundred Jews left Sanaa and nearby settlements for Palestine, and migration from Yemen continued almost without interruption until 1914; in that period about 10% of Yemen's Jews left, according to a reference account.[cite:rs_rd1_yemen_wiki] In 1884 Yemenite families settled in a new neighborhood, Kfar Hashiloach, in Silwan, outside the walls of Jerusalem.[cite:rs_rd1_yemen_wiki] These early arrivals came independently of the Zionist Organization, which did not yet exist.

In 1911 the organization took a hand. Shmuel Yavne'eli went to Yemen, at his own request, on behalf of the Palestine Office of the Zionist Organization, to encourage immigration; his journey lasted more than a year.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_yavneeli] He returned in April 1912. About 1,000 Jews left central and southern Yemen as a result of his efforts, and several hundred more arrived before 1914, according to the 2003 study by Simon, Laskier and Reguer as summarized in the same reference account.[cite:rs_rd1_yemen_wiki] The National Library of Israel calls the Yemenite immigration of 1911–12 "the first planned immigration operation in the history of Zionism," and counts about 2,000 Yemenite immigrants in the Second Aliyah as a whole, about half of them sent to farming settlements.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_yemen] Yavne'eli's own report, written in 1913, gives the scale from the inside: over the past year, he wrote, "some two hundred and fifty to three hundred Yemenite families have settled in the colonies."[cite:rs_rd1_posen_yavneeli]

Why the colonies wanted them is where the dispute begins. On Gershon Shafir's reading, as summarized by Joel Beinin, the planters brought in Yemenite Jews "who were to be paid Arab wages": Jewish workers who would satisfy the national ideal of Hebrew labor at the price of Arab labor.[cite:rs_rd1_beinin_shafir] One group's later story rests on a single source. Yemenite families from Sharab reached the Kinneret area in spring 1912 and cleared and farmed land there. According to the Yemenite heritage site Wysinfo, which reproduces the group's letters, their disputes with the neighboring Kvutzat Kinneret over land ended in 1930 with the Yemenites' removal to Marmorek, near Rehovot.[cite:rs_rd1_wysinfo] That ending falls well beyond this period and rests on a single heritage source; a later volume returns to it.

The historians' dispute: land and labor, or ideals. Two readings of the Second Aliyah stand against each other, and each has real evidence behind it. Gershon Shafir's study of 1989, as summarized by Joel Beinin, argues that the First Aliyah colonies relied on cheap Arab labor; that the Second Aliyah workers' "conquest of labor" failed to exclude Arab workers but did split the labor market in two; that the planters imported Yemenite Jews to be paid Arab wages; and that the kibbutz and Hashomer were forged in conflict over land and labor rather than mainly by prior ideology.[cite:rs_rd1_beinin_shafir] On this reading the institutions grew chiefly out of competition over land and labor on a settler frontier, not mainly out of prior ideology. The record above gives this case its strongest evidence: the farmers' continued hiring of Arab workers, Hashomer's condition that only Jewish laborers be employed, and Yavne'eli's families settled in the colonies.

Against such readings, Anita Shapira argues, as summarized by Rebecca Stein, that the early socialist settlers became conquerors reluctantly, out of "good will and purity of faith."[cite:rs_rd1_stein_merip] On this reading the motive force was a socialist-national ideal, and the settlers became conquerors reluctantly rather than by design. This case rests on the settlers' own convictions, and the material gathered here records what they did, not what moved them, so it cannot by itself decide between the readings. The two readings agree on what happened. They disagree on why, and on whether intention or structure should carry the weight.

What the record does not settle. The size of the wave is known only within a range of 35,000 to 40,000, and the share who left only as "about half." Degania's founding date depends on which group counts as the founders, and Ben-Gurion's arrival is dated to two different years by two reputable sources. The characterization of the Arab watchmen Hashomer replaced comes from one account, the Jewish Virtual Library's; the material gathered here holds no account from the watchmen, the Arab workers displaced by Hebrew labor, or the villages near the new settlements. The Yemenite figures come partly from reference summaries, and the Kinneret group's later removal from a heritage site. And the central question, whether the Second Aliyah's institutions were driven chiefly by ideals or by the competition for land and work, remains a matter of interpretation on which serious historians differ.
