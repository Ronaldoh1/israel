The Messengers: Who Was Punished, and Who Was Not

Almost everything in the previous section is known because someone broke the law or a promise to make it known. That is the paradox this section sets out. The people who revealed the programmes were prosecuted, imprisoned or driven into exile; the officials who misdescribed the programmes to Congress, or ran them past their legal limits, were not charged. Both halves are documented. What they mean is argued, in good faith, on both sides, and both arguments are given here.

The disclosers

Edward Snowden was charged in June 2013, in a sealed complaint in the Eastern District of Virginia, with theft of government property, unauthorised communication of national defence information and wilful communication of classified communications intelligence, the last two under the Espionage Act; each count carries up to ten years. [cite:de29_jurist_snowden] He flew to Moscow, received asylum, and on 26 September 2022 was made a Russian citizen by President Vladimir Putin's decree. [cite:de29_npr_citizen] The government also sued him. In a civil case, not a criminal one, a federal court found in December 2019 that he had broken the agreements he signed with the CIA and the NSA by publishing his memoir, Permanent Record, and giving 56 speeches without submitting them for review, and on 29 September 2020 it entered a judgment of more than $5.2 million and placed his future earnings from them in trust for the United States; the suit did not try to stop the book. [cite:de29_doj_snowden] President Donald Trump said on 15 August 2020 of a pardon, "I'm going to start looking at it." [cite:de29_aawsat_trump2020] A viral claim in July 2026 that he would be pardoned on 4 July had no basis, and no pardon could be found as of October 2026. [cite:de29_meaww_pardon] At her confirmation hearing as Director of National Intelligence on 30 January 2025, Tulsi Gabbard said he "broke the law" but had exposed "illegal and unconstitutional programs", and declined senators' requests to call him a traitor. [cite:de29_rec_gabbard]

Chelsea Manning, an Army intelligence analyst, gave WikiLeaks hundreds of thousands of military and diplomatic records in 2010, including video of a 2007 helicopter attack in Baghdad, the Afghan and Iraq war logs, published in July and October 2010, and 251,287 State Department cables, published from 28 November 2010. [cite:de29_wiki_cables] [cite:de29_npr_manning] A court-martial acquitted her of aiding the enemy in July 2013 and she was sentenced to 35 years; President Barack Obama commuted the sentence in January 2017, and she left prison on 17 May 2017 after about seven years. [cite:de29_npr_manning] The publisher, Julian Assange, was indicted in 2019 on 18 counts, fought extradition from a British prison for five years, and on 26 June 2024 pleaded guilty in the Northern Mariana Islands to one count of conspiracy to obtain and disclose national defence information, receiving a sentence of time served. [cite:de29_cbs_assange]

Two later cases drew the longest sentences. Reality Winner, an NSA contractor, mailed a classified report on Russian attempts to penetrate an American voting-software supplier to a news outlet; on 23 August 2018 she was sentenced to 63 months, which prosecutors called "the longest sentence served by a federal defendant for an unauthorized disclosure to the media." [cite:de29_cbs_winner] Daniel Hale, a former Air Force analyst, gave The Intercept documents on the drone programme, including one showing that in a five-month operation in Afghanistan nearly 90 per cent of those killed were not the intended targets; on 27 July 2021 he was sentenced to 45 months. Judge Liam O'Grady told him, "You are not being prosecuted for speaking out about the drone program killing innocent people," but for taking the documents. [cite:de29_globe_hale] Joshua Schulte, the source of the CIA's Vault 7 tools, received 40 years in 2024, part of it for a separate child-abuse-material conviction ([scene:de29_the_catalogue|the previous section]). [cite:de29_ng_schulte] The prosecutions ran under three presidents of both parties: Manning and the Snowden charges under Obama, Winner and the Assange indictment under Trump, Hale's sentence and Assange's plea under Joe Biden. [cite:de29_npr_manning] [cite:de29_cbs_winner] [cite:de29_globe_hale] [cite:de29_cbs_assange]

The officials

On 12 March 2013, three months before the first Snowden story, Senator Ron Wyden asked the Director of National Intelligence, James Clapper, at an open hearing whether the NSA collected "any type of data at all on millions, or hundreds of millions of Americans." Clapper answered, "No, sir," and then, "Not wittingly." After the phone-records order was published he told NBC he had given the "least most untruthful" answer available in an open setting, and on 21 June 2013 he wrote to the committee's chair that "My response was clearly erroneous," explaining that he had been thinking of a different programme. [cite:de29_pf_clapper] The five-year limitation periods for charging false statements to Congress ran out on 12 March 2018 with no charge brought. [cite:de29_wex_clapper] No public Justice Department document explains why.

In 2014 CIA officers searched computers used by the Senate Intelligence Committee's staff, who were writing the committee's report on the agency's detention and interrogation programme. The CIA's inspector general found the access improper and the director, John Brennan, apologised to the committee. An internal accountability board chaired by the former senator Evan Bayh called the search "clearly inappropriate" but found no bad faith, and no one was disciplined; the agency had meanwhile referred the committee's own staff to the Justice Department. [cite:de29_npr_ciassci] The surveillance court's repeated findings that the government had misdescribed its own programmes, and the 2023 rebuke over FBI searches described in [scene:de29_the_precedent|the first section], likewise led to rule changes rather than charges. [cite:de29_ap_fisc2023]

The paradox, argued both ways

The case for prosecution is that secrecy agreements are the condition of access, that a disclosure cannot be limited to the parts that serve the public, and that harm need not be visible to be real. The House Intelligence Committee, summarising a 36-page classified report on 15 September 2016, said Snowden's disclosures "caused tremendous damage," that most of what he took concerned military and intelligence matters of interest to adversaries rather than privacy, and that he was "not a whistleblower." [cite:de29_cnbc_hpsci] In Manning's material, the unredacted release of all the cables in September 2011, after a password published in a Guardian journalist's book let others decrypt a copy, exposed the names of people who had spoken to American diplomats in confidence; the newspapers that had worked with WikiLeaks condemned it. [cite:de29_wiki_cables] Prosecutors made the same point when Assange pleaded guilty. [cite:de29_cbs_assange]

The case for the disclosers is that the record vindicated much of what they exposed. Courts found the bulk phone-records programme unlawful and Congress ended it ([scene:de29_the_catalogue|the previous section]). Eric Holder, who as attorney general oversaw the charges against Snowden, said in 2016 that he had "actually performed a public service by raising the debate," while adding that "He harmed American interests" and "there has to be a consequence." [cite:de29_npr_holder] No death has been publicly tied to Manning's leaks: at her sentencing a brigadier general's testimony about one Afghan killed was struck because the man's name was not in the files, and Defense Secretary Robert Gates had called the consequences "fairly modest." [cite:de29_pf_mccain] At Assange's plea the judge noted that no actual physical harm had been shown. [cite:de29_db_manglona] The fair reading is narrow: no documented death, but harm unproven in public rather than disproven, since the damage assessments remain classified.

The asymmetry in outcomes is a documented fact. A deliberate double standard is not: no Justice Department document gives a reason for declining to charge Clapper or the CIA officers, and the leak prosecutions followed statutes that apply to anyone who discloses classified material, whatever their motive. The structural point stands without a motive. The law as written punishes disclosure of a programme more surely than it punishes the programme's excesses, and most of the Church-era abuses in [scene:de29_the_precedent|the first section] were ended, or first brought to account, through a disclosure of one kind or another.

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis is the Sophia platform's, not a finding: immigration enforcement as the occasion, a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination, and in this part the question of whether surveillance would be turned on Americans who object. Documented: it has been done before. Between the Second World War and 1975 five arms of the federal government watched or disrupted lawful domestic political activity under presidents of both parties, from Martin Luther King Jr. and two senators of opposite parties to the Klan and the IRS's lists of activist groups, and the proof is in the government's own reports. [cite:de29_rock_chaos] [cite:de29_nbc_minaret] [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro] [cite:de29_hnn_irs] Documented, too: much of what was exposed in 2013 still exists in some form, and the authorities least subject to Congress, the president's own Executive Order 12333 and the purchase of commercial data, are the ones that grew. [cite:de29_ap_ciabulk] [cite:de29_tc_patel] And documented: those who disclosed the programmes were punished more surely than those who misdescribed them. [cite:de29_wex_clapper] [cite:de29_cbs_winner]

Probable, as a structural inference rather than a finding of abuse: capability now outruns statute in the same places where the Church-era abuses ran, executive-only authority and purchased data, and a 702 certification that runs to March 2027 with the statute lapsed leaves Congress with less leverage, not more, until it expires. [cite:us10_bcj_702] [cite:de29_tc_patel] Capability is not intent; this says what could be done, not what is being done.

Not supported, as of October 2026: any documented current use of the catalogued NSA or CIA programmes against domestic political opponents. The most recent documented misuse, the FBI's searches of 702 data in 2020 and 2021, touched people on the left and the right, was caught by the surveillance court and was answered with rule changes. [cite:de29_ap_fisc2023] The record also runs against the destination at several points. Courts ruled the bulk phone programme unlawful; Congress ended it and let section 215 expire; Congress let 702 lapse in June 2026 rather than renew it on the executive's terms; and the surveillance court put its rebuke of the FBI on the record. [cite:us10_wiki_clapper] [cite:de29_eff_215] [cite:us10_eff_702lapse] [cite:de29_ap_fisc2023] The precedent is proven; its present repetition is not. What the law now permits, and how the walls between government datasets came down, is the subject of [scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|Part Thirty]; the consequences that would follow if the tools were turned are graded in [scene:de35_the_consequence_ladder|Part Thirty-Five], and these grades carry into [scene:de40_worst_case_scorecard|Part Forty].
