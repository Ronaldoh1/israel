The Ladder and the Map

Absolute mobility asks whether children out-earn their parents. Relative mobility asks a different question: whether the rung a child starts on decides the rung where the child ends up. A society can have low absolute mobility and steady relative mobility at once, if the whole ladder stops rising while the chances of moving up it stay the same; that is close to what the American record shows. The same research also found that the chances of climbing differ greatly from one city, and one neighbourhood, to the next, and that part of that difference is caused by the places themselves. This section sets out both findings and the evidence for each. [scene:ad3_absolute_mobility|The share who out-earn their parents]

A ladder whose rungs stayed the same distance apart

The usual measure of relative mobility ranks every parent from 1 to 100 by income, ranks every child the same way as an adult, and asks how closely the two ranks move together. The result is called the rank-rank slope. A slope of 0 would mean a child's rank had nothing to do with the parent's; a slope of 1 would mean every child landed exactly on the parent's rank. In a 2014 study using the tax records of children born from 1971 to 1993, Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Patrick Kline, Emmanuel Saez and Nicholas Turner found the slope at about 0.30 across the cohorts they could measure: 0.299 for children born in 1971–74, 0.291 for 1975–78 and 0.313 for 1979–82, with no meaningful trend. [cite:ad3_trends_aerpp] [cite:ad3_uchicago_e350] In plain terms, ten percentile points of advantage for the parents went with about three points for the child, and that relationship did not change.

The chance that a child born to parents in the bottom fifth of incomes would reach the top fifth was 8.4 per cent for the 1971 cohort and 9.0 per cent for the 1986 cohort. For children too young to have settled incomes, the authors used college attendance instead: the gap between the highest- and lowest-income families was 74.5 percentage points for children born in 1984 and 69.2 points for those born in 1993. [cite:ad3_nber_digest_trends] The authors' conclusion was that children entering the labour market at the time of the study had the same chances of moving up, relative to their parents, as children born in the 1970s. [cite:ad3_trends_aerpp]

The same authors drew the necessary qualification. Because the rungs of the income ladder had moved further apart over those decades, the same rank-to-rank pattern now meant larger differences in actual living standards. In their words, "the consequences of the 'birth lottery'" were "larger today than in the past." [cite:ad3_trends_aerpp] The odds of climbing held steady; the distance between the bottom and the top grew.

The map: where a child grows up

The national average hides very large differences between places. In a companion 2014 study of more than 40 million children born from 1980 to 1982, Chetty, Hendren, Kline and Saez measured mobility for every commuting zone, an area defined by where people live and work, roughly a metropolitan region. Nationally, a child raised in the bottom fifth had a 7.5 per cent chance of reaching the top fifth. In Charlotte the chance was 4.4 per cent; in Salt Lake City, 10.8 per cent; in San Jose, 12.9 per cent. [cite:ad3_land_opp_pub] Areas with higher upward mobility shared five traits: less residential segregation, less income inequality, better primary schools, more social capital, meaning the strength of community ties and civic life, and more stable families. The authors stated that these were correlations and did not by themselves identify causes. [cite:ad3_land_opp]

Correlation raised the obvious objection: families who choose or can afford better places may differ in ways that would help their children anywhere. Three later bodies of evidence address it. First, Chetty and Hendren followed more than seven million families who moved between areas and found that children's adult outcomes shifted toward those of children already living in the destination by about 4 per cent for each year of childhood spent there. Because the gain accrues year by year, the earlier in childhood a family moves, the larger it is. [cite:ad3_exposure_qje] Second, the federal Moving to Opportunity experiment offered housing vouchers, by random assignment, to families living in high-poverty neighbourhoods, for use in lower-poverty ones, and measured the children's outcomes nearly two decades later. Children whose families moved before they turned 13 earned 31 per cent more a year in their mid-twenties, $3,477 on a control-group average of $11,270, and were more likely to attend college; for children who moved as teenagers, the effects were, if anything, negative, which the authors attribute partly to the disruption of moving. [cite:ad3_mto_aer] [cite:ad3_jpal_mto] A widely repeated version of the finding gives the 31 per cent gain without its age condition; it applies only to moves made before 13.

Third, the Opportunity Atlas, released by the Census Bureau with Chetty, Hendren and John Friedman on 1 October 2018, mapped adult outcomes for more than 20 million Americans born from 1978 to 1983 down to the census tract, a neighbourhood of a few thousand people. [cite:ad3_nlihc_atlas] For children of parents at the 25th percentile, average household income at 35 varied by about $5,000 (one standard deviation) between tracts within the same county, and a tract's own characteristics mattered far more than those of tracts a mile away. Upward mobility was only weakly related to conventional signs of local prosperity such as job growth, and the map's estimates matched the causal effects measured in the experiment. The study was published in the American Economic Review in 2026; data for children born from 1984 to 1989 were added in 2024. [cite:ad3_atlas] The Atlas also identified "opportunity bargains", neighbourhoods with good outcomes and median two-bedroom rents under $1,500 a month. [cite:ad3_nlihc_atlas]

What in a place makes the difference

The strongest single predictor found so far is social. A 2022 study in Nature, using 21 billion friendships among 72.2 million Facebook users aged 25 to 44, measured "economic connectedness", the share of friends from higher-income backgrounds among people from lower-income backgrounds. It was among the strongest predictors of upward mobility ever identified. The authors estimated that if lower-income children grew up in counties where they had as many higher-income friends as higher-income children do, their adult incomes would be about 20 per cent higher; that estimate rests on assumptions about causation that the study tests but cannot fully prove. [cite:ad3_nature_ec] A 2024 study of children born from 1978 to 1992 pointed in the same direction: children's outcomes rose or fell with the share of parents in work in their own community, and the effects ran through the people children grew up among. [cite:ad3_changing_opp]

Some of the friction in this map is designed and some is the residue of documented departures from equal treatment. School funding tied to local property taxes is a designed feature of American public finance, and its effect on places is examined in [scene:ad7_k12_property_tax|Part Seven]. Residential segregation, one of the five correlates, is traced in [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six] in part to federal mortgage policy and lending practices that excluded Black neighbourhoods, a documented departure from equal treatment. The mobility research measures the result; it does not by itself assign the cause to either.

The same map, by race

Holding parents' income constant, Black boys grew up to earn less than white boys in 99 per cent of census tracts; among women, there was no comparable gap. The gap was smallest in low-poverty neighbourhoods with low measured racial bias among white residents and many Black fathers present, but fewer than 5 per cent of Black children grew up in such places. [cite:us8_oi_race] The researchers found that parents' marital status, education and wealth explained very little of the gap at equal incomes, and that differences in ability did not explain it either. [cite:us8a_oi] The trend, however, is toward convergence. Among children born from 1978 to 1992, Black children's adult earnings rose at every level of parental income, and the earnings gap between white and Black children from low-income families narrowed by 30 per cent; over the same years, the gap between white children from high- and low-income families widened by 30 per cent. [cite:ad3_changing_opp] Part Thirteen takes up these gaps and their documented causes. [scene:ad13_black_americans|Who gets the Dream]

Where it works as promised

In parts of the country the ladder works much as taught. A child from the bottom fifth raised in San Jose or Salt Lake City had close to the odds that the Dream implies, about one in eight or one in nine of reaching the top fifth, and the Atlas found affordable neighbourhoods with strong outcomes in many metropolitan areas. [cite:ad3_land_opp_pub] [cite:ad3_nlihc_atlas] The relative odds nationally did not worsen over two decades of cohorts, and for Black children they improved. [cite:ad3_trends_aerpp] [cite:ad3_changing_opp] The causal evidence also shows that the effect of place can be changed: when low-income families with young children moved to lower-poverty areas, those children's incomes rose. [cite:ad3_mto_aer] The record therefore does not show a ladder that is fixed everywhere; it shows one whose rungs are spaced very differently depending on where a child is raised.
