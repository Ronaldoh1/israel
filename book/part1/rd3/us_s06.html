Money, Currency and the Mandate Economy, 1922–1936

New states, it is often said, need years to build a financial system: a currency, a central bank, the machinery of public credit. When Israel declared independence in 1948 it did not start from nothing. It inherited a currency managed by the Mandate, and a banking network that the Zionist movement had built and run for half a century before the state existed. The pieces that mattered were in place by the 1920s and 1930s, the years of this chapter. This section follows them: the Palestine pound and the board in London that issued it, the movement's own banks, the growth of the Mandate's revenue and spending, the case the Jewish Agency made about the economy it had helped to create, and the oil pipeline that reached Haifa in 1934.

A currency managed from London. In 1926 the British Colonial Secretary appointed a Palestine Currency Board, and from 1 November 1927 the board issued the Palestine pound [cite:wiki_palestine_pound] [cite:israelmint_pcb_notes]. The pound was fixed at par with sterling and divided into 1,000 mils [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. The arrangement is worth stating plainly, because it explains who could act. The board was appointed by the Colonial Secretary, and the currency it issued was tied one for one to the British pound. It was a currency managed from London. No body in Palestine, Jewish or Arab, issued the country's money or set its value. The Palestine Currency Board's notes stayed in circulation until 18 August 1948 [cite:wiki_palestine_pound] [cite:israelmint_pcb_notes].

The movement's own banks. Alongside the Mandate's currency ran a separate financial structure, owned by the Zionist movement. The Jewish Colonial Trust was chartered in London in 1899. Its operating subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, was founded in 1902, was London-based, and was owned through the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_israeli_pound] [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. Together they carried the movement's finance for half a century. Their founding belongs to an earlier volume of this library; what matters here is that by the 1920s the Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine, had a bank of its own that operated in the Mandate's currency but answered to the movement's institutions.

The structure had a clear shape. The money that built the national home came from abroad, as donations to the movement's funds and as the capital that immigrants brought with them. It moved through institutions chartered in London and denominated in a currency pegged to sterling. It was invested in building, citrus and industry. The figures for that investment come mainly from the Jewish Agency itself, and they have to be read as what they are.

The Agency's case, 1937. In 1937 David Horowitz of the Jewish Agency's Economics Department prepared Economic and Social Transformation of Palestine for the Agency's case before the Peel Commission [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. It is an advocacy document, written to persuade a British commission, and its figures are the Agency's own. They are reported here as Horowitz gave them.

According to Horowitz, "LP 80,000,000 have been invested by Jews in economic enterprise" over sixteen years. Annual Jewish investment in building, he wrote, rose from LP 900,000 in 1924 to LP 7,000,000 in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. He gave citrus exports rising from £200,000 in 1921 to £2,700,000 in 1934, and the area under citrus growing from 30,000 dunams in 1922 to 260,000 in 1935. Industrial output, in his figures, rose from under LP 1,000,000 in 1921 to over LP 8,000,000 in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937].

He gave the population too. In his account there were 84,000 Jews in 1922, 13 percent of the population, and 375,000 in 1935, 28 percent; the non-Jewish population was 565,000 in 1922 and 975,000 in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. His Jewish figure for 1922 is close to the census count of 83,794. His share is not. The 1922 census counted 757,182 people in all and put Jews at about 11 percent [cite:jvl_census_1931]. Horowitz's 13 percent rests on a smaller non-Jewish base of 565,000, and the source does not explain how that base was drawn. The country as the census counted it is described in [scene:cen_s01|the opening section of this chapter].

The Mandate's own accounts. The Mandate government grew along with the economy. Horowitz gave government revenue as LP 2,371,000 in 1922–23 and LP 5,452,000 in 1934–35, and expenditure rising over the same years from LP 1,900,000 to LP 3,200,000 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. On his figures the administration took in more than it spent in both years, and its revenue more than doubled while its spending grew by about two-thirds. The cost of holding the country militarily was a separate account. The British garrison cost £1.5 million in 1923–24 [cite:league_report_garrison], more than three-quarters of the civil spending of 1922–23, and it was paid by Britain. The full ledger of what the Mandate cost Britain, to its end in 1948, is told in [scene:gf_s02|Charity for an Army].

The rows of that table for these years tell the story in brief: Mandate civil spending of £P1.9 million in 1922–23 and £P3.2 million in 1934–35 [cite:horowitz_1937]; the garrison's £1.5 million in 1923–24 [cite:league_report_garrison]; and, set beside them, the Agency's estimate of £P80 million of Jewish capital invested between 1921 and 1937 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. The figures come from different payers and different kinds of account, and they are shown side by side for scale, not added together.

Who gained: the dispute. Horowitz went further than investment totals. He argued that Arabs gained directly from Jewish economic activity: from Jewish contributions to government revenue, from land prices that had risen 400 to 500 percent, and from falling death rates in the districts of Jewish immigration. He set daily wages of 10–15 piastres in Palestine against 2–3 in Iraq and Egypt [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. This is the strongest form of the Zionist economic case, and it was made in the movement's own words, to a British commission, for a political purpose.

A British official had reached a different conclusion seven years earlier. Sir John Hope Simpson, reporting in October 1930, found that Jewish National Fund leases required Jewish labour only, that a landless Arab class was forming, and that families displaced by land sales had not been resettled [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal_l]. On his account the benefits of Jewish investment did not flow freely across the population; the institutions that directed it were built to employ Jews, and some of the cost fell on Arab cultivators who lost their land. His figures, and the Agency's rebuttal of them, are set out in [scene:rd3_land|the section on land and labour].

The two positions are not strictly contradictory, since one speaks of average wages, prices and revenue and the other of the people displaced and excluded. But the sources used here do not measure how gains and losses were distributed among Arabs, and neither Horowitz's figures nor Hope Simpson's settle that question. Each was produced for a purpose, the first to argue for the national home before Peel, the second to advise the British government on land and immigration. The record supports neither the claim that Jewish investment benefited everyone nor the claim that it benefited no one.

Oil to Haifa. One more flow of these years ran through Palestine without passing through either the Mandate's budget or the movement's banks. The Iraq Petroleum Company built a pipeline from Iraq to the Mediterranean between 1932 and 1934. At Haditha it split into two branches, one to Tripoli and one to Haifa [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline]. The transit agreements with Palestine, Transjordan, Lebanon and Syria had been signed between 5 January and 25 March 1931 [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline]. The first oil reached Haifa on 16 October 1934, and official opening ceremonies were held in January 1935. The line could carry four million tons a year [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline]. Palestine had become a transit country for the oil of a neighbouring state, with a terminal on its coast. The arrangement lasted until 1948, when Iraq stopped sending oil to Haifa [cite:rs_rd3_gulbenkian_pipeline].

Where it led. The banking structure built in these years carried the state through its birth. Before independence the Anglo-Palestine Bank's chairman, Eliezer Hoofien, persuaded the American Bank Note Company in New York to print new notes. Because neither the state's name nor its currency had been decided, they were denominated in "Palestine pounds." According to Israel's state mint, the notes were not marked "legal tender," and the printer's name was left off, to get around the need for the US Secretary of State's approval to print foreign currency [cite:israelmint_aplb_series] [cite:jhom_anglo_palestine_notes]. They reached the country secretly in July 1948 and became legal tender by law on 17 August 1948, the day before the Mandate's notes went out of circulation [cite:israelmint_aplb_series] [cite:jhom_anglo_palestine_notes] [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. On 1 May 1951 the bank's assets passed to Bank Leumi le-Israel, and the currency became the Israeli pound [cite:wiki_israeli_pound].

The common assumption, then, has it backwards for this case. The state did not have to build a financial system after 1948. One already existed: a London-chartered bank that had operated for decades, inside a currency system the British had set up in 1926–27. What 1948 required was a change of name and of sovereign, and the first notes for that change were printed in New York.

What remains open. The record here is strong on structure and weaker on totals. The investment, output and export figures for 1922–1936 come from a single advocacy document, and no independent series in the sources checks them. The sources do not give the Anglo-Palestine Bank's own balance sheets, its lending, or how much of the Agency's £P80 million passed through it. They do not say how the Currency Board's reserves were held, or what the peg to sterling cost or gained the country. And they do not measure how the gains of the Mandate economy were divided between Jews and Arabs, or among Arabs themselves, which is the question on which Horowitz and Hope Simpson most sharply disagree.
