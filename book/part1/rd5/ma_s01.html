Deir Yassin, the Hadassah Convoy and Tantura

Two opposite simplifications have shaped popular memory of the killings of 1948. One holds that no massacres happened. The other holds that only one side suffered them. The documented record supports neither. It also shows something about how such records are made: much of what is now known emerged over decades, and a good deal of it came from Israeli historians, students and veterans themselves.

Deir Yassin was a village west of Jerusalem. On 9 April 1948 it was attacked by the Irgun and Lehi, supported by the Haganah and Palmach. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] [cite:aljazeera_deir_yassin_75] For decades the death toll was given as 254. Current scholarship puts it at about 110, generally within a range of 107 to 120, and many of the dead were women, children and elderly people. A Red Cross representative reported seeing some 150 bodies in a cave. The historian Benny Morris wrote that the killings were followed by mass Arab flight. Material on Deir Yassin in Israeli military archives remains classified, so the record is still incomplete. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The revision of the toll downward is itself part of the history: the larger figure was cited for decades before current scholarship narrowed it.

Four days later came a killing on the other side. On 13 April, Arab forces attacked a convoy bound for Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus and killed 78 Jews, most of them doctors and nurses. The attack was a reprisal for Deir Yassin. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The two events are bound together in time and in cause, and an honest account cannot tell one without the other.

Tantura shows how slowly some parts of the record surfaced. The coastal village was captured by the Alexandroni Brigade on 22 and 23 May 1948. A master's thesis by Theodore Katz recorded testimony of killings after the village had surrendered. Veterans sued him for libel, and he retracted. [cite:npr_tantura] [cite:jewishindependent_tantura] The matter did not end there. In the 2022 Israeli documentary Tantura, veterans in their nineties described killings and a mass grave near what is today the popular Dor Beach. A document shown in the film acknowledges that soldiers dug a mass grave. Other veterans deny that any massacre took place, and the number killed cannot be established. [cite:npr_tantura] [cite:jewishindependent_tantura] What stands on the record, then, is testimony from participants, a document about a grave and continuing denials, without a firm count.

Taken together, the three episodes resist both simplifications. Massacres did happen in 1948, and they were committed against Arab villagers and against Jews in the same weeks. The Deir Yassin killings came amid the two-sided civil war that preceded the invasion by the Arab states ([scene:s09b|the two escalations]), and Deir Yassin was followed, in Morris's account, by mass Arab flight.

The way this evidence emerged deserves notice. A death toll was corrected by scholarship. A student's thesis was retracted under legal pressure and later echoed by veterans speaking on camera. Archives remain partly closed. The record of 1948 is still being written, and much of it has been written by Israelis examining their own history.
