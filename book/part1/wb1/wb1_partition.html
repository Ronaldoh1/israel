The Plan of 1947

Two states, six pieces. On 29 November 1947 the UN General Assembly adopted, by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, a plan to divide Palestine into a Jewish state and an Arab state, joined in an economic union, with Jerusalem under international rule. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] Each state was to consist of three sections linked at two crossing points. The Arab state was to include western Galilee, the hill country of Samaria and Judea, excluding Jerusalem, and the coastal strip from Isdud to the Sinai frontier. The Jewish state was to include eastern Galilee, the Jezreel Valley, most of the coastal plain, and the Negev. [cite:wb_ej_partition] Most of the hill country assigned to the Arab state became the West Bank. [cite:wb_wiki_westbank]

Land and people. The figures most cited for the plan come from two sides of the debate and agree on its shape. The Palestinian historian Muhsin Yusuf gives the Jewish state 56 percent of the land and the Arab state 43 percent. The Jewish state would hold about 498,000 Jews and about 494,000 Arabs, and the Arab state about 725,000 Arabs and 11,000 Jews. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] The Jewish Virtual Library, citing the historian Howard Sachar, gives the Jewish state about 5,500 square miles with 538,000 Jews and 397,000 Arabs, and the Arab state 4,500 square miles with 804,000 Arabs and 10,000 Jews. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] When the plan was adopted, Jewish buyers legally owned about 6.6 percent of Palestine's land. A further 46 percent was state domain, much of it held on hereditary lease or of undetermined ownership. [cite:wb_wiki_landpurchase] By Yusuf's figures, Jews owned about a tenth of the land inside the Jewish state they were allotted. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] A Pakistani amendment to cap land ownership at 10 percent per group was voted down, 22 to 8. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181]

Desert, and the valleys. The quality of the land is argued from both sides. The Institute for Palestine Studies says the Jewish state received about two-thirds of the coast, including Haifa and Tel Aviv, "and the fertile lowland plains," and the plan gave it the Jezreel Valley and most of the coastal plain. [cite:wb_palquest_partition] [cite:wb_ej_partition] The Jewish Virtual Library answers, citing the scholar Aharon Cohen, that about 60 percent of the Jewish state was Negev desert, "while the Arabs occupied most of the agricultural land." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] A British inquiry of 1930 had found that only about 40 percent of the hill country could be cultivated. [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal] The two cases read the same map: the Jewish state's share took in most of the Negev and most of the coastal lowland; the Arab state's was chiefly hill country, with western Galilee and the southern coast.

Acceptance and rejection. The Jewish Agency accepted the plan. Jewish leaders were dissatisfied with the territory and with Jerusalem's separation, and the Jewish Virtual Library says they "welcomed the compromise." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected it. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Iraq, Syria and Yemen called it contrary to the UN Charter, illegal and immoral, and said they would not feel bound by it. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] Yusuf sets out the Arab reasons as he reads them: the fear of later expulsion and land confiscation, and the injustice of granting statehood to a largely immigrant minority while the majority was denied self-determination. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] The library's volume The Road to 1948 tells the story of the vote and the plan in full. [scene:s09|The partition vote]
