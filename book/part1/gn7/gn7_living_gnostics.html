The Living Gnostics: Manichaeans and Mandaeans

The Gnostic schools of the second century faded, but the outlook they shared, salvation through knowledge in a world governed by lesser powers, did not die with them. It became the basis of two religions with their own scriptures, clergy and communities. One spread from Mesopotamia to China and was destroyed. The other survives today, and its recent history is a reminder that the suppression of these traditions is not only ancient history.

The first was founded by Mani, a prophet of the third century in the Persian empire, whose religion taught a cosmic struggle between light and darkness and the liberation of the light trapped in matter. It was organised in two ranks. The Elect took strict vows, abstaining from meat and wine; the Hearers, the ordinary believers, supplied them with food and supported them. [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] Augustine of Hippo, later the most influential theologian of the Latin church, was a Hearer for about nine years before he converted. [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] The Roman state treated the religion as a foreign threat long before it became Christian: Diocletian's edict of 302 ordered the Manichaean leaders burned together with their writings. [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] Christian bishops continued the hostility. Cyril of Jerusalem warned his students that "the Manichaeans also wrote a Gospel according to Thomas". [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4]

The second religion is the one that lived. The Mandaeans, whose sacred language is Mandaic, a form of Eastern Aramaic, are, in the words of the Wikipedia article on them, the last surviving representatives of Gnosticism. They revere John the Baptist as their greatest teacher, practise repeated baptism in flowing water, and keep their own scripture, the Ginza Rabba. [cite:gn_wiki_mandaeans] [cite:vt24_wiki_mandaeans] Their origins are disputed. Their survival for many centuries in Iraq and Iran, as a small minority under successive empires, is not.

That survival nearly ended in this century. Before the US-led invasion of 2003 there were about sixty to seventy thousand Mandaeans in Iraq. In the violence that followed, around ninety per cent of them were killed or fled; members of the community, many of whom worked as goldsmiths, were kidnapped and murdered, and some were forced to convert. By 2007 about five thousand remained, and one estimate in 2019 put the number at three thousand. [cite:gn_wiki_mandaeans] In Iran, where estimates of their numbers vary widely, they have no legal protection from persecution, and a law of 1985 restricts their participation in civic life. [cite:gn_wiki_mandaeans] Most Mandaeans now live in exile: between ten and twenty thousand in Sweden, ten to fifteen thousand in Australia, four to seven thousand in the United States. The worldwide total is perhaps sixty to a hundred thousand. [cite:gn_wiki_mandaeans]

The Mandaeans' story belongs in this book because it shows the other face of the history traced so far. The Nag Hammadi texts were preserved by burial and recovered by accident; their power to unsettle was intellectual. The Mandaeans preserved their tradition by living it, and their danger came not from councils or lists but from war, which destroyed in a few years a community that had lasted for centuries. No institution decided that their knowledge should end. The collapse of order around them nearly ended it anyway.
