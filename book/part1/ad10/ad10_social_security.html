Social Security: The Floor That Held

If the 401(k) is the part of retirement where the taught promise and the record have drifted furthest apart, Social Security is the part where they sit closest together. It is the one income most Americans can count on in old age that does not depend on an employer's choice, on stock prices or on how long they live, and its record against poverty is the strongest of any programme examined in this book. Its difficulties are real, but they are financing problems with known sizes and dates, not the disappearance that is often predicted.

How it was built

President Franklin Roosevelt signed the Social Security Act on 14 August 1935. The first payments, in 1937, were one-time lump sums; the first monthly benefit, a cheque for $22.54, went to Ida M. Fuller on 31 January 1940. Amendments in 1939 added benefits for dependants and survivors, and in 1956 for workers with disabilities. In 1961 the earliest age at which anyone could claim a reduced retirement benefit was set at 62. In 1972 Congress made benefits rise automatically each year with consumer prices, starting in 1975, and created Supplemental Security Income for older, blind and disabled people with very low incomes. [cite:ad10_aarp_sstl]

The programme is financed mainly as it goes. Workers and employers each pay a payroll tax on wages up to a yearly ceiling; that money pays current retirees, and any surplus is lent to the Treasury and held in two trust funds, one for old-age and survivors insurance and one for disability. In 2025, 91.3 per cent of the programme's income came from payroll taxes, 4.0 per cent from income tax that higher-income retirees pay on their benefits, and 4.8 per cent from interest on the trust funds. [cite:ad10_crs_tr26] A worker's benefit is based on his or her own earnings record, and the yearly cost-of-living adjustment keeps it from losing value to inflation, the protection the federal minimum wage lacks. [cite:ad10_aarp_sstl] On 14 October 2026 the Social Security Administration announced a 3.6 per cent increase for 2027, raising the average monthly benefit from about $2,088 to about $2,163; more than 76 million people receive Social Security or SSI. [cite:ad10_napa_cola27]

The record against old-age poverty

In 1959, the first year the Census Bureau's official poverty series covers, 35.2 per cent of Americans aged 65 and over were poor. The rate was 28.5 per cent in 1966, 24.6 per cent in 1970 and 15.3 per cent in 1975, after the large benefit increases of the early 1970s and the start of automatic adjustments. It fell to about 10 per cent by the mid-1990s and has stayed near there since: 9.9 per cent in 2024. [cite:ad10_census_a3] On the official measure the rate fell by more than two-thirds, from 35.2 to 9.9 per cent.

The poverty table alone cannot say how much of that fall Social Security caused; Medicare from 1965, SSI from 1972, and the pensions of the post-war generation all arrived in the same years. The Census Bureau's supplemental poverty measure, which counts benefits and taxes as income, gives the programme's present weight directly: in 2025 Social Security moved 28.8 million people out of poverty, more than any other programme. [cite:us18_census_p60290] This is the plainest case in the volume of a mechanism that does what it was taught to do.

The rules that were changed: retirement age and the 1983 rescue

The programme's last major overhaul came when its reserves were running low. On 20 April 1983 President Ronald Reagan signed amendments based on the recommendations of the National Commission on Social Security Reform. They raised the age for a full, unreduced benefit, then 65, in two stages to 67, beginning with people born in 1938 and completed for those born in 1960 or later, who reach 67 in 2027; claiming at 62 remained possible with a larger reduction. They made up to half of benefits taxable for retirees whose income exceeded $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (married), delayed one cost-of-living increase by six months, brought forward scheduled payroll-tax increases, and brought new federal employees, members of Congress and the President into the system from 1984. [cite:ad10_ssa_1983] [cite:ad10_aarp_sstl]

The higher retirement age is a benefit cut by another name: claiming at any given age now pays less than it would have under the old rules. It was enacted openly, phased in over decades, and fell on every worker born after 1937, which is what distinguishes it from the hidden erosion of a frozen dollar threshold. It is a case of designed change, not captured friction.

The cap on taxable wages

The payroll tax stops at a ceiling. In 2026 no Social Security tax is due on wages above $184,500; in 2027 the ceiling will be $192,300. [cite:ad10_napa_cola27] Because benefits are also calculated only on wages up to the ceiling, the cap is a designed feature of an earned-benefit programme, not a loophole. What has changed is its reach. The 1977 amendments raised the cap faster than the automatic formula so that about 90 per cent of all wages in covered jobs would be taxed, and in 1982 that was the share. By 2021 it had fallen to 81 per cent, because earnings above the cap grew faster than earnings below it, even though only about 6 per cent of workers earn more than the ceiling. [cite:ad10_crs_base] In plain terms, the growing share of the nation's pay going to its highest earners has moved a growing share of pay outside the programme's tax base. A worker earning $60,000 pays the tax on every dollar; one earning $1 million pays it on less than a fifth, since $184,500 divided by $1,000,000 is 0.18.

The trust-fund date

The programme's trustees report each year on its finances. Their 2026 report, released on 9 June 2026, projects that the old-age and survivors fund will run out of reserves in the fourth quarter of 2032, and the combined old-age, survivors and disability funds in the third quarter of 2034; after that, continuing tax income would pay about 83 per cent of scheduled benefits, falling to 65 per cent by 2100. The disability fund alone is not projected to run out. [cite:ad10_aarp_sstl] [cite:ad10_crs_tr26]

The long-term shortfall widened from 3.82 per cent of taxable payroll in the 2025 report to 4.42 per cent. The trustees gave three main reasons: a lower assumed birth rate (1.75 children per woman rather than 1.90), lower projected immigration, which means fewer future workers paying in, and P.L. 119-21, the 2025 budget law signed by President Donald Trump, which reduces the programme's income from the tax on benefits. [cite:ad10_crs_tr26] The 4.42 per cent figure is the size of the problem in the trustees' own units: closing it over 75 years would take the equivalent of a payroll-tax increase of that many points, an equivalent cut in benefits, or a mix, including changes to the cap. [cite:ad10_crs_tr26] [cite:ad10_crs_base]

A widely repeated claim holds that Social Security "will not be there" for younger workers. The trustees' projections say otherwise: without any change in law, the programme would still pay roughly four-fifths of promised benefits after 2034 and about two-thirds at the end of the century, because payroll taxes do not stop when the reserves do. [cite:ad10_crs_tr26] The real risk is an automatic, across-the-board cut of about 17 per cent in 2034 if Congress does not act, which for the average benefit announced for 2027 would mean about $370 a month (17 per cent of $2,163). [cite:ad10_napa_cola27] The remedies on offer are the ones the 1983 law used: more revenue, such as raising or removing the cap, which the Congressional Research Service notes could reduce the long-term deficit, or lower benefits, such as a later retirement age or slower growth for higher earners. [cite:ad10_crs_base] No such package had been enacted when the trustees reported in June 2026, and none has been since, as of October 2026.

Social Security also cannot carry a retirement alone, and was not designed to. The average benefit of about $2,088 a month in 2026 comes to about $25,000 a year (12 × $2,088).
