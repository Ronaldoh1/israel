Jesuit Universities: Counting Them and Asking Who Governs Them

The Society of Jesus is the religious order most often named in claims about Catholic influence over education, and the figure attached to it varies with the speaker. The order itself is described in Part Eight; this section deals only with its universities. [scene:vt8_jesuits|Part Eight: the Jesuits] Two questions run through it: how many Jesuit universities there are, which turns on definition, and who governs them, which turns on documents that changed in the late 1960s.

The count

The most recent count by the Jesuit networks themselves is the second edition of a printable map, "Jesuit World of Universities and Schools (2024)", produced by the International Association of Jesuit Universities (IAJU), the Jesuit Global Network of Schools (JGNS) and Educate Magis and published on 30 October 2024. It lists 177 universities in the IAJU, 937 schools in the JGNS, 2,127 schools of Fe y Alegría, a popular-education movement, and 388 schools of the Jesuit Refugee Service: 3,629 institutions in all. [cite:vt13_em_map2024] The map calls its figures a snapshot, noting that institutions join or leave the networks each year. [cite:vt13_em_map2024] The IAJU was founded in 2018 with the approval of the Superior General, Arturo Sosa, and is organised in six regional associations, from AUSJAL in Latin America to the Kircher Network in Europe and the Association of Jesuit Colleges and Universities (AJCU) in North America. [cite:vt13_javeriana_iaju]

Other figures circulate. The earlier sources behind this volume give about 190, "over 200" and "250+" universities; the brief for this part used 228. [scene:vt1_how_to_read|Part One: how to read this volume] Wikipedia's list of Jesuit educational institutions, which carries no date, gave a total of 189 colleges and universities when checked for this volume. [cite:vt13_wiki_jesuitlist] No source was found for 228. The differences are mostly definitional. The IAJU counts members of its regional associations; a looser count may include institutions founded by Jesuits but no longer run by them, Jesuit faculties within other universities, or colleges that are not universities. Historical totals are larger still: the historian Paul Grendler, cited on the same list, counted over 700 Jesuit colleges and universities in Europe by 1749 and about a hundred elsewhere, before the order's suppression in 1773. [cite:vt13_wiki_jesuitlist] For the present, the defensible figure is 177 IAJU members in 2024, stated with its definition. [cite:vt13_em_map2024]

The American figure has a similar history of error. Several earlier sources say there are 28 Jesuit colleges and universities in the United States. The AJCU does have 28 members, but they are in 17 states, the District of Columbia and Belize: 27 are in the United States, and the 28th is St. John's College in Belize City, which joined in July 2020, the year after Wheeling Jesuit University ended its affiliation with the order. [cite:vt13_ajcu_home] [cite:vt13_ajcu_about] [cite:vt13_jesuits_stjohns] Three Canadian institutions are associate members. [cite:vt13_ajcu_home] As of October 2026 the association's own page gave the same count. [cite:vt13_ajcu_home]

Notable institutions

Georgetown University, in Washington, D.C., describes itself as "the oldest Catholic and Jesuit institution of higher learning in the United States." It was established in 1789 by John Carroll, the first Catholic bishop in the United States and a former Jesuit, the same year the federal government under the Constitution began. [cite:vt13_gu_history] Its presidents were Jesuits until 2001, when John J. DeGioia became its 48th president and its first lay one. [cite:vt13_gu_history] Boston College received its charter in 1863 and opened on 5 September 1864, at the request of Bishop John Bernard Fitzpatrick and after years of fund-raising by the Jesuit John McElroy; Jesuits ran it as presidents, teachers and administrators. [cite:vt13_bc_jesuithist]

The Jesuit institution with the closest tie to the papacy is in Rome. Ignatius of Loyola opened a school of grammar, humanities and Christian doctrine there in 1551; Gregory XIII built it a new seat, inaugurated in 1584, and the university calls him its "founder and protector"; in 1873, after the Italian state had expelled its faculties from the Collegio Romano, Pius IX gave it the name Pontifical Gregorian University. [cite:vt13_unigre_history] [scene:vt2_roman_question|Part Two: the Roman Question] As recently as 2006 Benedict XVI confirmed that the university is entrusted to the Society of Jesus. [cite:vt13_unigre_history] A pontifical university of this kind is one of the "ecclesiastical" universities whose statutes the Apostolic See approves under canon 816; most Jesuit universities elsewhere are not. [cite:vt13_cic_803]

Who governs them

The governance of the large American Catholic universities changed in a few years at the end of the 1960s. At Notre Dame, founded by the Congregation of Holy Cross rather than the Jesuits, the order's superior general approved in January 1967 a plan to transfer the university's property and governance to a two-tier board of lay and religious trustees and fellows, after Theodore Hesburgh had sought and received written permission from Rome. [cite:vt13_nd_hesburgh] The same year a meeting of Catholic university leaders sponsored by Notre Dame at Land O'Lakes, Wisconsin, issued a statement, drafted by the Jesuit Neil McCluskey, that a Catholic university must have "true autonomy and academic freedom" in the face of authority, lay or clerical, from outside the academic community. [cite:vt13_wiki_landolakes] At Boston College a new board of Jesuit and lay trustees took legal responsibility for the university in 1972. [cite:vt13_bc_jesuithist]

Rome's answer came in 1990. John Paul II's apostolic constitution Ex corde Ecclesiae, issued on 15 August 1990, set general norms for Catholic universities and has been read as a repudiation of the Land O'Lakes position. [cite:vt13_usccb_excorde] [cite:vt13_wiki_landolakes] The American bishops approved an application of it for the United States on 17 November 1999; the Congregation for Bishops granted the required approval on 3 May 2000, and the norms took effect as particular law on 3 May 2001. [cite:vt13_usccb_excorde] They require Catholics who teach theology at a Catholic university to hold a mandatum from the diocesan bishop, ask that boards have a Catholic majority and faculties a majority committed to the faith "to the extent possible", and ask that the president be Catholic. [cite:vt13_usccb_excorde] The text also limits the instrument: the mandatum is "not an appointment" or approval of a professor's teaching, it is the professor's personal responsibility rather than the university's, and no fixed percentage of Catholic trustees or faculty is set. [cite:vt13_usccb_excorde]

The record therefore shows three distinct layers of authority over a Jesuit university, which claims of a single chain of command tend to merge. The Society of Jesus sponsors it and, in many cases, supplies some of its leaders; a board of trustees, usually mixed and often mostly lay, holds it in civil law; and the local bishop, under norms approved in Rome, has a defined say over the teaching of theology and the use of the name Catholic. [cite:vt13_bc_jesuithist] [cite:vt13_usccb_excorde] [cite:vt13_cic_803] No document found for this volume shows the Holy See or the Superior General directing the admissions, appointments or research of these universities outside those channels. Part Eight treats the separate myth that the Superior General, the so-called Black Pope, commands the pope himself. [scene:vt6_black_pope|Part Six: the "Black Pope"]
