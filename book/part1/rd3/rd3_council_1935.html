The Last Offer Before the Revolt: Wauchope's Council, 1935–1936

Five weeks after al-Qassam's death, Britain made the last political offer of the stage. On 23 December 1935 the High Commissioner for Palestine, Sir Arthur Wauchope, called in the leaders of the Jewish community and laid before them a plan for a legislative council. It would have 28 members: 14 Arabs, 7 Jews and 7 Britons. The government, he told them, was "determined to establish the assembly" [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. It was not the first such offer: an earlier one, made in 1922, is told in [scene:rd2_council|the council of 1922–1923]. Thirteen years later the question behind it was still open. Who would have a voice in governing Palestine, in what proportion, and over which questions?

The demand for an elected body had come, from the beginning, from the Arab side. From 1919 the Palestine Arab congresses had asked Britain for a national government responsible to a representative assembly chosen by the country's inhabitants [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress]. As recently as December 1934 Raghib al-Nashashibi's new National Defense Party had called for parliamentary government [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi]. The Zionist Congress, meeting at Lucerne in 1935, had taken the opposite view, resolving against any council that would fix the Jews as a minority [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. Wauchope's offer therefore tested both sides at once, and the way each answered it is the best single view of where Palestinian and Zionist politics stood at the end of 1935.

The terms mattered as much as the numbers. As reported at the time, the basis of representation was to be fixed for five years; one of the elected Arab members was to be a Bedouin; the High Commissioner could nominate members to fill the seats of any community that boycotted the elections; and the council could not deal with the labor immigration schedule [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. That last exclusion went to the center of the conflict. The schedule, allotted in half-year periods, had just been cut from 8,000 places for April–September 1935 to 3,250 for the half-year that followed [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d531]. It was also only one channel of entry: in the peak year of 1935 fewer than one immigrant in four came under it, as the immigration section shows [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d531]. A council barred from the labor schedule would have had no hand on the immigration of workers, a question the Arab congresses had raised since 1919, when they rejected mass Jewish immigration [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress]; and the High Commissioner's power to fill a boycotting community's seats meant that refusing to take part would not stop the council from sitting.

The Zionist answer was reported the next day. Chaim Weizmann rejected the plan as contrary to the Mandate, citing the resolution of the Zionist Congress at Lucerne that such a council would fix the Jews as a minority. Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, speaking for the Vaad Leumi, concurred, and so did A. L. Maleh for the Sephardi community [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. The case behind the rejection is best put in its own terms. The Zionist reading of the Mandate held that Britain's obligation ran not only to the Jews already in Palestine but to the Jewish people as a whole; the British government itself had put it that way in 1931, calling the Mandate "an undertaking to the Jewish people and not only to the Jewish population of Palestine" [cite:rs_rd3_macdonald_unispal]. On that reading, a legislature apportioned by the population of the moment and frozen for five years would hand a permanent majority a veto over the growth that the Mandate was meant to permit, at the very time that growth was fastest. The Jewish Agency's own figures, prepared for its case to the Peel Commission and so to be read as advocacy, put the Jewish population at 84,000, or 13 percent, in 1922 (the census put that year's share at about 11 percent) and 375,000, or 28 percent, in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937] [cite:jvl_census_1931]. Seven seats of 28 was a quarter.

Not every Jewish voice gave the same answer. Rabbi Moshe Blau, speaking for Agudath Israel, said only that the time was "not yet ripe" [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. Agudath Israel had been founded in 1912 as a movement of Torah-observant Jews that held itself apart from secular Jewish nationalism [cite:unpacked_neturei_karta_history]. Its answer was a postponement, not a rejection on the Zionist ground of minority status.

The Arab answer was slower and less unanimous. Arab leaders withheld their reply and objected to women voting; the Mufti of Jerusalem, Hajj Amin al-Husseini, was reported to be still opposed [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_council]. The arithmetic of the offer allows the Arab objection to be reconstructed in outline, though the sources here do not record it in the leaders' own words. By the Jewish Agency's figures for 1935, the non-Jewish population was 975,000 against 375,000 Jews [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937], roughly seven in ten of the inhabitants; the council would have given Arabs half its seats, with British officials holding a quarter and the High Commissioner able to fill boycotted seats, and it would have had no say over immigration under the labor schedule. To a movement that had asked since 1919 for a government responsible to a representative assembly, it offered half the seats of a body with no hand on immigration under the labor schedule. To the Zionist leadership, the same body offered the majority too much. The record held here does not give the detailed arguments of the Arab parties, and it should not be assumed that all of them shared the Mufti's position. Some, as events showed, did not.

The decision did not rest in Jerusalem. On 24 March 1936 the Prime Minister, Stanley Baldwin, announced a debate on the council in the House of Commons, and the Colonial Secretary, J. H. Thomas, said that final action would wait for it. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported that day that Jewish organizations were "unitedly opposed" and that some Arab groups were accepting [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1936_commons]. The chain of authority is plain in the record: the High Commissioner proposed, the Colonial Secretary held final action, and a Commons debate could hold it up. The research behind this book does not establish what the Commons debate concluded, and this account does not guess.

The offer's most lasting effect was on Palestinian unity. By late 1935 the five Arab parties had formed a united front, which the American Consul General described as a response to a "national emergency" [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512]. In April 1936 the same consul reported that the council proposals had broken that front, and that "all thought of united Arab action" on the council had been abandoned [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512_m]. On 5 May 1936, according to the Center for Israel Education, the Mufti and other Arab leaders declined to attend a conference in London on the proposal [cite:rs_rd3_cie_mufti_council]. By then events in Palestine had overtaken the council entirely; what followed belongs to [scene:s07a5|the Arab Revolt].

A common way of telling this period holds that one side or the other simply refused every chance at shared government. The record of 1935–36 is more specific. The offer was put to Jewish leaders in a fixed form; the Zionist leadership rejected it in principle; Agudath Israel deferred; the Arab leadership hesitated and divided, and the Mufti was reported opposed; and the decision was in any case held in London. Several things remain unsettled by the sources available here: what the Commons debate decided, what reasons the Arab parties that accepted gave, how the women's-suffrage objection weighed against the others, and whether the council would ever have sat had the country not moved into revolt.

The council's failure is a fitting place to close this stage, because it showed the structure that fourteen years of building had produced. At the top stood the British state. The Mandate had been in force, by the League Council's own minutes, since 29 September 1923, a date that Professor John Quigley argues had no legal force, so that Britain held Palestine without title [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_mandate_force] [cite:rs_rd3_quigley_2026]. Under it the High Commissioner proposed policy, allotted the labor schedule in half-year periods, and answered to the Colonial Secretary and, through him, to Parliament.

Beside the Mandate government, the Jewish community had built the institutions of a state in all but sovereignty. The Jewish Agency, enlarged in 1929 to give half the seats on its Council to non-Zionists, with Keren Hayesod as its main financial instrument [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14], was chaired from 1935 by David Ben-Gurion [cite:wiki_histadrut]. The Histadrut, founded in 1920 and restricted to Jews, ran its own construction company, bank and health fund [cite:wiki_histadrut]. The Haganah, placed under the Histadrut's direct control in December 1920, had since 1931 been led by a six-member national command with parity between three Histadrut members and three non-labor members; its urban branches had memberships "in the hundreds," and it ran workshops producing hand grenades [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. Outside this structure, and against it, stood the Revisionists. Their New Zionist Organization had met in Vienna in September 1935, said to have been "elected by 713,000 voters," with Ze'ev Jabotinsky as president [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists], and the Irgun had existed since 1931, formed by Avraham Tehomi's group with Revisionist groups after their secession from the Haganah [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. The Jewish population, by the Agency's count, had grown from 84,000 to 375,000 in thirteen years [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]; Tel Aviv alone had about 120,000 people in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_telaviv].

The Palestinian Arab side had no comparable apparatus and no recognized representative body. The Arab Executive, never recognized by Britain, had dissolved in 1934, the year Musa Kazim al-Husseini died [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]. In its place stood parties built around rival families and rival programs: Nashashibi's National Defense Party, founded in December 1934 and close to Emir Abdullah [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi]; the Palestine Arab Party, established on 27 March 1935 under Jamal al-Husseini, close to the Mufti [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_jamal]; and the Istiqlal, announced in August 1932, which named British imperialism as the main enemy but never built a large membership [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_hajibrahim] [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_istiqlal]. In late 1935 the five parties stood briefly together, and outside all of them a Haifa preacher had taken a band to the hills and died there, as told in [scene:rd3_qassam|the section on al-Qassam].

Read as a whole, the architecture at the end of 1935 was lopsided in a specific, documented way. One community had a funded agency, a labor federation with its own economy, an armed organization under shared command and a rival one outside it, and, by its own agency's count, a population that had more than quadrupled since 1922. The other had a national movement of long standing, a demand for representative government it had pressed since 1919, five parties that could unite only briefly, and no institution that Britain recognized as speaking for it. Britain held the decisions, and had just offered one that neither side would take on its terms. That was the structure that entered 1936.
