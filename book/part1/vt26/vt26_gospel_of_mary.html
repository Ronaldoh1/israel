The Gospel of Mary: A Woman Teaches the Apostles

The Gospel of Mary is often described as one of the Nag Hammadi texts. It is not. It reached modern scholars half a century before the jar was opened, in a different codex, and it is the only early gospel named after a woman. What survives is less than half of the original, but the surviving pages contain the sharpest scene in any early Christian text about who had the right to teach after Yeshua was gone: Peter and Andrew refuse to believe a woman, and another disciple tells Peter he is wrong. This section sets out what the text is, what it says in order, what it teaches, and what the institution said and did about Mary Magdalene, the woman most scholars take it to be about. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary]

What it is

The main copy is in the Berlin Codex, catalogued as Papyrus Berolinensis 8502 and also called the Akhmim Codex. The German scholar Carl Reinhardt bought it in Cairo in January 1896. The dealer's account was that it had been found wrapped in feathers in a niche in a wall at a Christian burial site near Akhmim in Upper Egypt. It is a single-quire papyrus book in Sahidic Coptic, copied in the early fifth century or possibly the late fourth. The Gospel of Mary is its first text; the others are the Apocryphon of John, the Sophia of Jesus Christ and a short Act of Peter. Carl Schmidt presented the codex to the Prussian Academy of Sciences on 16 July 1896, but the full edition of its Gnostic texts did not appear until Walter Till's of 1955. It is held today in the Berlin papyrus collection. [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex]

Two small Greek fragments show that the text was copied in its original language a century or more earlier: Papyrus Rylands 463, published in 1938, and Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 3525, published in 1983, both from the third century. They add little to the Coptic text, but they fix a latest possible date. Karen King places the composition in the early second century, noting that the author seems to know the Gospel of John and perhaps Paul's letters; Christopher Tuckett places it in the first half of the second century; others prefer the second half. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary]

The loss is severe. Pages 1 to 6 and 11 to 14 of the Coptic codex are missing, so the text opens in the middle of a conversation and breaks off again in the middle of Mary's vision. The scholarly bibliography on it is now large: King's The Gospel of Mary of Magdala (2003), Esther de Boer's study of 2004 and Tuckett's Oxford edition of 2007 are the standard works. [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary] Quotations below follow Mark M. Mattison's translation, which he has placed in the public domain, cited by codex page. [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary]

Outline

The surviving text falls into five parts. First, the lost opening, which most scholars reconstruct as an appearance of the risen Saviour. Second, his final teaching (pages 7 to 9): the disciples ask about matter and sin; he answers, gives them parting instructions and leaves. Third, the disciples' grief (page 9): they weep and fear that they will be killed as he was, and Mary comforts them and turns their hearts "toward the Good". Fourth, Mary's account of her vision (pages 10 to 17), which includes a teaching on how a vision is seen and, after the four-page gap, the ascent of a soul past hostile powers. Fifth, the dispute (pages 17 to 19): Andrew and Peter reject her account, Mary weeps and answers, Levi rebukes Peter, and the disciples go out to preach. [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary] [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary]

What it teaches

On matter, the Saviour teaches that every created thing will return to its own root. On sin, he gives the text's most quoted answer: "Sin doesn't exist, but you're the ones who make sin" when they act in accordance with what he calls the nature of adultery, meaning a mixing of what does not belong together. Sin here is not a debt recorded against a person and cancelled by a priest; it is a confusion that a person produces and can stop producing. [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] De Boer reads these ideas against Stoic and Jewish parallels rather than a developed Gnostic myth; the NASSCAL entry likewise notes Stoic ideas on the dissolution of matter. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary]

His parting words carry three instructions. He warns them against anyone who says the Saviour is "here" or "there", "because the Son of Humanity exists within you" (other translations render it "Son of Man"). He tells them to go and preach the gospel. And he adds a limit: "Don't lay down any rules beyond what I've given you, nor make a law like the lawgiver, lest you be bound by it." [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] The first instruction stands close to the canonical saying on the kingdom "within" or "among" you, whose translation is discussed in [scene:vt20_yeshua_record|Part Twenty]. The third is the passage most often cited for the view that some early Christians resisted the growth of church law; it is one text's instruction, and how far its readers lived by it is not recorded. Part Thirty-One sets it beside the institution's precepts. [scene:vt31_sunday_and_law|Sunday and the law]

Mary's vision turns on a teaching about perception. She asks whether a person sees a vision through the soul or the spirit; the Saviour answers that it is seen through the mind, which lies between them, and earlier tells her that "where the mind is, there is the treasure". [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] After the gap, the soul climbs past a series of powers that try to hold it. Desire claims it never saw the soul descend; Ignorance tries to judge it; the fourth power takes seven forms, named Darkness, Desire, Ignorance, Zeal for Death, the Kingdom of the Flesh, the Foolish "Wisdom" of Flesh and the "Wisdom" of Anger. The soul answers each and goes free, declaring that "what binds me has been killed" and that "ignorance has died". [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] The ascent is reached by the soul's own understanding; no rite, office or intermediary appears in it.

The dispute: where the text cuts against authority

The scene that follows is why the text matters to this book. Peter had opened the exchange by asking Mary to speak: "Sister, we know the Savior loved you more than all other women", so tell us what you remember that we have not heard. When she finishes, Andrew says: "I myself don't believe that the Savior said these things", because the teachings "seem like different ideas". Peter goes further and asks whether the Saviour would really have spoken with a woman privately, without their knowledge, and adds: "Will we turn around and all listen to her? Did he prefer her to us?" Mary weeps and asks whether he thinks she invented it or is lying about the Saviour. [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary]

Levi answers for her. "Peter, you've always been angry," he says, and now he is treating the woman as an adversary; "if the Savior made her worthy, who are you then to reject her?" The Saviour, Levi adds, knew her well and loved her more than them. He tells them to be ashamed, to put on the perfect human and to preach as they were told, without laying down rules beyond what the Saviour gave. The text ends: "they started to go out to teach and to preach." [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary]

Two challenges sit in this scene. One is about revelation: Mary's authority rests on a private vision, not on public commissioning, and Andrew rejects it because it is unfamiliar. The other is about gender: Peter objects that she is a woman. Karen King reads Peter and Andrew as voicing second-century positions that rejected both esoteric revelation and women's teaching authority, and the text as taking Mary's side against them. Tuckett also reads Mary as a model who fulfils the Saviour's role. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] Peter is the apostle from whom the bishops of Rome later traced their succession, and the text gives the last word against him to another disciple. The same conflict between Peter and Mary recurs in the Gospel of Thomas and Pistis Sophia, which Esther de Boer cites as evidence that the Mary of these texts is the Magdalene. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary]

The identification is not unanimous. Most scholars, including King, take the Mary of this gospel to be Mary Magdalene. Stephen Shoemaker and F. Stanley Jones have argued for Mary the mother of Yeshua, and one writer has proposed a sister of Yeshua named Mary. Richard Valantasis, who accepts the Magdalene reading, adds that nothing in it supports the modern claim of a marriage. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary]

What the institution said about the text

The record here is thin, and it should be stated precisely. No surviving church council, papal letter or heresy catalogue names the Gospel of Mary. The sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum, which lists dozens of rejected books and consigns their authors and followers to anathema, names gospels of Thomas, Peter, Matthias, Barnabas, Bartholomew and Andrew, but not one of Mary. [cite:vt26_tert_decretum] [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] The nearest ancient reference is hostile and probably concerns a different book: Epiphanius of Salamis, in the 370s, described a "Greater Questions of Mary" used by a group he called the Borborites, a name meaning "the filthy", and attributed obscene content to it. That text does not survive, and its contents are known only from his attack. [cite:vt26_nasscal_qmary] [cite:vt26_wiki_panarion] The Gospel of Mary fell out of copying for reasons the record does not show; it was not, on present evidence, condemned by name.

What the institution said about Mary Magdalene

The canonical record of Mary Magdalene is substantial. Luke lists her among women who supported Yeshua from their own means and says seven demons had gone out of her. Mark, Matthew and John name her at the crucifixion. All four gospels name her at the empty tomb, and in John she is the first to see the risen Yeshua and is sent to tell the others. [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene] That is the basis on which later writers called her "apostle of the apostles".

In a homily given in Rome in about 591, Pope Gregory I joined three women into one: the unnamed sinner of Luke 7 who anointed Yeshua's feet, Mary of Bethany, and Mary Magdalene. He read the seven demons as a figure for every vice: "what are these seven demons, if not the universality of all vices?" [cite:vt26_pearse_greg33] [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene] The homily calls her a sinner in the city; it does not use the word prostitute, but it ties her sins to the flesh, and the image of the penitent prostitute that dominated Western art grew from this reading. [cite:vt26_pearse_greg33] [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene] The Eastern churches did not make the conflation and honour her as a myrrh-bearer "Equal to the Apostles". [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene]

The Roman calendar revised in 1969 separated the three women: the 22 July memorial now refers to Mary Magdalene alone, and Mary of Bethany is commemorated with Martha on 29 July. [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene] A popular claim holds that the Church "declared her a prostitute in 591" and reversed itself in 1969 because of the Nag Hammadi find. The homily was a sermon, not a declaration; the Gospel of Mary was published from the Berlin Codex in 1955, not from Nag Hammadi; and no document links the 1969 change to either text.

On 3 June 2016 the Congregation for Divine Worship issued a decree, at the "express wish" of Pope Francis, raising her memorial to a feast, the same rank as the apostles. Its secretary, Archbishop Arthur Roche, explained it with the title "apostolorum apostola", citing Rabanus Maurus and Thomas Aquinas, and acknowledged that Western tradition after Gregory had identified her with the anointing woman and the sister of Lazarus. [cite:vt26_va_feast2016]

The question the Gospel of Mary raises, whether a woman may hold teaching authority among the apostles, has a present-day answer from Rome that does not refer to the text. John Paul II's apostolic letter Ordinatio Sacerdotalis of 22 May 1994 declared that "the Church has no authority whatsoever to confer priestly ordination on women" and that this judgment "is to be definitively held", resting the case on Christ's choice of "the twelve men" as the foundation of the Church. [cite:vt26_va_ordsac1994] A commission chaired by Cardinal Giuseppe Petrocchi, whose report Pope Leo XIV had made public on 4 December 2025, ruled out women deacons as a grade of holy orders by seven votes to one, while stating that the question did not yet allow a definitive judgment "as is the case with priestly ordination". [cite:vt26_vn_petrocchi2025] As of October 2026 that remains the position. The institution has thus raised Mary Magdalene's liturgical rank to the apostles' while keeping ordained authority male, and the Gospel of Mary's scene, in which Peter doubts a woman's teaching and is rebuked for it, is the ancient text that bears most directly on that choice.
