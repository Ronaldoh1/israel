1948: How the Strip Was Made

No one planned the Gaza Strip. The UN partition plans of 1947 assigned the southern coastal plain to a prospective Arab state, and the war that followed decided what was left of it. [cite:gz_golan_2024] The strip is the ground the Egyptian army still held when the fighting stopped. Its boundary with Israel was fixed in the armistice of 1949, which adopted a line set out in a UN memorandum of November 1948 and required Israeli forces to pull back from Beit Hanun; the historian Arnon Golan argues that the line was finally delineated only by a local agreement in 1950. [cite:gz_armistice_text] [cite:gz_golan_2024] This section follows the war in the south from the first attacks to the armistice, and sets out what that line was, and was not, in law.

The first months. Fighting in the south began before the British left. By April 1948 Arab forces had surrounded Kfar Darom, and on 10 April a battalion of Muslim Brotherhood volunteers from Egypt attacked it and was beaten back with heavy losses. When the Mandate ended and Egypt's regular army crossed the frontier on 15 May, the outpost was among its first targets; the attack was repelled, with Egyptian losses given in Israeli accounts as 70 killed and 50 wounded. [cite:gz_wiki_kfardarom] Egypt was one of the Arab states whose forces entered Palestine that day, with Syria, Iraq and Lebanon, while Transjordan's forces intervened in areas assigned to the Arab state or to Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] Kfar Darom held out until the night of 8 July, when the Israeli command approved its evacuation; Egyptian troops entered it the next day and found it empty. [cite:gz_wiki_kfardarom]

A Palestinian government in Gaza. In September 1948, with the Egyptian army holding the southern coast, the Arab League agreed to an Arab government for all of Palestine with its seat in Gaza. It was announced on 20 September; on 22 September the Arab Higher Committee declared Palestine independent under the All-Palestine Government headed by Ahmad Hilmi Abdul Baqi, and a Palestinian National Council meeting in Gaza on 30 September adopted a provisional constitution. Egypt recognized the government first, followed by Iraq, Syria, Lebanon and Saudi Arabia, among others; King Abdullah of Transjordan refused. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] The historian Avi Shlaim argues that Egypt sponsored it chiefly to block Abdullah's plan to join Arab Palestine to his kingdom, and calls it "a phantom deliberately created by the Arab states, with Egypt at their head"; after Israel's October offensive its ministers were moved to Cairo. [cite:rs_rd6_shlaim_apg_1990] The library's account of [scene:rd6_arab_palestine|what became of Arab Palestine in 1948] follows the rival claims in full.

Operation Yoav. The decisive fighting came in the autumn. On 15 October 1948, during the second truce, which had no fixed end, Israeli forces launched Operation Yoav, designed to break the Egyptian line that cut the Negev off from the north. According to the Institute for Palestine Studies' encyclopedia, drawing on Benny Morris, Israeli troops provoked Egyptian fire on a supply convoy to open the offensive, and artillery and aircraft were used to "soften up" villages before they were occupied. By the time the operation ended on 4 November, Israeli forces had broken the Egyptian line and occupied most of the villages of the Gaza district. [cite:gz_palquest_yoav] Egyptian troops remained besieged at al-Faluja until the armistice. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] Al-Majdal, a town of about 10,000 residents before the war, was bombed in October and captured on 4 November; about 1,000 of its Arabs remained. [cite:gz_stein_majdal] In the course of the war, tens of thousands of Palestinians fled or were expelled into what became the strip. [cite:gz_wiki_history]

The armistice line. Egypt and Israel signed a general armistice at Rhodes on 24 February 1949, one of four agreements Israel concluded with its neighbours that year; Egypt kept the strip, as Jordan kept the West Bank. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] The agreement separated the two armies along a demarcation line, and both parties declared that the line was a military arrangement and not an international border. [cite:gz_wiki_history] The library's account of [scene:rd6_armistices|the 1949 armistices] sets out their terms. What the line enclosed was the remnant of a district: Israel held all of the Beersheba subdistrict and most of the Gaza subdistrict, and the rest, about 361 square kilometres or 1.3 percent of Mandate Palestine, became the Gaza Strip. [cite:gz_palquest_refugees] Other sources give 365 square kilometres. [cite:gz_golan_2024] A year later, on 22 February 1950, Egyptian and Israeli officers meeting at El Auja agreed a modus vivendi dividing the Gaza–Rafah area into patrol zones, to reduce friction between soldiers and villagers. It stated that it was "of a purely local character" and changed neither side's position on the demarcation line. [cite:gz_un_modus] The historian Arnon Golan, who traced the strip's formation from 1947 to that agreement, describes the result as an "allegedly provisional" entity whose formal status was never determined. [cite:gz_golan_2024]

The record therefore settles how the strip came to be: not by any plan for it, but as the ground one army still held when the war stopped, inside a line both sides insisted was temporary. What it left unsettled, from the first day, was what the strip was: neither part of Egypt nor of Israel, nor the seat of a state that functioned.
