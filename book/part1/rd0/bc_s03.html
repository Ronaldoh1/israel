The Pope's Bankers: Torlonia, the Rothschilds and the Papal Loans, 1794–1870

London was not the only sovereign borrower the Rothschild houses served. A common assumption holds that the papacy has always managed its money through its own institutions, apart from any private banking family. For the nineteenth century the record points the other way. Before 1870 the Pope was also the ruler of a territorial state, the Papal States, and like other rulers of his time he borrowed. One lender was a Roman banker whom the papacy ennobled. Then came the Rothschild houses. The arrangement between them, and the hostility it drew, are a small but revealing part of the European board on which Palestine's future would later be decided.

This section rests on a narrower base of evidence than most in the chapter, and the reader should know that from the start. Almost every detail of the papal loans below reaches this book through a single reference article on the Rothschild loans to the Holy See, which in turn relies on Niall Ferguson's history of the house of Rothschild. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] The specific terms of the 1831 arrangement, its reported exclusivity clause, the 1834 episode and the 1906 encyclopedia phrase have not been independently checked for this book. The accounts also contradict one another at several points. Those contradictions are set out below rather than resolved.

The story begins with Giovanni Torlonia, who lived from 1754 to 1829. According to the account summarized here, he was born to modest circumstances in the Auvergne region of France and built one of the largest banking fortunes in Rome through cloth trading, money-lending and papal patronage. He opened his banking house in Rome in 1780. By the 1790s he had become the Holy See's principal banker, providing loans, managing financial transactions and handling the papacy's increasingly complex needs during the Napoleonic period. Pope Pius VI made him a noble in 1794, on 9 March according to the date the account gives from the Archivio di Stato, as Don Giovanni Torlonia, 1st Prince of Civitella Cesi. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see]

The mechanism here is plain. The banker supplied the papacy with financial services, and the papacy supplied the banker with aristocratic standing. The account describes the exchange as mutually reinforcing: the Torlonia house became the papacy's main financial institution and gained the social legitimacy of papal nobility at the same time. The French Revolutionary period enlarged its role. When French forces occupied Rome in 1798 and 1799 and papal finances were thrown into disorder, the account says, Torlonia's house managed the transfer of papal funds and lent the money that kept the papacy's operations going through the occupation and the restoration that followed. By Napoleon's final defeat in 1814, the family was established as the papacy's principal financial manager. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see]

The papacy also stood at the center of an older Roman aristocracy. The Orsini and the Colonna, two of its oldest families, had held the ceremonial office of Prince Assistant to the Papal Throne in alternation since 1735. The Orsini had produced three popes: Celestine III in 1191, Nicholas III in 1277 and Benedict XIII in 1724. None came in the modern era. [cite:wiki_black_nobility] [cite:wiki_orsini_family]

In 1823 the new banking family married into this old one, when Giovanni's son Alessandro Torlonia married an Orsini. The bride's name is itself recorded two ways in the same account: once as Maria Luisa Orsini, and once as Teresa Orsini. The account cites the evidence of the marriage as Rome diocesan records, the Orsini family genealogies held in the Archivio Segreto Vaticano and in private family archives, and Pompeo Litta's Famiglie celebri d'Italia (1819–83), a record of the great Italian noble lineages. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] It goes on to interpret the match: Alessandro needed the legitimacy that only centuries-old noble blood could provide, while the Orsini needed to keep their finances afloat after the disruptions of the Napoleonic years, and the marriage was arranged, as such marriages were, with both families' positions in mind. In this telling, the timing of the Rothschild connection eight years later was "not accidental." That is the account's own interpretation. No document quoted in it shows the marriage being made with the later banking arrangement in view.

The Rothschilds entered Roman finance in 1831. The reference account explains why. After the Napoleonic wars and the Restoration, the Papal States were in fiscal distress. The Torlonia house lacked the capital to meet the papacy's needs at the scale now required. And the Rothschild houses were, by 1830, the dominant private banking operation in Europe. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] On 30 November 1831, according to the account, Alessandro Torlonia co-signed a contract in Rome with James Mayer de Rothschild of the Paris house. The account's short summary describes the contract as giving the Rothschilds an exclusive right of first refusal on all papal sovereign debt, by a clause kept secret. Its more detailed description, drawn from Ferguson, describes the terms differently, as exclusive management of papal loan issues and key papal financial operations through the London house of Nathan Mayer Rothschild and the Paris house of James Mayer de Rothschild, with the Torlonia house as local intermediary. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] A right of first refusal and the exclusive management of loans are not the same thing. The record available here does not settle which description fits the contract, because the contract itself is not quoted.

The loan that followed is the part the account itself calls documented. In 1832 the Rothschilds lent £400,000 to Pope Gregory XVI. The loan was negotiated through Carl Mayer von Rothschild, with James acting as the official papal banker. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] According to the account, a letter from Gregory XVI dated 15 January 1832 acknowledges the Rothschilds' help in terms that confirm the relationship. Who repaid the loan, over what term and at what rate are not stated in the sources available for this section. Nor do they say from what revenues of the Papal States it was serviced.

The episode of 1834 is where the sources diverge most sharply, and the two versions cannot both be true as told. In one, the 1834 affair was the enforcement of the secret clause. A cardinal named Tosti tried to refinance the papal debt with six other banks, and Carl Mayer de Rothschild traveled to Rome, produced the original contract and blocked him. In the other, Antonio Tosti was not a cardinal refinancing the debt but the papal treasurer (elsewhere, the papal envoy), and he had embezzled papal funds. The Rothschild houses' records of their dealings with the papacy could be compared against the papacy's own accounts, the discrepancies revealed the embezzlement, and the Rothschild correspondence, as Ferguson describes it, refers to the affair and to the banking relationship's role in uncovering it. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] The second version comes with an interpretation: that the papacy's own financial management could not detect fraud by its own treasurer, that the external auditor, however informal, was the Rothschild house, and that the papacy's finances before any internal institution existed, before the Vatican bank, the IOR, was founded in 1942, depended on this outside relationship. That reading depends on which version of 1834 is right, and the record available here does not decide it.

The question of which Rothschild house was the counterparty is itself unsettled. The account's summary names James Mayer de Rothschild of the Paris house as the signatory in 1831. Its detailed description names the London and Paris houses together. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] A separate explanatory treatment in this book's research names the Paris house and the Naples house of the Rothschilds as the lenders, and in another version names the London house, N M Rothschild & Sons. What the account does show is that Carl Mayer and James both appear in the record of the 1832 loan.

The same explanatory treatment offers an analogy for the relationship, given here as an analogy, not a finding. Imagine a family that takes out one loan from one lender in a crisis. From then on, whenever money is needed, everyone simply calls the same number. Nobody decides it each time. It was decided once, long ago, and the number stays taped to the wall. The analogy's authors insist that this is not a conspiracy but the way long-lived institutions prefer to work: a relationship that is expensive to set up and hard to replace, so that the first lender becomes the default for every later need. As a description of institutional habit it is useful. As history it claims more than the sources show, because it assumes a continuity of lending that the record of this period does not document beyond the loans of the 1830s.

The relationship was public knowledge by the early twentieth century. The Jewish Encyclopedia, published by Funk & Wagnalls between 1901 and 1906, and compiled, according to the account, by about 400 Jewish and non-Jewish scholars, describes the Rothschilds in its "Rothschild" entry as "guardians of the papal treasure." [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] The account reads the phrase in strong terms: as proof that the connection was publicly known by 1906, was not treated as secret by the parties, and was understood as a custodial relationship in which the Rothschilds managed papal assets rather than simply lending. The phrase itself has not been checked against the encyclopedia for this book. Even if it is exact, a single phrase in a reference work describes a reputation. It does not show what assets, if any, were held, or on what terms.

The loans drew hostility from Catholic critics, and the record is clear about its character. Catholic criticism at the time, as a remark by Alfred de Vigny shows, centered on the Rothschilds' Jewishness. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] Objection to the terms of a loan, or to a sovereign's debts, is a political argument. Objection to a lender because he is a Jew is antisemitism, and this book names it as such. The documented business was a set of loans to an indebted state. The trope built on top of it is a different thing.

The territorial state that did the borrowing ended in 1870, with the fall of the Papal States. The Roman families who stayed loyal to the Pope afterwards, the Black Nobility, including the Orsini and the Colonna, distanced themselves from the new Italian monarchy in a specific act of protest: they kept the doors of their palaces closed in mourning. [cite:wiki_black_nobility] From this point the papacy was a church without a state, and it remained so until 1929. The settlement that later compensated it for the lost territory, and what it did to the Black Nobility's court positions, belong to 1929 and are told in [scene:vat_s00d|a later volume].

The same account draws a line from these loans to the present. It runs from the 1831 arrangement through the Torlonia family's rental of their Roman villa to Mussolini (from 1923 in one account and from 1925 in another, until 1943) to the appointment in 2026 of François Pauly, a former chief executive of the Edmond de Rothschild group, as president of the supervisory board of the Vatican bank, dated 25 March 2026 in one account and 28 April 2026 in another. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] Those events lie far outside this chapter and are told in [scene:bc_s08|a later volume]. The discipline that applies to them applies here too: each is separately documented, nothing in the record shows that they form one continuous project, and the 2026 appointment is an institutional adjacency, a former executive of a bank that bears the Rothschild name, not a family tie.

The documented core is modest and real: the 1832 loan of £400,000 to Gregory XVI, and the religious hostility it drew. What the record available here does not establish is the exact text of the 1831 contract, whether it contained an exclusive clause and of what kind, which house signed it, what happened in 1834, and whether "guardians of the papal treasure" meant more than a reputation. Each of those rests, for now, on a single reference account and its reading of one historian, and each awaits a check against the documents themselves.
