The Basket, Item by Item

A general price level hides the items that matter most to a given family. This section takes the household budget apart: food, cars and phones, where cross-country price levels exist; childcare, college and health, where each country's own system decides most of the cost; fuel and debt; and finally the closest honest version of a family's monthly budget then and now. Cross-country comparisons here use the OECD's purchasing-power price levels, which compare what the same basket costs in each country, not market exchange rates. [cite:us14_toi_food52]

Price levels: Israel among the dearest in the OECD

In 2022 Israel had the highest overall price level of any OECD member, 38 per cent above the member average, and its food and beverage prices were 52 per cent above the developed-country average in the data the OECD released in 2024, second only to South Korea. Dairy and eggs were 64 per cent above average, meat 64 per cent, bread and grains 49 per cent and cars 52 per cent, the highest in the OECD. One category ran the other way: telecommunications were 30 per cent below the average, while the United States was among the dearest for telecoms at 51 per cent above. [cite:us14_toi_food52] In its 2025 Economic Survey of Israel the OECD ranked Israel fourth for comparable price levels, gave a whole chapter to the cost of living, and warned that it "continues to reduce welfare and spur social tensions". [cite:us14_toi_oecd2025] [cite:us14_oecd_survey2025]

The OECD's explanation is mostly about the system that brings goods to market. It cites Israel's distance from its trading partners and tense relations with its neighbours, difficult border procedures, complex regulatory standards and agricultural tariffs, and recommends lowering tariffs on vegetables, fruit and dairy, cutting red tape at the border and negotiating new trade agreements. [cite:us14_ecoscope] [cite:us14_oecd_survey2025] Concentration compounds it: in a 2016 Brookings analysis, Dany Bahar found Israeli manufacturing, including food producers, about 70 per cent more concentrated than the OECD average and services about twice as concentrated, with low import competition to offset it. [cite:us14_brookings_bahar] Part Eleven sets out the 2013 law that tried to break up Israel's business groups. [scene:us11_concentration|Concentration]

Phones: the reform that worked

The one category in which Israel is cheap is the result of a deliberate policy. In 2011–12 the communications ministry under Moshe Kahlon pushed through a reform to bring competition into the mobile market. By the minister's own figures, presented to the cabinet in December 2012, the average price per minute fell from NIS 0.42 to NIS 0.10, average monthly spending per user fell from NIS 145 in 2010 to NIS 93, and the public saved NIS 5.7 billion in two years. [cite:us14_globes_kahlon] Bahar, writing independently, put the fall in average mobile bills at over 70 per cent. [cite:us14_brookings_bahar]

Childcare and college: where each system sets the price

Israel made preschool free for three- and four-year-olds after the 2011 protests. Following the Trajtenberg Committee, the Knesset extended the Compulsory Education Law to all children of those ages from the 2012–13 school year, and the Finance Committee added NIS 1.3 billion to the Education Ministry's budget to start building the classes. [cite:us14_taub_preschool] [cite:us14_jpost_saar] The saving to families was smaller than the law suggests: by the Taub Center's study, average parental payment for three- and four-year-olds fell 11.5 per cent, and household spending per child aged two to five only 3 per cent. [cite:us14_taub_preschool] The United States has no national free preschool; its childcare costs, subsidies and parental leave are compared in Part Eighteen, which uses the Labor Department's childcare price database. [scene:us18_family|Part Eighteen: family policy]

College is set nationally in Israel and by each state and institution in America. The Council for Higher Education links tuition to the consumer price index; for 2025–26 a bachelor's degree at the Hebrew University cost Israeli citizens NIS 12,017 a year. [cite:us14_huji_tuition] In the United States the average published tuition at a public four-year college for in-state students was $11,950 in 2025–26, and $45,000 at a private non-profit; in 2025 dollars the public figure had about doubled from $5,940 in 1995–96. Grants change the picture: after grant aid, the average in-state public student paid about $2,300 in net tuition in 2025–26, down from a peak of $4,450 in 2012–13. [cite:us14_cb_trends2025] [cite:us14_ncan_cb] Neither figure includes living costs; Part Seventeen compares the systems in full. [scene:us17_higher|Part Seventeen: universities]

Health: universal coverage, rising private payments

The two health systems put costs on families in different ways. Israel's 1995 law covers every resident through the health funds, but the private share of national health spending has grown: it was 39.9 per cent in 2012, when the public share was 60.1 per cent in Israel, 76.1 per cent across the OECD and 47.8 per cent in the United States. Real private health spending per Israeli rose 69.5 per cent from 1995 to 2011, against 20.8 per cent for public spending, and about 80 per cent of Israelis held supplementary insurance. Out-of-pocket payments alone were 2.05 per cent of GDP in Israel in 2011, more than in the United States (1.80 per cent) or the OECD average (1.47 per cent). [cite:us14_taub_health2014] American families pay mainly through premiums: the average employer family plan cost $26,993 in 2025, of which the worker paid $6,850, and premiums rose 26 per cent in five years against wage growth of 28.6 per cent; the average deductible for single coverage was $1,886. [cite:us14_kff_ehbs2025] [cite:us14_rise_kff] The Taub figures are a 2011–12 snapshot; Part Sixteen brings both systems' spending to 2024. [scene:us16_systems|Part Sixteen: health systems]

Fuel, energy and debt

Energy was the most volatile item in 2026. American energy prices rose 20.4 per cent and petrol 34.2 per cent in the year to September 2026, while Israeli fuel prices fell 3.1 per cent in June alone. [cite:us14_bls_cpi_sep26] [cite:us14_globes_cpi_june26] This book did not rebuild a comparable fuel-price series for the two countries, and the snapshot figures in the simulation's comparison came from aggregators converted at exchange rates; they are not used.

Debt is where America's system carries more risk. American households owed $18.77 trillion in the second quarter of 2026: $13.12 trillion in mortgages, $1.26 trillion on credit cards, $1.71 trillion in car loans and $1.65 trillion in student loans, with 4.7 per cent of all debt in some stage of delinquency. [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] Israeli households owed about NIS 890 billion at the end of the third quarter of 2025, NIS 644 billion of it for housing, which was growing about 8 per cent a year. [cite:us14_boi_debt_q3_2025] Set against each economy, by figures Globes compiled from two data services, household debt was 42.3 per cent of GDP in Israel and 69.2 per cent in the United States in 2024. [cite:us14_globes_mortgagegdp]

A family's budget, then and now

The only honest way to build a monthly budget over time is from each country's expenditure survey, and the two surveys differ: America's counts the average "consumer unit", Israel's the average household, and both report means, not medians. In the United States, the average consumer unit in 1984, of 2.6 people, had pre-tax income of $23,464 and spent $21,975, about $1,830 a month; housing took 30.4 per cent of spending, transport 19.6, food 15.0, insurance and pensions 8.6, health care 4.8 and education 1.4. [cite:us14_bls_ce1984] In 2024 it had pre-tax income of $104,207 and spent $78,535, about $6,545 a month; housing took 33.4 per cent, transport 17.0, food 12.9, insurance and pensions 12.5, health care 7.9 and education 2.0. [cite:us14_bls_ce2024] Housing, health and pensions grew as shares; food and transport shrank.

Israel's survey runs back to 1979–80, but this book could verify only recent points. In 2010 the average household spent NIS 13,496 a month on consumption, 35 per cent of it on housing and home maintenance; for the poorest fifth, housing and food together took half of spending, for the richest a third. [cite:us14_globes_hes2010] The 2023 consumer price index, whose weights the CBS takes from the survey, gave housing 26 per cent, food 18.3 per cent and transport and telecoms 17.5 per cent. [cite:us14_reuters_cpiweights] The categories are not the same as America's, and a price-index weight is not the same as a survey share, so the shares are read within each country, not across. Food's weight in Israel's index (18.3 per cent) is higher than its share of American spending (12.9 per cent), which fits the price levels above, but the difference in definitions means the gap should not be read as exact.
