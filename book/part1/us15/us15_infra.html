Water, Power, Transit and Disasters

Infrastructure is where the difference in scale between the two countries matters most. Israel is a small, dry, densely settled state with a national water carrier and a state-owned electricity utility; the United States is a continent whose water, power and roads are run largely by state, local and private bodies. The comparison below therefore concerns how each system is organised and how it has performed over time, not which country has more. It covers water, electricity and climate policy, public transport, and the response to disasters, including war damage in Israel.

Water: Israel's turn to the sea

Israel's water is run nationally. Mekorot, the state-owned bulk supplier, operates the National Water Carrier and supplies about 70 per cent of all water and 80 per cent of drinking water; a Water Authority created by a 2006 amendment to the 1959 Water Law regulates the sector. [cite:us15_wiki_water] Over two decades the state changed where its drinking water comes from. Large seawater desalination plants opened at Ashkelon in 2005, Palmachim in 2007, Hadera in 2009, Soreq in 2013 and Ashdod in 2015, and a second Soreq plant of 200 million cubic metres a year in March 2025. Desalination supplied about 35 per cent of drinking water in 2014 and 86 per cent of drinkable water in 2022. Almost 90 per cent of treated sewage is reused, mainly on farms. [cite:us15_wiki_water] The result is a supply largely independent of rainfall, paid for through progressive household tariffs that rose by more than 30 per cent in the years before 2013. [cite:us15_wiki_water]

The national grid does not serve everyone under Israel's control on the same terms. Under the 1995 Interim Agreement Israel sold the Palestinian Authority 52 million cubic metres in 2011, and the agreement bars Palestinians from drilling new wells in the shared Mountain Aquifer. [cite:us15_wiki_water] Part Nine sets out the two legal systems in the West Bank under which that allocation runs. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] In the United States the American Society of Civil Engineers (ASCE), which grades the country's infrastructure every four years, gave drinking water a C-minus and wastewater a D-plus in 2025. [cite:us15_asce_2025]

Electricity and climate policy

Israel's power system was rebuilt around offshore gas. The Tamar field was discovered in 2009 and Leviathan began producing in late 2019; coal's share of generation fell from 52 per cent in 2013 to 17 per cent in 2023. [cite:us15_ita_israelenergy] By 2024 natural gas supplied 71 per cent of electricity, coal 14 per cent and solar and wind 14 per cent, up from 12 per cent in 2023. The government had set a 20 per cent renewable target for 2025, and an analyst at BDO estimated it would reach about 16 per cent; the coal phase-out date set in 2019 for 2025 had slipped to 2027 at the earliest. [cite:us15_calcalist_renew] A government decision of October 2020 targets 30 per cent renewable electricity by 2030. The state-owned Israel Electric Corporation still produced 56 per cent of electricity in 2023 and holds a monopoly of transmission and distribution, after a 2018 reform opened generation to private producers. [cite:us15_ita_israelenergy]

American utility-scale generation in 2025 came about 41 per cent from natural gas, 18 per cent from nuclear plants, 17 per cent from coal and 24 per cent from renewables, chiefly wind (11 per cent), solar (7 per cent) and hydropower (6 per cent). Renewables had supplied about 12 per cent in 1990. [cite:us15_eia_mix2025] Federal climate policy has reversed with each change of administration. The United States joined the Paris Agreement, left it on 4 November 2020 under President Trump, and, having rejoined, left again on 27 January 2026 under an order he signed on 20 January 2025; on 7 January 2026 the administration also announced withdrawal from the UN climate convention itself. The Inflation Reduction Act, signed on 16 August 2022, had been projected to help cut emissions to about 40 per cent below 2005 levels by 2030. [cite:us15_harvard_paris]

Israel's climate policy has been slower to reach statute. The OECD's environmental review, released on 31 May 2023, found Israel not on track to meet its greenhouse-gas targets: emissions rose from 2014 to 2020 and fell afterwards, mainly because gas replaced coal, and renewables were 5 per cent of total energy supply against an OECD average of 12 per cent. It urged the Knesset to adopt "the government-approved draft Climate Law with its binding targets". [cite:us15_jpost_oecd_env] This book did not confirm whether that law had been enacted by October 2026. Over time, American fossil-fuel carbon dioxide emissions per person fell from about 21 tonnes in 2000 to about 13.6 tonnes in 2024, by the European Commission's EDGAR database. [cite:us15_wm_co2_us] The same database reports Israel together with the Palestinian territories, so its per-person figures are not Israel's own and are not used here. [cite:us15_wm_co2_il]

Transit and roads

Israel's crowded centre has paid for decades of delay in mass transit. The State Comptroller reported in December 2025 that congestion in the Tel Aviv area cost about NIS 10 billion in lost time in 2018 and would cost NIS 25 billion by 2040, that road service there was at the lowest grade, and that public transport's share of trips had fallen from 24 to 20 per cent against a target of 40 per cent. [cite:us15_comptroller_metro] Tel Aviv's first light-rail line, the Red Line, opened on 18 August 2023 after a private concession awarded in 2006 was revoked in 2010 and the project nationalised; it runs 24 kilometres and carries about 100,000 riders a day, with the Purple and Green lines due from 2027 to 2030. [cite:us15_wiki_tlvlr] A metro, authorised by a 2021 law at an approved cost of NIS 177 billion in 2025 prices, is scheduled to open its first phase in 2034; the Comptroller found its drilling surveys running behind. [cite:us15_comptroller_metro]

The United States has the opposite problem: an enormous road network in poor repair and thin transit outside a few cities. ASCE's 2025 report card gave the country an overall C, its highest grade since the report cards began in 1998 and up from C-minus in 2021, crediting the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021. It graded transit D, roads D-plus, bridges C and energy D-plus, and estimated a $3.7 trillion gap between planned and needed investment, up from $2.59 trillion four years earlier. [cite:us15_asce_2025]

Disasters and war damage

Federal disaster relief in the United States runs through the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), which pays states and households after a presidential disaster declaration. The administration has moved to redesign it. A FEMA Review Council created by executive order in January 2025 approved its final report on 7 May 2026. It recommended raising the damage thresholds for federal aid, replacing site-by-site reimbursement with up-front lump-sum grants to states, sliding-scale cost shares, and a single payment for survivors whose homes are uninhabitable. It backed away from a 50 per cent staff cut that drafts had proposed; the Government Accountability Office said earlier staff reductions had worsened FEMA's workforce problems. Many of the changes would need legislation. [cite:us15_crs_fema]

Israel's largest recent disasters have been wars. About 60,000 residents of the north were displaced after October 2023, and about half had not returned by March 2025. [cite:hz7_afp_north_return] Damage to homes and property from enemy fire is compensated by the state through the Property Tax Fund: the Bank of Israel put compensation for the June 2025 war with Iran at about NIS 4 billion, later about NIS 6 billion, financed by an addition to the budget. [cite:gi16_boi_2025b] The wider costs of the wars, and Israel's defence spending, are set out elsewhere. [scene:gi16_costs|The bill for the wars] [scene:us19_spending|Part Nineteen: defence spending]

Both records have gaps. Israel solved its water supply through national planning and desalination, but its electricity remains mostly fossil-fuelled, it missed its 2025 renewable target, its climate law had not been enacted as of the OECD's review, and its metro is a decade away. The United States has the larger and faster-growing renewable share and falling emissions per person, but its federal climate commitments have been reversed twice, its transit and roads are graded D and D-plus, and its disaster agency is being redesigned to shift costs to the states.
