The Campuses and the Visas

Columbia

On 7 March 2025 the administration cancelled about $400 million in grants and contracts to Columbia University, citing its failure to protect Jewish students; on 10 March the Education Department warned 60 universities of possible action. [cite:doj_columbia_400m] [cite:cnn_60_universities] In May 2025 the Department of Health and Human Services found Columbia in violation of civil-rights law, citing specific, dated failures, among them the lack of a reporting mechanism until 2024 and uninvestigated antisemitic vandalism. [cite:hhs_ocr_finding] On 23 July 2025 Columbia agreed to pay $200 million over three years and $21 million to settle claims by Jewish employees, without admitting wrongdoing; it agreed to apply a federally endorsed definition of antisemitism in discipline, review its Middle East curriculum for balance, and give the government on request information about disciplinary action against students on visas. [cite:iw_pbs_columbia]

The visas

On 6 March 2025 Axios reported that Secretary of State Rubio was launching "Catch and Revoke", an AI-assisted review of student-visa holders' social media for support of Hamas or other designated groups. On 25 March the State Department made applicants ineligible over social-media posts "sympathizing with" such groups, and Rubio said more than 300 visas had been revoked in three weeks. [cite:axios_catch_and_revoke] [cite:collegefix_300_visas] On 8 March immigration agents arrested Mahmoud Khalil, a Columbia graduate and permanent resident who had been a negotiator for the campus protests; on 25 March they detained the Tufts doctoral student Rumeysa Öztürk on the street. Federal judges ordered both released. [cite:axios_catch_and_revoke] [cite:abc7_khalil]

In January 2026 a divided appeals court in Philadelphia ruled 2–1 that the judge who freed Khalil had lacked jurisdiction, opening the way to his re-arrest and deportation, and in May the full court declined, 6–5, to reconsider. [cite:iw_aje_khalil_jan] [cite:iw_dawn_khalil] Status as of 26 November 2026: his immigration case continues.

Two things are true in the record at once. Columbia's failures toward Jewish students were real, dated and found by a federal agency. And the government's response, at a speed and scale without clear precedent, reached students whose documented conduct was protest and speech; free-speech groups, including FIRE, warned that automated review could not judge that speech fairly.
