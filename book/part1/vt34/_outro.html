Part Thirty-Three established that the early modern inquisitions were three institutions, not one. The Spanish tribunal was licensed by Sixtus IV in 1478 but named and steered by the crown, over a pope's protest in 1482; it pursued converted Jews and Muslims after royal decrees forced them to choose baptism or exile, and killed, by the estimates of named historians, from under 3,000 to about 7,300 people before its final abolition on 15 July 1834. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] [cite:vt34_wiki_alhambra] [cite:vt34_eretico_victims] [cite:vt34_wiki_abolition] Portugal's, founded in 1536 at John III's request, executed about 1,180 people in person by 1794; Rome's, founded in 1542, sentenced about 1,250 to death in Italy by Del Col's estimate and continues as the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. [cite:vt34_wiki_portinq] [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] [cite:vt34_wiki_rominq] In Goa the Portuguese crown, its viceroys and the religious orders razed hundreds of temples, outlawed Hindu rites and the Konkani language, while the tribunal burned 57 people in person and punished converts for their families' customs; an archbishop's synod condemned the Syriac books of Kerala's Christians in 1599. [cite:vt34_wiki_goainq] [cite:vt34_gedsh_diamper] In the Americas the crown kept Indigenous people out of the Holy Office's reach from 1571, and the destruction of their books, shrines and ancestors was the work of friars and bishops: Landa's Maní campaign of 1562, which burned 27 codices and left at least 157 dead, the Lima council's order against religious quipus, and Peru's extirpation campaigns to 1670; Mexica rulers had burned books before them, and Sahagún and his Nahua collaborators preserved what the Florentine Codex holds.
