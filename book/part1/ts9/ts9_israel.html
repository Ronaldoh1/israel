Israel's Side: Courting a Constituency

An alliance has two ends. Earlier parts followed the end-times system from the Brethren assemblies to the Scofield Bible and into CUFI's summit halls; this section turns to the other end and asks what Israeli governments did to cultivate American Christian Zionists, and what flowed to Israel in return. The documented record here is of meetings, awards, ministry campaigns, tourism counts and charitable transfers. Why Israeli leaders pursued the relationship is a separate question; their own stated reasons and their critics' readings are given at the end, labelled as what they are. [scene:ts8_cufi|CUFI and its summit]

Begin and Falwell, 1980–81

The first sustained courtship came from the Likud. In November 1980, three years after Menachem Begin became prime minister, the Jabotinsky Foundation of New York awarded a Jabotinsky Centennial Medal to 100 Americans for service to Israel and the Jewish people, and Begin presented the awards at a dinner in New York. Jerry Falwell, whose Moral Majority had been founded the year before, was among the recipients; Senator Frank Church declined his own medal in protest at Falwell's. [cite:ts9_wiki_jabmedal] [cite:ts9_jta_falwell1999] On his visit to the United States in September 1981 Begin agreed to meet Falwell again. Rabbi Marc Tanenbaum, writing that month, called the "singling out of Rev. Falwell as the only American Christian leader" a mistake, and pointed out that some 30 million evangelicals, together with about 50 million Catholics and 45 million liberal Protestants, did not share the Moral Majority's politics. [cite:ts9_djn_tanenbaum]

Tanenbaum's objection was not to Christian support but to its narrowness: Begin's government had chosen one wing of American Christianity, the one most aligned with his own party's view of the land. The Anti-Defamation League's Leon Klenicki later made a related observation, that evangelicals received "a much warmer welcome" from Likud governments than from Labor ones. [cite:ts9_jta_falwell1999] That is a contemporary observer's judgement rather than a measured finding, but the pattern it describes, closer ties under the right, recurs in the record below.

Netanyahu's second channel

Benjamin Netanyahu made the channel a fixture. In January 1998, during his first term, he arrived in Washington for talks with President Bill Clinton on the Oslo process, and his first meeting in the capital was not with the president but with Falwell and more than 1,000 fundamentalist Christians, who hailed him as "the Ronald Reagan of Israel." Falwell pledged to contact more than 200,000 evangelical pastors and ask them to "tell President Clinton to refrain from putting pressure on Israel." [cite:ts9_cc_wagner1998] Netanyahu told the gathering, a Voices United for Israel conference that Falwell helped convene: "We have no greater friends and allies than the people sitting in this room." [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021] Falwell, by his own account, had "pledged to help him from our pulpits and our media ministries." [cite:ts9_jta_falwell1999]

The meeting had costs. Falwell was a vocal opponent of Clinton, and later reporting held that the association damaged Netanyahu's relationship with the president. [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021] It also divided Jewish opinion. Rabbi Yechiel Eckstein, himself the founder of an evangelical fundraising body, opposed Falwell's lobbying plan and said most evangelicals were "apolitical, contrary to public perception," and the Southern Baptist Convention said it did not intend to urge its pastors to lobby Congress. [cite:ts9_jta_falwell1999] Two decades on, the relationship was stated as doctrine by Netanyahu's closest adviser. Ron Dermer, his former ambassador in Washington, said in 2021 that "the backbone of Israel's support in the United States is the evangelical Christians," and CUFI's spokesman answered critics of the tie by saying that "Christian Zionists have had a relationship with every prime minister since Ben-Gurion." [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021] Netanyahu went on to address CUFI's summit by video in 2010 and in person in 2019. [cite:gi11_wiki_cufi]

Pilgrims and tourists

The most measurable return is tourism. By Israel's own figures, Christians made up nearly 1.8 million of about 3 million visitors in 2008, and the number of American Christian visitors had risen by 40 per cent since 2000; Christians were about a third of American visitors, second only to American Jews. The Ministry of Tourism ran a campaign under the slogan "Visit Israel – You'll Never Be the Same," fronted by the Christian personalities Jack Hayford and Kay Arthur, and described its outreach to American church leaders, ministries and media. [cite:ts9_cp_tourism2009] In 2013, a record year of 3.54 million visitor entries, Christians were 53 per cent of overnight tourists and about 60 per cent of all visitors. Catholics were the largest Christian group at 49 per cent, the Orthodox 28 per cent and Protestants, "mostly Evangelical," 20 per cent; about four in ten Christian tourists called themselves pilgrims, and the United States was the largest source country. [cite:ts9_mfa_tourism2013]

Those shares matter for what this volume claims. Evangelical Protestants are a significant part of Christian tourism, but by the ministry's own breakdown they were a minority of it, well behind Catholic and Orthodox pilgrims. The state's interest in tourism therefore runs wider than Christian Zionism; the evangelical share is the part tied to an organised political network. The two met in the Galilee. Pat Robertson led evangelicals in pledging $50 million for a Christian Heritage Center there, and in January 2006, after Robertson suggested that Ariel Sharon's stroke was divine judgement for dividing the land, Israel's tourism minister Abraham Hirchson said Robertson's help was no longer welcome; Robertson wrote to Sharon's son asking forgiveness. [cite:ts7_cbs_robertson] The numbers rose to about 4.5 million tourists in 2019 and then fell with the war: foreign visitors in 2024 were down by about three-quarters, to 900,000, and Catholic pilgrim groups fell from about 16,000 in 2019 to fewer than 2,000. [cite:ts9_vn_pilgrims2024]

Money sent to Israel

The largest documented flow of evangelical money to Israel runs through a Jewish organisation. The International Fellowship of Christians and Jews, founded by Rabbi Yechiel Eckstein in 1983, raised more than $1.4 billion by 2019, mostly from evangelical donors, and took in about $140 million a year; a decade before his death 98 per cent of its roughly 800,000 donors were Christian. It is credited with helping more than 730,000 Jews move to Israel. [cite:ts9_ct_eckstein] MinistryWatch, an evangelical donor watchdog, put its revenue at a peak of more than $135 million in 2015 and $117 million in 2019, and criticised fundraising and management costs of just under 30 per cent of revenue, Eckstein's pay of more than $700,000 a year, and a Jerusalem outreach centre for which tens of millions were raised and which was then cancelled; it issued a donor alert from 2013. [cite:ts9_mw_ifcj] Eckstein's own account was that Jewish suspicion of evangelical support was long "utilitarian" and that the work was building friendship. [cite:ts9_ct_eckstein] John Hagee's ministries, separate from CUFI, have reportedly directed $100 million to Israeli charities, including right-wing and settlement groups. [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021]

The return also came as diplomacy. The Israel Allies Foundation, which grew from the Knesset Christian Allies Caucus founded in 2004, described its method as "faith-based diplomacy" and by December 2020 counted caucuses in 50 parliaments and about 1,200 parliamentarians, linking new caucuses to embassy moves by Honduras and Guatemala. [cite:pm_ih_iaf50] [cite:pm_wiki_iaf] [scene:pm9_test|Follow the Money: the caucus network]

The 2025 campaign

After 7 October 2023 the courtship became a budget line. In 2025 Israel's public-diplomacy spending included a $4.1 million campaign aimed at evangelical churches and about $40 million to host some 400 foreign delegations "of lawmakers, pastors, influencers and university presidents"; the 2026 budget set aside about $730 million for public diplomacy. [cite:pm_jta_730m] Among the contracts registered under the US Foreign Agents Registration Act was $3.2 million to a new firm, Show Faith by Works, working under the Foreign Ministry through Havas Media; its pitch proposed geofencing major churches in California, Arizona, Nevada and Colorado and all Christian colleges, to reach about 3.8 million churchgoers. Its principal, Chad Schnitger, said the plan was "an early planning document" with nothing "set in stone" and that "everything we do is vetted through teams of Christian pastors." [cite:ts9_rs_showfaith] [cite:pm_quincy_eighth] [scene:pm9_israel_direct|Follow the Money: Israel's direct spending]

Why: the stated reasons and the critics' readings

Israeli leaders have given their reasons on the record. Netanyahu called the gathering of 1998 Israel's greatest friends and allies, and Dermer called evangelicals the backbone of Israel's support in America. [cite:ts9_j_netanyahu2021] Critics within the Jewish community read the same facts as a strategic choice with a price. In 1981 Tanenbaum argued that tying Israel to one political wing of Christianity neglected the larger part of American Christian opinion; Klenicki noted the Likud's preference; Eckstein, while raising money from evangelicals himself, warned that most of them were not a political bloc. [cite:ts9_djn_tanenbaum] [cite:ts9_jta_falwell1999] What the record supports is narrower than either side's rhetoric: successive Israeli governments, most intensively under the Likud, cultivated named evangelical leaders and organisations, measured the return partly in visitors and donations, and in 2025 began paying contractors to reach churchgoers directly. Whether that investment changed American policy is the question of the next section and of Part Ten. [scene:ts9_us|The American side] [scene:ts10_mechanism|How the shield works]
