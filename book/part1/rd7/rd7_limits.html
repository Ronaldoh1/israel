What the Map Cannot Know

Every figure in this appendix depends on choices that the record alone does not make, and the map discloses each of them. They are listed here so that a reader who weighs them differently can change them and see what follows.

It measures the founding, not the war. The map asks whether a state is founded on 14 May 1948, not whether it survives the war that followed. Arms ties are weighed by their connections, not by the number of rifles and aircraft they carried, with one exception where the record allows a count. The Czechoslovak sales brought 34,500 rifles, over 6,000 machine guns and the first fighter aircraft through October 1948, and Ben-Gurion said that without them "we would have not survived." [cite:d_wp_czarms] [cite:d_mef_prague] The exception is this supply: what arrived before 15 May, about 14,700 rifles and 1,655 machine guns, is counted as close to half the rifles in the Haganah's hands at the founding, for a force of about 30,000 of whom many had been unarmed in November. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] Even so, the map measures the founding, not the war, in which the aircraft and most of the arms arrived.

It counts only what is documented. A person or organization that would have stepped in but left no record cannot be a substitute. Some of what the map reads as lost years may be work that someone undocumented would have done; some of what it reads as covered may be work that a documented substitute would not have chosen to do.

It cannot weigh a cause. The map counts ties in which one party did something to or for another: funded, founded, signed, voted. It cannot turn "this persecution prompted that movement" into a dependency. The pogroms of 1881, the May Laws, the Damascus affair and the Mortara case therefore score nothing when removed, and so do the thinkers whose writings set out the idea, such as Hess, Kalischer and Alkalai. A zero for them means the map cannot measure them, not that they did not matter. A test that removes the pogroms together with the bodies formed in response to them is offered, and it is marked as an assumption.

Some of its sources are reference works. Most ties rest on the sources cited in the stages of this book. The eight nodes added for the years before Herzl rest mostly on encyclopedias and general histories rather than on the original documents, and where only one source states a tie, the tie is marked as still to be verified and counts for nothing.

Its legitimacy channel is a judgment about weight. Public sympathy, religious attachment and political endorsement are real forces, and the record shows them moving decisions, but no document says how much they weighed beside money or arms. The map counts the legitimacy channel as heavily as land, arms, diplomacy, organization or recognition, and half as heavily as money. A reader who thinks it weighed more or less can change that judgment; the documented ties underneath it stay the same. It also counts only the ties in which support reached a decision-maker. The broad shift in Western opinion after 1945, which much of the record describes but no single tie captures, is not measured at all.

It cannot weigh persuasion that left no tie. Newspapers, news agencies, pageants and propaganda shaped what editors, voters and officials believed, but a belief is not a tie unless a document states that it moved a decision. The map therefore records the movement's own press and the Arab Office's campaign without weight, and counts only the mail campaign and the opinion polls that Truman himself described. What the press did beyond that is real and unmeasured.

Its channels saturate. Every channel follows the same curve, so the first part of anything counts for more than the last, and losing even half of a channel leaves much of its contribution standing. For most channels that is a reasonable reading of the record. For population it is probably too gentle: removing the Mandate's certificates takes away about 350,000 people and leaves the chance at about 78 percent, though a community of about 300,000 would have made the UN's partition of 1947 far harder to draw. The curve is one of the things to be stress-tested before the figures are treated as final.

Its land figures are partly estimates. The Jewish National Fund's holdings are documented by year. PICA's are taken from a single report of 1934, and the land held by private buyers and companies is a remainder, what is left of about 2 million dunams once the other two are subtracted. Neither can yet be split into what was bought before and after 1920, so only the Fund's purchases are credited to the Mandate's land law.

Its weights are assumptions. Money counts twice; a money tie with no stated sum counts as $1 million; a reported tie counts a little over half as much as a verified one; each push and each resistance in a key decision counts once; a successor with no documented position is an even chance; each year of delay costs 2 percent of the chance; a channel starts counting lost years at 5 percent of its 1948 total. Each is the simplest choice the record allows, and each can be argued.

It reads the record of what happened. A removal shows what the documented network would have lacked, not what the people in it would have done instead. Where the map names a successor or a substitute, it names the one the record shows was there, with what the record shows he had done.

The other homelands are judgments. The shares given to the other homelands in the next appendix are read from the record by hand, not counted from the map, and each is shown with its reason.
