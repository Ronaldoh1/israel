The Mufti and the Supreme Muslim Council

While the Zionist movement built institutions of its own, the British administration was building Muslim ones. In the space of a year, between the spring of 1921 and the first days of 1922, Britain's first High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel, made a man who had been convicted over the riots of 1920 Mufti of Jerusalem, and that man then became president of a new Muslim religious council that Britain had created. Amin al-Husseini became Mufti in May 1921 and president of the Supreme Muslim Council in January 1922. The first was Samuel's appointment; whether the second was an election or an appointment is described differently by the sources. How it made them, and why, is one of the most argued-over episodes of the early Mandate. The documents allow it to be reconstructed in some detail. They do not settle what it meant.

The story begins in April 1920. After the violence at the Nebi Musa festival, described in [scene:ri_s01|the first riots], a British military tribunal convicted Amin al-Husseini in his absence and sentenced him to ten years' imprisonment. He had fled to Syria. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_jvl] Walid Khalidi's biography of him presents the sentence as imposed for having led demonstrations, a political protest for Syrian unity. [cite:rs_hg_palquest_khalidi] Samuel pardoned him, and he returned to Jerusalem later in 1920. [cite:rs_mu_palquest_khalidi] [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] The sources do not agree on when the pardon came. Shira Klein, summarizing the scholarship for the COJS website, dates it to August 1920. The USHMM's Holocaust Encyclopedia says autumn 1920. Khalidi gives no month and says only "later in 1920." [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_palquest_khalidi]

On 21 March 1921 the Mufti of Jerusalem, Kamil al-Husseini, died. He was Amin's brother. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] On 12 April 1921 a Muslim electoral body met to choose three candidates for the office. Amin al-Husseini was not among the three. Raghib al-Nashashibi backed another candidate, Husam al-Din Jarallah. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] One account puts Amin fourth, with "about 7% of the vote." That figure comes from an opinion article by David Storobin, which relies on Joseph Schechtman's book The Mufti and the Fuehrer. It is the only vote figure in these sources, and it has not been confirmed elsewhere. [cite:rs_mu_hnn_storobin]

The day before the election, Samuel had already met Amin. In a note dated 11 April 1921, as published on the COJS website from Elpeleg's 1993 study, Samuel recorded the meeting. Ronald Storrs, by then civil governor of Jerusalem, was present. Samuel recorded Amin's pledge that he and his family would work to keep Jerusalem calm. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_samuel_note] After the vote, Samuel asked Storrs to persuade Raghib al-Nashashibi to drop Jarallah. Jarallah withdrew, and his withdrawal allowed Amin to qualify. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] Amin al-Husseini became Mufti of Jerusalem on 8 May 1921. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein]

Why did Samuel do it? The USHMM's Holocaust Encyclopedia says the British backed Amin because they accepted his assurances of cooperation. They also believed that only he had the credentials and family connections to keep Palestine calm. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] Samuel's own note of 11 April fits that account, since it records exactly such an assurance. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_samuel_note] The Jewish Virtual Library adds a name. It states that the British Arabist Ernest Richmond pressed Samuel to make the appointment, and that Samuel made it with "some trepidation." [cite:rs_mu_jvl] There was also a political balance to strike. Britain had dismissed Musa Kazim al-Husseini as mayor of Jerusalem in 1920 and put Raghib al-Nashashibi in his place, as described in [scene:pp_s01|the Palestinian national movement]. After May 1921 the mayoralty was held by a Nashashibi and the office of Mufti by a Husseini, the two rival families. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

The appointment has been read in three ways. The first reading stresses the overridden vote. Amin was not in the top three, and one account places him fourth with about 7 percent. British officials then induced the withdrawal of a qualified candidate in order to make him eligible. On this view Britain did not merely choose among candidates. It changed the list so that its preferred man would be on it. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] [cite:rs_mu_hnn_storobin] The second reading treats it as an ordinary use of government choice for the sake of order. Officials accepted his assurances and judged him the one figure able to keep the peace. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_cojs_samuel_note] The third, Khalidi's Palestinian biographical account, presents Amin as succeeding his brother in May 1921 and does not discuss the election. [cite:rs_mu_palquest_khalidi]

The office of Mufti was only half of what Samuel created. The other half was a new institution to run Muslim religious affairs. A Muslim conference on the Sharia courts and waqf, the religious endowments, was convened on 9 November 1920. Samuel approved a Regulation on 12 March 1921. It was published in the official gazette on 15 May 1921. Secondary electors met at Government House on 24 August 1921. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order] The result was the Supreme Moslem Sharia Council Order, issued in Jerusalem on 20 December 1921. It created a Council made up of a Rais al-Ulema, the head of the religious scholars, and four members: two for Jerusalem and one each for Nablus and Acre. Each was to serve a four-year term. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order]

Article 8 of the Order set out the Council's powers. It was "to administer and control Moslem Awkaf," the endowments. It nominated the Kadis, the judges of the Sharia courts. It appointed Muftis from three candidates chosen by a special electoral college. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order] The Order thus set down a three-candidate procedure like the one used for the vacancy Amin had filled a few months earlier. On 9 January 1922 Amin al-Husseini became president of the new Supreme Muslim Council, which controlled the Sharia courts and the waqf funds. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm]

How Amin reached the presidency is itself disputed. Klein describes him as elected, and to a presidency for life. Khalidi describes him as appointed president of a council that the High Commissioner instituted. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] [cite:rs_mu_palquest_khalidi] The Order's own text cuts against the idea of a life term on paper, since it sets four-year terms for the Council's members. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order] What the documents do show is the mechanism. A British High Commissioner created the body by Order, set its membership and its electoral rules, and the man he had already made Mufti became its president. Through Article 8 that body received control of the waqf, the nomination of the Sharia judges and the appointment of Muftis. These functions now came under a single council, created by the mandatory government.

The contrast with the national movement is plain. In these same months the Palestinian national movement's executive body, the Arab Executive of the Palestine Arab Congresses, went unrecognized by Britain. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] The Supreme Muslim Council, by contrast, was a body Britain created and whose powers it defined, and the Mufti Samuel had chosen stood at its head. Control of the waqf funds and the Sharia courts went to the Council, and so to the man at its head. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_smc_order]

Several questions remain open. The full vote count of 12 April 1921 is not established. The only figure, Amin's 7 percent, comes from one opinion article relying on one book. Samuel's note records Amin's pledge, but it does not by itself show how much weight Samuel gave it against Richmond's urging or against the balance between the families. The month of the pardon is reported three ways. Whether the presidency of the Council was won by election or by appointment, and whether it was meant for life or for four years, is described differently by the sources and is left open here. The record establishes the sequence: the vote, the withdrawal, the appointment and the Order. Whether that sequence was a manipulation or a judgment about public order is a question each reader has to weigh from the evidence above.
