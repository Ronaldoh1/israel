The Price of the Credential

The second half of the education promise is college. The national standards teach that investment in human capital raises living standards, and the civic version that follows from it is simple: a young person who works hard in school can go to college, the public colleges and federal grants will keep it within reach, and the degree will pay for itself. [cite:ad1_c3] [cite:ad1_cee2010] This section sets that version beside the price record, the record of the federal grant meant to keep college open to low-income students, the record of the for-profit colleges that grew on federal aid, and the spread of degree requirements to jobs that did not need them. It ends with the strongest evidence that the promise still holds: what a degree is worth.

What college costs, in real dollars

Prices in this section are in "real" or inflation-adjusted dollars: each year's price is restated in 2025 dollars so that a change shows how much more or less a year of college costs after general inflation is removed. Two prices matter. The published price, or "sticker price", is the tuition and fees a college lists. The net price is what students pay after grants and scholarships, which do not have to be repaid. By the College Board's annual survey, average published tuition and fees for in-state students at a public four-year college were $5,940 in 1995–96 and $9,050 in 2005–06, rose to a peak of $13,150 in 2019–20 and 2020–21, and then fell to $11,950 in 2025–26, all in 2025 dollars. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The 30-year rise is a doubling: $11,950 divided by $5,940 is 2.01. Since the peak the real price has fallen about 9 per cent, as increases in current dollars stayed below inflation. Private non-profit four-year colleges averaged $45,000 in 2025–26 and public two-year colleges $4,150. [cite:us14_cb_trends2025]

A widely repeated figure holds that college tuition has risen more than 1,200 per cent since 1980, faster than any other cost. Figures of that kind compare tuition in current dollars, without removing the general rise in prices over the same decades, against an inflation index; in real terms the published in-state price at public four-year colleges roughly doubled over the 30 years the College Board's current series covers. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The doubling is real and large. The larger figure is not a measure of it.

Net tuition tells a different story from 2012 onwards. For first-time, full-time in-state students at public four-year colleges, average net tuition and fees peaked at $4,450 in 2012–13 and fell to an estimated $2,300 in 2025–26, a fall of 48 per cent, as federal, state and institutional grants grew. At public two-year colleges, the average grant now exceeds tuition and fees, leaving a net figure of minus $1,190 that students can put towards other costs. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] Tuition is not the whole bill. Adding housing and food, the average budget for an in-state student living on campus at a public four-year college was $25,850 in 2025–26, and for most students living costs, not tuition, are now the larger share of what must be found or borrowed. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25]

Part of the rise in sticker prices traces to states. Public colleges are financed mainly by state appropriations and by tuition, so the two tend to move against each other. By the State Higher Education Executive Officers' count, education appropriations reached $12,082 per full-time student in 2025, 9.2 per cent above their 2008 level after inflation; the 2025 figure was down 1.0 per cent on 2024, the first per-student fall since 2012. Students' own tuition provided 38.4 per cent of public colleges' education revenue. [cite:ad7_shef_fy25] The College Board, using a broader measure of state and local funding, found it up 34 per cent per student after inflation between 2013–14 and 2023–24, to $11,680. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The record shows per-student state support falling after 2008 and recovering after 2012, which matches the timing of the net-price decline above.

The Pell Grant, then and now

The federal Pell Grant is the main federal grant for low-income undergraduates. Its maximum award is set by Congress. In the late 1970s the maximum covered roughly three-quarters of the full cost of a year at a public four-year college, counting tuition, fees, room and board: 72 per cent in fiscal 1976 and 77 per cent in fiscal 1979. [cite:ad7_ace_pell] [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell] The share fell below half in the late 1980s, reached 32 per cent in fiscal 2006, and stood at 34 per cent in 2010. [cite:ad7_ace_pell] The maximum has been $7,395 since 2023–24. Against the College Board's 2025–26 budget of tuition, fees, housing and food at a public four-year college, $25,850, it covers 28.6 per cent ($7,395 divided by $25,850). [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The National Association of Student Financial Aid Administrators, using federal cost-of-attendance data that also count books and other expenses, put the 2024–25 share at 27.2 per cent of $27,182. [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell]

The grant did not shrink; costs outgrew it. The NASFAA brief converts the 1978 maximum of $1,400 into about $7,095 in 2026 dollars, so today's $7,395 buys about as much in general goods as the maximum did half a century ago, 4 per cent more. [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell] What changed is the price of a year at college, which rose much faster than general prices. The gap between what the grant covers and what college costs is the space that loans came to fill. The 2025 reconciliation act, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, also made students ineligible for Pell if their family's calculated ability to pay, the Student Aid Index, is at least twice the maximum award. [cite:us17_unmc_obbba]

The for-profit colleges

The sharpest test of the promise came at for-profit colleges, companies that run schools for profit and draw much of their revenue from students' federal grants and loans. Average for-profit tuition was $16,030 in 2024–25, well above public four-year tuition. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] Adam Looney and Constantine Yannelis, in a paper for the Brookings Institution, traced the rise in student-loan defaults after 2000 to a change in who borrowed and where. For-profit borrowers were 18 per cent of borrowers starting repayment in 2000 and 31 per cent in 2011, about 930,000 people; with community-college borrowers they made up almost half of those starting repayment in 2011 and accounted for 70 per cent of the defaults among them. Within five years, 47 per cent of for-profit borrowers in the 2009 repayment group had defaulted, against 29 per cent in 1999. [cite:ad7_ly_press] [cite:ad7_ly_draft] The authors attributed the defaults to large borrowing at institutions with low completion rates, followed by weak earnings. [cite:ad7_ly_press] The finding cuts both ways for the taught version. It shows that the "student debt crisis" is concentrated: within two years of entering repayment, about 2 per cent of graduate borrowers and 8 per cent of traditional undergraduate borrowers had defaulted, against about 21 per cent of borrowers from for-profit and two-year colleges. [cite:ad7_ly_draft] It also shows that the federal aid system paid for a large expansion of programmes whose students often left with debt and without the earnings the promise assumed.

The credential requirement

The degree's value depends partly on employers demanding it. A 2017 study by Harvard Business School researchers led by Joseph Fuller, Dismissed by Degrees, estimated that 6.2 million jobs were at risk of "degree inflation", a rise in degree requirements beyond what the work needed. In job postings for production supervisors, 67 per cent asked for a college degree, while only 16 per cent of people already doing the job held one. Employers surveyed said they used the degree as a stand-in for hard skills (70 per cent) and soft skills (60 per cent). [cite:ad7_nysscpa_degrees] When many large employers and some states announced that they would drop degree requirements, a 2024 follow-up by Fuller's group and the Burning Glass Institute found that the change reached "not even 1 in 700 hires"; about 45 per cent of the firms changed in name only, and nearly all real change came from a minority of firms. Where firms did hire workers without degrees into such roles, those workers earned about 25 per cent more than before and, at the leading firms, stayed longer than degree holders. [cite:ad7_hbs_skills] The requirement works as a filter that keeps a degree necessary for jobs that do not need its content, which adds to what non-graduates lose without adding to what graduates know.

Where it works as promised: the college wage premium

The strongest counter-evidence to the illusion reading is the earnings record. Georgetown University's Center on Education and the Workforce, using Census survey data for 2009–2019, estimated median lifetime earnings for full-time, full-year workers aged 25 to 64 at $1.6 million with a high-school diploma, $2.0 million with an associate degree and $2.8 million with a bachelor's degree, and found that bachelor's holders earn 75 per cent more over a career than they would with a diploma alone ($2.8 million divided by $1.6 million is 1.75). [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] The same study warns against reading the medians as a guarantee: at least a quarter of high-school graduates earn more than the typical associate-degree holder, and field of study and occupation matter as much as level. [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] Debt among graduates has also fallen. Of students who earned bachelor's degrees in 2023–24, 47 per cent had borrowed, owing $29,560 on average, against 56 per cent and $35,310 in inflation-adjusted dollars for the 2018–19 graduates. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25]

Read together, the record says the education promise is true on average for those who finish a four-year degree, and that the net price at public colleges has fallen for a decade. Where it fails, it fails at identifiable points: a grant that no longer covers most of a year's costs, programmes with low completion and weak earnings, and a credential demanded for work that does not use it.
