The Shipowner's List

The first person known to have drawn up a closed list of Christian scripture was not a bishop. He was a businessman. Marcion came from Sinope, a port on the southern shore of the Black Sea, and the writers who attacked him, Rhodon and Tertullian, both describe him as a nauclerus, a shipowner. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] He travelled widely, and the Catholic Encyclopedia, no friend to his memory, says plainly that his wealth gave him influence in Rome. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites]

When he arrived in the capital, Marcion gave the Christian community there a gift that Tertullian reports as two hundred thousand sesterces. The English translation of Tertullian most often reproduced renders the sum as "two hundred sesterces", but the Catholic Encyclopedia reads it as two hundred thousand, a very large donation for a congregation that met in private houses. [cite:cn_tert_praescr30] [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] What happened next is the first documented case in this book of money meeting doctrine. Marcion's teaching put him outside the community, and when he broke with it, in the autumn of 144 according to the Catholic Encyclopedia, the church of Rome gave the money back. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] Tertullian, writing around 200, tells the story with satisfaction: Marcion and Valentinus had been members of the Roman church and were expelled "more than once", and Marcion left with the sum he had brought in. [cite:cn_tert_praescr30] Tertullian places these events under the Roman bishop Eleutherus, which does not fit the date of 144; his chronology is loose, and the date of the break rests on the Marcionites' own reckoning of when their church began. [cite:cn_tert_praescr30] [cite:cn_ce_marcionites]

The returned gift is worth pausing over. A community that depended on the offerings of its wealthier members refused the largest gift it had received because the giver's teaching was wrong. Whatever else the episode shows, it shows that by the 140s the Roman church already treated doctrine as something its leaders could enforce, at a cost to themselves, against a rich man. It also shows the direction the conflict would take. Marcion did not go quietly. He founded churches of his own across the Roman world, which lasted for centuries. [cite:vt24_audit_wiki_marcionism]

His list was short and severe. Marcion taught that the God of the Jewish scriptures and the Father revealed by Jesus were two different gods, and he wrote a book, the Antitheses or "Contrasts", setting passages of the old scriptures against the new; the book is lost and is known only through his opponents. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] His scripture had two parts. The gospel was a single one, a form of Luke without its first two chapters. The apostle was ten letters of Paul: Galatians, the two letters to the Corinthians, Romans, the two to the Thessalonians, Ephesians, which Marcion knew as the letter to the Laodiceans, Colossians, Philippians and Philemon. Everything else was out: the other gospels, Acts, the letters to Timothy and Titus, Hebrews, the letters of James, Peter, John and Jude, and Revelation, as well as the whole of the Jewish scriptures. [cite:cn_ce_marcionites] [cite:vt24_audit_wiki_marcionism]

Marcion's list mattered because it forced his opponents to produce one of their own. The Muratorian fragment, a Latin list of uncertain date that many scholars place in the late second century, rejects letters "forged in Paul's name" for Marcion's cause, and the church writers of the next generations argued against him book by book. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger] Historians disagree about how far Marcion caused the catholic canon to form and how far he merely sped up a process already under way; what is not in dispute is that the first closed list was his, and that the larger list that defeated it was assembled partly in answer to it. [cite:vt24_audit_wiki_marcionism] [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon]

The episode sets out, at the very beginning, the three elements that recur through this book. There was a list, which defined who could claim to speak with the apostle's authority. There was an office, the leadership of the Roman congregation, which decided who belonged. And there was money, which the office could accept or refuse according to its judgment of the list.
