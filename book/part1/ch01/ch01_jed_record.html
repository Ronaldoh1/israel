What Was Left Out, and Who Noticed

The schools, camps and trips described in this part were designed to build attachment first and complexity later, if at all. The clearest evidence of what that design left out comes from the people it taught. When young American Jews meet the fuller record as adults, many say the same thing: they were not told. Surveys over the past five years show how far their views have moved from those of their parents and grandparents.

What former pupils say they missed

The "You Never Told Me" campaign, launched by IfNotNow in 2018, gathered former campers and day-school pupils who said their Jewish education had left out the occupation, the Green Line that marks the 1967 boundary, and the Palestinian experience of 1948 that Palestinians call the Nakba. The journalist Emma Goldberg, writing in +972 Magazine as the campaign began, put the complaint in one sentence: most Jewish students "can trace Israel's borders but do not know what the Nakba or Green Line are." [cite:jed_972_ynt2018] Defenders of the schools answer that their first job is to pass on attachment and identity, with the conflict taught later; as a Ramah camp director put it, "First we teach a love for Israel." [cite:jed_jta_counselors2018] The two accounts agree on the order: attachment first. They disagree on whether the second step ever comes.

How the views of younger Jews differ

The surveys below were run by different sponsors, asked different questions and used different samples, and they should not be added together. Read side by side, they point the same way.

In July 2021 a poll for the Jewish Electorate Institute, a group led by prominent Jewish Democrats, surveyed 800 Jewish voters. A quarter agreed that "Israel is an apartheid state," and 22 percent agreed that Israel "is committing genocide against the Palestinians"; among voters under 40, more than a third agreed with the first statement and a third with the second. The poll asked these questions only of respondents who had first said the statement was not hateful toward Jews, a method that narrowed who was asked each question. [cite:jed_toi_jei2021]

In February 2024, four months into the Gaza war, the Pew Research Center found that 52 percent of American Jews under 35 called Israel's conduct of the war acceptable and 42 percent unacceptable; among those 50 and older, 68 percent called it acceptable. [cite:jed_pew_2024] A survey by the Jewish Federations of North America, carried out in March 2025 and published in February 2026, found that among Jews aged 18 to 34, 57 percent felt emotionally attached to Israel, compared with 88 percent of those over 75; 35 percent called themselves Zionists and 32 percent called themselves anti-Zionist or non-Zionist, while 76 percent still supported Israel's existence as a Jewish and democratic state. [cite:jed_ji_jfna2026] In 2026 an AP-NORC poll of 1,022 Jewish adults found that about a quarter of religiously affiliated Jews, and about four in ten unaffiliated Jews, said Israel had committed genocide in Gaza, and that supporting Israel was less central to younger Jews' sense of their own identity. [cite:jed_apnorc_2026]

What the numbers do and do not show

These surveys show a generation gap. They do not show its cause. Young people's views on most issues differ from their parents', and the Gaza war, social media and campus politics all bear on these figures. No study located for this book measures whether people who had a particular kind of Jewish education hold different views later, except the trip's own commissioned research, which found participants returning more attached to Israel (see the section on how the story is taught). What the record does show is narrower and firmer: the institutions were designed to put attachment before complexity; their own leaders say so; and a visible share of the people they taught now say, in their own words, that the complexity was missing.

It also shows that the boundary this volume has drawn throughout runs through the Jewish community itself. Large numbers of young American Jews hold strong Jewish identities while rejecting the political claims their schools and trips joined to them. To treat "Jewish" and "Zionist" as one word is to erase them, along with the Satmar schools, the Jewish anti-Zionist tradition and the many Jews who support Israel without calling themselves Zionists. That boundary was not drawn by critics from outside. Jewish Americans drew it themselves.
