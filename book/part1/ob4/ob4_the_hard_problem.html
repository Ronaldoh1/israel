The Hard Problem

In 1994, at a conference in Tucson, the Australian philosopher David Chalmers drew a distinction that has framed the field ever since, and in 1995 he set it out in a paper titled "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness". [cite:ob_consc_facing] [cite:ob_wiki_hard] The easy problems, he wrote, are those of explaining what the brain does: telling one stimulus from another, combining information, controlling behaviour, producing speech. They are easy only in principle; each can be explained, eventually, by describing a mechanism. [cite:ob_wiki_hard]

The hard problem is different. Even when every function has been explained, one question remains, which Chalmers put in a single line: "Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" [cite:ob_wiki_hard] There is something it is like to see red or to feel pain, and no description of neurons firing, however complete, seems to contain that something.

The question is not settled, even as a question. In the 2020 PhilPapers survey of professional philosophers, 62.4 percent said the hard problem is a genuine problem and 29.7 percent said it is not. [cite:ob_wiki_hard] Those in the second group hold that once the functions are explained there will be nothing left over, and that the sense of a further mystery is itself something the brain produces.

This is where the physics of Parts One to Three and the question of this shelf meet. Physics needs no observer to make a measurement; but the hard problem asks what an observer is, and on that the physics is silent. The reading in Part Eight starts here.
