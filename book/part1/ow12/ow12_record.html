What the Courts and the Justice Department Established

Jeffrey Epstein appears in this book for one reason: a widely repeated claim that he worked for, or on behalf of, Israeli intelligence. Before that claim can be weighed, the ground has to be cleared of everything else, and the firmest ground is the record that courts and the Department of Justice have made. This section sets it out, in order, without the allegations against particular people that fill much of what is written about Epstein. Only findings of courts, official documents and named reporting on them are used.

The money and the first complaint. In July 1991 the retailer Leslie Wexner gave Epstein full power of attorney over his affairs, the documented foundation of Epstein's wealth and access. [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein] In 1996 the artist Maria Farmer reported to the FBI that Epstein had stolen and sold nude and semi-nude photographs of her sisters, aged 12 and 16, and had threatened her. [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein] The record used here shows no federal case arising from it.

The Florida deal, 2008. When federal prosecutors in southern Florida, under the United States Attorney Alexander Acosta, did investigate, they negotiated a non-prosecution agreement. It granted immunity from federal charges to Epstein, to four named co-conspirators and to unnamed "potential co-conspirators." On 30 June 2008 Epstein pleaded guilty in a Palm Beach County court to two state charges of soliciting prostitution, one involving a minor under 18; he was sentenced to 18 months, served 13 and was released on 22 July 2009. [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein] In 2019 Judge Kenneth Marra of the federal court in southern Florida ruled that the agreement had violated the Crime Victims' Rights Act, because the victims were not told of it; in 2020 the Eleventh Circuit reversed him. A 2020 report by the Justice Department's Office of Professional Responsibility found that Acosta had shown "poor judgment" in granting the agreement but had acted within the "scope of his authority." [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein]

New York, 2019. Epstein was arrested on 6 July 2019 at Teterboro Airport, New Jersey, and charged two days later by federal prosecutors in Manhattan with sex trafficking and conspiracy; bail was refused. He died in his cell on 10 August 2019, and his death was ruled a suicide by hanging. The charges were dismissed on 29 August. [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein] His associate Ghislaine Maxwell was convicted on 29 December 2021 on five of six counts, including sex trafficking of a minor, and sentenced on 28 June 2022 to 20 years. The Second Circuit upheld the conviction in September 2024, and in October 2025 the Supreme Court declined to hear her appeal. [cite:ow12_wiki_gmaxwell]

The Justice Department's memorandum, July 2025. On 7 July 2025 the Justice Department and the FBI released an unsigned two-page memorandum describing what they called a systematic review of their investigative holdings. "This systematic review revealed no incriminating 'client list,'" it said; there was "no credible evidence found that Epstein blackmailed prominent individuals as part of his actions"; and the department "did not uncover evidence that could predicate an investigation against uncharged third parties." It reaffirmed that Epstein had killed himself. [cite:ow12_abc_memo] It said further disclosure would not be "appropriate or warranted," citing child sexual abuse material and victims' privacy. [cite:ow12_npr_memo] This is the government's own position on the record, and it is the position against which any claim of a blackmail or intelligence operation must be set.

The Maxwell interview, July 2025. On 24 and 25 July 2025, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche questioned Maxwell for about nine hours in Tallahassee, Florida, with an FBI agent present; the department released the full transcript and audio on 22 August 2025. Blanche told her he was "not promising to do anything" in return, and warned that false statements could be prosecuted. Asked about a client list, Maxwell said: "There was never a list." [cite:ow12_gaz_maxwell] [cite:ow12_npr_maxwell] Her account has to be weighed with her record: prosecutors had earlier charged her with perjury over sworn statements in civil suits, counts dropped after her 2021 conviction. [cite:ow12_gaz_maxwell] Days after the interview she was moved from a Florida prison to a minimum-security camp at Bryan, Texas; the Bureau of Prisons confirmed the move on 1 August 2025, and the White House called it routine. On 9 February 2026, deposed by the House Oversight Committee, she invoked her Fifth Amendment right and declined to answer. [cite:ow12_wiki_gmaxwell]

The files, 2025–2026. The memorandum's refusal to release more did not hold. A discharge petition forced a House vote, and on 18 November 2025 the House passed the Epstein Files Transparency Act by 427 to 1; the Senate passed it by unanimous consent, and President Trump signed it on 19 November. It required release within 30 days and set no penalty for failing. The department released a first partial batch on 19 December 2025, about 3.5 million pages on 30 January 2026, which it said completed its obligations, and 16 further pages in March after saying they had been wrongly coded as duplicates; Representative Ro Khanna noted that the department itself had identified more than 6 million potentially responsive pages. Members of Congress began reading unredacted files in a department reading room in February 2026, the department's inspector general opened a compliance review in April, and in July Representative Thomas Massie introduced a second transparency bill. [cite:ow12_wiki_efta]

What this record establishes is a long failure by American prosecutors to stop a sex offender, a federal agreement that the Justice Department's own review called poor judgment, a conviction of his chief associate, and a government that has, on the record, found no client list and no evidence of blackmail. What it does not contain is any court finding or released official document stating that Epstein worked for any intelligence service, Israeli or other.
