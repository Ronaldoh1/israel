The Uncomfortable Record: Lehi's Offer to Germany and the Killing of Lord Moyne

A familiar account of the war years holds that every Zionist faction stood with the Allies against Nazism and set itself against terrorism. For the mainstream of the movement that account is close to the record. For its militant fringe it is not, and two episodes in particular are often left out of the story: an offer made to Nazi Germany in January 1941, and the assassination of a British minister in Cairo in November 1944. Both are documented, and so is the mainstream leadership's response to them. So, too, is what the State of Israel later did with the memory of the men involved.

The group at the centre of both episodes was Lehi. In 1940 Avraham Stern led it out of the Irgun so that it could keep fighting Britain during the war [cite:academickids_lehi]. The armed organizations of the Yishuv before the war, and the Haganah's later campaign against the Irgun and Lehi, are told in [scene:s07e2|the earlier account of the Irgun and Lehi]; this section takes up the two wartime episodes themselves.

The Ankara document, 11 January 1941. The first episode survives on paper. A document dated 11 January 1941, found after the war in the files of the German embassy in Ankara, records Lehi's offer to Germany. Naftali Lubenchik carried it to the German diplomat Werner Otto von Hentig in Beirut, then under Vichy control. In it Lehi proposed to "actively take part in the war on Germany's side" in exchange for German support for a Jewish state [cite:academickids_lehi] [cite:marxists_ankara_document].

What the German files do not contain matters as much as what they do. There is no record of any German response [cite:academickids_lehi] [cite:marxists_ankara_document]. Nothing in the sources gathered here shows that Berlin took the offer up, that any arms or money changed hands, or that Lehi acted on the proposal in any way. The document records an offer from an underground group. It does not record an alliance, and it should not be read as one. Nor does it speak for the Zionist movement as a whole, whose leaders, as shown below, disowned the fringe.

Cairo, 6 November 1944. The second episode came near the end of the war, and it had consequences. On 6 November 1944 two Lehi members, Eliyahu Hakim and Eliyahu Bet-Zuri, shot Lord Moyne outside his residence in Cairo [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:jewishpress_moyne]. Moyne was Britain's Minister Resident in the Middle East and a close friend of Winston Churchill. Lehi blamed him for turning back refugee ships [cite:daat_moyne]. The ships that the immigration limit turned away, and the deaths aboard two of them, are described in [scene:s07b2|the preceding section]; the sources here do not say which ships Lehi had in mind. Hakim and Bet-Zuri were hanged in March 1945 [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:jewishpress_moyne].

The killing was an assassination of a named official by members of an armed group acting outside any recognized authority, and it is described here by what it was. Churchill's papers show its effect in London. When a request came for a 70th-birthday greeting to Chaim Weizmann, Churchill wrote "no message" on it. He also warned against pressure from Zionists and American Jews for clemency for the two men, according to the Churchill Archives Centre [cite:churchill_archives_moyne]. The rebuff fell on Weizmann, and nothing in these sources connects him to the shooting.

The mainstream response. The leadership of the movement did not treat Lehi as its own. The Zionist Executive and the mainstream youth movements denounced the Irgun and Lehi as "traitors" [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:executedtoday_moyne]. The Haganah had already acted against them, in the campaign of 1944 and 1945 known as the "Hunting Season," which is told in [scene:s07e2|the account of the Irgun and Lehi].

Denounced, then honoured. The second half of the record came decades later. Yitzhak Shamir, one of Lehi's leaders, later became prime minister of Israel, and he had Hakim and Bet-Zuri reburied on Mount Herzl, the national cemetery [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:executedtoday_moyne]. The movement's leadership condemned the fringe at the time; the state that movement founded later raised one of that fringe's leaders to its highest offices and laid its assassins in its national cemetery. Both facts are documented, and neither cancels the other. A reader who cites only the denunciation leaves out the reburial; a reader who cites only the reburial leaves out the denunciation.

The record leaves several questions open. The sources here date Lehi's break from the Irgun only to the year, and they do not give the terms of the debate inside Lehi over the Ankara offer, or whether any further contact with German officials followed it. They do not set out whether or how the two men were tried, only that they were hanged. They do not give the date of the reburial, or what was said at it. And they do not show how far Churchill’s response to Cairo changed British policy on Palestine, as distinct from his personal stance toward Weizmann. What they do establish is the answer to the claim this section began with. Not every faction stood with the Allies or rejected terror: one underground group offered to fight on Germany's side and killed a British minister. The movement's leadership condemned it at the time, and the state that followed later honoured its men.
