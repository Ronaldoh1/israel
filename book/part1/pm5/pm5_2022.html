2022: The First Cycle

The United Democracy Project spent its first money in the Democratic primaries of 2022, and it spent almost all of it there. By early August it had made nearly $24.3 million in independent expenditures, every dollar of it for or against candidates in Democratic contests, which made it the largest outside spender in that year's Democratic primaries. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] The pattern of that first cycle set the template for the next two: large sums, concentrated in a handful of safe Democratic districts where the primary decided the seat, for candidates described as mainstream or moderate and against candidates on the party's left.

The races. In Maryland's 4th District the super PAC spent about $4.26 million against Donna Edwards and $1.7 million for Glenn Ivey, who won. In North Carolina it spent $2.13 million for Valerie Foushee in the 4th District and $2.1 million for Don Davis in the 1st, with $335,000 against his opponent Erica Smith; both won. In Texas's 28th District it spent $423,000 for Representative Henry Cuellar and $1.43 million against his progressive challenger Jessica Cisneros, and Cuellar won the runoff. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] Those were victories by the committee's own measure.

Not every race went its way. In Pennsylvania's 12th District it spent $2 million against Summer Lee, a state legislator, and $660,000 for her opponent Steve Irwin; Lee won the primary and the seat. In Michigan's 13th District it spent $2.74 million for Adam Hollier and $1.43 million against Shri Thanedar, and Thanedar won. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] The committee's first cycle established, from the start, that money could be outspent by voters.

Andy Levin. The most discussed race of the year was in Michigan's 11th District, where redistricting set two sitting Democrats, Haley Stevens and Andy Levin, against each other. Levin is Jewish. The super PAC spent $3.88 million for Stevens and $342,000 against Levin, and Stevens won. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] The Guardian reported that pro-Israel groups had poured millions into the race. [cite:guardian_levin_2022] The defeat of a Jewish member of Congress by a committee formed to support Israel made plain, in a way the other races did not, that the committee's line did not follow religion; it ran between candidates the committee judged reliable on its positions and those it did not.

The concentration. The Guardian's later analysis of the money reaching sitting members showed how concentrated the 2022 effort had been. The six largest recipients of pro-Israel donor spending in that cycle, all centrist Democrats who had beaten progressive opponents, accounted for about $25 million, some 42 percent of all pro-Israel donor money reaching current members; nearly $5.4 million had been spent backing Haley Stevens alone and about $2.8 million backing Don Davis. [cite:pm_guardian_2024] Democratic Majority for Israel, a separate pro-Israel PAC, claimed that its endorsed candidates won more than 80 percent of their races that year, a claim the Guardian did not verify. [cite:pm_guardian_2024]

One race in 2022 showed another feature of the method. In Minnesota's 5th District, Representative Ilhan Omar faced a primary challenger, Don Samuels. The super PAC did not advertise in its own name; it gave $350,000 to a separate committee backing Samuels, and the transfer became public only after the vote, which Omar won by about 2,500 votes. [cite:jewishinsider_udp_omar_2022] Money routed through other committees, which became a large share of the super PAC's spending by 2026, began here, in a single race and a single transfer.

The first cycle's results were mixed in the ledger and clear in their effect on the argument. Supporters of the super PAC pointed to its record of victories in open seats and its defeat of candidates they regarded as hostile to Israel. Critics pointed to the sums, to the defeat of a Jewish Democrat over policy, and to advertising that, in many of these races, said little or nothing about Israel at all, attacking candidates instead on other issues. [cite:punchbowl_udp_no_israel] Both camps agreed that the money had changed the conduct of Democratic primaries. The next cycle tested how far it could go against sitting members.
