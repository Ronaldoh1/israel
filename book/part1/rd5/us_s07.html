Golda Meyerson's Tour: The Money of Early 1948

A common account holds that the new state was built and defended chiefly on its own resources. The man who led it said otherwise. When David Ben-Gurion looked back on the winter of 1948, he credited a single fundraising campaign in the United States, and the woman who ran it, with making the state possible. The campaign lasted about two months. It was run by Golda Meyerson, later Golda Meir, and it depended on an American fundraising network that had been built over a quarter of a century and that, through one man, reached directly into the arms network of the same years.

A failed trip, and a substitute. After the partition vote of November 1947, the Jewish Agency needed money quickly. Its treasurer, Eliezer Kaplan, travelled to the United States to raise $7 million and failed. Ben-Gurion proposed going himself; the Agency's executive sent Meyerson instead. [cite:jta_golda_1948] [cite:reformjudaism_golda] She was then acting head of the Jewish Agency's political department, and she left for America in January 1948. [cite:jta_golda_1948]

The organizer. Her opening came through Henry Montor, executive vice-chairman of the United Jewish Appeal. Montor pressed the Council of Jewish Federations to give her a speaking slot at its assembly in Chicago, and it did. [cite:jta_golda_1948] He was not a newcomer to the cause's covert side. Three years earlier, in 1945, Montor had gathered seventeen wealthy businessmen to meet Ben-Gurion at Rudolf Sonneborn's apartment, the meeting that founded the arms-buying network described in [scene:s07d|the Sonneborn meeting]. [cite:jta_golda_1948] The same organizer thus stood behind both the network that bought weapons and the public appeal that paid for them.

The money. The figures come from later reporting, and they are given here as reported. The early response, according to reports, came to about $25 million in the first month. Chicago's Jewish federation took out a $5 million loan and handed the proceeds to her. [cite:reformjudaism_golda] [cite:jewishpress_golda_25m] She went on to some twenty cities, and when she returned on 19 March 1948 she was reported to have raised $50 million. [cite:reformjudaism_golda] [cite:jwa_golda_50m] By one comparison, that was several times Saudi Arabia's oil revenue for 1947. [cite:jwa_golda_50m] The money went largely to weapons, including the arms bought from Czechoslovakia. [cite:reformjudaism_golda] [cite:jwa_golda_50m]

Ben-Gurion's response is on record in his own words. He wrote in his diary that her success was "the only ray of light," and he said that history would record that "there was a Jewish woman who got the money which made the state possible." [cite:jta_golda_1948] [cite:yu_golda_archives] Her return date gives the diary entry its context. She came home on 19 March 1948, the same day the United States told the Security Council that partition could not be carried out by peaceful means, a retreat told in [scene:rd5_trusteeship|Washington's retreat]. The American government was stepping back from partition in the week that private American money arrived to pay for its defence.

The machinery behind the speech. The tour was a personal campaign, but it ran on structure. Ben-Gurion's tribute, in the record's own summary, credits American donors' money raised through a federation-and-appeal network that had been built since the 1920s and that, through Montor, overlapped directly with the arms network. [cite:jta_golda_1948] [cite:yu_golda_archives] Meyerson did not have to build an audience. The federations already existed in the cities she visited, and the Chicago assembly brought their leaders into one room. Kaplan had failed to raise $7 million; Meyerson, speaking through that apparatus, was reported to have raised about seven times as much in about two months.

The library's funding ledgers set the tour inside the larger flow of American money that year. The first chart below ranks the dollar sums in the record. The reported $50 million is the second-largest sum shown, behind only the $100 million Export-Import Bank loan of 1949, and well ahead of the $14.5 million paid for Czechoslovak arms in 1948.

The tour also overlapped with the United Jewish Appeal's regular campaign, so its sum cannot simply be added to the appeal's. The ledger, compiled from the JTA archive's year-end report on the 1948 campaign, records a record $150 million for the United Jewish Appeal that year, about $2.0 billion in today's dollars. Of that, $74.7 million went to the United Palestine Appeal, the share for Palestine and then Israel; $64.9 million to the Joint Distribution Committee for relief in Europe; and $10.4 million to resettlement in the United States. The Palestine share was just under half. The ledger notes that Keren Hayesod's own history lists defence among the things its money financed before statehood, and that in 1948 Meyerson's campaign money went largely to weapons.

In constant dollars the 1948 campaign stands at the top of a steep rise. The ledger converts the appeal's totals to 2025 dollars: about $0.32 billion in its first year, 1939; an average of about $0.35 billion a year during the war; then about $1.65 billion in 1946, about $1.59 billion in 1947 and about $2.0 billion in 1948. The peak year was the year of the war.

What the record supports, and what it does not. The documents support three things firmly. Kaplan's attempt failed; Meyerson's succeeded on a scale far beyond his target; and the money paid largely for arms, including the Czechoslovak arms told in [scene:cz_s01|the Czechoslovak pipeline]. They also support the point Ben-Gurion made himself: the claim that the state was built chiefly on its own resources does not survive his own account of where its money came from in the months before independence. What they do not support is any reading of the money as American government money. It came from private donors through private institutions, and in the same weeks the American government was retreating from partition and keeping its arms embargo in place.

The gaps are real. The $25 million and $50 million figures are reported totals, not audited accounts, and the sources do not say how much of the $50 million was new money and how much came through the United Jewish Appeal's own campaign. They do not break down what the money bought item by item, or how much of it reached Czechoslovakia. The comparison with Saudi oil revenue is given without its underlying figure. Ben-Gurion's verdict is a judgement, by the person best placed to make it, and not a measurement; other parts of this chapter show that weapons, diplomacy and the state's own forces each have a claim on the outcome of 1948.
