The Church of Aksum and Its 81 Books

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church keeps the largest Bible in traditional Christianity, and it keeps it without ever having been under Rome. That makes it the natural place to end Book Two's survey of excluded texts: two books that the Latin and Greek churches let fall out of use, 1 Enoch and Jubilees, are read in Ethiopia as scripture today. This section tells how the church began, how it relates to the councils described in earlier parts, and how it counts its canon, which turns out to be more interesting than the single number 81 suggests. The church's canon is its own, not a museum piece, and it is described here in the church's own terms first. [cite:vt27_eotc_books]

Aksum, Frumentius and Ezana

The account of how Christianity reached the kingdom of Aksum, in what is now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, comes from the Latin church historian Rufinus, who said he heard it from one of the people involved. Around 316 two young brothers from Tyre, Frumentius and Edesius, were travelling with their uncle Meropius when their ship put in at a Red Sea harbour. Local people killed the crew and took the boys to the royal court at Aksum. Frumentius became the king's secretary and treasurer, and after the king's death the widowed queen asked both brothers to help educate the young heir, Ezana. Frumentius encouraged Christian merchants to worship openly. When Ezana came of age, Frumentius went to Alexandria and asked its bishop, Athanasius, to send a bishop to Aksum. Athanasius consecrated Frumentius himself, traditionally in 328, though some historians prefer a date between 340 and 346. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius] Ethiopians remember him as Abba Salama, "Father of Peace", and Kesate Birhan, "Revealer of Light". [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius]

Ezana, who reigned from about 320 to about 350, became a Christian. His coins show it: the crescent and disk of the older South Arabian religion were removed and replaced with a cross. [cite:vt27_met_aksum] Around 356 the Roman emperor Constantius II, who favoured the Arian party that Athanasius opposed, wrote to Ezana and his brother asking them to replace Frumentius with an Arian bishop, Theophilos; the request was refused. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius] For about two centuries Christianity remained mainly the faith of the court; it spread more widely in the later fifth century, when monks from the eastern Roman provinces, remembered in Ethiopia as the Nine Saints, arrived after the Council of Chalcedon of 451. [cite:vt27_met_aksum] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

The Old Testament was translated into Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, from the Greek Septuagint, and later revisions show the influence of Hebrew, Syriac and Arabic texts. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] The two Garima Gospel books, kept at the monastery of Abba Garima, have been radiocarbon dated to about 390–570 and 530–660, which may make the older of them earlier than the Rabbula Gospels of 586, often cited as the oldest dated illustrated gospel book; one scholar, Curt Niccum, argues that most of the New Testament was in Ge'ez before the end of the fourth century. [cite:vt27_wiki_garima]

A church of the first three councils

"Tewahedo" is a Ge'ez word meaning "made one". It names the church's teaching that in Christ divinity and humanity are united in one nature, the position usually called miaphysite. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] The church belongs to the Oriental Orthodox communion with the Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Eritrean and Malankara churches, which, in the church's own words, "followed the teachings of the first three Ecumenical councils", Nicaea in 325, Constantinople in 381 and Ephesus in 431, and rejected Chalcedon. [cite:vt27_eotc_chalcedon] The popular claim that the Ethiopian church was "never subject to Nicaea" is therefore wrong: it holds the Nicene faith. Its independence is from Rome and from Chalcedon, not from the councils discussed in [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|Part Twenty-Two].

Until the mid-twentieth century the head of the church, the Abuna, was always a cleric of the Coptic Church, sent from the patriarchate of Alexandria. [cite:vt27_wiki_basilios] After an agreement of 13 July 1948, Basilios became the first Ethiopian to lead the church in 1951, and on 28 June 1959 the Coptic pope Kirillos VI installed him in Cairo as the first Patriarch of Ethiopia: the church became autocephalous, self-governing. [cite:vt27_wiki_basilios] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] The Eritrean church received its own autocephaly on 28 September 1993. The present patriarch, Abune Mathias, the sixth, has led the church since February 2013. The World Council of Churches gives the church about 60 million members worldwide; the Pew Research Center counted about 38 million in Ethiopia in 2017. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

How the church counts to 81

The church's own English website states the number plainly: "The Ethiopian Orthodox Church has 46 books of the Old Testament and 35 books of the New Testament that will bring the total of canonized books of the Bible to 81." [cite:vt27_eotc_books] Its New Testament of 35 is the familiar 27 books plus eight more: the four parts of the Sinodos (named on the site Sirate Tsion, Tizaz, Gitsew and Abtilis), the two books of the Covenant (called Dominos), the Ethiopic Clement and the Didascalia. Its Old Testament of 46 includes Jubilees, Enoch and the books of Meqabyan, and ends with "The Book of Josephas the Son of Bengorion", the Josippon, a medieval history of the Jews. It reaches 46 by grouping some books, such as 1 and 2 Samuel or Ezra and Nehemiah, under a single number. [cite:vt27_eotc_books]

That is not the only Ethiopian count. In 1974 the scholar R. W. Cowley set out to find the list that the church's most respected authorities treated as normative, and published the result in the journal Ostkirchliche Studien. He found that the corpus is "generally called 'the 81 books'" but that the number is reached in two different ways. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The broader canon, taken from the traditional Amharic commentary on the Fetha Negest, the church's book of law, counts 46 Old Testament and 35 New Testament books, as the website does. The narrower canon, used in the church's book of prayers and in the large Ge'ez–Amharic and Amharic Bibles printed by order of Emperor Haile Selassie, counts 54 Old Testament books and the 27 of the New Testament. It gets to 54 by counting the 39 books of the Hebrew Bible as 40, with Proverbs split into two books, Messale (chapters 1–24) and Tagsas (chapters 25–31), and adding fourteen more. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] A third arrangement, in a work called Kwakweha haymanot, counts 49 and 32. All three come to 81. [cite:vt27_cowley1974]

So 81 is the number that stays the same, and the books that make it up change with how they are counted. Cowley's conclusions are precise. The books in the narrower canon are "undisputed" in the church. The extra works of the broader canon are, from a traditional point of view, "equally canonical", but in practice they may be treated more as commentary, with authority derived from scripture. And claims by some European writers that the Ascension of Isaiah and the Shepherd of Hermas are canonical in Ethiopia "may have been true in the past", but he did not find them so in the present. [cite:vt27_cowley1974]

The number is older than either list. The Fetha Negest was compiled in Arabic around 1240 by an Egyptian Coptic jurist, Abu'l-Fada'il ibn al-Assal, and translated into Ge'ez in the fifteenth century; it remained Ethiopia's supreme law until 1931. [cite:vt27_wiki_fetha] Its chapter on scripture, which draws on the Sinodos, has a heading saying the books are 81. Cowley noted that "the list does not total 81": read as written, it gives 46 Old Testament and 27 New Testament books, 73 in all, and whether some entries count as one book or two is unclear. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The commentary tradition that grew up around the Fetha Negest is what filled the gap, and it is that tradition's count, 46 plus 35, that the church's website gives today. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] [cite:vt27_eotc_books]

Stated precisely, then: 81 is the church's count of its canon under either reckoning; the narrower canon is 54 plus 27, and the broader canon is not "81 plus nine" but a different 81, 46 plus 35, which contains more distinct works because it groups some Old Testament books and adds the Josippon and eight books of church order. The figure of nine extra works describes the difference between the two lists, not a separate total of 90. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] [cite:vt27_eotc_books] Part Twenty-Three's comparison of four Bibles gives both reckonings. [scene:vt23_four_bibles|Four Bibles]
