Eshkol's Answer: The Dimona Agreement, July–September 1963

When David Ben-Gurion left office in June 1963, the last letter Kennedy had written him was still in the State Department's files. It had never been delivered. The exchange that produced it, from the first instruction to Ambassador Barbour in March to the undelivered text of 15 June, is told in [scene:s11b|the battle of the letters]. This section follows the same demand into the hands of Ben-Gurion's successor, Levi Eshkol, and through the seven weeks it took his government to answer. The result was an agreement that the record, read to its end, shows to have been something narrower than Kennedy's language of "basic agreement" suggested.

The letter that was waiting. On 4 July 1963 the State Department sent telegram 193, carrying Kennedy's letter to the new prime minister. It repeated the text and the schedule of the letter that Ben-Gurion never received, and Ambassador Barbour delivered it on 5 July. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That schedule had been drafted in the State Department on 11 June: a visit to Dimona in mid-summer 1963, another in June 1964, and visits at six-month intervals after that. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The letter also carried the warning that Kennedy had first written into his letter of 17 May (another document gives 19 May): that the American "commitment to and support of Israel could be seriously jeopardized" without "reliable information" on Dimona. The editors of the declassified correspondence, William Burr and Avner Cohen, note that this language "remained in all subsequent letters." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The new prime minister therefore inherited, in his first weeks, both the request and the threat. Some of his advisers read the letter as "a real ultimatum, a crisis in the making." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Whether it was one is a question the sources answer in two ways. The editors call Kennedy's tone "near ultimatum," and Yuval Ne'eman (1925–2006) called the demands an "ultimatum." Against that, the letters named no sanction and no cut in aid; they said support "could be" jeopardized, and left the consequence unstated. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Both readings rest on the same text. What they disagree about is whether an unnamed consequence was a threat or a negotiating posture, and the documents do not record Kennedy saying which he meant.

Asking for time, 17 July. Eshkol did not answer the substance at once. On 17 July 1963 he sent an interim reply asking for time, and raised the idea of an arrangement under which the two governments would "consult in advance." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The phrase moved the question a step away from the one Kennedy had asked. Kennedy's letters asked to see the reactor on a fixed schedule; on one reading, consultation in advance described a relationship between governments, in which Israel would tell Washington of its intentions rather than show American scientists its facilities.

Seven weeks in the cabinet. The answer took about seven weeks. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] In that time Eshkol told Barbour that Israel "would do what it had to do for its national security," and Barbour reported to Washington that Eshkol "has not had an easy time in obtaining agreement of his colleagues." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The two sentences together describe the structure on the Israeli side. The decision was the prime minister's to sign, but not his alone to make: he needed his colleagues' agreement, and some of them, by Barbour's report, were hard to persuade. Eshkol's own phrase stated Israel's case in its own terms, the same case Ben-Gurion had pressed in May when he wrote of "another Holocaust" and asked for a security agreement with the United States: that Israel, facing an Arab Federation whose proclamation of 17 April 1963 called for "the liberation of Palestine," would decide for itself what its security required. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Kennedy, through the summer, remained "unwilling to make 'explicit' guarantees" of Israel's security. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Washington was asking for access without offering the guarantee that Ben-Gurion had asked for in return. Barbour's cables do not name which of Eshkol's colleagues resisted, or on what grounds, in the documents the editors reproduce.

The reply of 19 August. Eshkol's answer came on 19 August 1963. He agreed in principle to regular visits. He agreed to a visit in late 1963, before the reactor went critical, and to a follow-up visit in mid-1964. He made no commitment to inspections twice a year. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Set beside the schedule Kennedy had sent, the reply moved the first visit from mid-summer to late 1963, kept a second for mid-1964, accepted the principle of regularity, and declined the interval that would have given the principle its force. A visit every six months would have let American scientists watch a reactor as it came into operation. A visit before criticality and another mid-1964, with nothing fixed after that, left the frequency of later visits to be negotiated each time.

Kennedy's acceptance, 26 August. Kennedy chose to read the reply as a settlement. His letter of 26 August 1963 assumed "basic agreement on regular visits." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The word "basic" carried the weight. It allowed the President to treat the principle as conceded, while the interval, the one point Eshkol had declined, remained unagreed, as a State Department official noted in September.

The working arrangement, 16 September. The terms were filled in by officials. A memorandum of conversation dated 16 September 1963 recorded Israel's agreement to a "third visit prior to the activation of the reactor," to be "thorough" and to include the "plutonium separation plant." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The count reflected the visits already made: the editors call the improvised American visit of late September 1962 the second, and describe it as a "fiasco." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The memorandum's reference to a plutonium separation plant is the most specific item in the whole exchange. By Cohen and Burr's later account, Israel completed an underground separation plant in 1965. Rodger Davies, for the State Department, noted in the same memorandum that semi-annual visits had not been agreed. He also recorded the Israeli assurances of "peaceful purposes," which, he wrote, the United States was "inclined to believe." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The memorandum thus records three things at once: what had been gained, what had not, and the degree of trust on which the remainder rested.

The visit that came after the reactor. The visit before activation did not take place. According to the Israelis, the reactor went critical on 26 December 1963. The Hebrew-language website of the Israel Atomic Energy Commission gives July 1963 for the start of operations. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The two dates cannot be reconciled from the sources reached here, and they may not measure the same event: one names criticality, the other the start of operations. What both show is that the visit Eshkol had promised for before criticality did not happen before it, on either date. The first visit under the new arrangement took place on 18 January 1964. The editors write that it "may have been delayed because of Kennedy's assassination." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] They offer that as a possibility and draw no other connection; what the record shows about the weeks around 22 November is set out in [scene:s11c|22 November 1963].

What the first team found. The January 1964 team's report, written by Harry S. Traynor and dated 30 January 1964, found no suspicion of weapons work. It called Dimona "an ambitious project for a country of Israel's capabilities," put its cost in the range of $60 million, and recorded that the team had been told of 20 tons of uranium concentrate from a foreign source that was not named. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The team added a caution: "even such an intrusive technique would not prove that the facility was being used only for peaceful purposes." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The inspectors themselves, in other words, said in their first report that what they were doing could find evidence of weapons work but could not establish its absence.

The money in the report raises questions the report does not answer. Who paid the $60 million, through what budget and from what sources, is not in the material reached for this section. Nor is the origin of the 20 tons of uranium concentrate, whose foreign source the report does not name. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

What became of the arrangement. Visits followed every year from 1964. The sources do not agree on how many. The National Security Archive's 2019 briefing book speaks of "a series of six annual U.S. inspections" from 1964 to 1969, ended under Nixon. Cohen and Burr, writing in 2025, say seven visits "took place after Kennedy forced Israel to accept regular visits in 1963." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_majalla] The State Department's own historians record that under Kennedy Israel agreed to periodic visits by American scientists, that the Johnson administration kept insisting on them, and that the visitors found no evidence of a weapons program while suspicions continued. [cite:rs_cw5_frus6468_summary] Cohen and Burr, drawing on the later record, write that Israel completed a "super-secret underground separation plant" in 1965 and began producing weapons-grade plutonium in 1966; that the American scientists "never found direct evidence" of weapons work; and that the CIA's assessments were never shared with the inspection teams. [cite:rs_cw5_majalla] [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_findings] The visits under Johnson, and how much they could see, are the subject of [scene:s11d|Johnson's inspections].

Did Kennedy get what he asked? Two answers can be given from the same documents, and each has real support. The first is that, in principle, he did. Kennedy's own letter of 26 August described "basic agreement on regular visits," and visits followed every year from 1964. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] On this reading, an American president obtained from a new Israeli prime minister, against the resistance of some of his cabinet, a standing arrangement under which American scientists entered Dimona year after year.

The second answer is that, in substance, he did not. Eshkol never accepted visits twice a year with full access; the visit promised for before criticality did not take place before it; and the visitors never found direct evidence of the weapons work that, by Cohen and Burr's account, followed, including an underground separation plant completed in 1965. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_majalla] [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_findings] On this reading the agreement gave Washington the form of verification without its function: annual visits on terms the host controlled, by teams who, as their first report warned, could not prove a negative, and who were not given what American intelligence suspected. The difference between the two readings is largely the difference between the principle, which Eshkol conceded, and the interval and access, which he did not. Kennedy's word "basic" covered the gap between them.

The mechanism. The structure of the arrangement explains why both readings are possible. Washington's instrument was a letter, carrying a warning without a named sanction, from a government that refused Israel an explicit guarantee. Israel's instrument was consent: the prime minister and his cabinet decided whether, when and how often the visits happened, and the hosts controlled the site the visitors toured. The State Department could ask; it could not compel. The inspectors could look; they could not look at what they were not taken to. Within those limits, the outcome of 1963 can be read as a negotiated compromise in which each side kept the point it cared about most. Israel kept control of the timing and frequency of access, and the United States kept the right to come back.

The record leaves several things open. The cabinet debate that Barbour described is known only from his report; which ministers resisted, and why, is not in the documents reproduced here. The date on which the reactor began to operate is given two ways, and the sources reached do not resolve them. Whether the delay of the first visit to January 1964 had anything to do with the events of November is offered by the editors only as a possibility. The number of later visits is given as six and as seven. And how the reactor and its uranium were paid for and supplied is not answered in this material. What is not open is the shape of the agreement itself: Eshkol conceded regular visits in principle, declined the six-month interval, and the visit he promised for before criticality came after it.
