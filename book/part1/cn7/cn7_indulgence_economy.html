Selling Forgiveness: The Indulgence Economy, 1471–1517

The Western canon was broken in two by an argument about money. The argument has been told many times as the story of Martin Luther's conscience. It can also be told as a financial transaction among four parties, a pope, an archbishop, a bank and the people of Germany, and the documents support that version in detail. This section sets out the transaction. The next shows how it reached the list of books in the Bible.

An indulgence, in Catholic teaching, is a remission of the temporal punishment still due for sins already forgiven, granted by the church from what it calls its treasury of merit. [cite:vt31_brit_indulgence] For centuries indulgences had been attached to pilgrimages, crusades and good works, including gifts of alms. In 1476 Pope Sixtus IV extended the system to the dead: a person still living, he decreed, could obtain an indulgence for someone in purgatory. [cite:cn_nwe_indulgence] From then on, a living relative's gift could be presented as relief for a parent or child already dead. The market for the product grew to include every family that had lost someone.

Sixtus IV himself shows what the papacy of the period was. Born Francesco della Rovere, he was pope from 1471 to 1484 and was noted for his nepotism: six of the thirty-four cardinals he created were his nephews, and he gave his relatives wealth and offices. One of those nephews, Giuliano della Rovere, later became Pope Julius II. Sixtus filled the papal coffers by selling high offices and privileges, and his family was implicated in the Pazzi conspiracy of 1478 against the Medici of Florence. He also built the Sistine Chapel, which bears his name. [cite:cn_wiki_sixtus4]

The Medici had their turn. Giovanni de' Medici, son of Lorenzo the Magnificent, became pope as Leo X in 1513. Britannica describes him as "lavish in his spending not only of the church's money but also of his own", and says that he depleted the papal treasury; on 1 July 1517, after an attempt on his life, he named thirty-one new cardinals at once to secure the support of the College. [cite:cn_brit_leo10] The great project of the papacy in these years was the rebuilding of St Peter's Basilica, and in 1517 Leo X offered indulgences to those who gave alms toward it. [cite:cn_nwe_indulgence]

The German end of the transaction belonged to a prince of the Hohenzollern family. Albert of Brandenburg became archbishop of Magdeburg and administrator of the diocese of Halberstadt in 1513, and elector and archbishop of Mainz in 1514. Holding more than one see was contrary to church law, and to win Leo X's agreement he made a large contribution toward the rebuilding of St Peter's. [cite:cn_brit_albert] The money was borrowed from the banking house of the Fugger, 20,000 guilders from Jacob Fugger according to one account, and it was to be repaid through the sale of indulgences in Albert's territories, half the proceeds going to Albert and half to Leo X; the papal bull for the campaign was dated 31 March 1515. [cite:cn_brit_albert] [cite:vt31_wiki_albrecht] Albert's commissioner for the campaign, from January 1517, was the preacher Johann Tetzel, to whom a couplet was attributed that has outlived everything else he said: "As soon as the gold in the casket rings, the rescued soul to heaven springs". The Catholic Church has never taught that, and historians regard the couplet as Tetzel's exaggeration rather than official doctrine. [cite:vt31_wiki_tetzel]

On 31 October 1517 a professor at Wittenberg, Martin Luther, sent his Ninety-five Theses against the indulgence campaign to Albert himself. [cite:vt31_wiki_albrecht] Two of them name the mechanism directly. Thesis 27 rejects as "human doctrines" the preaching that "as soon as the money clinks into the money chest" a soul is released from purgatory. Thesis 28: "It is certain that when money clinks in the money chest, greed and avarice can be increased." [cite:vt31_wiki_tetzel] Luther did not yet attack the pope or the canon. He attacked the sale. Within four years the attack on the sale had become an attack on the authority behind it, and the authority behind it rested, in part, on a book that Luther would soon move out of his Bible.

The reform came later and from inside. After the Council of Trent, in 1567, Pius V forbade attaching the receipt of any indulgence to a financial act, including the giving of alms. [cite:cn_nwe_indulgence] It was a sweeping change, and it is the institution's own acknowledgement that the arrangement of 1517 had joined forgiveness to money in a way it could not defend. By 1567 half of Germany had left.
