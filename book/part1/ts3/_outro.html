Part Three established that the system later identified with the Scofield Bible was built in the British Isles in the 1820s and 1830s and was well rooted in America long before 1909. Darby, by his own account, separated the prophets' "kingdom" from the church in 1827 and taught at Geneva in 1840 that "The church is in relationship with the Father, and the Jews with Jehovah"; the claim that his rapture doctrine came from Margaret MacDonald's 1830 vision is unproven and, on her own text, unlikely. [cite:ts3_ice_darby] [cite:ts3_darby_hopes1] [cite:ts3_wiki_macdonald] [cite:ts3_strand_auss] His system did not make a Jewish return a precondition for Christ's coming: the rapture was "any-moment," and the restoration he described, in which "there will only be a remnant saved," belonged to the judgments that follow it; as late as 1905 Gaebelein called Zionism "a movement ... of unbelief." [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] [cite:ts3_darby_hopes10] [cite:ts3_wiki_gaebelein] Americans adopted the eschatology without the Brethren church order, and through Brookes's Niagara Creed of 1878, with its Israel "restored to their own land," the prophetic conferences from 1878, Moody and the Bible institutes, the system had institutions, a creed and a teaching network before any reference Bible existed. [cite:ts3_stunt_akenson] [cite:ts3_ak_niagara] [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] [cite:ts3_wiki_mbi] Part Four turns to the man who came out of that network and gave the system its most durable vehicle: Cyrus Ingerson Scofield, and what the record shows of his life.
