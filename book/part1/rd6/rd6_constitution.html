The Constitution That Was Deferred, 1948–1950

Most readers assume that Israel, like most democracies, has a written constitution. It does not, and it never has. The absence was not an oversight. It was the result of a decision taken in the state's first two years, by its first parliament, for reasons that were argued openly at the time and are documented. What was put in place instead, a promise to write the constitution chapter by chapter, is a compromise that, according to the sources used here, remains in effect.

The promise came first. The Declaration of Independence of May 1948 committed the new state to a constitution "adopted by the Elected Constituent Assembly not later than the 1st October 1948." [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] The date passed in the middle of the war. The assembly that was to write the constitution was elected in January 1949 and convened on 14 February 1949. Two days later it renamed itself the Knesset. [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] The body elected to draft the founding charter thus became the First Knesset, the state's ordinary legislature, and the constitution became one item on its agenda among many.

The man most responsible for what followed was David Ben-Gurion, the prime minister. He opposed a written constitution, and the account by Michael Dickerman gives two reasons. The first was that a constitution would place judicial limits on what the government could do. The second was a fear that a fight over religion, which a constitution would force into the open, would deepen the divide between religious and secular Israelis while the conflict with the Arab states was still unresolved. [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] The religious parties, for their part, had a demand that any constitution would have had to meet or defeat: they insisted that religious courts keep exclusive authority over matters of personal status. [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] On one reading, a constitution with guaranteed individual rights would have collided with that demand, and deferral avoided the collision.

The question was debated nine times. On 13 June 1950 the Knesset adopted a compromise moved by Yizhar Harari of the Progressive Party, known since as the Harari Resolution. It provided that the constitution "will be made up of chapters, each of which will constitute a separate basic law," and that "all the chapters together will constitute the constitution of the state." [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] As quoted, the resolution set no date for any chapter, and it did not say how a basic law would differ from any other law in the way it was passed or amended. The First Knesset, in the summary of a later account, deadlocked over what the constitution would contain, above all over the place of religion in public life, and chose to enact Basic Laws one at a time instead. [cite:gov_legalclarity]

The mechanism the state ran on in the meantime was a parliamentary one. Voters did not elect a head of government. They elected a parliament. All members of the Knesset are elected in a single nationwide district by proportional representation, so that the whole country forms one constituency and parties win seats in proportion to their share of the vote; under today's rules a party must clear a threshold of 3.25% to win any seats. No party has ever won an outright majority of 61 seats. [cite:knesset_idi] Every government has therefore been a coalition, in which the parties trade their votes for ministerial posts and policy concessions, and in which a small faction holding the balance can carry weight out of proportion to its size. [cite:gov_goodauthority] The religious parties' insistence on their demand in 1949 and 1950 fits that arithmetic, although the sources used here do not record the coalition bargaining of those years directly.

The head of state stood outside that bargaining. Israel's president is elected by the Knesset, not by the public, and under the present rules serves a single, non-renewable term of seven years. The office's formal powers are limited to granting pardons, tasking a member of the Knesset with forming a government, and representing the country abroad. The president has no part in day-to-day government, no veto over legislation and no command of the armed forces. [cite:president_time] Executive power rests with the prime minister and the cabinet. The procedure for forming a government, as it now stands, gives the member most likely to command 61 seats a mandate of 28 days, extendable by 14. If that member fails, the president may task another, or the Knesset can name a candidate with a 21-day window if 61 members support it; if every attempt fails, the country goes to new elections. [cite:pm_brookings]

The Harari Resolution's effect became visible only slowly. The first Basic Law, on the Knesset itself and its elections, was not passed until 1958. Others followed: on the military and its subordination to civilian government in 1968, on Jerusalem as the united capital in 1980, on human dignity and liberty in 1992, a partial bill of rights that the Supreme Court later used to justify judicial review, and in 2018 on Israel as the nation-state of the Jewish people, which enshrined Jewish self-determination as a national right without parallel language for Arab citizens. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] As the sources used here count them, there are 14 Basic Laws, covering the Knesset, the government, the military, the judiciary, land, Jerusalem and civil rights. The Supreme Court treats them as having constitutional weight, but they can be amended by a simple majority of 61 of the 120 members, unlike the United States Constitution, which needs two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of the states, or Germany's Basic Law, some provisions of which cannot be amended at all. The Nation-State Law passed by 62 votes to 55; the 2023 law on the "reasonableness" standard passed 64 to 0 because the opposition walked out. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] The tension between laws called constitutional and a parliament that can change them by an ordinary majority lay at the root of the judicial crisis of 2023, which is followed in [scene:ilgov_s01|the later history of the Basic Laws]. The coalition arithmetic described above produced five national elections between April 2019 and November 2022 [cite:gov_wiki], and in July 2023 the Biden administration invited President Isaac Herzog, rather than Prime Minister Netanyahu, to address a joint session of Congress and visit the White House, a choice TIME described as honouring "a largely ceremonial role with no executive authority" in place of "the man who runs the country." [cite:president_time]

Two judgments about 1950 remain open. The first concerns motive. Dickerman's account gives Ben-Gurion two reasons: fear of deepening the religious–secular divide in wartime, and reluctance to bind the government by courts. Read with the first in mind, deferral looks like a truce that kept a divided society together during a war; read with the second, it looks like a government keeping its own hands free. The record cited here documents both reasons and does not say which weighed more. The second judgment concerns consequences. Whether a formal constitution adopted in 1948 or 1950 would have prevented the crisis of 2023 is a question on which scholars disagree, and the record does not settle it. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] Nor does it settle how much of Israel's later instability belongs to the structure set up in these years and how much to the choices of individual leaders; political scientists contest that attribution, and this book treats the structure as a documented contributor, not a sole cause. [cite:pm_brookings]

What the record does settle is the sequence. A constitution was promised for a fixed date; the date passed; the body elected to write it became a parliament instead; and after nine debates that parliament resolved to write it in pieces, without a deadline. The pieces began to arrive eight years later, and the whole has never been completed.
