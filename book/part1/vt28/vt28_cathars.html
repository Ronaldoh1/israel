The Good Men: What the Cathars Taught

Eight centuries separate the last ancient Gnostic texts from the people the medieval Church called Cathars. No manuscript, named teacher or community record bridges that gap, and this part treats them for what the record shows: a medieval Christian dualist movement in northern Italy and southern France, first clearly identifiable around 1143, when the cleric Eberwin of Steinfeld reported a group with these beliefs at Cologne, and largely gone by about 1350. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] They are in this book because their teaching shared a structure with the ancient texts of Parts Twenty-Four to Twenty-Six (a good God beyond the world, a lesser power that made matter, a soul that must find its way back), and because what the papacy built to destroy them became the Inquisition. [scene:vt24_what_they_taught|What the Gnostics taught]

Names and origins

"Cathar" comes from the Greek katharoi, "the pure ones". It was a label used by Catholic theologians and later by inquisitors; the people questioned never used it of themselves. They called themselves Good Christians, Good Men and Good Women (bons hommes, bonnes femmes), and their opponents also called them Albigensians, after the town of Albi. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] The same Greek word had been used nine centuries earlier for the rigorist Novatian Christians, whom the Council of Nicaea called Cathari; the two groups are unrelated. [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided]

The movement's eastern roots lie in the Balkans. In tenth-century Bulgaria a priest named Bogomil led a dualist movement, known from a treatise written against it by the priest Cosmas around 970. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] Its ideas probably reached the West from Byzantium and the Balkans along trade routes, and the historian Malcolm Lambert judged that "a substantial transmission of ritual and ideas from Bogomilism to Catharism is beyond reasonable doubt". [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] One Bogomil text, the Interrogatio Iohannis (the "Secret Supper", a dialogue in which Christ answers John's questions about creation and the devil), was brought from Bulgaria to Italy around 1190 by Nazarius, Cathar bishop of Concorezzo. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars]

By the 1160s the Good Men of Languedoc had bishops. A bishopric existed at Albi around 1165; a council at Saint-Félix in the Lauragais, dated 1167 (the surviving charter's date and authenticity are debated), was attended by Nicetas, a Bogomil leader (papa), and was followed by bishoprics at Toulouse, Carcassonne and Agen, with Razès added around 1225. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] Britannica counts eleven Cathar bishoprics across France and Italy by 1200. [cite:vt28_brit_cathari] Each bishop was assisted by an "elder son", who usually succeeded him, a "younger son" and deacons. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] The popular picture of a church with no hierarchy does not fit this record.

What they believed

Cathar teaching began from two principles. A good God made the spiritual realm: souls, light, what is eternal. A second power, which Cathar texts and their opponents call Satan or the "king of the world" (rex mundi), made the visible, material world. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Cathars disagreed among themselves on how far this went. "Mitigated" dualists held that there was only one God and that Satan was a fallen creature; "absolute" dualists, whose position is set out in the Italian Book of the Two Principles (attributed to John of Lugio, mid-thirteenth century), held that good and evil were two eternal principles. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] Many identified the maker of the material world with the God of the Old Testament and rejected most of the Old Testament. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] This was a medieval Christian reading of Christian scripture; it was not a teaching about Jews, and it should not be read as one.

Human souls, in this teaching, were angels who had fallen and been trapped in bodies. They passed from body to body, man to woman and back, until they were released. Christ was a spirit sent by the good God; his body, suffering and death only appeared real (the view called docetism), so the cross was not a sign of salvation but an instrument of torture, and Cathars refused to venerate it. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] [cite:vt28_brit_cathari] Because sexual reproduction kept souls in the cycle of bodies, the fully committed renounced sex and every food they believed came from it: meat, eggs, cheese and milk. Killing animals was abhorrent, since they might carry souls. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]

Two grades: believers and the Good Men

Most people attached to the movement were credentes, believers, who married, worked, ate meat and lived ordinary lives while honouring the Good Men. A small elite, whom modern writers call the perfect (perfecti, women perfectae), had received the one Cathar sacrament and lived under its rules. [cite:vt28_brit_cathari] Believers greeted a Good Man with a ritual bow and blessing, the melioramentum, and many made the convenenza, an agreement to be given the sacrament when they were dying. [cite:vt28_wiki_perfect] [cite:vt28_lyons_traditio] The perfect travelled in pairs of the same sex, after Christ's instruction to the apostles, preached and cared for the sick, and many worked for their keep, often as weavers. [cite:vt28_wiki_perfect] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]

That sacrament was the consolamentum, the "consoling". It was a spiritual baptism by the laying on of hands, and it served at once as baptism, ordination and the forgiveness of sins. No bread or wine was used. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] [cite:vt28_wiki_consolamentum] The Occitan ritual preserved in a manuscript at Lyon describes the preparation: an elder taught the candidate the Lord's Prayer phrase by phrase, and the candidate promised never to eat or drink without saying it; the elder asked, "Do you give yourself to God and the Gospel?", and the candidate promised "that you will not lie, that you will not swear, that you will not kill", along with the rules on food and chastity; then the Good Men laid their hands on the candidate. [cite:vt28_lyons_traditio] Some rites placed the Gospel of John or the New Testament on the candidate's head. [cite:vt28_wiki_consolamentum]

Because the rules were hard to keep and a lapse undid the sacrament, most believers received the consolamentum only on their deathbed. [cite:vt28_brit_cathari] A few dying people who had received it then refused food until death, a practice called the endura. It is known from a small number of cases late in the movement's history; Catholic writers' claims that Cathars smothered people who recovered have little supporting evidence, and how common the endura was is disputed. [cite:vt28_wiki_consolamentum] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]

Women

Women could be believers and Good Women. The encyclopedia entries on the movement describe women perfectae who "performed evangelical, pastoral, and sacramental functions", including giving the consolamentum, and who ran communal houses where girls and widows were taught the faith. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Esclarmonde of Foix became a Good Woman after raising eight children. [cite:vt28_wiki_perfect] The record is not uniformly egalitarian: some later French Cathars taught that a woman's soul must be reborn as a man to be saved, and women perfect became rarer in Languedoc toward the end, though not in Italy. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]

The Roman clergy, the sacraments, tithes and oaths

The best single account of Cathar criticism of the Church comes from their prosecutor. Bernard Gui, inquisitor at Toulouse, wrote in his manual that they called themselves "good Christians, who do not swear, or lie, or speak evil of others", said "that they do not kill any man or animal", and called Catholic prelates, clerks and monks "Pharisees and false prophets, who say, but do not". He recorded that they denied the consecrated host could be Christ's body, called baptismal water a corruptible creation of the evil power and accused the clergy of selling it, said confession to sinful priests was useless, compared the cross to a gallows, and read the Gospels and Epistles in the vernacular. [cite:vt28_gui_fordham] Gui was hostile, but his list matches the Cathars' own ritual on the central points. [cite:vt28_lyons_traditio]

Oaths are well attested: the ritual has the candidate promise not to swear, Gui records the refusal, and Gordon Leff's encyclopedia entry lists "the swearing of oaths" among the things the perfect rejected. [cite:vt28_lyons_traditio] [cite:vt28_gui_fordham] [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] Tithes are thinner. The historian Beverly Mayne Kienzle writes that Catharism "exacted no tithes" and placed no economic restrictions on believers, but no Cathar text located for this part, and nothing in Gui's excerpt, addresses tithes directly, so the point rests on her secondary account. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] If it holds, the refusals reached beyond doctrine: a church that took no tithe offered an alternative to the clergy's main income, and a clergy that would not swear stood outside the oaths on which courts and feudal loyalty rested. That is an inference from the documents, not a statement any Cathar text makes. [cite:vt28_ency_cathars] [cite:vt28_gui_fordham] How the Church's tithe became a compulsory tax is the subject of Part Thirty-One. [scene:vt31_tithe|Tithe]

Whose voice survives

Most Cathar books were destroyed, and most of what is known comes from their opponents: polemical treatises, sermons, and above all inquisitors' interrogation records. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] A few of their own texts survive: the Book of the Two Principles, the Occitan ritual at Lyon, a Latin ritual, and the Bogomil Interrogatio Iohannis they used. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] [cite:vt28_ency_cathars]

That imbalance is the basis of a live dispute among historians. R. I. Moore, in The War on Heresy, argues that an organised "Cathar church" was largely "contrived from the resources of well-stocked imaginations" of churchmen, laid over local anticlericalism and a broader religious revival; Mark Pegg goes further on the crusade's character. Others, including Claire Taylor, Rebecca Rist and Lucy Sackville, accept that the Church exaggerated the threat but hold that a real heresy with an organised theology existed, pointing to the Cathars' own texts. The 2016 volume Cathars in Question, edited by Antonio Sennis, gathers both sides. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] This section has used the term "Cathar" because the record needs a name; it describes the Good Men's beliefs from their own surviving ritual wherever possible, and from their prosecutors only where the two agree.

The modern memory adds its own layer. The Aude department markets the region as "Pays cathare"; Otto Rahn, later an SS officer, tied Montségur to the Holy Grail in the 1930s; The Holy Blood and the Holy Grail linked the Cathars to the Templars. None of these links has documentary support. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]
