Suns Elsewhere

A widely repeated claim holds that Christianity suppressed the sun gods of the world wherever it went. The previous two sections showed that in Peru and Mexico the record supports a version of that claim, with named actors who were mostly agents of the Spanish crown and its Church. This section tests the claim in five more places: imperial Rome, Egypt, Portuguese India, the American Great Plains and Japan. In two of them the actor was not the Church or Rome at all, and in one the Christians were the suppressed party. It ends by grading the broadest form of the claim, that every saviour figure, Christ included, is a disguised sun god.

Rome: Sol Invictus and the temple laws

The Roman cult of Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, was given new imperial standing by Aurelian in 274, and Constantine kept Sol on his coins until about 325, more than a decade after he began to favour the Christians. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] Part Twenty-One tests the claims built on this, including the Sunday law of 321 and the origin of 25 December, and it is not repeated here. [scene:vt21_sun_and_title|Sun, Sunday and the Chief Priest's Title] What ended the sun cult as a public religion was imperial law. The laws of Theodosius issued in 391 and 392 forbade anyone to go to the sanctuaries or walk through the temples, closed the temples and banned all pagan sacrifice and worship. [cite:vt21_wiki_theodpagan] These were laws of the emperor, applied by his officials and, in places, by bishops and monks acting under them; Part Thirty-Two follows how they were enforced. [scene:vt33_the_closings|The Closings]

Egypt: the last temple on the Nile

Egypt's ancient religion was in large part a religion of the sun, with Ra at its centre for much of its history. No surviving law singles out Ra; what the record shows is the end of the temple system as a whole. Its last stronghold was the island of Philae at the southern frontier, sacred to Isis rather than to the sun. The last known inscription in Egyptian hieroglyphs was carved there in 394, and the last known demotic one in 452; a treaty of 452 between a Roman commander and the Blemmyes and Nobades, peoples from beyond the frontier, guaranteed their access to the goddess's image. [cite:vt35_wiki_philae] According to Procopius the temple was closed on Justinian's order by the local commander Narses, an act dated to about 535–537, and five of the island's temples became churches, the Isis temple rededicated to St Stephen. [cite:vt35_wiki_philae] The historian Jitse Dijkstra argues that organised worship at Philae had already ended in the fifth century, so that the order closed a temple already near its end. [cite:vt35_wiki_philae] The actor was an emperor and his general, not a pope.

Goa: Hindu worship banned

Hindu devotion to the sun, Surya, is part of Hindu worship in general, and in Portuguese Goa it fell under bans on Hindu worship in general. A Portuguese order to destroy Hindu temples was issued on 30 June 1541; King John III's order of 8 March 1546 directed officials to forbid Hinduism, destroy the temples and prohibit public Hindu feasts; some 160 temples on the island of Goa had been razed by 1566, and laws of 1567 made public Hindu worship unlawful. [cite:vt35_wiki_goainq] The actor was the Portuguese crown and its viceroys, pressed by clergy; the Goa Inquisition (1560–1812) that followed is told in the next part. [scene:vt34_goa|Goa] Hindu sun worship itself continued across India beyond Portuguese rule.

The Great Plains: a ban by the United States government

The Sun Dance, the great annual ceremony of many Plains peoples in which bands gather to renew their relation to the world and the powers above it, with participants offering their own bodily sacrifice as prayer for the community, was suppressed in the nineteenth century, but not by Rome. [cite:vt35_wiki_sundance] In his report for 1883 the US Secretary of the Interior, Henry M. Teller, called for an end to "the old heathenish dances, such as the sun-dance, scalp-dance, etc.", and the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, Hiram Price, issued rules for Courts of Indian Offenses on 30 March 1883 to enforce it, with punishments ranging from withheld food rations to imprisonment. [cite:vt35_tah_teller] [cite:vt35_narf_civ] No act of Congress authorised the code. [cite:vt35_narf_civ] Canada's Indian Act was amended in 1895 to outlaw dances involving wounding, a ban dropped in 1951. [cite:vt35_wiki_sundance]

Both countries' laws aimed at assimilation, including assimilation into Christianity; but the ban was a government's regulation, enforced by government agents. [cite:vt35_wiki_sundance] People kept the ceremony alive by holding it quietly, without the piercing, or with an agent's permission. [cite:vt35_wiki_sundance] In the United States the prohibition was not formally removed until Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act in 1978. [cite:vt35_narf_civ] The Sun Dance is held every year in many communities today. [cite:vt35_wiki_sundance]

Japan: the sun goddess, and the Christians suppressed

Japan's sun goddess, Amaterasu, appears in the country's earliest texts, the Kojiki of about 712 and the Nihon Shoki of 720, as the ruler of the heavenly realm and the mythical ancestress of the imperial house; her chief shrine is at Ise. [cite:vt35_wiki_amaterasu] When Christianity reached Japan in the sixteenth century, it was the newcomer that was suppressed. Twenty-six Christians were crucified at Nagasaki in 1597; a decree of 27 January 1614 abolished Christianity across Japan; missionaries were imprisoned; and every Japanese household was eventually required to register at a Buddhist temple. [cite:vt35_wiki_xjapan] [cite:vt35_ebsco_japan] After the Shimabara rebellion of 1637–1638 an estimated 37,000 people, mostly Christians, were killed. [cite:vt35_wiki_xjapan] Hidden Christians kept their faith for more than two centuries behind Buddhist forms, praying to the Virgin as "Maria Kannon"; when religious freedom was restored in 1873, about 30,000 came out of hiding. [cite:vt35_wiki_kakure] The record in Japan runs the other way: a state that honoured a sun goddess as its ancestress suppressed a Christian minority, for reasons historians describe as mainly political. [cite:vt35_ebsco_japan]

"Every saviour is the sun": the claim graded

The broadest version of the solar thesis holds that Christ himself is a sun myth: that the gospel story is an astronomical allegory copied from Horus, Mithras and other solar saviours. Its best-known modern form is the first part of the film Zeitgeist (2007), which drew on the writer Acharya S (D. M. Murdock). [cite:vt35_wiki_zeitgeist] The ancient historian Chris Forbes of Macquarie University found that the film's first part had no basis in serious scholarship or ancient sources and that "it is extraordinary how many claims it makes which are simply not true"; the journalist Michelle Goldberg described the film as a whole as steeped in conspiracy theories that were covertly antisemitic, which is a reason for care with it beyond its errors of fact. [cite:vt35_wiki_zeitgeist] The specific parallels most often cited, a Mithras born on 25 December with twelve disciples, are tested in Part Twenty-One and fail. [scene:vt21_sun_and_title|Sun, Sunday and the Chief Priest's Title]

What stands is narrower. Early Christians did use solar language and images for Christ: Justin Martyr explained around 155 that Christians gathered on "the day called Sunday", and a mosaic in the Tomb of the Julii beneath St Peter's, from around 300, is generally read as Christ pictured as the sun in his chariot. [cite:vt21_justin67] [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] Borrowing an image is documented; inventing a saviour from a sun myth is not. The record supports the first and not the second.

Interpretation, not finding: the suppressed sun as inner light

The Sophia platform reads the suppressed suns as more than lost cults. In this reading, the sun in the mystery traditions stood for an inner light that could be known directly, the goal of the ascent the mystery schools taught, and the destruction of solar temples, calendars and books was also the removal of a path of direct knowing in favour of a light that could only be approached through the Church's sacraments, as the Corpus Christi monstrance replaced the Punchao in Cusco. This is a reading of symbols, not a documentary claim; the record does not show any of the named actors aiming at an inner practice. The strongest counter-readings are that the Mexica and Inca sun cults were state religions of empire and sacrifice rather than private paths of illumination; that the Plains Sun Dance was suppressed by a secular government; that Andean and Maya communities folded the sun into their Christianity rather than losing it; and that Christianity itself was the suppressed light in Japan.

The pattern across eight peoples is therefore not one actor but several: Roman emperors, a Spanish army, Spanish and Portuguese crowns and their churchmen, a United States department and a Japanese shogunate. Where the Church acted, it acted through bishops, friars and royal patronage, and the same institution later displayed the Sun Stone and keeps the Codex Borgia. The next part turns from the suns to the mothers: the goddesses whose places, images and titles were taken over or replaced, beginning with Tonantzin and Guadalupe.
