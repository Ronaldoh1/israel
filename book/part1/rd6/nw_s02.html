The Network Map, 1897–1948

The American pro-Zionist effort of the half-century before 1948 is sometimes described as a loose crowd of unconnected sympathizers. The record describes something more structured. It shows a set of organizations tied to one another by founding, funding and coordination, reaching outward to named legislators, White House aides and donors. This section maps those ties by their distance from the centre and names the kind of each tie. It maps only documented organizational, political and financial relationships. It asserts no tie on the basis of identity.

At the centre stood the Jewish Agency and the World Zionist Organization, under Weizmann and Ben-Gurion. They set the movement's goals. A Justice Department order of 1962 alleged that the Jewish Agency funded the American Zionist Council, the postwar successor of the wartime lobbying body; the department later withdrew its demand that the Council register. [cite:azc_fara_doj]

One step out were the bodies tied directly to that centre by organization. The American Zionist Emergency Council, or AZEC, co-chaired by Abba Hillel Silver and Stephen Wise, ran the national campaign. The Zionist Organization of America had reached 147,000 members by 1918. Hadassah completed this first circle. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]

A second step out were bodies that those organizations created or coordinated. The American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine were both organized by the Zionist executive Emanuel Neumann, and one historian has called them "hardly autonomous." Henry Montor ran the United Jewish Appeal. The Sonneborn Institute, with its front company Materials for Palestine, was set up at Ben-Gurion's meeting in 1945, linking fundraising to arms. [cite:hnn_clark_truman]

The third step reached the political and financial endpoints. In Congress they included Senator Robert Wagner, chairman of the American Palestine Committee, and Senator Robert Taft, co-sponsor of the Palestine resolution; Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and Representative Hamilton Fish III, sponsors of the 1922 resolution; and Representative Sol Bloom, chairman of the Foreign Affairs Committee and a target of telegram campaigns. [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation] In the White House they included the aides David Niles, Clark Clifford and Max Lowenthal, and Truman's friend Eddie Jacobson, all of whom carried the Zionist case to the President. [cite:wiki_david_niles] Among donors they included Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann, whose $100,000 for Truman's 1948 campaign is documented, and the 1948 tour that raised about $50 million through Montor's network.

Two loops stand out in this structure, and they are the map's clearest finding. The first might be called the commitment loop. AZEC collected legislators' statements of support, printed them in its book America and Palestine, and mailed the book to every member of Congress. It then used the book to press for votes and platform planks. Those produced new statements, which fed the next round. Public statements became leverage for more statements.

The second is the Montor loop. Henry Montor gathered the donors for the 1945 Sonneborn meeting, out of which the arms network formed. Montor then secured Golda Meyerson's platform for 1948, and her tour raised about $50 million, largely for arms. One fundraiser's network ran from the arms meeting of 1945 to the tour of 1948.

The map does not show a single chain of command, and it should not be read as one. Some ties were organizational, some political, some financial, and the degree of control varied. What it does show is that the effort was not a loose crowd of sympathizers. It was a connected structure, built over decades, whose links can be named and sourced.
