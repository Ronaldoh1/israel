Medical Debt: When the Bill Outlasts the Illness

The taught Dream treats debt as something a family chooses: a mortgage for a house, a loan for a degree. Medical debt is different. Nobody shops for a heart attack, and a bill can arrive after a denial, a deductible or an out-of-network charge that the patient did not know about in advance. This section measures how much medical debt Americans carry, explains why the published totals differ so widely, follows a denied claim to a debt on a credit report, sets out the disputed link between medical bills and bankruptcy, and gives the state of the credit-reporting rules as of October 2026.

Three numbers that measure different things

Three figures circulate, and they are often confused. The first comes from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the federal agency that supervises credit reporting. In March 2022 it estimated that $88 billion in medical bills sat on Americans' credit reports as debts in collections, meaning bills that a hospital or doctor had passed to a collection agency. As of mid-2021, 58 per cent of all collection debts on credit records were medical, and medical collections appeared on 43 million credit reports. [cite:ad8_cfpb_88bn] This number counts only debts that reached a collector and were reported to a credit bureau.

The second figure is larger. Researchers at Peterson-KFF, using the Census Bureau's Survey of Income and Program Participation, a household survey, found that Americans owed at least $220 billion in medical debt in December 2021. About 20 million adults owed more than $250, about 14 million more than $1,000 and about 3 million more than $10,000. [cite:us16_pkff_debt] This counts what households report owing for medical care, whether or not it has gone to collections, so it includes bills on payment plans that will never appear on a credit report.

The third figure is a share of people rather than a sum of dollars. KFF's Health Care Debt Survey of February and March 2022 found that 41 per cent of adults currently owed money for medical or dental care, using the broadest definition of the three: past-due bills, payment plans with providers, loans, credit card balances run up on medical bills and money borrowed from family or friends. [cite:ad8_kff_debtsurvey] The widely repeated statement that more than 100 million Americans carry health care debt comes from KFF Health News's reporting built on this kind of survey evidence, not from the CFPB. [cite:ad8_kffhn_100m] The three figures should never be added together or swapped for one another. They rise in size because each counts more: debt in collections, all medical debt owed, and any health care debt, including debt that has been moved onto a credit card.

Who carries it, and what it does

Medical debt is unevenly spread. In the Census-based data, 13 per cent of Black adults reported medical debt, against 8 per cent of white adults and 3 per cent of Asian adults; 11 per cent of people uninsured all year had it, against 8 per cent of those insured all year. [cite:us16_pkff_debt] The CFPB found past-due medical debt among 28 per cent of Black and 22 per cent of Hispanic people, against 17 per cent of white and 10 per cent of Asian people, and found it more common in the Southeast and Southwest, partly because states there did not expand Medicaid. [cite:ad8_cfpb_88bn] These gaps follow documented differences in insurance coverage, income and the state where a person lives; they are not traits of any group, and Part Thirteen follows the wider record. [scene:ad13_black_americans|Black Americans and the Dream] Insurance does not remove the risk. Among the underinsured, 44 per cent reported medical or dental debt, and people in employer and marketplace plans reported higher rates of medical debt than people covered by Medicaid. [cite:ad8_cmwf_underins24]

The consequences reach past the bill. Of adults with health care debt in KFF's survey, 63 per cent had cut back on food, clothing or other basic household items in the past five years because of it, and 18 per cent said they did not expect ever to pay it off. [cite:ad8_kff_debtsurvey] Some patients move the bill onto a medical credit card. These often carry deferred interest: no interest during a promotional period, but interest charged back to the first day if the balance is not cleared in time. The CFPB estimated that Americans paid about $1 billion in deferred interest on medical credit cards and similar financing from 2018 to 2020, at rates up to 27 per cent; in 2013 it had ordered CareCredit, a medical credit card issued by Synchrony Bank, to set up a $34.1 million fund to repay patients. [cite:ad8_kffhn_medcards]

The disputed link to bankruptcy

A widely repeated claim holds that medical bills are the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States. The record is contested, and the two best-known studies measure different things. David Himmelstein and colleagues surveyed 910 people who filed for bankruptcy between 2013 and 2016 and reported, in the American Journal of Public Health in 2019, that 66.5 per cent cited medical issues, either bills or income lost to illness, as contributing to their bankruptcy. That figure counts contribution as filers reported it, not cause. [cite:us22_himmelstein2019] Their work appears in peer-reviewed journals and is also published by Physicians for a National Health Program, an organisation that campaigns for a single-payer system; that link is part of the record when the number is weighed. [cite:us16_pnhp_reply]

Carlos Dobkin, Amy Finkelstein, Raymond Kluender and Matthew Notowidigdo took a different route. Linking California hospital records from 2003 to 2007 to credit and bankruptcy records for adults, they estimated in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2018 that hospitalisations cause about 4 per cent of bankruptcies among non-elderly adults, and argued that survey shares overstate causation because many filers have medical bills that would not by themselves have led to bankruptcy. [cite:us22_dobkin2018] [cite:us16_mit_bankruptcy] Himmelstein, Woolhandler and Senator Elizabeth Warren replied that the method undercounts medical bankruptcy, and the dispute remains unresolved. [cite:us16_pnhp_reply] Both findings can be true at once: medical costs can be present in most bankruptcies while a hospital stay is the decisive cause in a small share. Neither number belongs in a headline alone.

Credit reports: voluntary changes, a vacated rule and state laws, status as of October 2026

The CFPB's own research found that medical debt on a credit report predicts future repayment less well than debts such as loans and credit cards. [cite:ad8_cfpb_88bn] In March 2022 the three national credit bureaus, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, announced that paid medical collections would be removed from reports from 1 July 2022, that unpaid medical debts would wait a year rather than six months before appearing, and that medical collections under $500 would be dropped in the first half of 2023. Together, they said, these steps would remove nearly 70 per cent of medical collection entries. [cite:ad8_cra_2022]

In January 2025 the CFPB went further, with a rule that would have barred most medical debt from credit reports used by lenders; it estimated the rule would remove $49 billion of debt from the reports of 15 million people and raise their credit scores by about 20 points on average. On 11 July 2025 Judge Sean Jordan of the federal court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated the rule, holding that the Fair Credit Reporting Act does not give the CFPB that power, in a case brought by industry groups including the Consumer Data Industry Association. [cite:ad8_ap_vacatur] [cite:ad8_nclc_states] On 28 October 2025 the CFPB issued an interpretation stating that federal law pre-empts state laws on what credit reports may contain, while noting that the interpretation is not binding and that courts decide the question. As of September 2026, fifteen states and the District of Columbia had laws restricting medical debt on credit reports: California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia and Washington. [cite:ad8_nclc_states] Part Nine follows how credit reports and scores shape the rest of a family's finances. [scene:ad9_credit_and_scores|Credit and scores]

Where it works as promised

Some protections did arrive. The bureaus' 2022–2023 changes removed most medical collection entries from credit reports, and a growing number of states have added their own limits. [cite:ad8_cra_2022] [cite:ad8_nclc_states] Most insured Americans do not carry medical debt, and the best causal study finds that hospital stays drive only a small share of bankruptcies among working-age adults. [cite:us16_pkff_debt] [cite:us22_dobkin2018] The insured are less exposed than the uninsured, 8 per cent against 11 per cent in the Census-based data, so wider coverage reduces the risk. [cite:us16_pkff_debt]

Interpretation, not finding: the system inside the system

The Sophia platform reads American health care as the clearest case of its broader thesis: a system inside the Dream's system that converts illness into a revenue stream at each step, from the premium deducted from pay to the deductible, the denial, the financing charge and the collection entry, so that the cost of being sick is carried by the household while the institutions in between are paid in every case. In this reading, medical debt is not a failure of the machine but one of its outputs, and the employer link built in 1943 and 1954 keeps workers dependent on the job that carries their coverage.

The strongest counter-readings are documented. Most claims and most authorization requests are paid, and the share of national health spending paid directly out of pocket has fallen. [cite:ad8_kff_denials24] [cite:us16_wb_oop] The causal research of Dobkin and colleagues finds that hospital bills alone cause a small share of bankruptcies. [cite:us22_dobkin2018] The industry's stated purposes, checking that costly care is needed and using buying power to win discounts, are real functions, and its trade bodies dispute both the hospital-price benchmark and the regulators' account of PBMs. [cite:ad8_bcbsa_pa] [cite:ad8_rand_hosp] [cite:ad8_pcma_resp] And the record shows the system being changed through ordinary politics: coverage expanded in 2010, credit-reporting practice changed in 2022, PBM pay was rewritten in 2026. Equally, the 2025 law will reduce coverage, and the federal rule on medical debt was struck down. The documented finding is narrower than the interpretation: specific mechanisms, named above with their numbers, move the cost of illness onto households, and each can be measured and changed.
