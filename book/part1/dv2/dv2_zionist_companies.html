The Movement's Companies Were London Companies

A political movement without a state cannot issue currency, levy taxes or borrow on its own credit. What it can do is form companies, and the Zionist movement did so from its second year. The striking fact about those companies, set out across the earlier volumes of this library and gathered here, is where they were formed. The movement's bank, its land fund and its permanent fundraising arm were each created as English legal entities in London, and two of them kept their London headquarters for decades. If London was a financial parent of the Jewish state, this is where the relationship is written down most plainly: not in the Corporation's minute books, but in the register of English companies.

The bank. The Second Zionist Congress, meeting in 1898, decided to create a financial institution of its own. On 20 March 1899 the Jewish Colonial Trust was incorporated in London under the English Companies Acts, with an authorized capital of £2,000,000 in shares of £1 each. [cite:jct_wiki] It was a company selling shares, not a charity collecting gifts, and the shares were priced low and could be paid in installments so that working people across Europe could become owners. Theodor Herzl bought the first 1,000 shares himself. [cite:jct_wiki] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] [cite:bia_jewish_colonial_trust] The Trust fell well short of its target: about 250,000 shares were sold, raising about £250,000, one-eighth of the authorized sum. Control, meanwhile, stayed with a small founding group through a block of founders' shares carrying extra votes. [cite:jct_wiki] [cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust] [scene:s04c|The Trust's own history] follows the design and the shortfall in detail.

A bank incorporated in London still had to reach Palestine. On 27 February 1902 the Trust founded the Anglo-Palestine Company, with initial capital of about £50,000, as its operating arm. It opened its first branch in Jaffa in 1903, financed land purchase, agriculture and settlers' credit cooperatives, and by 1914 had branches in Jerusalem, Beirut, Hebron, Haifa, Safed, Tiberias and Gaza. In 1909 it lent to the Ahuzat Bayit association, which used the money to build the first neighborhood of what became Tel Aviv. [cite:apc_wiki] [cite:encyclopedia_jewish_colonial_trust] It became the Anglo-Palestine Bank around 1930 and Bank Leumi in 1950–51, one of the major banks of the State of Israel. [cite:apc_wiki] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] The parent company in London raised the capital; the subsidiary in Jaffa put it to work.

The land fund. The Jewish National Fund was established at the Fifth Zionist Congress in Basel in 1901, four years after Hermann Schapira had first proposed a national land fund at the First Congress in 1897. Its legal home, too, became London. On 8 April 1907 it was incorporated there as an association limited by guarantee, under the name "Juedischer Nationalfonds (Keren Kajemeth Le Jisroel), Limited." The London company was renamed in October 1921 and again in January 1926, and it was dissolved and reconstituted as an Israeli company only in 1953, five years after the state was founded. [cite:dv2_wiki_jnf] An English company with the Hebrew name Keren Kayemeth Leisrael Limited still appears on the register at Companies House, number 00092825, incorporated on 8 April 1907 and recorded as dormant since 2009. [cite:dv2_powerbase_kkl]

From its beginning the fund leased its land on long terms rather than selling it. By the eve of statehood it had acquired 936,000 dunams, and other Jewish bodies and private buyers about 800,000 more. In 1948 the fund held 54 percent of the land owned by Jews in Palestine, which was a little under 4 percent of all the land of Mandatory Palestine. [cite:dv2_wiki_jnf] Those two proportions, measured against different totals, are easy to confuse, and they are best read together: the fund was the largest Jewish landholder, and Jewish landholding as a whole was a small share of the country. The land system the fund built, and how most of the territory of the new state came instead through the war of 1948, is set out in [scene:s04j|the land-purchase pipeline] and [scene:s07|the Mandate].

The fundraising arm. The third institution was born in London in the same way. On 24 December 1920, at a Zionist conference in London, the World Zionist Organization founded Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund, as its permanent fundraising arm. It was registered in Britain in 1921, aimed to raise £25,000,000 in five years, accepted donations from non-Zionists, and asked supporters to pledge a fixed share of their income. It financed immigration, settlement, defense and water works, and invested in the Palestine Electric Company and the Palestine Potash Company. In 1926 it moved its headquarters from London to Jerusalem. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] [scene:s04i|Keren Hayesod's founding] gives its first campaigns, and [scene:us_s01|the first American campaigns] the fight over who controlled its American money.

Why London? The sources used here do not record the founders' own reasons for choosing English incorporation, and the reader should not supply one. What they do show is how the choice fitted what followed. From 1917 the British government was the power that had promised the movement a national home, and from 1920 it was the power that governed the territory in which the companies worked. [cite:balfour_letter] [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] By then the movement's bank and land fund were already English companies, and its new fundraising arm was founded at a conference in London. One reading is that an English company was the natural vehicle for a movement that hoped for, and then had, a British patron. That is an inference from the sequence of dates, not a documented motive, and it is offered as one.

The fifth line of the money chain above needs a word of explanation. Under the Ottoman land system, title to much farmland was registered in the names of village heads, merchants and absentee families, while the peasants who worked it went on doing so as tenants. When such land was sold, the record shows tenant cultivators being evicted, with compensation on terms set by the purchaser; the documented evictions of this kind belong mainly to the 1920s. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] That mechanism, and the disputes over its scale, are told in [scene:ot_s01|the Ottoman land code]. It is noted here because a money chain has two ends, and the companies chartered in London financed one of them.

What this section establishes is narrow and solid. The three central financial institutions of the Zionist movement before 1948 were formed in London as English legal entities: a bank in 1899, a land fund in 1907 and a fundraising arm in 1920. One moved to Jerusalem in 1926, and the land fund remained an English company until 1953. None of them was an arm of the City of London Corporation, and none was created by the British government. They were the movement's own companies, built on the legal and financial infrastructure that London offered to anyone who could use it. That use of London's legal machinery is the first documented sense in which London was a parent.
