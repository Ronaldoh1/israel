Edmond de Rothschild's Colonies, and His Refusal of Herzl

The colonies that grew through the gaps in the Ottoman rules had a patron in Paris. A durable story holds that the Rothschilds funded Zionism from the beginning, and that Herzl's movement was their project. The record separates two things that story fuses. One member of the family, Baron Edmond de Rothschild of the Paris house, paid for and ran Jewish agricultural colonies in Ottoman Palestine for some fifteen years before Herzl wrote a word about a Jewish state. When Herzl came to him in 1896 asking for backing for a political movement, Edmond refused. The First Zionist Congress met in Basel the following year without him [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild]. Both facts are documented, and the history of the period cannot be told accurately without both.

Edmond's money came from the Paris branch of the family bank, de Rothschild Frères, which his father, James Mayer Rothschild (1792–1868), the youngest of Mayer Amschel Rothschild's five sons, had built into the largest private bank in France, as [scene:s01|an earlier section] describes. When James died in 1868 the bank passed principally to his sons Alphonse and Gustave. Edmond, the youngest, was 23. He inherited substantial capital but not the management of the bank [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. That is the background to what he did with his inheritance fourteen years later.

Edmond was born in 1845 at Boulogne-Billancourt and trained in mining engineering and art collecting rather than banking [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. His turn to Palestine came out of the crisis of 1881–82. After the assassination of Alexander II in 1881, pogroms in the Russian Empire sent thousands of Jewish refugees westward; some reached Palestine and met agricultural failure and disease. In 1882 Rishon LeZion, a new settlement south of Jaffa, was collapsing within months of its founding for want of water and of agricultural knowledge. Rabbi Samuel Mohilever appealed to Edmond, and Edmond accepted the appeal and began to fund the colony [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. From there his commitment widened. In 1883, according to a reference summary, the settlers' central committee transferred Zikhron Ya'akov's assets to him [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. Over the following years he financed and administered roughly thirty Jewish agricultural colonies, including Rishon LeZion, Zikhron Ya'akov and Rosh Pinna, and earned the Hebrew title HaNadiv HaYadua, "the known benefactor" [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] [cite:betweenmyths_rothschild_israel]. The sources date this work differently: one gives 1882 to 1895, another 1883 to 1900 [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] [cite:schama_two_rothschilds].

The principal settlements are documented in Ran Aaronsohn's Rothschild and Early Jewish Colonization in Palestine (2000) and Simon Schama's Two Rothschilds and the Land of Israel (1978). Two of them carry the family in their names. Zikhron Ya'akov, "in memory of Jacob," was named for Edmond's father James, whose Hebrew name was Jacob; Mazkeret Batya was named for his mother, Betty, in Hebrew Batya [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. The naming was not concealed; the same account says it appears on the settlements' founding charters. The patronage was public and celebrated in the 1880s and became part of the settlements' own identity, which is the plainest evidence that this was open philanthropy and not a hidden hand.

The colonies were run on Edmond's terms, through an administrative system of his own, and the colonists complained of his administrators [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild]. That is the mechanism to hold on to. The money did not flow to a movement or a committee that set its own course. It flowed from a private fortune in Paris to officials employed by the donor, who managed the colonies on his behalf. The settlers depended on him, and the record of their complaints shows that the dependence chafed.

How much he spent is not settled. One detailed account gives approximately $5–6 million in period dollars, roughly $200 million in modern terms, and estimates it at about 40 percent of all early capital invested in the Jewish settlements, calling Edmond the single largest private funder of pre-state Jewish settlement. None of these figures carries a source, and they should be read as unsourced estimates, not as findings. The compiled record of pre-state funding in this library is more cautious. Its table of every documented money stream opens with Edmond's colonies, from 1882 to 1924, and records the amount as "Not totaled in the record," with what the money bought given as "~30 settlements, later handed to PICA (1924)." Every later stream in that table, from the Jewish Colonial Trust of 1899 to the Export-Import Bank loan of 1949, begins after Herzl's movement was founded; Edmond's is the only one that predates it.

In 1896 Edmond instructed the Jewish Colonization Association, the society founded by Baron Maurice de Hirsch in 1891 and described in the next section, to begin lending to colonists [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild]. The larger step came three years later. At the end of 1899 he transferred title to his colonies to the Association, together with fifteen million francs [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_pica]. The Encyclopaedia Judaica dates the handover to 1900 and gives the grant as fourteen million francs, covering about 250,000 dunams and twelve settlements, most of them close to supporting themselves [cite:rs_rd0_ej_edmond]. The two accounts differ by a few weeks and a million francs; they agree on what happened. The loans of 1896 and the transfer of 1899–1900 were two stages, first credit and then ownership and administration. Hirsch, who had died in 1896, was a separate philanthropist and no relation, and Edmond did not take over his Association: he handed his colonies to it [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_pica].

Into this world Herzl came in the summer of 1896, months after publishing Der Judenstaat. He was asking for something different in kind from what Edmond had been doing: a state secured by charter, not colonies secured by purchase. Edmond declined. Herzl's diary for 22–26 July 1896 records his "rude telegram to Edmond Rothschild" and his resolve that "from Rothschild's negative attitude I must salvage everything"; he turned instead to organizing the Basel Congress of 1897 [cite:herzl_diaries_vol2_archive]. A second account gives a different sequence. In it Edmond first refused to receive Herzl in Paris; then, in July 1896, the two met through an intermediary, and Edmond told Herzl to his face that he opposed political Zionism and would not fund it, an exchange this account also places in Herzl's diary [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. The two versions agree on the outcome and on the month. They differ on whether there was a meeting, and the diary entry quoted in the first account, which speaks of a telegram, does not by itself settle the point.

Why did he refuse? The same detailed account gives two reasons and says both appear in his correspondence. Edmond preferred quiet philanthropic settlement to political statehood, believing that Jewish safety could be advanced by slow agricultural facts on the ground without a statehood project that would attract international opposition. And he thought that political and diplomatic Zionism, with its public meetings with the Sultan and its talk of territorial concessions, would alienate the Ottoman rulers whose consent his settlements needed, and so poison arrangements that were working [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. The second reason fits what the Ottoman government was doing in the same years, when it was restricting Jewish immigration and land purchase in Palestine, as described in [scene:rd0_limits|the section on Ottoman limits]. The correspondence itself is not quoted in the material available here.

Quiet, but not hidden

One recent documentary on the family describes Edmond slipping into Palestine "in secret" in 1887, so as not to draw the attention of the Ottoman authorities, and the image has travelled. The record is more ordinary. Edmond and his wife arrived at Jaffa on 2 April 1887 on a regular Austrian steamer, having left their own yacht at Alexandria, and travelled as ordinary passengers. The Hebrew newspaper Hamelitz reported their arrival, and the "secret," in the words of the memorial foundation that keeps his record, was out at once. He toured Mikveh Israel, Jerusalem and six colonies, and came back four more times: about 1893, in January 1899, in 1914 and in May 1925, when he was nearly eighty [cite:rs_rd0_rh_visits]. Why he chose to travel without announcement is not stated in the sources found; the documentary's reason, avoiding the Ottomans, is its own reading.

What is documented is the setting that made discretion sensible. From 1882 the Ottoman government barred foreign Jews from settling in Palestine and, from 1892, from buying state land there, rules the section on Ottoman limits sets out [scene:rd0_limits|in full]. Those rules were weakened in practice in three ways that Neville Mandel's study of the Ottoman documents names: the European powers refused to accept them and their consuls protected Jews who held foreign papers; officials took bribes that "could not be detected"; and Jews who entered as pilgrims could not, in practice, be made to leave [cite:rs_rd0_mandel1974]. Edmond's colonies grew inside that gap. But they did not grow behind the government's back. In 1893, Mandel records, Edmond obtained building permits after an agreement with the Porte on how many houses could be built in the colonies he supported and how many settlers could live in them [cite:rs_rd0_mandel1974]. A patron who negotiates house counts with the Sultan's government is working with it, not hiding from it. The accurate description is the one his own correspondence gives for his method, as reported above: quiet settlement, without political announcements, kept below the level that would provoke a ban.

That distinction matters for how the colonies are judged. Quiet was a strategy, and it worked: it bought land and time that a public political campaign would have cost, which is exactly why Edmond told Herzl that the campaign would ruin it. It also meant that the people already living on and around the land, Palestinian Arab villagers and the long-established Jewish communities of the four holy cities alike, had no say in a programme designed in Paris and administered by his officials. Both facts stand on the record together.

The refusal did not end with 1896. Edmond did not attend the Basel Congress in 1897 and continued to fund settlement through his own channels, not through the Zionist Organization Herzl founded there [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. What happened after Herzl's death in 1904 is told two ways. One reference account says he remained separate from the Zionist movement and rejected institutional and ideological Zionism to the end [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild]. The Encyclopaedia Judaica tells a later convergence: after his 1914 visit he drew closer to the Zionist Organization, telling Chaim Weizmann that without him the Zionists could have done nothing, but without the Zionists his work would have been dead; during and after the war he aided Weizmann and Sokolow, above all around the Balfour Declaration; and when the Jewish Agency was enlarged in 1929 he was chosen its honorary president [cite:rs_rd0_ej_edmond]. The two accounts can both be partly right: the man who refused Herzl's charter politics in 1896 lent his name and help to Weizmann's diplomacy twenty years later, without ever becoming a party leader. What is not disputed is the order: his philanthropic colonization came first, and political Zionism, which produced the Congress, the Organization and eventually the state, was launched after he had refused to back it.

The colonies outlasted the century and their founder. In 1924 Edmond reorganized the Association's Palestine branch as the Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, PICA, and appointed his son James its president for life [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_pica] [cite:rs_rd0_rh_james]. PICA continued land purchase and settlement through the British Mandate [cite:betweenmyths_rothschild_israel]. One detailed account credits it with about 500,000 dunams (125,000 acres) by the 1930s [cite:schama_two_rothschilds]. Its end is now clear. PICA was wound up in 1957, the year James died; his will instructed it to transfer most of its land in Israel to the Jewish National Fund, and on 31 December 1958 it vested its rights to land in Syria and Lebanon in the State of Israel [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_pica]. The family's philanthropy in Israel continued under a different name, Yad Hanadiv, which James's widow Dorothy chaired [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_dorothy].

The next generation: James and Dorothy

The Rothschild who carried Edmond's work into the Mandate and the state was not a banker but his son. James Armand de Rothschild (1878–1957) served in the French and then the British army in the First World War, was posted to Palestine, and near the war's end recruited volunteers for the Jewish battalions under General Allenby. His father introduced him to Chaim Weizmann, and the two worked closely together. He was involved in founding the Hebrew University, sat in the British House of Commons for sixteen years, presided over PICA from 1924 and left the money for Israel's parliament building, the Knesset, completed after his death [cite:rs_rd0_rh_james]. His wife Dorothy (1895–1988) saw the Knesset gift through, chaired Yad Hanadiv, the family's charities in Israel, gave the building for Israel's Supreme Court, and remained a close friend of Weizmann [cite:rs_rd0_wiki_dorothy]. Edmond himself was buried first in Paris; in 1954 his remains and his wife Adelheid's were carried to Haifa aboard a naval frigate, met with a 19-gun salute, and reburied at a state funeral at Ramat HaNadiv, "the Hill of the Benefactor," with David Ben-Gurion giving the eulogy [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild]. Ben-Gurion called him "the builder of the Jewish Yishuv" [cite:betweenmyths_rothschild_israel]. The state funeral sometimes dated to his death in 1934 took place twenty years later, after there was a state to hold it.

The family's roles in this history are often merged into one. Walter Rothschild of the London branch was the addressee of the Balfour letter in 1917; he was a close friend of Weizmann and worked with him on the draft formula, which Balfour had asked the two of them to submit in June 1917 [cite:rs_rd0_jvl_walter] [cite:wb_toi_wineman]. Edmond of the Paris branch funded colonies, refused Herzl, and later helped Weizmann. His son James carried the settlement work into the Mandate and the state [cite:rs_rd0_rh_james]. Neither Walter nor Edmond set the course of political Zionism, and the money that built the state came overwhelmingly from elsewhere, as the next paragraph shows; but by 1917 the London and Paris branches were both working with the movement's leadership, in different ways. Walter's part is told in full in [scene:rd2_addressee|why the letter went to Walter Rothschild].

The story that "the Rothschilds paid for it all" is examined elsewhere in this library against the whole record of pre-state funding, which shows money coming overwhelmingly from mass small-donor giving, with over a million American contributors in 1948 alone, and records that the Paris Rothschild who funded the early colonies opposed Herzl; see [scene:pn_s01|seven founding narratives, checked]. The correction cuts in both directions. Rothschild money did build much of the physical foundation of the early settlements, and that is documented and substantial. What it did not do was launch or own the political movement. The idea that "the Rothschilds" as a family engineered Zionism collapses one man's philanthropy, another man's ceremonial role and a political movement that one of them refused into a single hidden hand, which is the shape of an old antisemitic story, not of the record. The opposite move, saying the family had nothing to do with the settlements, is just as wrong.

One interpretive reading, offered here as a reading and not as a finding, goes further. It holds that without Edmond's money the First Aliyah would probably have collapsed at Rishon LeZion; that his combination of capital, agricultural assistance and management of relations with the Ottoman authorities produced the first lasting colonies and a template for later waves of immigration, the Second Aliyah of 1904–1914 and the Third of 1919–1923; and that the Ottoman restrictions of the period were a reaction to what his colonies made possible. It traces a line from those colonies, through the Jewish population and territorial position of 1917, to the state of 1948 and to the displacement of Palestinians in that year, while stating that Edmond, who died in 1934, took no part in the events of 1948 and was not their sole cause. This reading is a counterfactual, and parts of it rest on dates that the diplomatic documents of the period complicate: it places the first Ottoman land restrictions in 1892, a date that is itself disputed. The same account lists what survives of his work today, without sources: the settlements as Israeli towns and cities, Rishon LeZion among the country's largest, with more than 300,000 people; Ramat HaNadiv, where he and his wife Adelheid are buried; an Edmond de Rothschild Foundation in Israel working in education, the arts and science rather than political advocacy; his portrait on a 500-shekel note of the 1982–1985 series; and streets named for the family in several cities. It concludes that the family has no significant role in present-day political lobbying for Israel. These statements are recorded as that account gives them; they are not verified here.

What the record does not settle is substantial. The total Edmond spent is not documented in any cited source, and the much-repeated figures for it are estimates without citation. Whether he met Herzl in July 1896 or only exchanged messages depends on which account of the diary is followed. The handover to the Jewish Colonization Association is dated to the end of 1899 or to 1900, with fourteen or fifteen million francs; whether Edmond's later help to Weizmann amounted to a change of position on political Zionism is disputed between the reference accounts. His reasons for refusing Herzl are attributed to his correspondence, which is not quoted in the material available. What is not in doubt is the order of events: the colonies came first, the political movement came second, and the man who paid for the first said no to the second, at least at the start.
