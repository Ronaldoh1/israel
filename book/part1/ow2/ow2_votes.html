The Votes, 1973–2025

Every law in Part One passed through Congress, and the margins are part of the record. This section sets the main recorded votes side by side, from the emergency aid of 1973 to the arms-sale resolutions of 2025. Read in sequence, they show three things: near-unanimity in the House on aid and missile defence that has held for half a century; a small number of moments when the lobby lost or the executive overrode it; and, since 2024, a growing minority in the Senate willing to vote to block specific arms sales. The votes record what members did. They do not record why, and this section attributes reasons only where members gave them on the record.

The settled majority. The emergency aid of 1973 passed the Senate 66 to 9, and an amendment by Senator Fulbright tying part of it to compliance with Resolution 242 was tabled 62 to 12. [cite:ow1_vv_rs587] [cite:ow1_vv_rs583] Nearly four decades later the two partnership acts passed almost unopposed: the 2012 act 411 to 2 in the House and by unanimous voice vote in the Senate, and the 2014 act 410 to 1 in its House version and unanimously in the Senate. [cite:ow2_stl_2012house] [cite:ow2_alg_2014house] [cite:ow_jvl_2012act] [cite:ow_jvl_2014act] On missile defence the margin has stayed close to unanimous. The House approved $1 billion to replenish Iron Dome 420 to 9 on 23 September 2021, after Democratic leaders had first removed the money from a stopgap bill because some members objected; Majority Leader Steny Hoyer said "the overwhelming majority of this Congress — not in a partisan way but in an American way" supported it, while Representative Rashida Tlaib, the only member to speak against it, called the increase "absurd and unjustifiable." [cite:ow1_pbs_irondome2021] In July 2025 the House rejected, 422 to 6, an amendment by Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene to remove $500 million a year in cooperative missile-defence funding; the six in favour were Greene, Thomas Massie, Ilhan Omar, Al Green, Summer Lee and Tlaib. [cite:pm_ji_mtg_amendment]

When the lobby lost. The clearest documented defeat came in 1981. The Reagan administration proposed selling AWACS surveillance aircraft and F-15 enhancements to Saudi Arabia, and AIPAC campaigned against it. The House voted 301 to 111 on 14 October 1981 to disapprove the sale, but on 28 October the Senate rejected disapproval 48 to 52, and the sale went ahead. [cite:ow2_vv_awacs_house] [cite:ow2_vv_awacs_senate] [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The episode is a reminder that a disapproval of an arms sale needs both chambers and, in practice, enough votes to survive a veto, which is why so few succeed in either direction.

After 7 October 2023. On 25 October 2023 the House adopted H.Res. 771, standing with Israel and condemning Hamas and Iran, 412 to 10. [cite:ow2_jns_hres771] The supplemental of April 2024 drew more opposition: on 20 April the House passed the Israel bill 366 to 58, with 37 Democrats and 21 Republicans against, and the Senate passed the combined package 79 to 18. [cite:ow1_clerk_roll152] [cite:ow1_lobby_hr815] On 1 May 2024 the House passed the Antisemitism Awareness Act, which would apply the IHRA definition of antisemitism in civil-rights enforcement, 320 to 91. [cite:ow2_wiki_aaa] Its Senate version stalled in committee in April 2025, and Part Eleven follows it.

The Senate minority grows. Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont has repeatedly forced floor votes on resolutions to block or examine arms transfers to Israel. A January 2024 measure of his seeking a report on Israel's human-rights conduct under the Foreign Assistance Act failed 72 to 11; [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] in November 2024 three resolutions to block sales drew 19 senators; on 3 April 2025 a resolution covering about $8.8 billion in sales failed 82 to 15, with every vote in favour coming from the Democratic caucus and the Democratic leader, Chuck Schumer, voting against. [cite:ow2_jta_sanders2025] On 30 July 2025 a resolution to block 20,000 automatic rifles for Israel's national police drew 27 votes and one to block $675 million in bombs drew 24, all from the Democratic caucus; senators who had voted no in April, among them Jeanne Shaheen, Jack Reed and Patty Murray, voted yes. [cite:ow2_duckworth_jul2025] [cite:ow2_bg_jul2025] On 15 April 2026 resolutions to block a $295 million sale of armoured bulldozers and a $151.8 million sale of 12,000 1,000-pound bombs failed 59 to 40 and 63 to 36, the largest minorities yet. [cite:ow2_mt_apr2026] Sanders argued that the sales would "clearly violate" the legal requirements for arms transfers because of civilian deaths in Gaza; the chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, Jim Risch of Idaho, called the resolutions "misguided" and said adopting them would abandon America's closest ally in the region. [cite:ow2_duckworth_jul2025]

What the votes show. On this record, measures that fund Israel's defence have passed by large and bipartisan margins for fifty years, while measures to condition or block aid have failed, also for fifty years, from Fulbright in 1973 to Sanders in 2025. The money described in the previous section flows to the members who cast those majorities, but so do party loyalty, constituency opinion and long-standing policy, and the record reached here cannot separate them for any individual vote. What it does show is a change at the margin: in the Senate, the number willing to vote to stop specific weapons sales roughly doubled between April and July 2025. [cite:ow2_jta_sanders2025] [cite:ow2_duckworth_jul2025] The parts that follow move from Congress to the agencies that carry out what it voted.
