Why People Compare, and How This Book Does It

The question this volume takes up is usually put in a single sentence: why the United States sends money to Israel when people in the United States lack services that Israel's system provides. It is now asked in Congress as well as at kitchen tables. In the Senate, a resolution by Bernie Sanders to block arms sales to Israel failed 72–11 in January 2024; similar measures drew 19 Democratic votes in November 2024; one failed 82–15 on 3 April 2025; and on 30 July 2025, 27 Democrats backed a resolution to block a sale of 20,000 assault rifles and 24 a resolution on a $675 million bomb sale, though both failed and every Republican voted against. [cite:ow2_jta_sanders2025] [cite:gi17_bg_senate2025] In the House in July 2025, Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene's amendment to remove about $500 million a year in missile-defence funding for Israel, which she called "money we don't have", failed 422–6; the six in favour were two Republicans and four Democrats. [cite:us1_ji_greene] Public opinion moved in the same period. In February 2026 Gallup found, for the first time since it began asking in 2001, that Americans' sympathies did not lie more with the Israelis: 41 per cent sided more with the Palestinians and 36 per cent with the Israelis, a difference Gallup called not statistically significant. [cite:gz_gallup_2026] In a Pew survey in May 2026, 32 per cent of American adults viewed the Israeli government favourably, against 47 per cent in 2022. [cite:ts11_pew_2026]

The scale of the money in America's budget

The regular aid is small beside the American budget. Federal outlays in fiscal year 2025 were $7.0 trillion, with a deficit of $1.8 trillion, by the Congressional Budget Office's count. [cite:us1_crfb_fy2025] [cite:us1_cbo_fy2025] The $3.8 billion a year under the 2016 agreement is about 0.05 per cent of those outlays, or about $11 for each of the 341.8 million people the Census Bureau counted in July 2025, by this book's arithmetic. [cite:us1_wa_uspop] [cite:us1_iowa_uspop] The wartime money since October 2023 is larger, at least $21.7 billion in military aid in two years by Brown University's Costs of War project, and America's own operations in the region have cost more again. [cite:gz_cow_aid] [scene:gi16_costs|The costs of the wars] On these figures, no plausible accounting makes the aid the reason the United States lacks any programme that Israel has, or the reason Israel has it. The differences between the two systems are the product of each country's laws, budgets and taxes, and those are what this book compares.

The claim and the record: who pays for Israel's health care

The clearest case is health care. A claim that American taxpayers pay for Israelis' health care circulates among American commentators on both left and right; a January 2026 opinion column in London's Jewish News documented it while arguing it was false. [cite:us1_jn_claim] The record is plain. Israel's National Health Insurance Law of 1995 "introduced universal health care for all citizens and permanent residents", delivered through four nonprofit health funds, Clalit, Maccabi, Meuhedet and Leumit. It is financed by a dedicated health tax, 3.2 per cent of income up to 60 per cent of the average wage and 5.2 per cent above it, collected by the National Insurance Institute, plus money from the state budget. Government sources paid 65 per cent of Israel's health spending in 2024, and households paid 19.9 per cent out of pocket. Asylum seekers, undocumented migrants and foreign workers are excluded from the law. [cite:us1_cmwf_israel] The United States has no law guaranteeing coverage to every resident; 26.7 million people, 7.9 per cent of the population, were uninsured for the whole of 2025, a level not statistically different from 2024. [cite:us1_census_uninsured]

Today's aid does not pay for Israel's health funds: it is military money, spent mostly with American contractors. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] The record does carry one qualification. When the health law was passed in 1995, Israel still received $1.2 billion a year in American economic aid, money not tied to weapons, which was about 1.1 per cent of its GDP by this book's arithmetic; that aid dwindled after 2000 and is no longer given. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1a_fred_ilgdp] Whatever the aid frees in Israel's budget now is limited to its military grant, about 0.6 per cent of GDP. [scene:us1_aid|The aid, stated precisely] Part Sixteen compares the two health systems in full. [scene:us16_systems|Part Sixteen: health systems]

How this book compares

The book holds to five rules. First, it compares systems, laws and governments, never peoples: a gap in outcomes between groups is reported as a measure of how a system treats them, with the mechanism named, and never as a trait of the group. Second, wherever possible it uses one source for both countries, such as the World Bank or the OECD, and states each figure's year and definition, for example whether dollars are converted at market exchange rates or at purchasing-power parity, which allows for the different cost of living in each country. Third, it measures over time, from 1980 where series exist, and labels a figure as a snapshot when only a snapshot exists. Fourth, it shows both countries' gaps and gives no overall verdict that does not disclose its measures; the composite scorecard in this platform's earlier simulation chapter on the two systems is not reused, and the total that Part Twenty-Two gives publishes every measure, goalpost and weight. Fifth, it states whom each figure covers.

The fifth rule matters most for Israel. Freedom House scores Israel at 73 out of 100 and states that the score does "not reflect the actions of the Israeli military or Jewish Israeli settlers in the Gaza Strip or the West Bank", which it rates separately at 22 and 2. [cite:us1_fh_israel2026] Israel's national statistics likewise count its citizens and residents, about 10.3 million people in September 2026 by the Central Bureau of Statistics' provisional figure, including 2.17 million Arabs. [cite:us1_jpost_ilpop] A comparison of how each state serves "its people" therefore covers different populations unless it says otherwise. This book treats the Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza, who live under Israeli control without Israeli citizenship, in a part of their own. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: people under control without citizenship] The United States also has people under its authority without citizenship, and that part sets out the two cases side by side while stating plainly how unequal the comparison is in scale.

Over time, not as a snapshot

Measuring over time changes the picture as much as measuring who is covered. At market exchange rates, Israel's GDP per person, by World Bank data, was 37 per cent of the American level in 1985, 56 per cent in 1990, 60 per cent in 2000, 65 per cent in 2010 and 68 per cent in 2019; it stood at 67 per cent in 2025, after two years of war. [cite:ir13_fred_ilpc] [cite:us1_fred_uspc] Over the same forty years the aid's weight in Israel's economy fell from about an eighth of GDP to just over half a per cent. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp] The two systems compared in this book are thus a much richer Israel and a far smaller aid relationship than the ones that existed in 1985. Part Eleven examines the economies in detail, including what the exchange-rate figures leave out. [scene:us11_growth|Part Eleven: growth over time]
