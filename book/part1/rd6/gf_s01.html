Who Paid for It All? The Ledger, 1899–1949

Two explanations of how the pre-state movement was financed have long circulated, and they point in opposite directions. One holds that it ran on a handful of wealthy patrons, the Rothschilds or a few Jewish bankers. The other holds that it ran on secret government money. The documented record supports neither. What it shows is organized private giving on a mass scale, conducted openly through permanent institutions, raised from donors in many countries and, by the 1940s, overwhelmingly from the United States. It was not cheap, and it was not secret. The slogan version of the patron myth is taken apart, alongside the founding slogans, in [scene:pn_s01|the closing account of the founding narratives]; this section sets out the ledger itself.

The panel above carries the headline sums, each shown as it was then and in today's dollars. The largest single figure is the United Jewish Appeal's campaign of 1948: $150 million, about $2.0 billion today, raised from more than a million contributors in 4,950 American communities. [cite:jta_uja_1948] [cite:bls_cpi] At the time it was described as the largest non-government humanitarian campaign in American history. [cite:cambridge_solidarity] Behind it lay the wartime appeals of 1939–45, more than $124 million, and before those the worldwide giving of the 1920s. [cite:raphael_uja] The panel also lists the transfer of German Jewish capital in the 1930s, whose terms and sums are told in their own place earlier in this volume.

The story begins with a permanent fund. Keren Hayesod, created as the Zionist movement's standing fundraising arm, raised $20.1 million worldwide between April 1921 and December 1929, roughly $370 million in today's money. More than half of it, $11.0 million, came from the United States. The rest came in descending order from South Africa ($1.5 million), Germany ($0.98 million), Romania ($0.88 million), Britain ($0.88 million), Poland ($0.82 million) and Canada ($0.74 million). [cite:jta_kh_decade] Measured over the fund's first twelve years in sterling, the United States gave about half of the £4.82 million raised. [cite:jta_kh_12yr] Before 1948 the fund's mandate covered immigration, settlement, defense, water development and public works. [cite:jvl_kh] Defense appears on that list from the start; it was not a later addition.

The country breakdown shows how early the balance had shifted. In the 1920s the American share was already larger than every other country's combined. The gap only widened. In the late 1930s American Jewish organizations set up their own joint campaign, the United Jewish Appeal, formed in 1939. It raised more than $124 million during the war years of 1939–45 and $100 million in 1946. [cite:raphael_uja] In 1947 it raised about $110 million. [cite:cambridge_solidarity] In 1948 it reached the record $150 million. [cite:jta_uja_1948] Over those last three years alone, 1946 to 1948, the campaigns raised some $360 million, about $5.2 billion in today's dollars. A single American year in 1948 brought in more than seven times what the entire world had given Keren Hayesod in its first decade.

The scale makes one distinction essential, and it is the one most often lost. The United Jewish Appeal did not raise money for Palestine alone. It split its proceeds three ways. Of the $150 million raised in 1948, $74.7 million went to the United Palestine Appeal, the Palestine and Israel arm; $64.9 million went to the Joint Distribution Committee for relief in Europe; and $10.4 million went to the United Service for New Americans, which resettled refugees in the United States. [cite:jta_uja_1948] Roughly half of the dollar went to Palestine, some 43 percent to Europe and about 7 percent to resettlement at home.

Much of the 1948 total, in other words, fed and housed Holocaust survivors in Europe. Counting all of it as "funding Israel" would roughly double the true Palestine figure. The Palestine share alone, $74.7 million, comes to about $1.0 billion in today's dollars. [cite:bls_cpi] That share paid for what Keren Hayesod's own history lists as its prewar purposes: immigration and absorption, settlement, defense, water and public works. [cite:jvl_kh]

The chain of giving can be followed from donor to purpose. Contributors gave through local federations; the federations passed their campaign totals to the national appeal; the appeal divided them among its three beneficiary agencies; and each agency spent on its own mandate.

War money is harder to count. Arms for the 1948 war were bought through front companies, and the record yields ranges rather than a total. Estimates of what was paid for Czechoslovak arms run from about $14.5 million to roughly six times that figure. Golda Meyerson's American fundraising tour in early 1948 raised about $50 million, which went largely to weapons. The government of Somoza's Nicaragua extended $3 million in letters of credit for arms buying. [cite:israelaffairs_italy]

The arms table shows why no single number can be given. Its Czechoslovak line spans a six-fold range, and Golda Meyerson's money flowed in part through the same community federations as the United Jewish Appeal, so that adding her $50 million to the appeal's $150 million would count some dollars twice. For that reason the ledger keeps every stream separate and never adds them into one sum.

Every "today's dollars" figure in this section follows one method, the method of the United States government's own inflation calculator: the amount is multiplied by the ratio of the consumer price index for 2025, which stands at 321.943, to the index for the year in question. Sums in pounds are first converted to dollars at the exchange rate of the period, and values from before 1913 are approximate. [cite:bls_cpi] The conversions are a guide to scale, not a claim that a 1948 dollar bought exactly what its modern equivalent buys.

The answer to the question this section asks is therefore plain in outline and incomplete in detail. The money came overwhelmingly from organized private giving: decades of worldwide contributions through a permanent fund, then American federated campaigns on a scale without precedent, joined in 1948 by arms purchases financed partly by the same donors and, in one documented case, by a foreign government's credit. What the record does not provide is a grand total. No audited sum for the whole pre-state operation has been published, the arms figures are ranges, and several streams overlap. The honest ledger is a set of documented streams side by side, not a single figure, and that is how it is given here. What the money for the army looked like inside these budgets, and what Britain spent on the other side of the ledger, is the subject of [scene:gf_s02|the operating budgets and Britain's bill].
