The 1940s Committees and Truman's Cyrus

In the decisive decade before 1948, the Christian voices most visible in American politics on Palestine did not come from the prophecy conferences. They came from two committees, the American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine, whose founding records survive in the American Jewish Archives. Those records show who started the committees, who sat on them and what they promised to do, and they complicate two familiar stories at once: that Christian support for a Jewish state in the 1940s arose on its own, and that it was the work of end-times believers. The Road to 1948 volume sets out the committees' place in the wider American campaign; this section looks at them as a chapter in the history of Christian Zionism. [scene:us_s04|The American Palestine Committee, 1932]

Two committees from one office

Emanuel Neumann, a member of the Zionist Executive, founded the American Palestine Committee in 1932 to organise prominent, mainly non-Jewish Americans in support of a Jewish homeland. In January 1941 he joined the Emergency Committee for Zionist Affairs as head of public relations and political action, and from that post revived the committee with Senator Robert Wagner of New York as its chairman. [cite:aja_neumann_bio] Carl Hermann Voss and David Rausch, writing in 1988, date the committee's founding to 1931 and record that Reinhold Niebuhr was a member from the early 1930s. [cite:ts6_voss_rausch]

The clergy's body was created at an all-day meeting on 14 December 1942 at the Pennsylvania Hotel in New York. Its minutes list thirty people present. Henry A. Atkinson chaired and W. Russell Bowie opened with prayer; the theologian Reinhold Niebuhr, Bishops Francis J. McConnell, James Cannon Jr. and Herbert Welch, and Carl H. Voss of Pittsburgh were among those present, and the archaeologist W. F. Albright sent a telegram regretting his absence through illness. Emanuel Neumann attended and explained the work of the American Palestine Committee; Rabbi Philip S. Bernstein of Rochester was also present. [cite:ts6_aja_ccp_minutes] The meeting elected Atkinson chairman, Howard B. Warren vice-chairman and Niebuhr treasurer. Its first policy point committed the new body "to the establishment of a Jewish commonwealth in Palestine" within a general post-war settlement. It named itself the Christian Council on Palestine, "an autonomous committee working in cooperation with the American Palestine Committee," to be funded by "popular subscription" in cooperation with that committee. [cite:ts6_aja_ccp_minutes] Neumann's own account in the Archives credits him with organising it. [cite:aja_neumann_bio] Two months later, in a statement filed with the minutes, he described it as a group of "hundreds of prominent Christian Clergymen." [cite:ts6_aja_ccp_minutes]

The committees grew quickly. By the end of the war the American Palestine Committee claimed some 6,500 members, among them senators, congressmen, cabinet members and governors, and the Christian Council about 3,000 clergy; these are the committees' own figures. [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee] [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby] In 1946 they merged as the American Christian Palestine Committee, which, in the description of one reference work, became the leading American Christian lobby for a Jewish state and later coordinated opposition to United Nations plans to internationalise Jerusalem. [cite:ts6_wiki_cz2] Its end date is disputed. The common account says it disbanded after 1948; Voss and Rausch, writing as participants, say it carried on and wound down only in the late 1950s and early 1960s as its financial support shrank. [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee] [cite:ts6_voss_rausch]

"Autonomous" or "hardly autonomous"

The founding minutes call the Council autonomous. The historian Peter Grose described the committees' operations as "hardly autonomous," and reporting drawn from his work states that Zionist funds reaching $150,000 by 1946 sustained them, a figure this volume has not seen in a primary account. [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby] The minutes themselves support part of each description. They record a Zionist executive explaining the political work the Council would join, and a funding arrangement made "in cooperation with" his committee; they also record named churchmen electing their own officers and adopting their own policy. [cite:ts6_aja_ccp_minutes] What the documents establish is specific: these bodies were organised by named officials of the Zionist movement, Neumann and the Emergency Committee, and staffed by Christians who lent their own names and, as far as any record shows, their own convictions. That is a finding about named organisations and named people, not about Jews or Christians in general.

Who the Christians were

The founding list is the clearest evidence of the decade's Christian Zionism, and it is not a list of end-times teachers. It names three bishops, several professors including the theologian Niebuhr, a rabbi, a Zionist official and pastors from New York, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts and Connecticut; none of the sources consulted for this volume identifies any of them as a teacher of the dispensationalist system that earlier parts describe. [cite:ts6_aja_ccp_minutes] [cite:ts6_voss_rausch] Voss and Rausch describe the Council as "composed, as planned, almost entirely of Christian ministers," and the merged committee as mostly Protestant. [cite:ts6_voss_rausch] The historian Daniel Hummel, reviewing Samuel Goldman's God's Country (2018), notes that Goldman treats Niebuhr as the "most outspoken postwar Protestant supporter of Israel," who backed a Jewish state on moral and realist grounds rather than literal prophecy, and that Goldman traces a continuous Protestant tradition, back to the Puritan covenant theology of Increase Mather and Jonathan Edwards, that saw America as a Cyrus for the Jews. [cite:ts6_hummel_goldman]

Preachers outside these committees took up the cause in their own way. The pastor J. Frank Norris taught his followers that support for the Zionist cause was a Christian duty and wrote to President Truman in its favour in 1947 and 1948. [cite:ts6_wiki_cz2] The political organisation of Christian support in the 1940s, though, ran through liberal Protestant clergy recruited by the Zionist movement, not through the dispensationalist schools that Part Seven describes. [scene:ts7_schools|The schools] Stephen Spector's later study of evangelical support points in the same direction for a different era: he found Genesis 12:3, the promise to bless Abraham's descendants, to be the reason most often cited, reported that most Christian Zionists did not base their support on "a catastrophic end-times scenario," and estimated that only about five million American evangelicals were premillennial dispensationalists. [cite:ts6_tablet_spector] [scene:ts5_notes|Scofield's notes]

Christian opposition was organised as well. In 1948 Virginia Gildersleeve, the former dean of Barnard College, and Kermit Roosevelt Jr. formed the Committee for Justice and Peace in the Holy Land, which warned that "extreme Zionist pressure" risked disrupting national unity. [cite:mosaic_cia_zionism] [cite:w_kermit_jr] Partition was voted and the state recognised regardless. [scene:rf_s01|The objections of 1948] [scene:ts11_churches|The churches' counter-record]

"I am Cyrus"

The decade's most quoted Christian Zionist line came after it was over. In November 1953, at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York, Truman's old friend and former business partner Eddie Jacobson introduced him as "the man who helped create the State of Israel." Truman is reported to have replied: "What do you mean 'helped to create'? I am Cyrus. I am Cyrus." [cite:ts6_merkley_cyrus] [cite:rs_cw2_judis_truman_cyrus] Cyrus was the Persian king who, in the biblical account, allowed the exiles of Judah to return. The remark is known from later accounts, not from a transcript, and the sources used here do not name the witness who recorded it.

Paul Merkley, the historian of Christian Zionism, reads the remark as the key to Truman. He notes that Truman joined a Baptist church in Kansas City at eighteen and stayed a member for life, writes that his Sunday School teachers had taught him that someone would one day be called to be a second Cyrus, and concludes that, although political calculation played its part, "personal commitment tipped the balance." [cite:ts6_merkley_cyrus] John Judis reads the same years the other way: the picture of Truman as an unwavering supporter is, he writes, "dead wrong," and the President was full of doubt and regret around the recognition of May 1948. [cite:rs_cw2_judis_truman_cyrus] Truman's own words from 1943 to 1948, which mix public conviction, open awareness of voters and private irritation at pressure, are set out in the Road to 1948 volume. [scene:tr_s01|Truman in his own words] On the evidence, "I am Cyrus" shows how Truman chose to describe his role five years later, before a Jewish audience; it does not by itself show why he acted in 1948.
