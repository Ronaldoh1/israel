The War for Assad

Hezbollah's founding claim was that its weapons existed to fight Israel. Within the first years of Syria's war it used them in another country's civil war, on the side of the government. When the uprising against Bashar al-Assad turned into civil war, Hezbollah first sent small groups across the border, presented as protection for villages inhabited by Lebanese Shia families on the Syrian side. By 2014 it had thousands of fighters in Syria, fighting alongside the Syrian army, Iranian advisers and Iraqi militias. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Mireille Rebeiz of Dickinson College describes the deployment plainly: Hezbollah sent thousands of fighters "to help the government suppress the uprising". [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz]

For months Hezbollah did not say so. On 25 May 2013 Hassan Nasrallah confirmed for the first time that its fighters were in combat in Syria. He gave two reasons. The first was strategic: "Syria is the backbone of the resistance (in the region) and its main supporter." The second was defensive: if rebels took the areas along the border, he said, that would be "a great threat to Lebanon". [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Both statements are Hezbollah's own account of why it went. The first is the more revealing, because it names the alliance itself, not any threat to Lebanon, as the reason to fight; the US State Department, for its part, reported Israeli concern that Iran was helping Hezbollah build the means to make rockets and missiles in both Lebanon and Syria. [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018]

Qusayr

The battle that made Hezbollah's role impossible to deny was fought for al-Qusayr, a town in Homs province about eight kilometres from the Lebanese border, on the route between Damascus and the coast. The UN Commission of Inquiry on Syria, in its report of 16 August 2013, recorded that "pro-Government forces, including Hezbollah, launched a major offensive" on the Qusayr region, "supported by foreign combatants including thousands of Hezbollah fighters". By early May, the commission found, Hezbollah had taken control of the Ain Tanour and Hamah water stations, and its fighters held the mainly Sunni villages to the south-west of the town; government forces besieged the town from one side and Hezbollah from the other. [cite:hz6b_coi2013] Government troops and Hezbollah fighters took the town on 5 June 2013 after about seventeen days of street fighting, and the remaining rebels withdrew along a narrow corridor. [cite:hz6b_coi2013] [cite:hz6b_csm_qusayr] A Hezbollah veteran told the Christian Science Monitor that about 1,200 of its special forces had spearheaded the assault; opposition sources put its dead at 70 to 110, and Hezbollah released no figure. Its deputy leader, Naim Qassem, called the victory "a sharp blow to the American-Israeli-Takfiri project". [cite:hz6b_csm_qusayr] Civilians in the town were trapped for weeks without water or electricity. [cite:hz6b_aje_qusayr] On 29 May, while the battle was still going on, the UN Human Rights Council condemned "the intervention of foreign combatants fighting on behalf of the Syrian regime in Al Qusayr", by 36 votes to 1; the resolution did not name Hezbollah. [cite:hz6b_hrc23_1]

The war soon went far beyond the border villages. In December 2016 Hezbollah, with other militias backed by Iran, played a central part in the battle that returned Aleppo, Syria's largest city, wholly to government control; the government declared victory on 22 December. [cite:hz6_wiki_hziran] [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Aleppo lies in the north of the country, far from the Lebanese border, and the fight there had nothing to do with Israel or with protecting Lebanese villages. It was a war to keep Assad in power, which is the first of Nasrallah's two reasons rather than the second. The UN Commission of Inquiry found that in the battle for eastern Aleppo, pro-government ground forces, "composed mostly of Syrian and foreign militias", executed fighters who had surrendered and people seen as opposition supporters, and that armed opposition groups shelled civilian areas of western Aleppo indiscriminately. Its public summary does not name Hezbollah among those militias, and this book does not attribute those executions to it. [cite:hz6b_un_aleppo]

Sieges: Madaya and Zabadani

The UN commission's 2013 report had already found that government forces, backed by Hezbollah, tightened sieges in Homs province before ground offensives, and concluded that the government and Hezbollah had both carried out siege operations in breach of their obligations under international humanitarian law. [cite:hz6b_coi2013] The best-known case came later, in the mountains west of Damascus near the Lebanese border. In July 2015 pro-government forces opened an offensive on the rebel-held town of Zabadani; a rebel alliance that included al-Qaeda's Syrian affiliate answered by besieging Foua and Kefraya, two towns in Idlib province with mainly Shia populations. [cite:hz6b_newarab_truce] Neighbouring Madaya, its population of about 40,000 swollen by displaced people, was besieged from July 2015 by the Syrian army and Hezbollah, according to residents interviewed by Human Rights Watch and to VOA's reporting. [cite:hz6b_hrw_madaya] [cite:hz6b_voa_madaya] Médecins Sans Frontières reported that 23 patients at a health centre it supported in Madaya had died of starvation after 1 December 2015, and on 29 January 2016 that 16 more had starved to death since the first aid convoy arrived that month; MSF's own statements attributed the siege to the "Syrian government-led coalition". [cite:hz6b_hrw_madaya] [cite:hz6b_msf_madaya] Paulo Pinheiro, chair of the UN commission, said such siege tactics "target the civilian population by subjecting them to starvation", and the commission condemned the use of starvation as a weapon by both sides; armed groups including al-Nusra held about 12,500 people under siege in Foua and Kefraya at the same time. [cite:hz6b_ap_coi_madaya] [cite:hz6b_hrw_madaya]

Hezbollah's response was to deny that the starvation was real or its doing. It said militants inside Madaya were "withholding food from the locals", and its television station, Al-Manar, called the photographs fabrications. [cite:hz6b_voa_madaya] A truce for Zabadani, Foua and Kefraya had been agreed in September 2015, negotiated between the rebel group Ahrar al-Sham and Iran, with the Syrian Observatory for Human Rights naming Hezbollah among the pro-government parties to it. [cite:hz6b_newarab_truce] It ended in April 2017 with an exchange of populations: people from Foua and Kefraya were bussed to government areas and fighters and civilians from Zabadani and Madaya to rebel areas. On 15 April a car bomb struck the waiting Foua and Kefraya convoy at Rashidin, outside Aleppo, killing at least 126 people, at least 109 of them evacuees and 68 of them children; no group claimed it. [cite:hz6b_aje_fourtowns] The record therefore supports a precise statement: Hezbollah took part, with the Syrian army, in sieges that the UN commission found unlawful and in which civilians starved; opposition groups ran sieges of their own; and the UN findings name the Syrian government and Hezbollah for the sieges they ran.

What it cost Hezbollah

The losses were heavy for an organisation of its size. The Wilson Center, drawing on published estimates, puts Hezbollah's dead at no fewer than 1,300 by 2015, and estimates for 2018 range from 1,000 to 2,000. The Associated Press in October 2018 reported an estimated 2,000 killed and thousands more wounded. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] Hezbollah does not publish a total, and none of these figures comes from a count the public can check; they are estimates, and the range should be read as such. Among the dead was Mustafa Badreddine, a senior Hezbollah commander, killed in Damascus in 2016. The Special Tribunal for Lebanon dropped its case against him as one of the accused in the Hariri bombing after his death. [cite:hz6_un_stl2020] [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016]

The war also strained its money. The AP reported that Hezbollah's budget had been under pressure since it entered Syria in 2013, because it kept paying stipends to the families of the dead and medical care for the wounded. By 2018, under new US sanctions on Iran, it was cutting the number of fighters it kept in Syria and reducing their time away from home and the movement of its vehicles. Nasrallah conceded in August 2018 that the sanctions "will have an effect for sure", and was reported to have told cadres in a closed meeting, "We have to tighten belts a bit." [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] Iran carried most of the cost of the wider war: the State Department said in 2018 that Iran had spent more than $16 billion since 2012 supporting Assad and its allied forces in Syria, Iraq and Yemen, and had given the Syrian government $4 billion in credit lines. [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] These are US government figures, given at a time when Washington was building the case for its pressure campaign on Iran, and are labelled as such.

In Lebanon the war reshaped how others saw the party. A movement that had built its standing on fighting Israel's occupation of the south was now fighting Syrians on behalf of a government whose troops had stayed in Lebanon itself for more than sixteen years after the 1989 Taif Agreement called for them to remain for two. [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz] The Arab Barometer figures in the previous section, with trust in Hezbollah below 10 per cent among Sunni, Druze and Christian respondents in 2024, measure a divide the Syrian war did much to deepen (see [scene:hz6_state|A Party With an Army]). [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz]

The fall of Assad

Eleven years of fighting did not save the government Hezbollah went to Syria to defend. On 6 December 2024 anti-Assad fighters took the Iraq–Syria border crossing, cutting the land route by which, under Assad, weapons and equipment had moved from Iran through Iraq and Syria into Lebanon; two days later, on 8 December, rebels led by Hayat Tahrir al-Sham entered Damascus and Assad's government fell. Before they arrived, Hezbollah sent officers to oversee the withdrawal of its own fighters from the city. [cite:hz6b_newarab_qassem] It came at the worst moment for the party: less than two weeks earlier a US-brokered ceasefire with Israel had taken effect, ending fourteen months of war in which many of its top officials, Nasrallah among them, had been killed (see [scene:hz7_war|the 2024 war]). [cite:hz6b_ap_assadfall]

Naim Qassem, by then secretary-general, gave Hezbollah's own account of the cost in a televised speech on 14 December 2024, his first since Assad's fall, in which he did not mention Assad by name. "Hezbollah has lost the military supply route through Syria at this stage," he said, calling the loss "a detail in the resistance's work"; a new government might reopen the route, he added, "and we could look for other ways." [cite:hz6b_newarab_qassem] Outside analysts judged the loss larger than a detail. Aron Lund of Century International told the AP that Iran could still "fly in some things and smuggle some things", but "not even close" to the previous scale; Firas Maksad of the Middle East Institute said Hezbollah faced "an entirely new reality". Hezbollah's MP Hassan Fadlallah called the change in Syria "major, dangerous and new" but said it would "not weaken us". [cite:hz6b_ap_assadfall] Measured against Nasrallah's own reason for going in 2013, to defend "the backbone of the resistance", the Syrian war ended in the loss of the thing it was fought to keep.
