Finance's Share and the Private Courtroom: Financialization and Forced Arbitration

Two changes in how the economy is organised sit underneath many of the household costs measured in earlier parts. The first is the growing weight of finance: the share of the country's business profits earned by lending, trading, insuring and managing money rather than by making goods or providing other services. The second is a change in where disputes between companies and the people they serve are settled. Over the past fifteen years, the Supreme Court has allowed a clause in a credit-card agreement, a phone contract or a job offer to move a customer's or worker's complaint out of the public courts and into private arbitration, one person at a time. This section measures the first, traces the second through its court rulings and the one federal attempt to limit it, and sets the evidence for and against each side by side.

What financialization means and how big it is

The sociologist Greta Krippner defined financialization as "a pattern of accumulation in which profits accrue primarily through financial channels" rather than through trade and the production of goods. [cite:ad15_krippner] Using national accounts, she found that the ratio of financial to non-financial profits at the start of the 2000s was roughly three to five times its level in the 1950s and 1960s, depending on the measure, and that ordinary non-financial companies had also come to earn far more of their income from financial assets: their portfolio income, relative to their cash flow, peaked in the late 1980s at about five times its earlier level. [cite:ad15_krippner]

The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes the current figures. Its measure of domestic corporate profits, adjusted so that inventory gains and depreciation are valued at current cost, was $3,551 billion in 2025, of which financial companies earned $840 billion. [cite:ad15_fred_domprof] [cite:ad15_fred_finprof] Dividing one by the other gives a finance share of 23.7 per cent. On the same industry classification, which begins in 1998, the share was 28.8 per cent in 2000 and reached 38.1 per cent in 2002 ($294.9 billion of $773.6 billion), the highest year in the series. [cite:ad15_audit_fred_early] It collapsed to 11.5 per cent in the crisis year 2008, recovered to 29.2 per cent in 2010, and stood at 18.1 per cent in 2023 and 19.9 per cent in 2024. [cite:ad15_fred_finprof] [cite:ad15_fred_domprof_data] [cite:ad15_fred_domprof] The series is volatile, because financial profits swing with asset prices and loan losses, and it measures where profits are booked, not who ends up owning them. What it shows is that roughly one dollar in four or five of domestic corporate profit in the 2020s was earned by finance.

For a household, finance's profit is in part the other side of its own payments: the interest on card balances and car loans, the fees on accounts, the margins on mortgages. Those were measured in Part Nine, and the shift of corporate cash toward share buybacks in Part Four. [scene:ad9_credit_and_scores|Part Nine] [scene:ad4_shareholder_turn|Part Four] Finance also provides services households value, from mortgages that make homes affordable to retirement accounts that hold their savings, so a high finance share is not by itself evidence of extraction. The question is how disputes over those services are resolved when something goes wrong.

Arbitration: from business disputes to consumer contracts

Arbitration is a private process in which a dispute is decided by an arbitrator chosen under the contract rather than by a judge or jury, usually in private and with limited appeal. The Federal Arbitration Act of 1925 requires courts to enforce agreements to arbitrate. [cite:ad15_lii_concepcion] What changed in recent decades was its use in contracts that customers and workers sign without negotiation, and the addition of a second clause: a waiver of the right to join with others in a class action, the lawsuit in which one or a few people sue on behalf of everyone harmed in the same way.

The decisive case concerned a mobile phone contract. The Concepcions, a California couple, were charged sales tax on phones that AT&T advertised as free, and their claim was joined to a class action. California's courts had held that class-action waivers in such consumer contracts were unconscionable, that is, too one-sided to enforce. On 27 April 2011, in AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion, the Supreme Court held by five votes to four that the Federal Arbitration Act overrides that California rule. [cite:ad15_lii_concepcion] Justice Stephen Breyer, dissenting, asked what "rational lawyer would have signed on to represent the Concepcions" in a case over a $30.22 claim. [cite:ad15_audit_concepcion_dissent] On 20 June 2013, in American Express v. Italian Colors Restaurant, the Court, by five votes to three, enforced a waiver even though expert proof of the restaurant's antitrust claim was estimated to cost several hundred thousand dollars or more, against a maximum individual recovery of $38,549 after trebling; Justice Elena Kagan's dissent summarised the majority's answer as "Too darn bad". [cite:ad15_audit_italiancolors] On 21 May 2018, in Epic Systems v. Lewis, the Court held, again by five to four, that employers may require workers to bring wage and other claims one by one, holding that "arbitration agreements providing for individualized proceedings must be enforced" and that federal labour law does not override them. [cite:ad15_lii_epic]

By then the clauses were common at work. A 2017 survey of private employers with 50 or more staff, run by Cornell University for the Economic Policy Institute, a labour-aligned research group, found that 56.2 per cent of non-union private-sector employees, about 60.1 million people, were bound by mandatory arbitration, against about 2 per cent of companies using it in 1992. About a quarter of all non-union private-sector workers, 24.7 million, had also waived class actions. The clauses were most common at establishments with an average wage under $13 an hour (64.5 per cent). [cite:ad15_epi_colvin]

What the regulator found, and what Congress did

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), the federal consumer-finance regulator created in 2010, studied arbitration in consumer finance. Its 2015 study found that card issuers holding more than half of all credit-card debt, and banks holding 44 per cent of insured deposits, used arbitration clauses; that three in four consumers surveyed did not know whether their card agreement had one; and that only about 2 per cent would consult a lawyer or consider legal action over a small-dollar dispute. In roughly a thousand arbitration cases over two years, arbitrators awarded a total of about $360,000 to 78 consumers. Over five years, group lawsuits paid about $1 billion to more than 34 million consumers. [cite:ad15_cfpb_arb2017] On 10 July 2017 the Bureau issued a rule that would have let companies keep arbitration clauses but barred them from using them to block class actions. [cite:ad15_cfpb_arb2017]

The rule never took effect. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and others sued on 29 September 2017. The House voted 231 to 190 to overturn it under the Congressional Review Act, which lets Congress cancel a new rule by simple majority in both chambers, and on 24 October 2017 the Senate voted 51 to 50, with Vice President Mike Pence breaking the tie. [cite:ad15_troutman_cra] President Trump signed the resolution on 1 November 2017 and the Bureau removed the rule on 22 November. [cite:ad15_cfpb_arb2017] The same law may bar the Bureau from issuing a substantially similar rule. [cite:ad15_troutman_cra] As of October 2026 no general federal rule limits class-action waivers in consumer finance outside the statutory bars described below, and the Bureau's own funding was in litigation. [cite:ad9_goodwin_cfpb]

The case for arbitration

The defenders' case is substantial. In October 2017 the Treasury Department argued that the Bureau's own projections showed four in five class actions yielding nothing for class members, that only about 4 per cent of class members typically claim their share, that the typical settlement offered $32.35 a person, and that the rule would have sent about $330 million more to plaintiffs' lawyers over five years. [cite:ad15_treasury_arb] The two sides measure different things: the Bureau counted total relief reaching consumers, the Treasury the small sums each person receives and the share that goes to lawyers. Both sets of numbers are accurate on their own terms. The design question is whether a small, widely shared harm should be recoverable at all. The documented pattern is that, once group claims are waived, very few individual claims are brought.

Where it works as promised

Congress has drawn limits where it chose to. Arbitration clauses in residential mortgages are barred by statute, and the Military Lending Act bars them in many forms of credit to servicemembers. [cite:ad15_audit_cfpb_bars] In 2022 a bill sponsored by Senators Kirsten Gillibrand, a Democrat, and Lindsey Graham, a Republican, passed the Senate by voice vote and was signed on 3 March, letting anyone alleging sexual assault or harassment at work choose court over arbitration, including in pending cases. [cite:ad15_npr_efaa] [cite:ad15_wh_hr4445] The Federal Arbitration Act itself is designed friction: a deliberate choice, made a century ago, to make private agreements to arbitrate binding. Its extension to standard-form consumer and job contracts with class waivers came through court rulings and a congressional repeal, each with recorded votes and named parties on both sides. That is a record a voter can read and a legislature can change.
