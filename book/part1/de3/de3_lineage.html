The Ancestors the Texts Name

Every body of political writing tells a story about where it came from, and the story is itself an argument. A writer who says he descends from Thomas Hobbes is claiming the authority of the founder of modern political science; a writer who says he descends from a slaveholding Victorian sage is making a different claim, and a more dangerous one. This section takes the ancestors the Dark Enlightenment texts name for themselves, in their own words, and sets them beside what those ancestors actually wrote and what scholars of the texts have found. It keeps two questions apart throughout. The first is who Curtis Yarvin, writing as Mencius Moldbug, and Nick Land say they inherited from. The second is who other people say they inherited from. Only the first is evidence of what the authors were reading; the second is interpretation, sometimes good and sometimes careless, and it is treated in the last section of this part.

The body of texts is small and its authorship is clear. Yarvin wrote the blog Unqualified Reservations under the Moldbug pen name from 2007, largely stopping in 2013, and Land wrote a ten-part web serial titled "The Dark Enlightenment" between 2 March and 20 July 2012, which was gathered into an e-book in 2014. [cite:de3_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de3_land_de] Both are described in [scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two]. Land's serial is useful here for a particular reason: it is a reader's guide to Moldbug, written by an admirer who quotes him at length and says plainly which ideas are Moldbug's and which ancestors Moldbug claimed. Where Land's account of Moldbug can be set beside Yarvin's own catalogued writing, the two agree.

Hobbes and the problem of the sovereign

The first ancestor Land names is Hobbes. In the opening instalment he calls the current he is describing "recognizably Hobbesian, a coherent dark enlightenment," and sets out its question in a single line: "From Thomas Hobbes to Hans-Hermann Hoppe and beyond, it asks: How can the sovereign power be prevented – or at least dissuaded — from devouring society?" [cite:de3_land_de] The Hobbesian inheritance, as Land states it, is the belief that sovereignty cannot be abolished or divided, only located. He credits Moldbug with the same starting point: Moldbug's turn to reaction, he writes, came with "the (Hobbesian) recognition that sovereignty cannot be eliminated, caged, or controlled," so that constitutions "have exactly as much real authority as a sovereign interpretative power allows them to have." [cite:de3_land_de]

This is a genuine borrowing and an honest one. Hobbes's Leviathan (1651) argued that people escape a war of all against all by handing an undivided power to a sovereign, and the Dark Enlightenment texts share his low opinion of human appetites. They part from him in one respect that matters for the rest of this book. Hobbes grounded the sovereign's authority in the consent of those who authorise it; Land's version keeps the undivided sovereign and drops the consent, treating the democratic masses as "a howling irrational mob." [cite:de3_land_de]

The Austrian school, and the journey away from it

The second inheritance is libertarian economics of the Austrian school, the tradition of Ludwig von Mises and his American student Murray Rothbard, which holds that markets coordinate knowledge better than planners and that the state is at best a necessary evil. Here the texts are unusually frank, because the inheritance is described as something abandoned. Land writes that "Moldbug's formative influences are Austro-libertarian, but that's all over," and quotes Moldbug's reason: libertarians "cannot present a realistic picture of a world in which their battle gets won and stays won." [cite:de3_land_de]

Yarvin's own titles say the same. Princeton University Library holds an 82-page compilation, "Moldbug on Carlyle" (2010), which gathers three of his blog essays; the one he placed first, as the introduction, was published in February 2010 under the title "From Mises to Carlyle: My Sick Journey to the Dark Side of the Force." [cite:de3_princeton_carlyle] The historian Joshua Tait, who wrote the chapter on Moldbug in Oxford University Press's Key Thinkers of the Radical Right (2019), reports that the law professor and blogger Glenn Reynolds introduced Yarvin to Mises and Rothbard, and that the Austrian school's preference for deduction from first principles over empirical testing shaped his habits of argument. [cite:de3_tait] [cite:de3_wiki_yarvin]

Hans-Hermann Hoppe: the bridge

The figure who connects the two inheritances is Hans-Hermann Hoppe, a German-born economist who taught at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas from 1986 to 2008 and called Rothbard his "principal teacher." [cite:de3_wiki_hoppe] His collection Democracy: The God That Failed (2001) argued that monarchy, which he called "privately-owned government," is preferable to democracy, "publicly-owned government," while holding that both are worse than a stateless "natural order" or private law society. [cite:de3_wiki_dtgtf] Land quotes Hoppe's central passage at length: a hereditary king treats the country as his property and so has reason to preserve its value, while a democratic politician is a "temporary and interchangeable caretaker" who owns only its current use, "usufruct," and so consumes its capital. [cite:de3_land_de]

This argument is the hinge of the whole body of texts, because it turns a libertarian case against the state into a case for a particular kind of ruler. Moldbug's answer to Hoppe's problem was to make the owner a corporation rather than a family. The journalist Ava Kofman, writing in The New Yorker in 2025, credited Hoppe's book with pushing Yarvin from libertarianism toward monarchism, and quoted the scholar of authoritarianism Julian Waller calling it "such a direct influence that it's kind of obscene." [cite:de3_wiki_dtgtf] Hoppe's own response to that attribution, which complicates it, is examined in the third section of this part.

Thomas Carlyle

The most consequential ancestor Yarvin claimed is the Scottish writer Thomas Carlyle (1795–1881), who held that history is made by great men, that societies need strong rulers rather than parliaments, and who defended slavery in the West Indies. Yarvin did not cite him in passing. He devoted three essays to him: "Why Carlyle Matters" and "Carlyle in the 20th Century: Fascism and Socialism," posted in consecutive weeks in July 2009, and the February 2010 introduction already quoted, compiled together as Moldbug on Carlyle. [cite:de3_princeton_carlyle] Tait names Carlyle, with James Burnham and Hoppe, as the writers whose arguments that democracy had failed prompted Yarvin's endorsement of authoritarian rule. [cite:de3_tait] [cite:de3_wiki_yarvin]

The Carlyle essays also contain one of the hardest passages in Yarvin's writing. A 2009 post, quoted by Media Matters in 2021, said that "the innate character and intelligence of some is more suited to mastery than slavery. For others, it is more suited to slavery," and that the pattern was "most parsimoniously explained by genetic differences." [cite:de3_mm] [cite:de3_audit_mm] The line is in Yarvin's own voice. The paragraph that precedes it in the original post is not quoted in the reports that carry the line, and the passage is examined, with that limit stated, in [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|Part Six]. A widely repeated line attributing to him the words "some races are better suited for slavery than others" is a paraphrase circulated as a quotation; it is not his wording.

James Burnham and Frederick the Great

Two further names come from the texts' account of how power really works. James Burnham, an American former Trotskyist, argued in The Managerial Revolution (1941) that a class of managers, neither capitalists nor workers, was becoming the new ruling class, and followed it with The Machiavellians (1943) on how organised elites govern behind democratic forms. [cite:de3_wiki_burnham] Tait lists Burnham among Yarvin's sources. [cite:de3_tait] The debt shows in the idea that the official map of power diverges from the real one, which Moldbug called formalism: the proposal to make the real distribution of power official, so that whoever actually rules owns the rule openly. Land summarises the step: "Unless ownership of the state is formally transferred into the hands of its actual rulers, the neo-cameral transition will simply not take place." [cite:de3_land_de]

The last named ancestor is a ruler rather than a writer. Neocameralism, Moldbug's word for his proposed state, takes its name from cameralism, the administrative science of eighteenth-century German states, and his model monarch was Frederick the Great of Prussia (reigned 1740–1786). [cite:de3_wiki_yarvin] A passage Land reproduces, without naming its source, as a summary of the neocameralist position says the "full neocameralist approach has never been tried," but its closest equivalents are "enlightened absolutism as represented by Frederick the Great," and Hong Kong, Singapore and Dubai, states "weak only in political freedom." [cite:de3_land_de] That claim is a testable one, and [scene:de8_tests_it_fails|Part Eight] tests it.

What the lineage claims, against what the ancestors held

A claimed ancestry is not the same as a faithful one, and three of these borrowings change the ancestor in transit. Hoppe's preference for monarchy was a preference for the lesser of two evils; his stated ideal remained a world without a coercive state. [cite:de3_wiki_dtgtf] The texts' own admission that neocameralism "has never been tried" means the Frederick and Singapore comparisons are analogies, not precedents. [cite:de3_land_de] And Hobbes's sovereign rested on the consent of those who created it, the very thing the later texts discard. The comparison below sets each claim beside the record.

What this lineage establishes is a reading list and a direction of travel: from free-market libertarianism, through Hoppe's argument for ownership over stewardship, to an open defence of hierarchy and concentrated rule. Each step is documented in the authors' own titles and quotations.
