NUMEC: The Uranium Shortage at Apollo, 1957–1967

In early 1965, while Washington was arguing with Israel over inspections at Dimona, a routine count at a nuclear-fuel plant in Apollo, Pennsylvania, found that a significant quantity of government-owned highly enriched uranium could not be accounted for. The plant belonged to the Nuclear Materials and Equipment Corporation, NUMEC. The question of where that uranium went has never been officially answered. It has been asked, in one form or another, since 1965, and its most serious form, whether some of it reached Israel's weapons program, has been argued for decades without a charge, a trial or a finding. This section tells what the documents show for the first decade, from the company's founding in 1957 to a government audit of June 1967, drawing chiefly on the primary records published by the National Security Archive in 2016 in a briefing book edited by Roger J. Mattson, Avner Cohen and William Burr. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] What happened from 1968 onward is told only in outline here, as it falls outside this volume's period.

The question is best approached from what the editors themselves concluded. They call the evidence of diversion "strong but not incontrovertible." The Atomic Energy Commission said it found no evidence of theft; there is no sign that the AEC or the FBI looked for such evidence when the loss was found; and many CIA records remain redacted. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] Each of those three facts limits the others. The absence of a finding of theft has to be read beside the absence of a search for one, and both beside the records that are still closed. The account that follows keeps them together.

Founding, 1957. NUMEC was formed in 1957, with investors from Apollo Industries and with Zalman Shapiro as a founder and later its president. Nuclear Regulatory Commission records say that processing of highly enriched uranium at Apollo began in 1957, and that fuel manufacture began in 1959. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The company processed government-owned enriched uranium into fuel for naval and commercial reactors. [cite:apolloaffair_summary] The uranium was not NUMEC's. It belonged to the United States government and was leased to the company, which meant that the government, through the AEC, was both the owner of the material and the regulator responsible for counting it. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

The shortage, 1965. In early 1965 a routine AEC inventory found a significant shortage of government-owned highly enriched uranium leased to NUMEC. On 10 August 1965 Shapiro told the AEC's commissioners that NUMEC had already paid the AEC "up to $1 million for material losses" in earlier years. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The admission suggests how losses had been handled until then: as a commercial matter, settled by payment to the owner. It also shows that the 1965 shortage was not the first.

The AEC's accounting experts then ran a plant-wide inventory in November 1965. An AEC survey report of 6 April 1966 put the cumulative inventory difference at 178 kg of U-235, of which 93.8 kg was unexplained. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

"Not now warranted," February 1966. Between the November inventory and the April report, the AEC's chairman made a decision of lasting consequence. On 14 February 1966 Glenn Seaborg told the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy that "an inquiry by the FBI is not now warranted." [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The mechanism matters here. The material was the government's, and the AEC both owned it and counted it. One account describes AEC leadership as having discouraged bringing in the FBI at all, keeping the matter deliberately "in-house." [cite:foreignpolicy_numec] Seaborg's words to the Joint Committee are the documented form of that choice.

The FBI's question, June 1966. The FBI did come to Shapiro in 1966, but on another matter. On 15 June 1966 its Pittsburgh office interviewed him about whether he should register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. He described ISORAD, a joint venture of NUMEC and the Israeli Atomic Energy Commission. He was not asked about the missing material. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The interview records, from Shapiro's own account, a formal business tie between his company and Israel's atomic energy authority; it records nothing about the uranium, because the question was not put.

A later summary account states that the FBI investigated NUMEC from 1965 to 1980. [cite:apolloaffair_summary] The primary documents for 1965–67 do not show an FBI inquiry into the loss in those years: the AEC chairman told Congress in February 1966 that none was warranted, and the FBI's one documented contact, in June 1966, concerned foreign-agent registration. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The two statements can be reconciled only if the 1965 start date is counted from the discovery of the loss rather than from an investigation of it. On the documents published by the editors, a request for an investigation of Shapiro's relationship with the Israeli government came from the CIA in 1968, as told below. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

The GAO's figure, June 1967. A report by the General Accounting Office dated 20 June 1967 said that NUMEC's records showed cumulative losses of U-235 through 31 December 1966 of about 260 kg, about 1.2 percent of receipts. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] It is the last of the figures that falls inside this volume, and it was dated ten days after the war of June 1967 ended.

How much was missing. No single number answers that question, because the sources measure different things at different dates. Each figure has to be kept with its unit, its date and its source. The AEC's April 1966 report gave a cumulative inventory difference of 178 kg of U-235, of which 93.8 kg was unexplained; the editors' introduction speaks of about 200 kg. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The GAO in June 1967 gave cumulative losses through the end of 1966 of about 260 kg. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] A later summary account gives documented inventory losses of 269 kg before 1968 and 76 kg after, and describes them as the largest at any American commercial nuclear facility. [cite:apolloaffair_summary] And the figure most often quoted, 206 pounds, is about 93 kg, close to the AEC's unexplained 93.8 kg rather than to its total. [cite:nsarchive_numec] [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

The distinction between these figures is the heart of the matter. A loss recorded in a plant's books, an inventory difference and an unexplained remainder are three different quantities. Part of an inventory difference can be explained, as the AEC's own split between 178 kg and 93.8 kg shows; a figure of losses is not a figure of material taken. The question whether any of it was diverted turns on the unexplained part, and on what happened to it. The documents of 1965–67 establish the quantities; they do not establish the cause.

Shapiro. The scrutiny of the case has always centred on NUMEC's president. In the documents of these years, his tie to Israel is a declared, formal one: ISORAD, the joint venture with the Israeli Atomic Energy Commission that he described to the FBI. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] Later records go further. According to FBI reports declassified between the mid-1980s and 2009, Shapiro had contacts with Israeli "Science Attaché" officials and with individuals from Shin Bet and Mossad believed to be tied to LAKAM, the scientific-intelligence unit that would later run Pollard. [cite:nsarchive_numec] Those reports belong to the later investigations, and they record contacts, not a transfer of material. Shapiro was never charged. In 2009 his lawyer asked the Nuclear Regulatory Commission to clear him formally; the Commission refused, stating that it had no documents proving that diversion had occurred but could not conclude that it had not. He died in 2016. [cite:apolloaffair_summary]

Dimona in the same years. The NUMEC shortage was found in the same months in which American scientists were trying, with limited success, to see inside Israel's reactor at Dimona, a story told in [scene:s11d|Johnson's two tracks]. The two questions ran in parallel, in different agencies, and nothing in the documents of 1965–67 connects them. At Dimona, Eshkol insisted on 4 January 1965 that the American visitors come as "invited guests," not "inspectors." [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] The team of 1966 found no evidence of weapons work but could not account for 80 to 100 tons of uranium concentrate bought from Argentina. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d289] And Dimona's reprocessing plant, William Burr and Avner Cohen write, was hidden from all nine AEC teams that visited between 1961 and 1969. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen]

The two cases should not be merged. The Argentine material was uranium concentrate, a different material from the highly enriched uranium missing at Apollo, and the 1966 team's inability to account for it is a separate finding. One reading finds a shared pattern in the American response in these years: in both, the government agencies responsible accepted limits on what they checked. At Dimona those limits were set by Israel; at Apollo, on the documents, the decision not to bring in the FBI was the AEC's own. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

After 1967, in outline. The case changed in 1968, beyond this volume's period, and is told in the volumes that cover those years. Three later documents frame it. On 2 April 1968 the CIA's Director, Richard Helms, asked Attorney General Clark for a "discreet intelligence investigation" of Shapiro's relationship with the Israeli government, which Hoover approved on 6 May; on 10 September 1968 four Israelis, among them Avraham Hermoni and Rafi Eitan, visited the Apollo plant. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] Eitan was later exposed as LAKAM's chief in the Pollard affair of 1985. [cite:nsarchive_numec] The briefing book reports a CIA conclusion that environmental sampling near Dimona in 1968 had detected uranium enriched to the same unusual specification as NUMEC's missing material, and that Helms raised it with Johnson personally, in an exchange still classified. [cite:nsarchive_numec] And a GAO study of 1978, declassified in May 2010, concluded that a concerted, timely effort by the AEC, the CIA and the FBI could plausibly have resolved the diversion question, "if they desired to do so": a finding about the effort, not about the uranium. [cite:apolloaffair_summary]

The claim and the record. It is often assumed that if American uranium had gone to Israel's weapons program, it would have been prosecuted as the serious national-security crime it would have been. The documents of 1965–67 show why that assumption cannot settle the question in either direction. No one was prosecuted, but no one, on the record, was looking for a crime in those years: the AEC declined an FBI inquiry, and the FBI's interview concerned registration, not uranium. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] The absence of a prosecution therefore tells us little about what happened to the material. Equally, the absence of a finding of theft does not establish that there was none: the AEC said it found no evidence of theft, but there is no sign that it looked for such evidence. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec]

What the record does not settle. Where the unexplained uranium went is not established by any document consulted for this book. The editors, who assembled the fullest primary record, judge the evidence of diversion "strong but not incontrovertible." [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] Against it stand the AEC's statement that it found no evidence of theft and the fact that no one was ever charged. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_numec] [cite:apolloaffair_summary] The quantity in question differs by source and by measure, from 93.8 kg unexplained in April 1966 to about 260 kg of recorded losses in June 1967. Many CIA records remain redacted. And the Nuclear Regulatory Commission's own position, as stated in 2009, is that it can neither show that diversion occurred nor conclude that it did not. [cite:apolloaffair_summary] Whether diversion took place remains, on the record, a contested question.
