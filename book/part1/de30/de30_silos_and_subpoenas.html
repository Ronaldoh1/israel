Silos and Subpoenas: How the Walls Between Datasets Came Down

The federal government holds more personal information than any company: tax returns, Social Security records, Medicaid claims, the files of children who arrived alone at the border and the adults who took them in. For half a century each store was collected for one purpose and walled off by law from the others. Officials call such separate stores "silos." The walls were not accidents. They were built in the 1970s, after investigations showed what a president could do when the files were joined. This section sets out those walls, how each was breached in 2025–26 for immigration enforcement, and what the courts said. The tools that join data once it moves, from brokers to fusion platforms, are the subject of [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four]; here the subject is the law.

Why the walls were built

The Privacy Act of 1974 was Congress's answer to two worries. The Justice Department's own account of the law names them: "curbing the illegal surveillance and investigation of individuals by federal agencies" exposed during Watergate, and "the government's increasing use of computers to store and retrieve personal data." The Act restricts what an agency may disclose about a person from its records. [cite:de30_doj_privacy] Tax records got a stronger wall two years later. Lawrence Gibbs, a former IRS commissioner who helped draft the 1976 rewrite of section 6103 of the tax code, has written that President Nixon "attempted to cause the IRS to provide him with confidential taxpayer information" to audit his enemies, a charge in the articles of impeachment of 1974; the rewrite made every return confidential unless the law expressly allowed disclosure. [cite:de30_bl_gibbs] Section 6103 still lets the IRS give another agency a person's address for a criminal investigation, but only on a written request that supplies the person's name and address and meets the statute's conditions. [cite:ad13_audit_irs_dcir] The domestic surveillance that produced these laws is described in [scene:de29_the_precedent|Part Thirty-One].

On 20 March 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14243, "Stopping Waste, Fraud, and Abuse by Eliminating Information Silos," directing agency heads to give designated federal officials "full and prompt access to all unclassified agency records, data, software systems, and information technology systems." [cite:de19_eo14243] The order did not repeal the statutes; it could not. What followed was a series of agreements between agencies, each tested in court against the old walls.

Tax records

The Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, and the Homeland Security Secretary, Kristi Noem, signed an agreement on 7 April 2025 under which Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) would send the IRS names and addresses of people it said were in the country unlawfully, to be checked against tax records. The Treasury said it rested on "longstanding authorities granted by Congress"; ICE's acting director said it was "strictly for the major criminal cases." The acting IRS commissioner, Melanie Krause, was reported by two people familiar with her decision to be resigning over it. [cite:de30_ap_krause] ICE then asked for the addresses of about 1.28 million people, and the IRS turned over about 47,000 before the courts intervened. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:ad13_audit_irs_dcir] In a suit by the Center for Taxpayer Rights, Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly stayed the arrangement in November 2025 and in February 2026 found that the IRS had broken the law "approximately 42,695 times"; on 8 September 2026 a unanimous appeals panel upheld her block, holding that the procedure failed to ensure ICE's requests met the statute's name-and-address requirement. [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:ad13_audit_irs_dcir] Another panel of the same court had declined in February to stop the sharing in a separate suit, and a federal judge in Massachusetts barred ICE from using the data already received. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:ad13_audit_irs_hoodline] Status as of October 2026: further disclosures are blocked while appeals continue. The effect on immigrant taxpayers is set out in [scene:ad13_immigrants_latinos|the library's volume on the American Dream].

Social Security

Social Security's records were used in two ways. In April 2025, according to an administration official speaking anonymously, about 6,300 immigrants whom DHS identified as paroled people on a terrorist watch list or with FBI criminal records were entered into the agency's Death Master File, the list of people reported dead, which the White House renamed the "Ineligible Master File." The White House said removing the "monetary incentive" would "encourage them to self-deport." AP reported that the people affected were in the country legally; without a working number they could not legally work or use many financial services. [cite:de30_ap_ssadead] The second use was larger. In April and May 2025 DHS, its immigration service and DOGE overhauled SAVE, the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements system built to check immigrants' eligibility for benefits, linked it for the first time to Social Security records, and opened it to bulk checks of whole state voter rolls. [cite:de19_dhs_save] [cite:de24_npr_save] The government says it ran more than 65 million voters in 26 states and flagged 28,635; in Texas about a fifth of those flagged were later found to be citizens. Judge Sparkle Sooknanan held in June 2026 that the system broke the Social Security Act and the Privacy Act; on 25 September the Supreme Court stayed her order over three dissents. [cite:de19_npr_save] A tool built to check immigrants was, by this route, used on citizens.

Medicaid and children's sponsors

Health records crossed in June 2025, when the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, on the direction of the Health Secretary and over objections from its own staff, began sharing Medicaid enrollees' personal data with ICE; a July agreement gave DHS access to names, addresses, birth dates, ethnicity and Social Security numbers. ICE had followed a policy since 2013 of not using Medicaid data for enforcement. Twenty states sued, and in August Judge Vince Chhabria blocked the sharing for their residents, writing that "The record in this case strongly suggests that no such process occurred," meaning the reasoned decision the law requires. [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] On 29 December 2025 he let a narrower exchange proceed: for people not lawfully present, five fields only, citizenship or immigration status, address, phone number, date of birth and Medicaid ID, which he found "clearly authorized by law"; medical information was not cleared. [cite:de30_bl_medicaid] The limits did not hold in practice. On 7 January 2026 CMS sent ICE a dataset covering millions of people, including lawful residents, beyond what the court allowed; ICE passed it to Palantir over a Microsoft Teams chat; Palantir says it was purged on the government's instruction, but CMS later re-sent the file by mistake and about six ICE users were found still holding copies. The judge paused the sharing in late May 2026, and at a hearing on 3 September signalled that valid visa holders' data should not be shared; no formal ruling had been located as of October 2026. [cite:de27_npr_medicaid] [cite:de24b_chn_medicaid]

The files of unaccompanied children show the wall moving in both directions under different presidents. In April 2018 the first Trump administration signed an agreement requiring the refugee office that places these children to share information on their sponsors with immigration enforcement. A Biden rule effective 30 April 2024 barred that sharing. An interim rule of 25 March 2025 removed the bar; in June 2025 a judge blocked new documentation demands on sponsors but left the rule itself in force. [cite:de30_tracker_orr]

Voter rolls

The largest demand was for state voter registration lists, which states hold, not the federal government. The Justice Department sued 30 states and the District of Columbia for unredacted rolls including Social Security and driver's-licence numbers, arguing that the National Voter Registration Act, the Help America Vote Act and the Civil Rights Act of 1960 require states to hand them over for list maintenance. [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] [cite:de30_wex_ninth] By 13 July 2026 it had lost every one of the 13 trial-court decisions on the merits and one appeal. Judge Thomas Johnston in West Virginia found no adequate stated basis or purpose and wrote that "one is left to wonder what the real purpose was," while noting that question was not before him. [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] The Ninth Circuit heard the California and Oregon appeals on 19 May 2026 and "sharply questioned" the department; the appeals were pending as of October 2026. [cite:de30_wex_ninth] [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls]

Taken together, the stores and the demands on them form one architecture: requests and bulk lists go in, and what comes out depends on which court has ruled on which link.

Side doors and subpoenas

A wall in law is only as good as the doors around it. Washington State bars its agencies from sharing data for civil immigration enforcement, yet by 13 October 2026 researchers at the University of Washington had confirmed 356 cases in which federal agents looked up the state's driver's-licence records before an immigration arrest, through a national police data network called Nlets, data brokers or local police; closing one route, they found, pushes agencies to another. [cite:de30_uwchr_dmv] Brokers are a door of their own: utility records on more than 170 million people reached ICE through a commercial database until the supplier stopped in 2021. [cite:de24_globe_utility] The government also has its own key, the administrative subpoena, a demand for records an agency can issue without a judge; DHS's use of it to unmask anonymous critics of ICE online is set out in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Thirty], and its reported use for the financial records of unions and nonprofits in the next section.

What lets one store be matched against another is an identifier both hold. Across the channels documented here, the address and the Social Security number recur most often.

Two things in this record cut against a simple story. The walls were not all built by one party or breached by one: the children's-sponsor arc runs from a Republican agreement to a Democratic bar to a Republican repeal. And the walls mostly held where they were statutory and specific: the tax-privacy section and the federal election laws stopped the largest transfers in court, while SAVE, defended under a 1996 immigration statute, survived the Supreme Court's stay. [cite:de30_tracker_orr] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] [cite:de19_npr_save] What is new in 2025–26 is not any single tool but the joining of civil records, collected to tax, insure and protect, to enforcement.
