The Bavarian Illuminati: The Order and the Myth

Two different things share the name Illuminati. One is a society founded in Bavaria in 1776, with named members, grades, correspondence and a short, documented life that ended under its own government's bans within a decade. The other is a conspiracy story, begun in the 1790s, that credits that society with surviving in secret and steering revolutions, wars and governments ever since, and that was grafted, early in the nineteenth century, onto the older hatred of Jews. Sophia's Suppressed Lineages catalogue states the rule this section follows: the documented organisation and the myth attached to it "are two different objects" and must be kept apart. It also lists the order among suppressed inner traditions, which the record does not support: the Illuminati were Enlightenment rationalists, and the power that suppressed them was a Catholic state, not a church tribunal. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt]

What the order was

Adam Weishaupt was professor of natural and canon law at the University of Ingolstadt. On the night of 1 May 1776 he and four students founded a society first called the Bund der Perfektibilisten, the League of the Perfectible, renamed the Order of the Illuminati in April 1778. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] Its stated aims were to oppose superstition and obscurantism, to reduce the influence of religion over public life, and to check abuses of power by rulers; Weishaupt said he wanted freedom from "religious prejudices" and "a state of liberty and moral equality". He had become strongly anticlerical after years of conflict with the Jesuits at his university. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt]

The order borrowed its structure from the Masonic lodges, which Weishaupt joined and found expensive and closed to his ideas. Members passed through grades, at first novice, Minerval and illuminated Minerval, after the Roman goddess of wisdom, and later through a thirteen-degree system designed by Baron Adolph von Knigge, who joined in 1780 and recruited heavily inside existing lodges. Members took classical code names: Weishaupt was "Spartacus", Knigge "Philo". Goethe was among those who joined. [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] Estimates of its size vary widely: about 650 members can be verified by the end of 1784, Weishaupt and a fellow leader claimed 2,500, historians have estimated about 1,300, and National Geographic's profile gives 2,000 to 3,000. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] What it taught was not a secret gnosis but the reading programme of the German Enlightenment, applied through a hierarchy that asked members to report on their own moral progress to their superiors.

What the state did

The suppression was the work of the Elector of Bavaria, Charles Theodore, not of Rome. An edict of June 1784 banned all societies formed without government authorisation; one of March 1785, later called the order's death blow, banned the Illuminati by name; and a third in August 1787 confirmed the ban and made recruiting punishable by death. Further edicts followed into 1790. [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] The Bavarian authorities seized the order's papers in 1786 and 1787 and published them in 1787, which is why so much of its internal correspondence is known. Weishaupt lost his chair, was banished and lived the rest of his life in Gotha; Knigge had already left in July 1784 after quarrels with him. By the government's own account the order was dismantled. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] Present-day groups that claim descent from it have no documented continuity. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati]

Rome's direct part in the story is small. The order recruited through Masonic lodges that the popes had already condemned in 1738 and 1751, and its anticlericalism was aimed in the first place at the Jesuits who dominated Bavarian education. The ban that ended it was a state ban, issued by a Catholic prince for reasons of public order. [cite:vt30_wiki_ineminenti] [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati]

How the myth was made

The order would be a footnote but for the French Revolution. In 1797 and 1798 two writers, working separately, argued that the Revolution had been planned. The French Jesuit Augustin Barruel, writing in exile in London, published his Mémoires pour servir à l'histoire du Jacobinisme, which traced the Revolution to a decades-long conspiracy of the philosophes, the Freemasons and the Illuminati, carried out by the Jacobin clubs. The Scottish natural philosopher John Robison published Proofs of a Conspiracy with a similar thesis. Both books sold widely and were translated; Edmund Burke praised Barruel. Contemporaries including Jean-Joseph Mounier and Joseph de Maistre rejected the conspiracy claims, and historians reject the master-conspiracy view, while treating Barruel as the father of the modern conspiracy theory. [cite:vt30_wiki_barruel_memoirs] The story crossed the Atlantic at once: a Fast Day sermon by the Massachusetts minister Jedidiah Morse on 9 May 1798 was among the first printed American accounts of the Illuminati. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati]

Barruel's Mémoires did not blame Jews; the historian Danny Keren is cited for that point. [cite:vt30_wiki_judeomasonic] The Jewish element entered through a letter. Dated 1 August 1806 and received by Barruel in Paris on 20 August, it came from a man signing himself Jean-Baptiste Simonini of Florence, who claimed to have posed as a baptised Jew in Piedmont and to have been told that Jews had founded both the Freemasons and the Illuminati and meant to take over Europe. Barruel could not trace the writer. He decided not to publish the letter, fearing it would provoke violence against Jews, but he circulated it in influential circles in France; the letter is thought to have been fabricated, probably by police opposed to Napoleon's liberal policy towards Jews. It was first printed in 1878, in the magazine Le Contemporain. [cite:vt30_wiki_simonini] [cite:vt30_wiki_judeomasonic] It is among the earliest texts of the "Judeo-Masonic" conspiracy theory, and its contents are believed to have fed the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, the forgery first serialised in St Petersburg in 1903 and exposed by historians and courts. [cite:vt30_wiki_simonini] [cite:hm_ushmm_protocols]

The graft spread through Catholic as well as secular channels, and the record should say so. Leo XIII's Humanum genus of 1884 did not mention Jews, but a French writer, Léo Taxil, staged a conversion to Catholicism the same year and then sold Catholic readers a fabricated exposé of a Luciferian "Palladist" rite at the top of Freemasonry; some bishops vouched for his invented witness, Diana Vaughan, until Taxil announced the hoax at the Société de Géographie in Paris on 19 April 1897. [cite:vt30_wiki_taxil] In 1902 the Catholic weekly Le Pèlerin printed a drawing captioned "Catholic France driven by Jews and Freemasons"; in the 1930s a Catholic priest, Juan Tusquets, translated the Protocols and built a "Judeo-Masonic-Communist" conspiracy theory that served as a justification for the Francoist regime; and in 1935 Nazi propaganda described Freemasonry as an organisation "beholden to Jewry". [cite:vt30_wiki_judeomasonic] In the United States the evangelist Pat Robertson's The New World Order (1991) linked the Illuminati and Freemasons to an alleged one-world plot by central bankers; reviewers in The Christian Century and The New York Review of Books showed that it drew on the antisemitic writers Nesta Webster and Eustace Mullins. Robertson said in 1995 that he had intended no antisemitism and regretted that his references to "European bankers" were read as meaning Jews. [cite:vt30_wiki_robertson_nwo] Contemporary "New World Order" and Bilderberg theories reuse the same tropes, often naming the Rothschilds or "international bankers". [cite:vt30_wiki_judeomasonic]

Every link in that chain fails on the documents. The Bavarian order's papers were seized and printed by its own government in 1787, and historians reject the claim built on them that it planned the Revolution; the order was dismantled between 1785 and 1787, before the French Revolution began; the Jewish element rests on a letter its own recipient would not vouch for and that historians treat as a fabrication; and the later forms rest on the Protocols, a proven forgery, or on Taxil, a confessed hoaxer. The claim that Jews direct the Freemasons or the Illuminati is an antisemitic conspiracy theory, not a contested historical question, and this book does not repeat it as anything else. The same applies to the "Rothschild" variant, whose documented core, papal loans arranged through the Rothschild bank from 1832, is examined and separated from the myth in [scene:vt11_papal_loans|Part Eleven: the papal loans].
