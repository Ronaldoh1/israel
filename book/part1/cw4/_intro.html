In March 1957 Israel left Sinai and Gaza, forced out by American and Soviet pressure after the war told in [scene:cw3_withdrawal|the withdrawal]. It came out of that war with less than it wanted and more than it showed. It stood outside every regional alignment, since Western defence in the region had been organized around the Baghdad Pact without it. It had no American weapons of consequence: the largest American arms sale to Israel had carried a ceiling of $1 million. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] It had no written guarantee from Washington, only the Eisenhower Doctrine's promise of support against a Communist attack, never put on paper. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d36] What it did have, unannounced, was a French commitment made in the autumn of 1956 to supply a research reactor, and a request on the table in Paris for a plant to extract plutonium. [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt] [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel]

This stage asks how Israel armed itself in the five years after Suez, and from whom: a reactor able to produce plutonium from France, money and arms from a West Germany that would not exchange ambassadors with it, secret partners beyond the Arab states, and, in August 1962, the first major American weapon. It asks with equal weight what each supplier wanted in return, who inside each government resisted, and what Israel told the governments that found out. The answer is not one decision but a set of channels, most of them secret, each run by a few officials and each contested inside its own government.

The cast is wide. In Israel, Ben-Gurion led the government until he resigned in protest in January 1961, a resignation that forced an early election; Shimon Peres ran the French and German channels; Isser Harel's Mossad ran the intelligence partnerships, the capture of Adolf Eichmann and the negotiation over Morocco's Jews; Golda Meir and Abba Eban carried the diplomacy. In France a small circle of ministers and atomic officials made the reactor agreement over the hesitation of the head of their own atomic agency, and Charles de Gaulle tried to take back part of it. In Bonn, Franz Josef Strauss and Konrad Adenauer made a partnership kept off the books. In Washington, Eisenhower and Dulles declined to arm Israel or guarantee it in writing, discovered Dimona late, and handed the question to Kennedy, who accepted a visit in place of inspection and in 1962 offered the Hawk. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] Around Israel stood Nasser's United Arab Republic, Adnan Menderes's Turkey, the Shah's Iran and a new king in Morocco. Palestinians appear in the governments' papers chiefly as refugees; in these same years Fatah took shape abroad and Al-Ard inside Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_palquest_fatah] [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij]

The sections that follow begin with [scene:cw4_french_deal|the French reactor], from Atoms for Peace in 1955 to de Gaulle's compromise of 1960. The regional story comes next: the periphery doctrine and its secret intelligence pact with Turkey and Iran, the upheavals of 1958 in Lebanon and Iraq, and the ten days in August when Israel closed its airspace to Western flights. Two sections turn inward and outward from Israel's own society, to the protest of North African immigrants at Wadi Salib in 1959 and to Palestinian politics in exile and under military rule. The armistice lines with Syria and the Sinai surprise of 1960 follow, then Bonn's quiet partnership and the politics of the Eichmann case, and Iran's oil, intelligence ties and unannounced recognition. The core of the stage is the reactor's discovery: how Washington found it, what each government said in the disclosure month of December 1960, and Kennedy's first visit and his meeting with Ben-Gurion at the Waldorf-Astoria. The Lavon affair's second round, which brought down the government, the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961, and the agreement that opened Morocco's gates complete the picture, and the stage closes with [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision], when American refusal became an offer.
