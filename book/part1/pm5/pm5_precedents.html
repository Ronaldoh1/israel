Before the Super PAC: Four Defeats, 1982–2002

The campaigns this part follows, from 2022 to 2026, are large, recent and fully documented in federal filings. They have a prehistory, and it is often cited, sometimes as proof that the pattern is old and sometimes as proof that it is a myth. Four defeats of sitting members of Congress, in 1982, 1984 and 2002, are the usual examples. This section sets out what the sources used here document about each, which is less than the stories told about them, and why the evidence is thinner than for the races that follow.

Findley and Percy. In 1982 Representative Paul Findley of Illinois, a Republican, lost his seat to the Democrat Richard Durbin. In 1984 Senator Charles Percy of Illinois, a Republican, lost to the Democrat Paul Simon. In both races activists associated with AIPAC backed the challenger. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The races entered the lobby's reputation as proof that crossing it could end a career. Like any close election they had other causes, which the sources used here do not weigh, and those sources do not quantify the pro-Israel money in either race, and this volume does not supply a figure.

Hilliard and McKinney. The two defeats of 2002 are closer to the modern pattern, and better reported. In June of that year Representative Earl Hilliard of Alabama, a member of the Congressional Black Caucus who had been critical of Israeli policy, lost a primary runoff; Black Caucus leaders publicly questioned the role pro-Israel money had played in his defeat. [cite:pm_jweekly_mckinney] In August Representative Cynthia McKinney of Georgia, a five-term incumbent whom that newspaper's headline called "anti-Israel," lost her primary to Denise Majette, a former state judge, by 58 percent to 42. Majette had strong support from pro-Israel contributors outside Georgia; the contemporary account in J., the Jewish weekly of Northern California, quoted a political scientist, Gilbert Kahn, saying that the money gave Majette the "nest egg" she needed to put herself before voters. [cite:pm_jweekly_mckinney]

The same account recorded the other causes. Republicans crossed over to vote in the Democratic primary, urged by Georgia party leaders, and McKinney herself blamed them: Republican voters, she said, "wanted to beat me more than the Democrats wanted to keep me." The regional director of the Anti-Defamation League attributed her defeat to her statements after the attacks of 11 September 2001, which, she said, voters saw as "fringe positions." McKinney also had substantial outside support of her own. [cite:pm_jweekly_mckinney] Both sides, in other words, had money in the race, and the contemporary record does not let a reader isolate the effect of either.

Why the old cases are weaker evidence. All four races share a feature that sets them apart from what came later. The money in them moved as individual contributions and through independent PACs, given to the challenger; it did not move through a super PAC spending in its own name, because no such thing existed before 2010. That makes the money harder to count and easier to dispute. In the races of 2022 to 2026, by contrast, a single committee connected to AIPAC reported every dollar it spent for and against named candidates. The modern record therefore answers a question the older one cannot: not whether pro-Israel donors preferred one candidate, which they often did, but how much an organization spent, on what, and against whom. The rest of this part follows that record, race by race.
