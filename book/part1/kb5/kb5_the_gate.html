Forty Years, the Ban of 1756 and the Opening

The rule that no one should study Kabbalah before the age of forty is often cited as ancient. The record shows a history. The historian Moshe Idel traced it to views on the age of forty in the Talmud and in Arab tradition, and found the first kabbalistic restrictions in the thirteenth-century circles of Moses ben Simeon of Burgos and Abraham Abulafia; Shem Tov ibn Gaon gave the prohibition special emphasis and traced it to his teacher, Solomon ibn Adret. [cite:kb_idel_age40] In the seventeenth century the legal commentator known as the Shach wrote that "some have written that one should not study kabbalah until age forty", adding that holiness and purity were also required. [cite:kb_ou_age40]

The rule was turned into law after the Sabbatean disaster. In 1756 a rabbinic court at Brody, endorsed by the Council of Four Lands, banned the study of Lurianic Kabbalah before the age of forty and permitted the Zohar and certain other works only to those over thirty. [cite:kb_ou_age40] The ban was explicitly anti-Sabbatean. [cite:kb_ou_age40] The same years saw Frank's movement come to its crisis. [cite:kb_wiki_frank] The gate around the orchard was now a written ban, enforced by the communal leadership.

At the same time, in the same region, the gate was being opened from below. Israel ben Eliezer, known as the Baal Shem Tov, the "Master of the Good Name", born around 1698 to 1700 and died in 1760, began teaching publicly around 1733 as a healer and mystic. [cite:kb_tau_hasidism] The movement that grew from his circle, Hasidism, had a popular literature "profoundly shaped by kabbalistic ideas" and "appealed to the masses and not only to the elite". [cite:kb_tau_hasidism] Its central change concerned devekut, cleaving to God. In the Kabbalah, Britannica notes, devekut had been "a privilege of the spiritual aristocracy"; in Hasidism it was, in principle, "open to every Ḥasid". [cite:kb_brit_devequt] The rabbi Jacob Emden, a fierce opponent of the Sabbateans, criticised the Hasidim for studying the Zohar regularly. [cite:kb_ou_age40]

The establishment fought back. From 1772 a campaign in Shklov, Vilna and Brody excommunicated the Hasidim, inspired and driven by Elijah ben Solomon, the Gaon of Vilna, who was regarded as a supreme religious authority. His followers saw Hasidism as "a threat to traditional structure and order" and feared a new outbreak of "quasi-Sabbatian tendencies". [cite:kb_tau_hasidism] The campaign failed. Hasidism "became exceptionally popular in Ukraine, where it appealed to the majority of the traditional Jewish community". [cite:kb_tau_hasidism] In 1912 Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook wrote that the old restriction applied to practical Kabbalah and that intellectual study was not off limits. [cite:kb_ou_age40]

The pattern of The Canon is visible here, with one difference. As there, a mass movement claiming direct access to the divine provoked the guardians to close the gate tighter, by age, by ban and by excommunication. [cite:kb_ou_age40] [cite:kb_tau_hasidism] The difference is the outcome. The Western church's gate held for centuries; the rabbinic gate around Kabbalah was opened within two generations, by a movement that stayed inside the tradition and gave its central experience to anyone. [cite:kb_brit_devequt]
