Keren Hayesod: Fundraising Becomes Permanent

The Balfour Declaration gave the Zionist movement a political opening. Three years after the declaration, the movement built a permanent institution to raise it.

On December 24, 1920, at a Zionist conference in London, the World Zionist Organization founded Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund, as its central and permanent fundraising arm. Its originators were Hillel Zlatopolsky and Isaac Naiditch. The fund was registered in Britain in 1921, and in 1926 its headquarters moved to Jerusalem. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] Two features defined it. It was open to non-Zionist donors as well as Zionists, and it relied on a self-imposed, tithe-style system of giving. Its founders contemplated raising £25,000,000 within five years. The records cited here document that target as an aim; they do not show whether it was reached.

A familiar account holds that Zionist fundraising stayed informal and improvised until American donors organized it in the late 1930s. The founding record of Keren Hayesod tells a different story. By the time the United Jewish Appeal formed in 1939, Keren Hayesod had already been operating as a structured, international fundraising institution for nearly two decades. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

The fund also drew on public figures who did not otherwise agree on much. Chaim Weizmann, president of the World Zionist Organization, and Albert Einstein, the most famous scientist of the era, were among the named early figures associated with its fundraising, alongside Ze'ev Jabotinsky, who would lead the movement's more militant Revisionist wing. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] The combination suggests a fund designed to reach across the movement's factions. It does not show that the three men worked closely together or agreed on strategy; their disagreements in this period were real and substantial.

What the money paid for is as revealing as how it was raised. Histories of this era tend to concentrate on land purchase, but Keren Hayesod's spending reached well beyond it. It financed the absorption of immigrants, settlement, defense and water development, and it backed major enterprises including the Palestine Electric Company and the Palestine Potash Company. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] These were the pieces of a national economy: power, water, industry and the means to receive new arrivals. The available record names these categories of investment; it does not give an itemized account of how much went to each.

The fund's institutional importance grew further at the end of the decade. In 1929 Weizmann and Louis Marshall launched an enlarged Jewish Agency for Palestine, giving non-Zionists equal representation alongside Zionists, and Keren Hayesod became its financial instrument. [cite:yivo_weizmann_encyclopedia] [cite:israeled_jewish_agency_expands] The fund was thereby folded directly into the body that would act as a government-in-waiting before statehood.

Marshall died in September 1929, within weeks of the Agency's launch. His successor as leader of the Agency's non-Zionist wing in the United States was Felix Warburg, who became chairman of its Administrative Committee and Weizmann's senior non-Zionist counterpart. [cite:britannica_jewish_agency] Warburg had earlier been a prominent, on-record opponent of Zionism. His new role was not a reversal of that position so much as an expression of the Agency's design: its non-Zionist seats were meant for people who supported the development of Palestine without endorsing political Zionism in full.

The arc from a London conference room in 1920 to the financial center of the enlarged Jewish Agency in 1929 shows how quickly the movement's fundraising became infrastructure. A fund with named founders, a stated target, a registered legal form and a headquarters in Jerusalem was a different kind of instrument from a collection plate. The first American campaigns, described in [scene:us_s01|the following section], show how hard its leaders fought over who would control it. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]
