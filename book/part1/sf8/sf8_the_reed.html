The Reed Cut from Its Bed

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

Rumi's great poem, the Masnavi, opens with a reed flute. In one modern translation it asks the listener to "Pay heed to the grievances of the reed / Of what divisive separations breed", and tells how it was cut from the reedbed. [cite:sf_ic_reed] In another English version the reed says: "Since I was cut from the reedbed, I have made this crying sound", and "Anyone pulled from a source longs to go back." [cite:sf_ua_reed]

The Sufi tradition has always read the reed as the soul. It was cut from its source, its bed, and the music it makes, the whole of the poem that follows, is the sound of its longing to return. The inner reading follows that tradition and draws one thread through this shelf. Enoch ascended to the throne; the Gnostic spark longed for the Pleroma; Kabbalah's sparks are gathered back to their source; and here the reed's song is the longing itself. [cite:et_cojs_enoch14] [cite:kb_brit_luria] In every case the inner traditions describe a soul away from home and a path back.

The image adds something the other books did not. A reed makes music only after it has been cut and hollowed. The separation that causes the longing is also what makes the song possible. Read this way, the soul's distance from its source is not only a loss but the condition of its voice, and the path back is sung, not only walked.

The record limits the reading, and adds a warning from Part Six. The second English version quoted here is by Coleman Barks and John Moyne, whose versions are interpretations rather than translations. [cite:sf_ua_reed] [cite:sf_pf_rumi] Rumi wrote as a Muslim, "a lifelong scholar of the Koran and Islam"; the reed's longing, in his own world, was the longing of a soul for God as Islam names Him, not for a universal spirituality without a name. [cite:sf_pf_rumi] The inner reading keeps that context in view.
