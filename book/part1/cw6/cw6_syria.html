Syria's Radical Turn, 1963–1966

Between March 1963 and November 1966 Damascus passed through two coups, a failed countercoup and a purge of the founders of its own ruling party, and emerged with a government that an American intelligence assessment called "the most bellicose Arab state," bound by treaty to Egypt and sheltering the Palestinian guerrilla organization to which, by the same assessment, Israel attributed 61 sabotage incidents since January 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt] This section follows that turn and the mechanism that tied it to the Israeli frontier. It stops short of the air battle of 7 April 1967, which belongs to the spring of the following year.

The Ba'th takes power, 1963. The Ba'th party came to power in Syria in the coup of 8 March 1963. From July 1963 the dominant figure was Amin al-Hafiz. The party was not united. Between May 1964 and February 1966, according to the Library of Congress's country study, power struggles between its centrist and leftist wings brought two Alawi generals, Salah Jadid and Hafiz al-Assad, to decisive power. [cite:rs_cw6_locsyria]

The same years saw Syria act against the line taken by Cairo on Palestinian matters. At the Arab summit at Alexandria in September 1964, the League recognized the new Palestine Liberation Organization over Syrian and Saudi opposition, according to the Institute for Palestine Studies' chronology. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] The PLO was, in the CIA's later description, an organization over which Nasser had considerable influence. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_plo] Damascus would find its own Palestinian instrument elsewhere.

The coup of 23 February 1966. On 23 February 1966 Jadid, Assad and Nureddin Atassi carried out a coup that put the more radical wing of the Ba'th in power. [cite:rs_cw6_locsyria] GlobalSecurity calls it "the bloodiest of the many coups d'etat since 1949." That is that source's characterization; the fetched sources give no casualty figure against which to test it. Amin al-Hafiz was wounded and arrested, and Aflaq and Bitar were expelled from the party. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_syria] The coup was therefore not only a change of government but a break within the Ba'th itself: the faction that won expelled leading figures of its own party.

The new order took shape within days. Assad's appointment as defence minister was announced after the coup. On 1 March 1966 a government was formed with Atassi as president and Yusuf Zuayyin as prime minister, while Jadid led through his post in the party. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_syria] The arrangement put the formal offices in one set of hands and the real authority, through the party, in another; and it put the army, the institution that would face Israel, under Assad. It did not end the contest for power. A countercoup by the Druze officer Salim Hatum failed on 8 September 1966. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_syria] The GlobalSecurity account, on which the details of March and September rest, is a reported source and not a scholarly study; the Library of Congress confirms the coup of 23 February and its leaders, but not the later details. [cite:rs_cw6_locsyria] [cite:rs_cw6_gs_syria]

The mechanism: base, training, propaganda. What tied Syria's internal upheaval to the Israeli border was its sponsorship of Fatah. The CIA's memorandum of 2 December 1966 sets out the instruments in three words: Damascus gave Fatah a base, training and a propaganda outlet. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] The FRUS editors record that Fatah's incidents along Israel's borders, supported by Syria, increased from mid-1965. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_f] The money, according to the same CIA memorandum, did not come mainly from Syria: Fatah's funds came chiefly from wealthy Palestinians in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] Nor did its raids leave mainly from Syrian soil. The CIA wrote that infiltrations were launched from Jordanian and Lebanese territory "without the approval of either the Jordanian or the Lebanese government." [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966]

That arrangement is the key to the period's logic. A government in Damascus could sponsor an armed campaign whose operations crossed into Israel from its neighbours' territory, and whose consequences, in Israeli reprisal, would fall first on those neighbours. Less than three weeks before the CIA wrote, Israel's raid at Samu had struck Jordan, not Syria, and an American official had called it "the wrong target." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] Fatah's own campaign, and the dispute over its first operation, is told in [scene:cw6_fatah|Fatah's campaign].

Syria was also, in these years, the site of the Arab plan to divert the Jordan's headwaters. The September 1964 summit chose diversion and financed works in Lebanon and Syria, and Israeli forces attacked the works in Syria in March, May and August 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_wolf_unu] That contest is told in [scene:cw6_water|the water war]. It meant that by 1966 the Syrian front carried two quarrels at once: one over water and the zones along the line, and one over the guerrillas Damascus supported.

The pact with Egypt, November 1966. In November 1966 Egypt and Syria signed a five-year defence pact. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt] The sources fetched for this section give its date and term but not its text. Its importance lay in what it linked. Before November 1966 a clash on the Syrian line was a matter between Israel and Damascus; afterwards it engaged Cairo's commitments as well. It was signed in the weeks before the CIA described Syria as the most bellicose Arab state. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] Syria's relations with its other neighbour also worsened that year: after Hussein withdrew Jordan's official endorsement of the PLO in July 1966, the PLO and the Syrian government turned against him, according to GlobalSecurity's account. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_a]

The Soviet dimension. Accounts of this period often place the Soviet Union behind the Damascus government. The sources fetched for this chapter do not document Soviet arms, advisers or political backing for Syria between 1963 and 1966, and this section does not assert any. The first dated Soviet act in this record that concerns Syria comes in May 1967, when Moscow told Egypt that Israel was massing forces to attack Syria, information that, Elbahtimy writes, "proved to be false"; that warning opens the crisis told later in this chapter. [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy] Whatever Soviet support Damascus enjoyed before that point is not established here.

The line on the map. The frontier where these forces met is drawn in the map below, which traces the Golan line from 1949 to the present. Its first two stages fall before June 1967. The map opens in 1949 with the armistice, which left small demilitarized zones along the line without agreed sovereignty. Its second stage, "DMZ & water war, 1949–67," is labelled "both sides," and the label is the record's. In a meeting with American officials in 1953–54, recorded by the State Department, Moshe Dayan said Israel claimed sovereignty over Jewish-owned land in the zones, that "someone must keep the area alive," and that Israel did not want Syria to have direct access to the Jordan River. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz] Syria fired on Israeli farmers and fishermen from the heights; Israel sent armoured tractors to cultivate disputed land and carried out reprisal raids. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz] [cite:w_dayan] From 1964 the dispute over the zones was joined by the dispute over water. The map's arrow from the second stage to June 1967 is labelled "escalation"; this section has followed the Syrian politics that drove it from the Damascus side, and the water war told elsewhere in this chapter follows it on the ground, including Dayan's own later account, given in 1976 and contested, that most of the clashes were provoked by Israel.

Who drove the Syrian front? The sources disagree on the central question of these years, and both positions rest on serious authority. The first emphasizes Israeli pressure. The Library of Congress's Egypt study says that in 1967 Israeli leaders repeatedly threatened to invade Syria and overthrow its government if raids across the Syrian border did not stop, and the historian Henry Laurens describes the Israeli Chief of Staff Rabin's "show of force" against Syria. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt] [cite:rs_cw6_laurens] On this reading, the danger on the Syrian line came at least as much from Israel's threats against the regime in Damascus as from the raids those threats answered.

The second emphasizes Syrian sponsorship. The CIA, writing in December 1966, called Syria "the most bellicose Arab state" and set out how it gave Fatah a base, training and a propaganda outlet. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] On this reading, the threats of 1967 were a response: a government that sheltered and trained a guerrilla organization raiding Israel from its neighbours' territory could not expect to stand outside the consequences. The two readings are not strictly exclusive. The CIA described Syrian conduct in 1966; the Library of Congress and Laurens describe Israeli statements chiefly in 1967; each can be true at once. What divides them is the weight each gives to cause and response, and the sources fetched here do not settle that weight.

What the record leaves open. The fetched sources leave much of Syria's own decision-making unseen. They do not contain Syrian government documents explaining why the Jadid regime backed Fatah, or how far the officers in Damascus controlled the raids they sponsored. They give no casualty figure for the coup of 23 February 1966 to support or test GlobalSecurity's description of it as the bloodiest since 1949. They do not give the text of the November 1966 pact with Egypt, or its exact day. And they do not document Soviet backing for Damascus before May 1967. What they do establish is the shape of the change: by the end of 1966 power in Syria had passed, by force, to a party faction that had expelled Aflaq and Bitar, that sponsored the guerrilla campaign against Israel, and that had a defence treaty with Egypt. The border with that government in the spring of 1967 is the subject of [scene:cw6_april_1967|the 7 April air battle].
