Jacob de Haan and the Orthodox Opposition, 1919–1924

On the evening of 30 June 1924, Jacob Israël de Haan left evening prayers at a synagogue on Jaffa Road in Jerusalem and was shot three times. He died soon after. No one was charged [cite:w_dehaan]. The killing is widely described by historians as the first political assassination inside the Jewish community of Palestine, and its victim was neither a British official nor an Arab leader. He was a Jew, a former Zionist sympathizer, and in the years before his death the most effective political voice, in English, of the Jewish community that rejected the Zionist movement's claim to speak for it [cite:w_dehaan].

A common account of these years holds that Jewish opposition to Zionism before 1948 was marginal, peaceful, and faded away on its own. De Haan's career does not fit that account. The opposition he represented was organized, it had a leadership, it took its case to the British authorities and to Arab rulers, and it was met, at least once, with a bullet.

A poet and lawyer in Jerusalem. De Haan was born in the Netherlands in 1881 and was a poet and a lawyer by training. He arrived in Palestine in 1919 as a newspaper correspondent, and he taught law in Jerusalem at the Mandate government's law classes [cite:w_dehaan]. He came with some sympathy for Zionism. He moved from that sympathy to the other side of the question entirely, into the Haredi camp, the strictly Orthodox community of the old Yishuv [cite:w_dehaan].

The "old Yishuv" was the name for the Orthodox Jewish communities of Jerusalem, Safed, Hebron and Tiberias, which had lived in the country for generations before political Zionism existed [cite:w_dehaan]. Its leadership, including Rabbi Yosef Chaim Sonnenfeld, rejected a secular Jewish state on religious grounds, and objected to the Zionist Organization speaking in its name. That objection had an organizational form older than the Mandate. Agudat Israel (also spelled Agudath Israel) was founded in 1912 as a worldwide movement to strengthen Torah-observant Judaism, and specifically to separate it from the emerging secular Jewish nationalist movement, which its founders regarded as a danger to Torah-observant Jewish life [cite:w_dehaan] [cite:unpacked_neturei_karta_history] [cite:wiki_neturei_karta]. Its opposition to political Zionism on religious grounds was thus organized thirty-six years before the State of Israel was founded. It was a theological position, not a reaction to any later political dispute [cite:unpacked_neturei_karta_history].

This was the community de Haan came to represent, and he became its most effective political voice in English. He wrote for British newspapers and lobbied Mandate officials directly [cite:w_dehaan].

The argument. De Haan's case was narrow and precise. The Zionist Organization, he argued, was one political movement among Jews. It was not the representative of all the Jews in Palestine [cite:w_dehaan]. For the old Orthodox community, whose presence in Jerusalem long predated the Zionist movement, that distinction was the whole point: a secular national movement should not speak in the name of religious Jews who rejected its aims. De Haan pressed that distinction on British officials, and argued to them that the Zionist Organization did not speak for all Jews in the country [cite:w_dehaan].

The method of this opposition is worth stating plainly. It was a claim about representation, and de Haan carried it to two audiences: the Mandatory power, whose officials he lobbied, and Arab leaders, with whom he sought a separate understanding [cite:w_dehaan].

The approach to the Hashemites. His most provocative act was diplomatic. He sought a separate understanding between Orthodox Jews and Arab leaders, on the premise that Jews who did not seek a state need not be in conflict with the Arab majority [cite:w_dehaan]. In 1924 this led him to meetings with the Hashemite leadership in Transjordan [cite:w_dehaan]. He was then preparing to travel to London with an anti-Zionist Orthodox delegation, to put the community's case at the seat of the Mandatory government [cite:w_dehaan]. He was killed before he could go.

The sources in this book do not record what was said at the Transjordan meetings, what, if anything, was agreed, or what the London delegation intended to ask for. They establish the direction of his effort, not its content.

The killing and sixty years of silence. After the shooting on Jaffa Road, each side looked to the other. The Zionist leadership publicly condemned the killing. Orthodox leaders blamed Zionist circles [cite:w_dehaan]. No one was charged, and for sixty years the question of who had ordered the killing remained formally open [cite:w_dehaan].

The silence broke in 1985. Avraham Tehomi, by then elderly, said on Israeli television that he had shot de Haan, and that he had done so on the orders of the Haganah, the underground defense force of the Jewish community [cite:w_dehaan]. Tehomi was no peripheral figure. He was the Haganah's Jerusalem district commander from 1929 to 1931, and he then led a group that seceded from it and, together with Revisionist groups, formed the Irgun Zvai Leumi; he returned to the Haganah in 1937 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_tehomi]. That later history is told in [scene:s07e2|the armed line].

The dispute over the order. Tehomi went further than naming the Haganah. He named Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, later Israel's second president, as the man from whom the order had come [cite:w_dehaan]. Here the record thins, and the two parts of the confession have to be weighed separately.

The first part, that the shooting was a Haganah operation, carried out by the man who was speaking, is the documented core of the case. It is a confession by the gunman himself, and it is what allows historians to treat the killing as an act of the organized Jewish community's armed wing rather than a private crime [cite:w_dehaan].

The second part rests on Tehomi alone. Ben-Zvi had died in 1963, twenty-two years before the interview, and could not answer. No second witness or document in the sources places the order with him, and some historians dispute his involvement [cite:w_dehaan]. Tehomi's account of his own act and of the organization behind it is a confession; his account of who stood above him is an accusation against a man who could no longer reply, given six decades after the event, with nothing else in the sources to support it. The honest conclusion is the narrower one: the record establishes the gunman and the organization he said he served, and it does not establish a single, settled chain of command above him [cite:w_dehaan].

What the case shows. Three things are established. Organized Jewish opposition to Zionism existed inside Palestine in the early Mandate years, with a leadership, an international organization behind it and a public spokesman. It was argued openly, to the British and to Arab leaders. And the Zionist movement's own armed wing, by its gunman's admission, killed its most effective spokesman [cite:w_dehaan]. The familiar picture of a dissent that was marginal and peaceful and simply faded is incomplete on each count.

This opposition was also distinct from the other Jewish dissent of these years. The binationalists of Brit Shalom, founded in 1925, argued from inside Zionism about the form a national home should take; the old Yishuv rejected the national project itself on religious grounds. Their story is told in [scene:rd3_brit_shalom|Brit Shalom and the Hebrew University].

Orthodox opposition did not end with de Haan's death. Its later history lies outside this chapter, but its next turn is documented: in 1938 Neturei Karta split from Agudath Israel to preserve a more uncompromising anti-Zionist position, after Agudath Israel began making practical accommodations with Zionist institutions [cite:britannica_neturei_karta]. The split itself shows that Agudath Israel, which had organized the old Yishuv's refusal, had by then begun to accommodate the institutions it once opposed. The 1912 founding marks where the organized religious objection began, not a full history of how each of its branches moved afterward [cite:unpacked_neturei_karta_history].

What remains unsettled is considerable. The sources do not say who, if anyone, above Tehomi gave the order, if Ben-Zvi did not. They do not record why no one was charged in 1924, what the British authorities established about the crime, or what the Hashemite leadership said to de Haan. They do not show whether his London delegation would have changed anything. They establish a spokesman, an argument, a killing and a confession, and they leave the decision behind the shot where Tehomi left it, resting on his word alone.
