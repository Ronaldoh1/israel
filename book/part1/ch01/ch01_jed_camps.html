Camps and Emissaries

For many American Jewish children, the most powerful Jewish education happens not in a classroom but at summer camp. It is also where the Israeli state's own educational arm reaches them most directly.

The emissaries

Each summer the Jewish Agency for Israel, the body that organized Jewish immigration before 1948 and still works closely with the Israeli government, sends young Israelis to work in North American Jewish summer camps. They are called shlichim, Hebrew for "emissaries." In 2025 it sent a record 2,050 of them to 190 camps, where the agency says they reached more than 150,000 campers, staff and community members. That summer's war with Iran closed Israeli airspace for seven days, and the agency moved some of them out by bus through Jordan to keep the programme running. [cite:jed_jns_shlichim2025]

To campers the emissaries are, first of all, staff: counsellors and activity leaders who happen to be Israeli. Nothing in the record suggests that individual emissaries are given scripts. But the programme exists, by the agency's own description, to build connection to Israel, and it is run and paid for in part by a body tied to the Israeli state.

"First we teach a love for Israel"

The leaders of the largest camp networks have been open about the order in which they teach. In 2018, asked about counsellors who wanted to teach campers about the occupation, the national director of the Conservative movement's Ramah camps, Mitchell Cohen, said he was open to nuanced Israel education, provided senior staff vetted any programme. He summed up the approach in one line: "First we teach a love for Israel. Then we teach the nuances and the conflict." [cite:jed_jta_counselors2018]

That sentence describes the design of most American Jewish Israel education, and it is a defensible choice for a community passing on its attachments to its children. Its consequence is a matter of timing: a child who leaves Jewish education early may hear only the first stage.

The counsellors who objected

In 2018 a group of young American Jews set out to change that. IfNotNow, a movement opposed to Israel's occupation of the West Bank and Gaza, launched a campaign called "You Never Told Me." Former campers, day-school pupils and youth-group members asked the institutions that had taught them to teach Israel more fully, including the occupation and Palestinian accounts of 1948. The group published a "Liberation Syllabus" of readings, among them Rashid Khalidi's Palestinian Identity and Edward Said's The Question of Palestine. [cite:jed_972_ynt2018] In May 2018 it ran a day-long training in Boston for about a dozen counsellors from eight Reform, Conservative and liberal Zionist camps, on how to bring Palestinian perspectives into their bunks. [cite:jed_jta_counselors2018]

The camps' answer was that programming is decided by camp leadership, not by counsellors or outside movements. Both sides were describing the same structure: what children hear about Israel at camp is decided by the camp's leaders. The emissaries are invited and paid to be there. The counsellors who wanted to add the occupation were not.
