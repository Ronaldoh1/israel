The 2009 Memorandum: Raw Intercepts, Americans Included

The signals-intelligence relationship between the United States and Israel is the one tie in this book whose working rules are known from the agencies' own documents. Israel's signals unit, called the Israeli SIGINT National Unit (ISNU) in American papers and Unit 8200 in Israel, is reported to have been founded in 1952 under military intelligence, as told in [scene:cw1_cia_liaison|the first ties with American intelligence]. [cite:unit8200_wiki] Its partnership with the National Security Agency grew over the following decades, but its terms became public only in 2013, when The Guardian published a memorandum provided by the former NSA contractor Edward Snowden. This section sets out what that memorandum says, how the data moves, and what limits it places on each side.

What the memorandum says. The memorandum between the NSA and ISNU, agreed in principle in March 2009, begins with the practice it regulates: "NSA routinely sends ISNU minimised and unminimised raw collection." [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [cite:ow_aj_nsamou] "Raw" is defined to include "unevaluated and unminimized transcripts," and the material is described in The Guardian's account as intercepts of telephone and internet traffic that had not been screened to remove information about American citizens. [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] "Minimization" is the American legal process for masking or deleting information about US persons before intelligence is shared or used; the memorandum covers material sent before that step. [cite:ow4_techdirt]

The obligations on Israel are few, and none is legally binding. Israel agreed not to deliberately target Americans identified in the data, to handle it according to US law and to respect Americans' constitutional privacy rights. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] Israel may retain "any files containing the identities of US persons" for up to a year, and is to consult an NSA liaison when such files are found. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] It must "destroy upon recognition" any communication to or from an official of the United States government, a category The Guardian described as covering the executive branch, Congress and the federal courts. [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] Access is limited to Israeli personnel with a "strict need to know." [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] Israel may pass material to "outside parties" with the NSA's written permission, after stripping out identifying details. [cite:ow4_techdirt] On the American side, the NSA undertakes to "regularly review a sample of files transferred to ISNU to validate the absence of US persons' identities." [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian]

The memorandum then removes its own force: "This agreement is not intended to create any legally enforceable rights," and it disclaims being an international agreement or a legally binding instrument. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] Techdirt's summary of the document put the result plainly: the agreement "places no legally binding limits on the use of the data by the Israelis." [cite:ow4_techdirt]

The stated purpose. The NSA did not deny that Americans' communications were included. It said that any US-person information it acquires "is handled under procedures that are designed to protect privacy rights," that "whenever we share intelligence information, we comply with all applicable rules," and that "NSA cannot, however, use these relationships to circumvent US legal restrictions." [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] It declined to say whether the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court had approved the arrangement. [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] The rationale for sending raw rather than finished intelligence is not stated in the memorandum itself; the NSA's general position is that partnerships with foreign services strengthen the security of both nations. [cite:ow_int_cash2014]

The minimization asymmetry. Inside the United States, Americans' communications collected by the NSA pass through minimization before they are used or shared. The memorandum moves a part of that material to a foreign service before that step, and puts in its place non-binding Israeli undertakings (not to target Americans deliberately, one-year retention, destruction of officials' communications, need-to-know access) and one American sampling check. The protection is uneven even on its own terms: the communications of members of Congress, judges and executive officials are to be destroyed when recognized, while those of other Americans may be kept for a year. [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] And none of it is enforceable. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] The published accounts of the memorandum describe no reciprocal clause obliging ISNU to send its own raw collection, or Israelis' communications, to the NSA under comparable terms. [cite:ow4_techdirt] [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian]

The limits the NSA did hold. The relationship has not been without American refusals. In an internal account of the 2006 war in Lebanon, published by The Intercept in 2019, the NSA's liaison officer in Tel Aviv described ISNU's heavy reliance on the agency for time-sensitive tasking, warning and geolocation on Hezbollah. [cite:ow4_int2019] The geolocation requests were "particularly problematic," because the NSA was prohibited from providing information usable in targeted killings; ISNU's commander sought an exemption, and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence brokered a framework whose terms the documents do not give. [cite:ow4_int2019] A 2007 NSA presentation put ISNU's size at about 5,500 conscripts and 1,200 career officers. [cite:ow4_int2019]

Is it still in force? The memorandum of 2009 is the last version of the rules to have been published. The public record does not show whether it remains in force, has been renewed or has been replaced, as of October 2026, and neither government has said. The general terms of American intelligence-sharing agreements with foreign governments are not published; the American Civil Liberties Union, seeking them through freedom-of-information requests, listed Israel among the countries with which the United States has unilateral data-sharing arrangements, and noted that even the FBI's rules for sharing data collected under Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act are entirely redacted in the court-approved guidelines. [cite:ow4_aclu]

What the record does not settle. How much American data has been sent under the memorandum, how often the NSA's sample review has found US-person identities, and whether any file has been destroyed or retained under its terms are not public. Whether the arrangement was ever presented to the surveillance court is not known. And whether its terms still govern the flow in 2026 is, on the public record, an open question.
