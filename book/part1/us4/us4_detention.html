Detention Without Trial, Military Courts and Children in Custody

Both states hold people without convicting them of a crime. The forms differ, and so does the scale relative to the population each governs. This section sets out Israel's administrative detention and military-court system, the American cases of Guantanamo and immigration detention, and how each system treats children. The comparison is unequal in one respect that should be stated at the start: most of the people Israel holds without trial are Palestinians from territory under its military rule who are not its citizens, while most of the people the United States holds without a criminal conviction are non-citizens held under immigration law. Neither group votes for the government that holds them.

How administrative detention works in Israel

Israel has two administrative-detention regimes. Inside Israel, the Emergency Powers (Detention) Law of 1979 lets the defence minister order a person detained for up to six months when there are "reasonable grounds to presume that the security of the state or public security require the detention". The detainee must be brought before the president of a district court within 48 hours, and the detention is reviewed every three months, with appeal to the Supreme Court. The law draws on Mandate-era emergency statutes and applies only during a declared state of emergency, which has been in force continuously since 1948. [cite:us4_acri_admin] Part Two sets out that standing emergency. [scene:us2_emergency|Part Two: emergency powers] In the West Bank, Military Order 1651 lets a military commander detain a person for up to six months at a time, renewable in six-month periods with no maximum total; at the time of the Association for Civil Rights in Israel's review, a military judge had to review the order within eight days. In both regimes most of the evidence is classified and not shown to the detainee or the lawyer, and the High Court may hear petitions with only the state present. [cite:us4_acri_admin] A third category, people classed as "unlawful combatants" under a separate law, is also held outside the criminal process and counted separately. [cite:us4_hamoked_charts]

The numbers show how far use of these powers has changed. HaMoked, an Israeli rights group that publishes monthly figures obtained from the Israel Prison Service, recorded 356 administrative detainees in June 2020. By August 2023 the number had reached 1,201, the most since its monthly data began in 2001. [cite:us4_hamoked_charts] [cite:wb_tna_hamoked2023] After the October 2023 attack and the war that followed, it rose much higher: on 1 September 2025 HaMoked counted 11,040 Palestinian prisoners in Prison Service custody, of whom 3,577 were administrative detainees and 2,662 were held as "unlawful combatants". [cite:wb_mem_hamoked2025] In October 2026 it counted 3,101 administrative detainees and 1,419 unlawful combatants among 9,265 Palestinians held in the "security" category; only 1,509 of them were serving a sentence after conviction, and another 3,236 were held on remand awaiting trial. [cite:us4_hamoked_charts] These figures cover the Prison Service only; people held in army facilities such as Sde Teiman are not included.

Two systems of courts on one territory

Administrative detention sits inside a wider arrangement. In the West Bank, Israeli citizens are tried under Israeli law in Israeli civilian courts, while Palestinians are governed by military orders issued since 1967 and earlier Jordanian law and tried in military courts for offences from traffic violations to theft. In the Association for Civil Rights in Israel's 2014 comparison, an Israeli suspect had to be brought before a judge within 24 hours and a Palestinian within 96. [cite:wb_acri_twosys] An internal army document reported by Haaretz showed that in 2010, 99.74 per cent of military-court trials of Palestinians ended in conviction: 25 acquittals in 9,542 cases. [cite:wb_972_conviction] The West Bank volume and Part Nine set out this dual system in full. [scene:wb7_courts|The West Bank: the courts] [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank]

Children

Military Order 1651 sets the minimum age of criminal responsibility in the West Bank military courts at 12. Children of 12 and 13 can receive up to six months in custody; those of 16 and over are sentenced as adults. A juvenile military court was set up in September 2009, and in 2012 Military Order 1685 cut the time a child could be held before a judge reviewed the case from eight days to four. [cite:us4_wiki_minors] UNICEF reported in March 2013 that about 700 Palestinian children aged 12 to 17 were arrested, interrogated and detained each year, and called their ill-treatment "widespread, systematic and institutionalized". [cite:wb_un_unicef2013] In June 2025, by B'Tselem's count from Prison Service data, 359 Palestinian minors were held. [cite:us4_wiki_minors]

The American juvenile system moved the other way over the same decades. The number of young people held in juvenile residential placement fell 65 per cent between 1997 and 2019, to 36,479; by 2022 the count was 75 per cent below 2000, though it had risen about 10 per cent since 2020. [cite:us4_ojp_juv2022] [cite:us4_nij_juv2024] The decline did not close the racial gap in who is held: Black youth made up 41 per cent of those in placement in 2019. [cite:us4_ojp_juv2022] The World Prison Brief records minors as 0.1 per cent of the American prison and jail population in 2023 and 2.3 per cent of Israel's in 2017, the latest Israeli figure it holds. [cite:us4a_wpb_us] [cite:us4a_wpb_il]

The American record: Guantanamo and immigration detention

The closest American counterpart to detention without trial on security grounds is the military prison at Guantanamo Bay in Cuba. Nearly 800 men have been held there as "law of war" detainees since January 2002. By 1 July 2026, 15 remained, according to Amnesty International; six of them had never been charged or tried, three of whom had been approved for transfer, some after more than two decades in custody. [cite:us4_amnesty_gtmo] The scale today is small, but the legal principle, indefinite detention without criminal trial on the executive's security assessment, is the same one that critics of Israel's administrative detention object to.

Immigration detention is far larger. It is civil, not criminal, detention: people are held pending a decision on whether they may stay, not as punishment. TRAC, a research centre at Syracuse University that tracks federal data, recorded a peak of 68,289 people in Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) detention in early February 2026, 73.6 per cent of whom had no criminal conviction; on 4 April 2026 the figure was 60,311, of whom 70.8 per cent had none. [cite:us4_trac_ice] Part Six takes up the law and politics of that system. [scene:us6_asylum|Part Six: asylum and detention]

Put side by side, the two records show the same tool used at very different scales. In 2026 Israel held about 4,500 people without charge in security detention, nearly all of them Palestinians from the West Bank and Gaza; the United States held 15 at Guantanamo. The United States, for its part, held tens of thousands of non-citizens in civil immigration detention, most with no criminal record. In both systems most of the people held have no vote in the government that holds them, and in both the executive, not a criminal court, decides how long security detainees stay.
