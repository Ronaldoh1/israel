Two Schools in One Cabinet: Sharett, Ben-Gurion and the Retaliation Policy, 1953–1956

Israeli policy on the borders in these years was not made by one mind. Between July 1953 and June 1956 the state's founding prime minister withdrew, returned and pushed out his successor, and the argument between them, over whether to answer attacks across the armistice lines with force or to hold back and negotiate, ran through the cabinet for three years. A common picture treats Israel's retaliation raids of the period as the settled policy of a united government. The record shows a contest, fought in cabinet votes, in a diary kept by the man who led the losing side, and in Washington's terms for a security guarantee.

The sequence began with David Ben-Gurion's withdrawal. Speaking to the Knesset in 1965, he recalled that the government granted him a long leave on 7 July 1953. On 18 October 1953 he submitted a three-year security plan, which the government ratified. He resigned with the government on 7 December 1953 and retired to Sde Boker on 13 December. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_bg_portfolio] The dates matter for what came next: the security plan was laid down by Ben-Gurion before he left, four days after the Qibya raid of 14–15 October had drawn condemnation at the United Nations, told in [scene:cw2_unit101_qibya|Unit 101 and Qibya].

Moshe Sharett, until then foreign minister, became prime minister. The Prime Minister's Office lists the Fifth Government, under Sharett, as serving from 26 January 1954 to 29 June 1955. Its listing of the Sixth Government, also Sharett's, gives an end date of 3 November 1955 and a start date that appears garbled, so the exact day the second Sharett cabinet took office cannot be read from that source. [cite:rs_cw2_pmo_govts] The Defence Ministry was held by Pinhas Lavon. Ben-Gurion's own account is that he "returned to the Ministry of Defense with the Knesset approval on February 21, 1955", replacing Lavon in Sharett's cabinet after fourteen months of semiretirement. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_bg_portfolio] [cite:rs_cw2_israeled_bg_return] Lavon's fall followed the failed operation in Egypt told in [scene:lv_s01|the Lavon affair]. From February 1955, then, Sharett was prime minister and Ben-Gurion was his defence minister.

The historians who have read this period most closely describe two schools. Avi Shlaim calls Sharett the leader of the "negotiation" school and Ben-Gurion of the "retaliation" school, and puts the difference between Sharett and Dayan sharply: Sharett "sought to avoid escalation in order to prevent war", while Dayan "needed escalation to bring about war". [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] Neil Caplan, who has edited selections from Sharett's diaries, writes that Sharett was "deeply troubled by the growing popularity of the competing 'activist' strategy of stiff reprisals" advocated by Ben-Gurion, Dayan and others. Caplan stresses something easily missed: Sharett led cabinet majorities for restraint, and he blocked proposals to capture the Gaza Strip or the Straits of Tiran. [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] The moderate was not simply overruled. For much of his premiership he won the votes.

The diary is the main witness to the argument, and how it reached print shapes how it can be used. Sharett's diary was first published in a self-censored form; his son Yaakov Sharett later incorporated the remaining entries into the multi-volume Yoman Ishi (Personal Diary). [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] The best-known English excerpts come from a 1980 study by Livia Rokach, published by the Association of Arab-American University Graduates with a preface by Michael Adams. In Rokach's translations the diary records Dayan, on 31 January 1954, answering "of course" when asked whether his course meant war with Egypt; Lavon, on 25 January 1955, proposing to occupy the Gaza Strip and the Syrian demilitarized zone; and Dayan, on 26 May 1955, calling retaliation actions "our vital lymph". [cite:rs_cw2_rokach] After the Israeli raid on Gaza of 28 February 1955, Sharett wrote on 1 March: "It is the same old story: hit and run and try to fool the world." [cite:rs_cw2_rokach]

These are dated entries, and the words are Sharett's record of what he heard and thought. The argument built on them is a different thing. Rokach read the diaries as documenting a deliberate strategy of provocation, retaliation, expansion and subversion driven by Ben-Gurion's activists, with Sharett as a largely powerless moderate. Her study is an argued reading of selected entries, published by an association with a declared position, not a neutral edition. [cite:rs_cw2_rokach] Caplan and Shlaim, reading the same diaries, describe a real internal contest instead: Sharett's restraint often prevailed in cabinet, and the activist course won only as the United States refused Israel arms and France offered them. [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] The two readings agree on the existence of the activists and on Sharett's opposition to them. They differ on whether the activists' course was a plan pursued from the start or a policy that prevailed as circumstances changed, and on how much power Sharett actually had.

The activists' case deserves to be stated in its own terms. Israeli civilians were being killed across the armistice lines; in March 1954, eleven Israeli citizens were killed on a bus at the Scorpion Pass, an attack for which the armistice commission, its chairman finding the evidence inconclusive, assigned no responsibility. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3196] [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3252] For Dayan, retaliation was what kept the state alive, its "vital lymph". [cite:rs_cw2_rokach] And the one alternative that might have replaced retaliation, an American guarantee, came with a condition Israel's leaders would not accept. In February 1955, Shlaim records, Dulles offered a mutual defence pact on condition that Israel neither expand its borders by force nor carry out military retaliation. Ben-Gurion and Sharett accepted the first condition but not the second. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] The refusal is notable because Sharett joined it: even the leader of the negotiation school would not give up the option of retaliation in exchange for a guarantee.

Sharett's case was equally concrete. In Shlaim's account he sought to avoid escalation precisely in order to prevent war, which the activists' course risked. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] His diary entry after Gaza objected not only to the raid but to the pattern around it, of striking and then trying to mislead the world about what had been done. [cite:rs_cw2_rokach] His cabinet majorities were the means by which he held the activists back, and his blocking of plans to take Gaza or Tiran shows what those majorities prevented. [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia]

The politics shifted with the election of the Third Knesset on 26 July 1955. There were 1,057,795 eligible voters, and turnout was 82.8 per cent. [cite:rs_cw2_idi_1955] Mapai won 40 seats with 274,735 votes; Herut 15; the General Zionists 13; the National Religious Front 11; Ahdut HaAvoda 10; Mapam 9; the Religious Torah Front 6; Maki 6; the Progressives 5; the Democratic List for Israeli Arabs 2; Progress and Work 2; and Agriculture and Development 1. [cite:rs_cw2_idi_1955] After about three months of negotiation Ben-Gurion formed a government of Mapai, the National Religious Front (soon the National Religious Party), Ahdut HaAvoda, Mapam and the Progressives, with 75 seats, or 80 counting the Arab lists affiliated with Mapai. The Prime Minister's Office dates this Seventh Government from 3 November 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_idi_1955] [cite:rs_cw2_pmo_govts_1955] Ben-Gurion was prime minister again; Sharett stayed on as foreign minister.

The partnership lasted seven months. Sharett resigned as foreign minister on 18 June 1956, and Golda Meir succeeded him. In a letter of 28 July 1956 Ben-Gurion wrote that Sharett's continuing at the Foreign Ministry "was not for the good of the country" and that foreign policy must serve security needs. [cite:rs_cw2_kaller_bg] [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] The letter survives in a dealer's catalogue description and transcription, which is how it is known here. Shlaim's judgment, quoted by Caplan, is that "Ben-Gurion failed to topple Nasser but he succeeded in toppling Sharett." Shlaim also writes that Ben-Gurion authorized secret talks in France and forced Sharett's resignation about a week later, "clearing the way for war". [cite:rs_cw2_caplan_asia] [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_east_west] Where those talks led is told in [scene:cw3_sevres|the Sèvres Protocol].

Several questions remain open on this record. The garbled start date of Sharett's second government in the official listing leaves the transition of mid-1955 imprecise. [cite:rs_cw2_pmo_govts] The sources here do not give the contents of Ben-Gurion's three-year security plan of October 1953, so how far it prefigured the retaliation policy of 1955–56 cannot be judged from them. The diary is known in English largely through selections, Rokach's and Caplan's, each made for its own purpose; a reader without the Hebrew Yoman Ishi sees the entries those editors chose. And the central dispute, whether the activists pursued war as a plan or arrived at it as American arms were refused and French arms offered, is not settled by the entries quoted here: they show what Dayan and Lavon said to Sharett and what Sharett thought of it, not what the cabinet as a whole intended.
