The Mysteries and the Ascent: What Is Documented

Ron's earlier Book 1 placed the IS-RA-EL lens at the end of a long account of the ancient "mystery schools" and argued that they taught, by ordeal and allegory, a single pattern: the ascent of the soul toward the divine. That pattern is a real subject of historical study, and much of it is documented. Some of what is said about it is not documented at all, and some is demonstrably a construction of the seventeenth to twentieth centuries. This section separates the two. It first sets out what the sources show about the Greek, Egyptian and Hellenistic mysteries; then the ascent pattern in Jewish, Greek and Indian traditions; then the later claims of an unbroken Egyptian "mystery school" lineage, and where they come from.

Eleusis: a secret that was kept

The Eleusinian Mysteries were, in Britannica's words, the "most famous of the secret religious rites of ancient Greece". They were built on the myth told in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, in which the goddess searches for her daughter Kore, or Persephone, carried off by Hades. Candidates were purified at the Lesser Mysteries at Agrai in late winter and initiated at the Greater Mysteries at Eleusis in the autumn, after a ritual sea bath and three days of fasting. The central rites in the hall of initiation "were and remain a secret": there is "no sure evidence of what the rites actually were", and the initiate was "promised benefits of some kind in the afterlife". [cite:ts13_brit_eleusis] The documented fact is the secrecy itself, kept over centuries by large numbers of initiates. What was taught inside is not known, and any confident account of it is reconstruction.

Isis: Apuleius, Book Eleven

The fullest literary account of an initiation into it is in a Latin novel of the second century CE, the Metamorphoses of Apuleius, also called The Golden Ass. In its eleventh book the narrator, Lucius, restored from the shape of a donkey by the goddess, is initiated into her mysteries. He tells only what may be told to the uninitiated. He says he "reached the very gates of death" and, "treading Proserpine's threshold", passed through all the elements and returned; that he saw "the sun at midnight shining brightly"; and that he came before the gods below and the gods above. [cite:ts13_cam_apuleius] It is a novel, and a literary source, but it is the nearest thing to a first-person description of an ancient mystery initiation, and its shape, a symbolic death, a passage through darkness, light at midnight, a return, is the pattern Book 1 drew on.

The Hermetica and Iamblichus

The Corpus Hermeticum is a collection of Greek treatises attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, a figure who combines the Greek god Hermes and the Egyptian god Thoth. The treatises were written in about 100–300 CE and compiled by Byzantine editors; the best known is the Poimandres. When a manuscript reached Florence, Marsilio Ficino broke off his translation of Plato for Cosimo de' Medici to translate it first, because it was believed to be older than Plato. In 1614 the scholar Isaac Casaubon showed from its Greek that it belonged to the Roman period, and it "lost its claim to predate Plato". Some scholars since the Nag Hammadi finds of 1945, such as Jean-Pierre Mahé, have argued for a background in Hellenistic Egyptian texts. [cite:ts13_wiki_hermetica] The Hermetica are therefore genuinely Egyptian in setting and partly in inheritance, and genuinely late in composition: they are not a pharaonic text.

On the Mysteries, attributed to the Neoplatonist Iamblichus (an attribution first found in Proclus), is a reply, written under the name of an Egyptian priest, Abammon, to the critical Letter to Anebo of Iamblichus's teacher Porphyry. It is "primarily concerned with ritual and theurgy", the use of rites to bring the soul into contact with the gods, and its Greek text was printed in Venice by Aldus in 1497. [cite:ts13_wiki_iamblichus] It is the main surviving defence, from inside late pagan philosophy, of the view that ritual initiation, not argument alone, raises the soul.

The ascent pattern across traditions

The pattern of ascent appears in traditions that did not borrow from one another, and it is documented in their own texts. In Plato's Symposium, the priestess Diotima describes a lover whose attention rises from beautiful bodies to souls, then to institutions and knowledge, "until he catches a glimpse of Beauty itself". [cite:ts13_hooper_plato] Plotinus, the third-century Neoplatonist, taught an ascent that reverses the soul's descent from the One: purification, awakening of the intellect, and finally union with the One, which cannot be forced, on the principle that "like knows like". [cite:ts13_mac_plotinus]

Jewish mysticism had its own. Merkabah mysticism took its start from Ezekiel's vision of the throne-chariot. Its Hekhalot texts, redacted between the third and ninth centuries CE, describe rabbis such as Akiva and Ishmael ascending through seven heavens and seven palaces, past guardian angels, with purification, hymns and secret names. Its practitioners were called, paradoxically, yordei merkavah, "descenders to the chariot", and the Mishnah restricted the teaching of the chariot to the qualified few. [cite:ts13_wiki_merkavah] In India, the early Upanishads, dated by many scholars to about 800–500 BCE, teach that the individual self, atman, is one with Brahman, summed up in the Chandogya Upanishad's "tat tvam asi", "Thou art that", and that liberation comes through realising it. [cite:ts13_wh_upanishads]

The resemblance across these texts is documented; a single source behind them is not. The record shows similar maps drawn in Greece, Egypt, Judaea and India, some with clear lines of influence (Plato on Plotinus, Plotinus and the Hermetica on Iamblichus) and some with none that can be shown. To call them "one teaching under many names", as Book 1 did, is an interpretation of that resemblance. It is a reasonable reading, but it is a reading, and this volume labels it so.

The "Egyptian mystery school" lineage: a later construction

A separate claim is often attached to this material: that an organised Egyptian mystery school passed its secrets in an unbroken chain to modern esoteric orders. That chain is not documented, and the record shows where much of it was made. In 1731 the Frenchman Jean Terrasson (1670–1750) published Sethos, a fantasy novel set in ancient Egypt that, in one reference work's words, "elided Masonic and ancient Egyptian ritual"; it inspired Mozart and Schikaneder's Magic Flute. [cite:ts13_wiki_terrasson] A Masonic encyclopedia notes that "under the form of fiction" it described Egyptian initiation and that later Masonic writers, Lenoir, Clavel and Oliver among them, drew on it freely for their accounts of ancient rites. [cite:ts13_umf_sethos] In 1779 the adventurer Cagliostro founded an Egyptian-themed lodge at Mitau, in today's Latvia; he claimed to have learned the secrets of Egypt and met Hermes Trismegistus, and a Masonic museum's account says he and an associate "invented the legend" of their Egyptian journey and calls the Egyptian rituals of the period "speculative and cosmetic in nature". [cite:ts13_mm_cagliostro]

The Rosicrucian story runs the same way. The manifestos that announced a secret Brotherhood of the Rose Cross were printed in Kassel in 1614, Frankfurt in 1615 and 1616; Johann Valentin Andreae, who claimed the third, later called the whole thing a "ludibrium", a jest; the historian Roland Edighoffer states that "there is no proof that the Brotherhood" ever existed; and actual Rosicrucian organisations appear only in the eighteenth century. [cite:ts13_wiki_rosicruc] The best known modern order, the Ancient Mystical Order Rosae Crucis (AMORC), was founded by Harvey Spencer Lewis in the United States in 1915, traces its origins to ancient Egypt and Egyptian pharaohs, and considers itself "a continuation of the ancient mystery schools". [cite:ts13_wiki_amorc] Those are the order's own stated claims; no document links it to Egypt.

The result is a clear division. The ancient mysteries, the Isis cult, the Hermetica, Iamblichus and the ascent literature of Greece, Judaea and India are documented, in texts that can be read and dated. The claim of an institutional Egyptian school surviving into modern lodges is a construction of the early modern period, much of it from a novel. Book 1's argument that the mystery traditions preserved knowledge through allegory rests on the first body of evidence; it does not need, and this volume does not use, the second.
