Both Sides Were Heard: The Anglo-American Committee of 1946

It is sometimes said that no international body heard the Arab case, or the case for a shared state, before the partition vote of 1947. The record shows otherwise. The year before the question of Palestine went to the United Nations, a joint British and American committee heard Zionist leaders, Arab spokesmen and Jewish advocates of a binational state, and then issued a report that rejected both a Jewish and an Arab state. What happened to that report is as revealing as what it said.

The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry was announced in November 1945. It had twelve members, six Americans and six Britons, and it was created as Washington pressed London to admit Jewish displaced persons into Palestine. Its American members included Bartley Crum and James McDonald; its British members included Richard Crossman. [cite:w_aac] Between January and March 1946 the committee held hearings in Washington, London, the displaced-persons camps of Europe, Cairo and Jerusalem. [cite:w_aac] It took evidence from governments, from the Jewish Agency, from Arab states and the Arab Office, from Christian and Jewish organizations, and from refugees in the camps. It was the most complete hearing of the competing cases before 1947. [cite:w_aac]

The range of witnesses mattered. The committee heard the leaders of the Jewish Agency. It heard Arab representatives, including the Arab Office, where the historian Albert Hourani gave notable testimony. And it heard Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, and the Ihud association, who argued that Arabs and Jews should share one state with political parity. [cite:w_aac] Their testimony is a documented record that a Jewish alternative to partition was put before an official body in person.

The committee reported unanimously in April 1946. It recommended the immediate issue of 100,000 immigration certificates for Jewish refugees, the repeal of the 1940 land-transfer regulations, and the continuation of the Mandate toward a United Nations trusteeship. It also stated that Palestine should be neither a Jewish state nor an Arab state, with neither people dominating the other. [cite:avalon_aac] The recommendations formed a package: relief for the refugees on one side, and on the other a constitutional settlement that denied either community a state of its own.

Washington did not take the package. President Truman publicly endorsed the figure of 100,000, which was popular with American voters, and said nothing about the recommendation against a Jewish state. Prime Minister Attlee made any admissions conditional on the disarmament of the Jewish and Arab armed groups. [cite:w_aac] Neither government adopted the report as a whole.

A follow-up attempt fared no better. In July 1946 an Anglo-American plan for provincial autonomy, known as the Morrison–Grady plan, was rejected by Zionist and Arab leaders alike, and Truman declined to back it. [cite:w_morrisongrady] The question went to the United Nations the following year. [cite:w_morrisongrady]

The episode does not support the claim that the Arab or binational case went unheard. Both sides, and a third option, were heard in detail and on the record. What it shows instead is selective use. The part of the report with domestic political value in the United States survived into American policy; the part that would have ruled out a Jewish state did not. Three months after the Morrison–Grady plan failed, Truman's Yom Kippur statement of October 1946 said the United States could support a viable Jewish state "in an adequate area of Palestine," as described in [scene:wh_s01|the White House section].
