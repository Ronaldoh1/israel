Genesis 12:3 Read Two Ways

One verse carries more of the weight of Christian Zionism than any other. In Genesis 12, God calls Abram, later Abraham, to leave his country for a land he will be shown, and promises: "And I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed" (King James Version). The New International Version has "I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse; and all peoples on earth will be blessed through you," and the English Standard Version renders the second clause "him who dishonors you I will curse." [cite:ts1_bg_gen123] The promise of land follows in the same narrative and is widened at Genesis 15:18 to run "from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates." [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] Bruce Fisk, a senior research fellow of the Network of Evangelicals for the Middle East, writing in the journal Bibliotheca Sacra in 2023, opened his study of the verse with the observation that "Genesis 12:3 often serves as warrant for standing with Israel and blessing the Jewish people." [cite:ts1_fisk_bsac]

The words are not in dispute. The dispute is over who the "thee" of the promise is today. One reading identifies it with Abraham's physical descendants, the Jewish people, and, since 1948, with the State of Israel. The other holds that the promise reached its fulfilment in Christ and passes, through him, to all who share Abraham's faith, so that it is not a standing instruction about any modern government. The opening volume set out the two readings in brief; this section names who holds each and what each commits them to. [scene:gx_s05|One verse, two readings]

The first reading: a promise to Abraham's descendants

The reading did not begin with the Scofield Reference Bible, but Scofield gave it a widely printed form. His note summarising the covenant with Abraham, in the 1917 edition, now in the public domain, takes the promise clause by clause. On "curse him that curseth thee" it says: "Wonderfully fulfilled in the history of the dispersion. It has invariably fared ill with the people who have persecuted the Jew—well with those who have protected him. The future will still more remarkably prove this principle." [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] The same note calls the covenant "wholly gracious and unconditional" and says that the gift of the land "is modified by prophecies of three dispossessions and restorations", two of them past, with the Jewish people in the third dispersion until a restoration at Christ's return. [cite:ts1_sac_gen12] [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] Three details are often lost when the note is quoted. It speaks of "the Jew", a people, because no Jewish state existed when it was written. It reads the verse's last clause, the blessing of all families of the earth, as "the great evangelic promise fulfilled in Abraham's Seed, Christ." And it reads the "great nation" clause as fulfilled three ways at once: in Abraham's natural descendants, in a spiritual offspring of all who have faith, and through Ishmael. [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] Scofield held the two readings together; what was distinctive was his claim that the blessing-and-curse clause still governed how nations fared. The note itself is examined in Part Five. [scene:ts5_notes|Part Five: the notes]

Fisk traces the same use of the verse further back, to Lord Shaftesbury, who argued that God blesses the nations that bless Israel and judges those that do not, and to William Blackstone, who petitioned President Benjamin Harrison in 1891. [cite:ts1_fisk_bsac] [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|Part Two: Shaftesbury] The step from "the Jew" to the state came after 1948. John Hagee, founding CUFI in 2006, stated it directly: "We believe in the promise of Genesis 12:3 regarding the Jewish people and the nation of Israel." [cite:ts1_jvl_cufi] In 2006 the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem, Bridges for Peace and Christian Friends of Israel jointly grounded their support in the Bible, including God's gift of the land of Canaan to the Jewish people. [cite:ts1_wiki_jerdecl]

The reading reached a national audience in June 2025. In an interview with the broadcaster Tucker Carlson posted on 18 June, Senator Ted Cruz of Texas said: "As a Christian, growing up in Sunday school, I was taught from the Bible that those who bless Israel will be blessed," and "I want to be on the blessing side of things." Carlson asked: "Those who bless the government of Israel?" Cruz replied that the text "doesn't say the government of, it says the nation of Israel." David Friedman, a former US ambassador to Israel, wrote afterwards that "This is the same nation of Israel referred to in the Bible." [cite:ts1_cp_cruz] The hinge of the dispute is whether the Israel of the promise and the state of 2025 are the same Israel; Friedman said they are. How officials in office used the verse is the subject of Part Twelve. [scene:ts12_officials|Part Twelve: officials]

No major poll asks about Genesis 12:3 itself; the closest measures are questions about the land. Pew's 2013 finding that 82 per cent of white evangelical Protestants say God gave the land to the Jewish people, and LifeWay's 2017 finding that 80 per cent of Americans with evangelical beliefs see 1948 as fulfilled prophecy, show a large constituency for the premise. [cite:ts1_pew_2013] [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] The same LifeWay survey shows how far the premise is from a policy: under a quarter backed a Palestinian state and nearly half were unsure. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] Even Hagee separates the two. He has said that "Israelis – and they alone – have the right to make existential decisions about land and peace." [cite:gi11_jpost_hagee]

The second reading: fulfilled in Christ and the church

The second reading takes its key from Paul. In his letter to the Galatians he argues that the promise was made to Abraham's "seed" in the singular: "He saith not, And to seeds, as of many; but as of one, And to thy seed, which is Christ." [cite:ts1_bg_gal316] Later in the same chapter (3:29) he tells his readers that if they belong to Christ they are Abraham's offspring and heirs according to the promise. Covenant theology, the system of the Reformed churches, builds on this. Mathison states its position as the affirmation "that there is one people of God", in which "Paul defines Abraham's seed in terms of Jesus Christ", so that Jewish and Gentile believers alike become Abraham's offspring through Christ. [cite:ts1_lig_dispens] On this reading the blessing of "all families of the earth" is the point of the verse, and it is not an instruction about any modern government.

The reading takes different forms in different churches. Modern Catholic teaching does not combine it with a claim that God has rejected the Jewish people: the Catechism states that the books of the Old Testament keep "a permanent value, for the Old Covenant has never been revoked." [cite:ts1_ccc_121] What the Catholic Church declines to do is read the modern state theologically. Its 1985 guidance, quoted in the previous section, says the state's political options are to be judged by "the common principles of international law." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985]

Several churches have gone further and rejected the first reading by name. The Jerusalem Declaration on Christian Zionism of 22 August 2006, signed by the Latin Patriarch Michel Sabbah, the Syriac Orthodox Archbishop Swerios Malki Mourad, the Anglican Bishop Riah Abu El-Assal and the Lutheran Bishop Munib Younan, called Christian Zionism a "false teaching that corrupts the biblical message of love, justice, and reconciliation." [cite:ts1_wiki_jerdecl] A Church of Scotland report of 2013, "The Inheritance of Abraham?", called on the church to "refute claims that scripture offers any peoples a privileged claim for possession of a particular territory." Jewish bodies objected strongly: the Scottish Council of Jewish Communities called it "an outrage to everything that interfaith dialogue stands for," and Rabbi David Rosen of the American Jewish Committee called it "rather pathetic"; a revised version was issued on 17 May 2013, ahead of the General Assembly's debate. [cite:ts1_scojec_cos2013] In 2024 the General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church (USA) adopted an overture "On Confessing our Complicity in Christian Zionism," which, in the words of a church official, called for "rejecting Christian Zionism in all its forms." [cite:ts1_pcusa_2026] Part Eleven sets out these and other statements, including Palestinian Christian ones, in full. [scene:ts11_palestinian|Part Eleven: Palestinian Christians]

The argument also runs inside evangelicalism. After Hagee's 2011 statement that Jerusalem should never be divided, Richard Mouw, president of Fuller Theological Seminary, argued that God's promise to bless Ishmael binds Christians too, and Joel Hunter of the National Association of Evangelicals' board said Hagee's view reflects "a limited portion of Christians who are millennial dispensationalists in their theology." [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] Fisk's 2023 article put the question directly to Christian Zionists, among four he posed: whether God promised to bless the modern state of Israel at all. [cite:ts1_fisk_bsac]

What the record settles

The record settles what the verse says, how Scofield annotated it, and which named churches, organisations and officials have taken which side, with dates. It shows that the first reading was in print long before the state existed and was applied to the state only after 1948, and that neither reading fixes a policy: a large share of those who hold the first are unsure about a Palestinian state, and churches that hold the second recognise Israel while declining to read it in scripture. [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] It does not settle the theology, which turns on prior commitments about how scripture is to be read; this volume does not adjudicate it. What it does test, from Part Two onward, is what happened when the first reading was turned into politics. [scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two: before Darby]
