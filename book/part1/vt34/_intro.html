Part Thirty-Three follows the inquisitions that grew up outside the medieval papal commissions of Part Twenty-Eight, and outside Europe altogether. The first section sets out the three early modern tribunals: the Spanish Inquisition of 1478, a court licensed by the pope but staffed and steered by the crown, which pursued converted Jews and Muslims, Protestants, mystics and books until 1834; the Portuguese Inquisition of 1536; and the Roman Inquisition of 1542, the one that survives today as the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, with every death toll given as a range with the historian who made it. The second goes to Goa, where the Portuguese crown destroyed Hindu temples, banned Hindu rites and the Konkani language, and a tribunal sat from 1560 to 1812, and where an archbishop's synod in 1599 condemned the Syriac books of India's oldest Christians. The third crosses to the Americas, separating the Holy Office's tribunals, which the crown kept away from Indigenous people, from the friars and bishops who burned Maya codices at Maní in 1562 and ran Peru's campaigns against "idolatry", and setting beside them the books Mexica rulers burned before the conquest and the knowledge friars and Indigenous scholars preserved.
