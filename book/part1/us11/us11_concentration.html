Concentration and Trade

Both countries have argued over whether a few owners hold too much of their economies, and they answered the question in different ways. Israel passed a law in 2013 that took apart the pyramid structures through which a handful of business groups controlled banks, insurers, retailers and phone companies. The United States has no comparable statute; its debate runs through antitrust cases and through a live dispute among economists over whether American industries have become more concentrated at all. This section sets out both records, then the trade between the two countries, which began under America's first free trade agreement and was shaken by the tariffs of 2025–26.

Israel's business groups

The case for Israel's law rested on a Bank of Israel study of 2010. It found that 30 per cent of businesses in Israel belonged to pyramid structures, in which a family or individual controls a holding company that controls listed companies, which in turn control others, so that a small stake at the top commands a large share of the economy below. Ten business groups owned banks and holding companies that controlled 41 per cent of the market value of all public companies, and the study called Israel's the most concentrated economy in the Western world. [cite:us11_toi_concentration] Among the groups named in reporting at the time were Nochi Dankner's IDB Holdings, whose holdings included the mobile operator Cellcom and Supersol, the largest supermarket chain; Yitzhak Tshuva's Delek Group, which owned the Phoenix insurance company; and Shari Arison's large interest in Bank Hapoalim. [cite:us11_toi_concentration]

The government set up a committee on concentration and competitiveness on 24 October 2010. It published an interim report on 11 October 2011 and final recommendations in February 2012; it was separate from the Trajtenberg Committee, which reported on 26 September 2011 and which Part Fourteen covers. [cite:us11_wiki_concentration] [scene:us14_protests|Part Fourteen: the 2011 protests] The groups opposed it. In a filing of March 2011, IDB argued that there was no link between concentration and competitiveness and that Israel's concentration was average and declining. [cite:us11_wiki_concentration]

The 2013 law

The Knesset passed the Law for the Promotion of Competition and Reduction of Concentration in December 2013, by 72 votes to none with one abstention; 42 coalition and 30 opposition members voted for it. [cite:us11_toi_concentration] [cite:us11_wiki_concentration] It works in three ways. New pyramids may have no more than two layers of listed companies; existing ones had to come down to three layers within four years and to two within six. A controlling owner of a major non-financial company, one with turnover or credit above NIS 6 billion, may not also control a major financial company, one with assets above NIS 40 billion. And the state must weigh concentration when it hands out licences and rights. [cite:us11_wiki_concentration] The Finance Ministry's list of large non-financial entities covered by the law ran to 1,786 entities, 232 of them linked to IDB. [cite:us11_wiki_concentration] The chairman of the Knesset Finance Committee, Nissim Smoliansky, said: "From now on, we will see pyramids only when we go to Egypt." [cite:us11_toi_concentration]

The divestments that followed show the law biting. Delek Group sold 9.8 per cent of Phoenix in two deals in August and September 2018, for NIS 509 million, citing the concentration law and a shift to energy, and in 2019 sold its remaining controlling stake of 32.5 per cent to the private-equity firms Centerbridge Partners and Gallatin Point Capital for NIS 1.57 billion. [cite:us11_globes_delek2018] [cite:us11_lii_delek2019] IDB had made similar swap deals involving Discount Investment Corporation and Clal Insurance. [cite:us11_globes_delek2018] The law addressed control structures across groups rather than market shares within industries. Banking was handled separately: a 2016 committee recommended that Hapoalim and Leumi sell their credit-card companies. [cite:us11_wiki_concentration] Whether it lowered prices is a different question: the OECD in 2025 still ranked Israel's price level fourth-highest among its members and recommended lowering trade barriers to bring it down, which Part Fourteen examines. [cite:us14_oecd_survey2025] [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the basket]

Corporate power in the United States

The American debate is about measurement as much as remedy. In a widely cited 2019 study, the finance economists Gustavo Grullon, Yelena Larkin and Roni Michaely found that "Since the late 1990s, over 75% of US industries have experienced an increase in concentration levels", and that firms in the industries where concentration rose most earned higher profit margins and made more profitable acquisitions. [cite:us11_gl_concentration] Others dispute the scale. A 2020 report by Joe Kennedy for the Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, a technology-policy think tank, found that in Census data for 2002–2012 the top four firms' share fell or held steady in 319 of 792 detailed industries, cited studies finding concentration in local markets steady or falling, and argued that the 2019 study's result depended on its 1997 starting point: measured from 1972, the median rise in concentration was about 25 per cent rather than over 70. [cite:us11_itif_kennedy] Both sides agree that national concentration measures are an imperfect guide to market power; they disagree on how much has changed.

The United States has handled the question case by case under its antitrust laws rather than by a structural statute like Israel's. The most important recent case concerns Google. Federal Judge Amit Mehta held that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in general internet search, through multi-billion-dollar payments to Apple, Samsung, Mozilla and others to make its search engine the default. In his remedies ruling of September 2025 he did not ban those payments, allowing them to continue with minimal restrictions, citing the harm a ban could do to the partners who received them and to consumers. [cite:us11_hks_google] Eugene Kimmelman, writing for Harvard's Kennedy School in June 2026, argued that this left the market "exceptionally resistant to change". [cite:us11_hks_google]

Trade between the two

The US–Israel Free Trade Agreement, America's first, entered into force on 1 September 1985, two months after Israel's stabilisation plan. [cite:us11_ustr_israel] [scene:us11_turn|The turn] In 2025 goods trade between the two countries was worth $34.4 billion: American exports of $13.7 billion and imports from Israel of $20.7 billion, leaving the United States with a goods deficit of $7.0 billion. Services trade added $19.7 billion, roughly in balance, for a total of $54.2 billion. Both goods exports and imports fell by about 7 per cent from 2024. [cite:us11_ustr_israel]

The tariffs of 2025 applied to Israel despite the agreement. On 2 April 2025 the Trump administration set a 17 per cent "reciprocal" tariff on Israeli goods; on 1 August it set a 15 per cent rate, when Israel had expected 10. Prime Minister Netanyahu had promised to cancel all of Israel's remaining tariffs on American imports, but the promise was not fully kept after pressure from Israeli farmers. [cite:us11_globes_tariff15] On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorise the president to impose tariffs, voiding the reciprocal tariffs. The administration announced a 10 per cent global tariff the same day under Section 122 of the Trade Act, which caps such surcharges at 15 per cent for 150 days, and ordered new investigations under Section 301. [cite:us11_ks_ieepa] This book did not establish what tariff applied to Israeli goods after that 150-day window; as of October 2026 the status should be checked against the latest proclamations.
