ICE, the Sole-Source Contracts and the Data They Run On

Palantir's longest civilian relationship is with Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and it is the clearest case of how the company's platforms meet the data of the American state. It has three parts: the contracts, which are public in outline; the data the platforms draw on, which is partly public; and the law that lets an agency acquire much of that data without a judge, which is the part least understood.

The contracts. In 2014 ICE began using an Investigative Case Management system built by Palantir for its criminal-investigations arm, Homeland Security Investigations; Mother Jones reported in 2019 that the first contract was worth $41 million. [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] A second Palantir tool, FALCON, a version of its Gotham software customised for HSI, lets agents pull data from other DHS offices, the FBI and other sources. The Intercept, reviewing ICE documents in March 2017, reported that the system "permits ICE agents to access information from the Central Intelligence Agency," and quoted a 2013 funding document saying Palantir "enables ICE/HSI to secure information sharing with other law enforcement agencies in real-time." [cite:ow9_int_falcon] The 2019 renewal of the case-management contract was worth about $49 million over three years, a figure revealed only because ICE's redactions could be undone by copying the text. [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] In September 2022 ICE awarded Palantir a five-year contract reported at $95.9 million for installing and sustaining the system. [cite:ow9_eb_icm2022] [cite:ow9_vv_icm2022]

In April 2025, under the second Trump administration, ICE moved to pay Palantir $29.9 million to build "ImmigrationOS," a platform meant to select and prioritise people for arrest, to track people who leave the country on their own, and to follow each case from identification to removal. ICE's justification letter said "Palantir is the only source that can provide the required capabilities." [cite:ow9_reg_immos] By June 2025 ICE was preparing a further limited-sources justification for replacing the case-management system itself, again concluding that only Palantir could meet its needs, with a deadline of September 2026. [cite:ow9_bu_immos] In January 2026, 404 Media reported a further Palantir tool used by ICE, ELITE, which compiles dossiers and "leads" on people ICE believes may be deportable and gives each person's current address a "confidence score." Fortune, reporting on it, noted that ICE and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services had signed a data-sharing agreement covering nearly 80 million Medicaid patients; neither Palantir nor DHS answered questions about how ELITE was used. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite] In February 2026 DHS signed its five-year blanket purchase agreement with Palantir, worth up to $1 billion, under which CBP, ICE, FEMA and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency can all buy its platforms without separate competitions. [cite:ow9_sa_dhsbpa]

Totals for Palantir's ICE business vary widely in circulation, from under $250 million to more than $400 million, because they count different things: obligations or ceilings, ICE alone or all of DHS, and different end dates. This section gives the awards one by one with their dates rather than adding them into a single figure.

The data. Palantir's software holds nothing until an agency puts data into it. Some of that data is the agency's own: case files, border-crossing records, fingerprints. Some comes from other agencies under sharing agreements, as the Medicaid agreement shows. A growing share is bought. Documents the American Civil Liberties Union obtained through a lawsuit, published in January 2026, show that CBP paid Venntel more than $2 million in 2019 and 2020 for location data drawn from phone apps, that CBP and the Secret Service bought from Babel Street, and that ICE was returning to such purchases in 2026 through a contract with Penlink. [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] Other agencies do the same: in January 2024 the NSA's director, General Paul Nakasone, confirmed to Senator Ron Wyden that the agency buys commercially available "netflow" records, data on the flow of internet traffic, including wholly domestic traffic. [cite:ow9_tc_nsanetflow]

The law. Agencies can buy what they could not seize because of the third-party doctrine. In United States v. Miller (1976) the Supreme Court held that a customer had no Fourth Amendment protection for bank records held by the bank; in Smith v. Maryland (1979) it held that recording the numbers dialled from a home phone was not a search, because the caller had "voluntarily conveyed" them to the phone company. [cite:ow9_cornell_tpd] In Carpenter v. United States (2018) the Court refused to extend that rule to historical cell-site location records, which require a warrant, but it did not overturn Miller or Smith. [cite:ow9_cornell_tpd] On 29 June 2026, in Chatrie v. United States, a 6–3 majority held that obtaining even two hours of a person's location history from Google was a search. [cite:ow9_pw_chatrie] Both decisions concern data the government compels a company to hand over. The analyses checked for this section do not say that either reaches data the government buys on the open market, and an ICE draft legal memorandum obtained by the ACLU argues that location data tied to advertising identifiers, rather than names, falls outside Carpenter; the ACLU calls this "a distinction without a difference." [cite:ow9_pw_chatrie] [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] Congress has considered closing the gap. The Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act, which would require a court order before law-enforcement and intelligence agencies buy Americans' data, passed the House on 17 April 2024 by 219 to 199, over the opposition of the Biden White House, a senior official of which called it "unworkable"; it did not become law in that Congress. [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] [cite:ow9_cs_4thact]

None of this chain runs through Israel. The point is worth stating because Palantir's ICE work and its Israeli work are often described together, as if one fed the other. The sources used here document no such link: ICE buys the platforms and owns the data in them, and the Israeli Ministry of Defense is a separate customer with separate data. [cite:ow9_calcalist_partner] [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] The Israeli phone-hacking and spyware tools that ICE has bought directly from Israeli-founded companies are a different matter, treated in [scene:ow6_nypd_ice|Part Six] and in Part Ten.

The governments' stated purposes are on the record. ICE describes these systems as tools for investigating crime and for enforcing immigration law efficiently, and its April 2025 justification framed speed as the reason for skipping competition. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] DHS, which announced in 2024 that it was ending bulk location-data contracts, has not explained its return to them. [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] Palantir's position is that it does not surveil anyone and that its customers work only with data they may lawfully hold. [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] The test of that position is the law on lawful holding, and on purchased data that law, as of October 2026, remains largely unwritten.
