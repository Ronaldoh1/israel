The Remark: "Belonged to Intelligence"

More than any document, one sentence has carried the intelligence theory of this case. In July 2019, days after Epstein's second arrest, the journalist Vicky Ward reported in the Daily Beast, citing an unnamed source, that Alexander Acosta had told the Trump transition team interviewing him for Secretary of Labor: "I was told Epstein 'belonged to intelligence' and to leave it alone." [cite:ep7_tpm_ward2019] It explained, in five words, the most anomalous fact in the case: a federal prosecutor giving a serial abuser of children a state plea, thirteen months of work release and immunity for his helpers.

The sentence has three weaknesses as evidence, and they need to be stated as plainly as its appeal. It came from an anonymous source, not from Acosta in public, so it reached print at best second-hand. It named no agency and no person who supposedly told Acosta. And if Acosta did say it, he said it while being vetted for a cabinet post, when he had every reason to explain away the deal that would end his career in July 2019. [cite:ep7_tpm_ward2019]

What Acosta said when asked

On 19 September 2025 Acosta gave a transcribed interview to the House Oversight Committee, released a month later. Asked whether he had ever said Epstein "belonged to intelligence", he answered, "I have not," and added, "I never made that assertion." He said no one from the CIA, the NSA, the State Department or the FBI's intelligence side had contacted him about Epstein; asked whether anyone had told him Epstein could not be prosecuted because he was an asset, he said, "No one approached me and said that." If there had been any secure information of that kind, he said, procedures "would have been triggered that were never triggered." He said he had denied the remark consistently, including to the Justice Department's own Office of Professional Responsibility. [cite:ep7_aol_acosta2025] The reports of the interview do not say whether he was under oath; a false statement to Congress is a crime either way.

The Office of Professional Responsibility had examined the deal in 2020. It found that Acosta exercised "poor judgment" but did not commit professional misconduct, and it did not report finding any instruction from an intelligence agency. [cite:abcnews_opr_report]

Where that leaves the remark

The record now holds an anonymous second-hand account of a statement and a later denial by the man said to have made it. Neither proves the other false. What the record does not hold is the thing that would settle it: a document, a witness who heard the words directly and will say so on the record, or a name for whoever supposedly gave the instruction. Until one of those appears, the remark is a lead, not a finding, and the book does not build on it.

What the remark does point to is real. The 2008 agreement was extraordinary, and its full deliberations, including who above the US Attorney's office was consulted, have never been made public. That gap is on the map as a withheld record, and Part Eight examines the agreement itself. An intelligence instruction is one possible explanation for the deal. A powerful defence team, a weak prosecutorial will and an office that wanted the case closed are others, and they are the ones the released documents describe.
