For sixteen centuries the people now called Gnostics were known almost entirely through the books written to destroy them. Bishops and theologians quoted them in order to refute them, described their rites in order to mock them and named their schools after their founders in order to show that they followed men rather than Christ. Their own writings had disappeared from use. Then, in December 1945, a jar was broken open at the foot of a cliff in Upper Egypt, and a library of their books came back. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

This book tells two stories. The first is what the Gnostics taught: that salvation comes through knowing rather than believing, that the world is governed by lesser powers who are not the true God, and that the divine is found within. Their books are read here in their own words, set beside what their enemies said of them. The second story is about ownership. In the second century the question was who could teach this knowledge, bishops or independent teachers. In the twentieth it was who could own the books that held it: farmers, dealers, a psychologist's institute in Zurich, the Egyptian state, the scholars who controlled access for three decades, a foundation and a television network. In both centuries the knowledge was described by its own texts as something that could not be sold, and in both it passed through hands that were paid for it.
