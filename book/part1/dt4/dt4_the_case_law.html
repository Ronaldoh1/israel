The Case Law: How Spending Became Speech

The rules on money in American federal elections were not mainly written by voters or by Congress. Congress passed campaign-finance laws in 1971, 1974 and 2002, and the Supreme Court and the federal appeals court in Washington then decided which parts of them the Constitution allows. Five decisions between 1976 and 2014 set the present system, and a sixth, in June 2026, extended it to the parties. Read together, they draw one line through the whole subject: money given to a candidate may be limited, because it can buy favours, while money spent independently to speak about a candidate may not, because spending is a form of speech. This section sets out each decision, its vote, and what it changed, then follows a dollar through the system the decisions built.

Buckley v. Valeo, 1976: the line is drawn

In 1974 Congress amended the Federal Election Campaign Act to limit both what donors could give and what candidates and others could spend; President Ford signed the bill on 15 October 1974. On 30 January 1976, in Buckley v. Valeo, the Court split the law in two. It upheld limits on contributions to candidates, parties and political committees, an annual cap on each person's total giving, disclosure of donors, and voluntary public financing tied to spending limits. It struck down limits on what candidates could spend, on what they could spend from their own pockets, and on independent spending by individuals and groups. A limit on spending, the Court reasoned, "necessarily reduces the quantity of expression". Preventing corruption or its appearance justified capping large gifts to candidates; spending made without coordinating with a candidate, the Court found, did not pose the same danger. [cite:dt4_wiki_buckley] Every later case works inside that distinction between a contribution, which can be capped, and an independent expenditure, which cannot.

Citizens United, 2010: corporations and unions join in

The 2002 Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act barred corporations and unions from paying from their general funds for broadcast ads naming a federal candidate close to an election. In 2008 Citizens United, a conservative nonprofit, wanted to promote and air Hillary: The Movie, a documentary critical of Hillary Clinton, during the Democratic primaries, and sued for fear the law forbade it. [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] On 21 January 2010 the Court ruled in its favour, and the vote matters. By five to four, with Justice Kennedy writing for himself, Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Scalia, Alito and Thomas, the Court struck down the ban on independent spending by corporations and unions, and overruled its own 1990 decision in Austin and part of its 2003 decision in McConnell. By eight to one, it upheld the law's disclosure and disclaimer rules; only Justice Thomas would have struck them down. [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] [cite:ad15_lii_cu]

The majority's reasoning carried Buckley forward: independent expenditures, it held, "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption", and only an exchange of favours for money, what lawyers call quid pro quo corruption, could justify a limit. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] Justice Stevens, writing for the four dissenters, answered: "A democracy cannot function effectively when its constituent members believe laws are being bought and sold." [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] Both readings remain live, and the record gives each something. The ruling freed unions and advocacy groups of every kind as well as businesses, and the speech at issue was a film about a candidate. It also removed the last federal limit on independent political spending from corporate and union treasuries, and the money described in the next section followed.

SpeechNow, 2010: the super PAC

Two months later, on 26 March 2010, the federal appeals court for the District of Columbia, sitting with all its judges and ruling unanimously, decided SpeechNow.org v. FEC. If spending by an independent group cannot corrupt, the court reasoned, then contributions to a group that only spends independently cannot be capped either. It upheld the group's duties to register and report its donors. [cite:dt4_volokh_speechnow] The Federal Election Commission applied the ruling in advisory opinions that July. The result is the super PAC: a political committee that may raise unlimited sums from individuals, corporations and unions, but may not give money directly to candidates or parties or coordinate its spending with them. Super PACs report their donors, but on schedules that can delay disclosure until after an election. [cite:pm_wiki_superpac] Citizens United alone did not create super PACs; the appeals court's ruling, built on it, did.

Bennett, 2011, and McCutcheon, 2014: the edges

Two more five-to-four decisions set the edges. On 27 June 2011, in Arizona Free Enterprise Club v. Bennett, Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the Court that Arizona's system of public financing could not give a participating candidate extra public money, up to three times the base grant, whenever an opponent or an independent group outspent them; that trigger, the Court held, "substantially burdens" speech. Justice Kagan dissented for four justices. [cite:dt4_wiki_bennett] The ruling reached only the trigger, not public grants as such, but it took away the tool Arizona had used to keep publicly funded candidates level with heavy outside spending. On 2 April 2014, in McCutcheon v. FEC, a plurality led by the Chief Justice struck down the overall two-year cap on what one person could give to all candidates and parties combined, which had stood at $117,000 for 2011–12. The cap on gifts to each candidate stayed. Justice Thomas supplied the fifth vote and would have struck down all contribution limits; Justice Breyer dissented for four. [cite:dt4_wiki_mccutcheon]

NRSC v. FEC, 2026: the parties

The most recent decision moved the line again, this time for the parties. Federal law capped how much a national party committee could spend in coordination with its own nominee, $65,300 for most House candidates in the current cycle, and the Court had upheld those caps in 2001 in FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee. On 30 June 2026, in National Republican Senatorial Committee v. FEC, the Court struck them down by six votes to three and overruled the 2001 decision. Justice Kavanaugh wrote for the majority, which held that such limits burden "the ability of the party to do what it exists to do"; Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson. [cite:dt7_skadden_nrsc] [cite:dt7_pac_nrsc] The ruling reaches parties alone. The caps on what an individual may give a candidate ($3,500 per election) or a national party, the ban on corporate gifts to national parties, the coordination rules for super PACs and nonprofits, and the disclosure rules all remain. [cite:dt7_skadden_nrsc] The case was brought by the Republican Senate committee; the ruling applies to the committees of both parties, which may now spend without limit in step with their candidates, using money raised under those caps.

Following a dollar

The decisions leave two kinds of money side by side. A donor may give a candidate's own campaign no more than $3,500 per election in the 2025–26 cycle. [cite:pm_fec_limits] The same donor, or a corporation, may give any amount to a super PAC that spends on that candidate's behalf. [cite:pm_wiki_superpac] A third path runs through nonprofit groups that are not required to disclose their donors publicly. When such a group gives to a super PAC, the super PAC reports the nonprofit as its donor, and the people behind the nonprofit stay unnamed. Money that reaches elections this way is called dark money. In 2024, OpenSecrets found, super PACs reported more than $1 billion from such groups and from shell companies. [cite:dt4_os_2024record]

What follows for reform

Buckley, Citizens United, Bennett and NRSC are rulings about what the First Amendment requires. Congress cannot reverse them by ordinary law. It can strengthen disclosure, which the Court upheld eight to one in 2010, and it can fund candidates through grants. [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] [cite:dt4_wiki_bennett] Anything more needs either a constitutional amendment or a later Court that overrules these cases. The attempts at both, and the state measures now testing the edges, are set out in [scene:dt5_government_pressure_and_the_way_back|Part Five] and [scene:dt7_money_fixes|Part Seven]. The design fact is plain. The present system of unlimited independent spending was built by the courts through constitutional interpretation, and only the courts or an amendment can take it apart.
