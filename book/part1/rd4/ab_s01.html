Aliyah Bet: The Clandestine Fleet

Postwar Jewish immigration to Palestine is often pictured as a flight of desperate refugees acting on their own. The passengers were desperate, and many of them were refugees in the strictest sense. But the ships that carried them were not spontaneous. From 1938 to 1948 they were organized, financed, crewed and routed by a network built for the purpose, run from within the institutions of the Jewish community in Palestine. Both things are true, and the history of Aliyah Bet only makes sense when they are held together.

Aliyah Bet means "immigration B." It was the name for the clandestine immigration of Jews to Palestine against the limits Britain set on legal entry. In 1938, the year of Évian, the Jewish leadership created the Mossad LeAliyah Bet as a branch of the Haganah to coordinate it. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:wiki_aliyah_bet] It did not hold a monopoly. Revisionist groups ran their own voyages separately. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] It was founded in the same year that 32 countries met at [scene:ev_s01|Évian] and almost all declined to take more Jewish refugees, while Britain kept Palestine off the agenda.

How the operation worked can be read from its parts. The Mossad LeAliyah Bet was an arm of the Haganah, the defense organization of the Jewish community, not an independent charity. It organized ships from Europe's ports, against a British limit on entry that the Royal Navy enforced at sea. The record gathered here does not break the voyages down year by year, and it does not detail the wartime sailings beyond their outcome. Two of the wartime ships, the Patria in 1940 and the Struma in 1942, ended in mass death and are told in [scene:s07b2|the ships the White Paper turned away].

After the war the operation changed in scale. With Britain's quota at about 18,000 a year, the clandestine route became the main one. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] The Brihah ("flight") network, run with Mossad emissaries, partisans and youth movements, moved nearly 200,000 survivors out of displaced-persons camps in Germany, Austria and Italy to ports in Italy, France, Yugoslavia and Greece. Zionist youth movements inside the camps supplied the passengers. [cite:hsb_aliyah_bet_exodus] [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps] Ships, including a fleet bought and crewed largely by American volunteers, made about 142 voyages in all. More than 90 percent of them were intercepted by the Royal Navy. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:wiki_aliyah_bet]

Interception did not end the journey, and Britain needed somewhere to put the people it stopped. From August 1946 it interned intercepted immigrants in camps on Cyprus. By 1948 it held about 52,000 people there. The last camp closed in February 1949. [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps] [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] Cyprus was meant to deter. In July 1947 the Exodus 1947, carrying about 4,500 people, was boarded in international waters, and its passengers were sent back not to Cyprus but to camps in Germany. The US Holocaust Memorial Museum reads that decision as an admission that the Cyprus deterrent had failed. [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps] [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] What the Exodus did to international opinion, and whether it moved the United Nations vote, belongs to the next stage and is set out, with both sides of the argument, in the account of the ships linked above.

By 1948 well over 100,000 people had come to Palestine this way, including more than 70,000 Holocaust survivors. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:jvl_aliyah_bet] Set against the whole of Jewish immigration under the Mandate, that is a large share. Britain's own records count 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946. A separate estimate puts total Mandate-era immigration, legal and illegal together, at about 480,000. [cite:jvl_aliyah_bet] The two figures measure different things. The British count stops in 1946 and records the entries Britain registered. The larger estimate runs to the end of the Mandate and includes people who entered without Britain's leave. The gap between them is consistent with a clandestine movement of the size given above, but the record here does not reconcile the figures line by line, and the reader should not treat the difference as an exact count of illegal entries.

The two numbers on those panels also show how the population was changing. In Britain's 1922 census, 757,182 people were counted in Palestine. By UNSCOP's estimate in late 1946, the population was about 1.85 million. Immigration, legal and clandestine, was one of the forces behind that growth.

The question of what Aliyah Bet was has two answers, and both have evidence behind them. One is the passengers' story. They were survivors with nowhere else to go after the doors closed at Évian. The other is the organizers' story. The movement that carried them was an organized state-in-waiting, with its own intelligence service, logistics, fleet and recruitment, and every intercepted ship became a public argument against the Mandate. An account that keeps only the first misses the organization. An account that keeps only the second misses the people. The record supports both, and neither cancels the other. The youth movements that supplied the passengers in the camps and the Haganah branch that bought the ships were part of the same operation as the people on board.

Some questions remain open on this record. It does not say how the fleet was paid for, beyond noting that American volunteers bought and crewed much of it. It does not break down how many voyages or passengers fall before 1945 and how many after, or how many of the people who took the route were stopped and how many reached Palestine directly. It does not give the Revisionist voyages separately. Those are the next questions for research. What is established is the structure: a branch of the Haganah from 1938, a network from the camps to the ports after 1945, a British blockade that stopped most of the ships, and well over 100,000 people who came anyway.
