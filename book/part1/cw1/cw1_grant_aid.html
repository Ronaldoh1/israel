The First Grant: How Washington Came to Give Israel Money, 1951–1952

By the spring of 1951 the United States had extended Israel credit; the sources cited here record no outright grant before then. How the new state met its first bills, through American government credit, bonds sold abroad and the opening of the German reparations claim, is told in Volume II, in [scene:rd6_financing|how the new state was paid for]. This section takes up the next step: the fifteen months, from March 1951 to June 1952, in which Israel asked for a grant, the Administration offered far less, Congress raised the figure, and the money arrived in a form that was economic, regional and argued over at every stage. The familiar picture of American support for Israel holds that Washington gave massive, unconditional military backing as an unbroken policy from 1948. The documents of 1951 and 1952 show something narrower and more contested. The first grant was for the relief of immigrants, not for arms. The State Department resisted its size. It was set beside a matching programme for Arab refugees and the Arab states. And the case that carried it through Congress was made partly in the language of the Cold War, by senators from both parties.

The request was made in March 1951. Abba Eban, Israel's ambassador, and David Horowitz of Israel's Finance Ministry handed Acheson a note asking for a grant-in-aid of $150 million for the period from 1 July 1951 to 1 June 1952. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_eban_request] The note gave three grounds. The first was population: 510,000 people had arrived since statehood, and 600,000 more were expected over the next three years. The second was the cost of defence. The third was Israel's commitments to the reintegration fund of UNRWA, the new UN agency for Palestine refugees. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_eban_request] In the same conversation the two men asked for American support for a separate claim of $1.5 billion in reparations from Germany, the claim whose negotiation and divisive passage through the Knesset Volume II follows. Acheson's answer on another matter was a refusal: an official invitation for Ben-Gurion to visit, he said, was impossible that year. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_eban_request]

The request was new in kind. Israel's earlier American money had come as credit; a memorandum written that summer inside the State Department lists $135 million in earlier Export-Import Bank credit to Israel. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] A grant would not be repaid, and it would have to be voted by Congress. The editors of the State Department's documentary series record that the request for a fiscal year 1952 grant of $150 million "had generated a great deal of discussion within the United States Government." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mcghee_note] The department's answer was to turn a request from one state into a programme for a region. On 17 April 1951 McGhee wrote a memorandum proposing a programme of economic aid to the Near East as a whole. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mcghee_note] The record cited here gives the memorandum's date and its proposal, not its full reasoning.

Congress had already moved. On about 3 April 1951, according to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency's report at the time, Senators Paul Douglas, a Democrat from Illinois, and Robert Taft, a Republican from Ohio, introduced a bill for $150 million in aid to Israel, the full sum of the Israeli note, and the House Majority Leader, John McCormack, introduced a companion bill. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_douglas_taft_1951] The senators described Israel as "a bulwark in that area of world democracy" against Communist threats, "particularly in Iran", and McCormack called it "a dependable ally of the United States." [cite:rs_cw1_jta_douglas_taft_1951] The phrase about Iran was written in the weeks after Iran's parliament voted to nationalize its oil, the crisis told in this chapter's section on Iran. The Israeli note had made its case on immigrants, defence and refugees. The sponsors in Congress made theirs, at least in part, on the Soviet threat to the region. The two cases sat side by side from the start.

The Administration's own figure came on 24 May 1951. Its recommendation to Congress was $23.5 million in economic aid for Israel, the same $23.5 million for the Arab states, $50 million for the Arab refugee programme, and authority for $41.5 million in direct military grants. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] The summary of the memorandum in this book's source does not say which states the military authority was meant for, and nothing in it assigns any of that sum to Israel. The shape of the proposal was parity: Israel and its Arab neighbours were offered equal sums, and the larger single line went to the refugees.

American advocates pressed for more, and the State Department's memoranda record how it answered them. McGhee declined a request from Jacob Blaustein for $75 million each. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] In July 1951 McGhee wrote to the Secretary of State about a visit from Abraham Feinberg, the memorandum's title noting that Feinberg came "at the Suggestion of the President". McGhee proposed telling Feinberg that the $50 million for Arab refugees could be seen as indirect aid to Israel. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] The memorandum, as summarized here, does not say why money for the refugees should count as help to Israel, and this book does not supply a reason. What the exchange does show is the structure of the argument. An advocate who came at the President's suggestion carried the case into the department, and the department defended its lower figure by pointing to money appropriated for someone else.

The mechanism of these months explains who could act. The Israeli government could ask, and did, through its ambassador. The Secretary of State and his officials shaped the Administration's recommendation, which set the figure Congress started from. Congress could raise or cut that figure, first by authorizing a sum and then by appropriating it. Between those points American advocates worked: Blaustein and Feinberg inside the department, and in the Senate the lobbyist I. L. Kenen, whose work and organization are told in [scene:cw1_lobby|the lobby in its Cold War frame].

The decisive moment came on the Senate floor in August 1951, and the best account of it is Kenen's own. In a letter from Washington to Abba Hillel Silver he described a Douglas amendment, backed by Taft, that raised the figure for Near East economic aid and, with it, Israel's total, to about $68.8 million. The scan of the letter is partly illegible, so that figure is approximate. The amendment passed on a close standing vote. [cite:rs_cw1_kenen_silver_letter] Kenen measured the gain as a share of the whole: about 1% of the Administration's bill had gone to Israel, against about 5.2% of the Senate's. And he warned his correspondent not to celebrate too soon: "all this is still an authorization." [cite:rs_cw1_kenen_silver_letter] An authorization permitted spending; it did not yet provide the money.

Truman signed the Mutual Security Act of 1951 on 10 October 1951. It provided $50 million for the UN refugee programme in fiscal 1952. [cite:rs_cw1_truman_unrpr_letter] How much it gave Israel is a point on which the sources differ, and both versions should be stated. Kenen's obituary in the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, published in 1988, says that the 1951 campaign sought $150 million and that, despite State Department opposition, Kenen won approval for $65 million. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit] Kenen's own letter of 1951 gives about $68.8 million, at the Senate authorization stage, and stresses that this was not yet money in hand. [cite:rs_cw1_kenen_silver_letter] The two figures need not contradict each other. One describes an authorization in August 1951; the other is a retrospective total written decades later, which may describe the sum finally approved after the Senate and House versions were reconciled. Neither source, as cited here, is the text of the appropriation itself, and the year-by-year official table summarized in Volume II counts American aid by a different method. This book gives both figures and does not choose between them.

A third account describes the design differently again. According to Israel Hayom's report of research by Kobby Barda, the refugee-aid plan provided $50 million each for Israel and the Arab states for the absorption of refugees, a further $53 million for the Arab states in technical cooperation, and $50 million for the Arab refugees, channelled through UNRWA. [cite:rs_cw1_israelhayom_barda] Those sums do not match the Administration's recommendation of May 1951, and the report, as cited here, does not say at which stage of the process they applied. The $50 million for the refugees through the UN agency agrees with the Mutual Security Act as Truman described it; the other figures are Barda's account and are not confirmed by the other sources.

The money for Arab refugees ran alongside Israel's throughout, and it was larger at the start. Truman's letter of 27 July 1951 to the chairmen of the congressional committees records an American contribution of $25,450,000 to the UN Palestine refugee programme in fiscal 1951, and a request for $50,000,000 in the Mutual Security Program for the following year. [cite:rs_cw1_truman_unrpr_letter] In the Administration's proposal of May 1951, the refugee line was more than twice Israel's. The refugees' own history in these years, the UN agency's first operations and the conference in Paris, is told in this chapter's section on the refugees.

The second round, for fiscal 1953, went differently, because this time the Administration began high. In March 1952 it asked Congress for $76 million for the relief and resettlement of Jewish immigrants in Israel and $65 million for the UN to resettle Arab refugees in Arab lands: together $141 million of a Near East request of $196 million. Acheson and W. Averell Harriman testified in support, according to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_76m_1952] The Administration's own figure for Israel was now more than three times the $23.5 million it had proposed ten months earlier. The language had changed too. The money for Israel was described as aid for immigrants, set beside the programme for Arab refugees.

Congress trimmed the Israeli figure only slightly. The House voted $76 million and the Senate $64,456,000. In June 1952 a conference of the two chambers split the difference, setting Israel's figure at $70,228,000 for the relief and rehabilitation of Jewish refugees, plus $2,772,000 in technical assistance: $73,000,000 in all. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_conference_1952] [cite:rs_cw1_jta_house_1952] The comparison the Jewish Telegraphic Agency drew at the time shows where the political weight now lay. Congress cut the Administration's overall aid by about 20%, but Israel's figure by less than 8%. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_conference_1952] [cite:rs_cw1_jta_house_1952] In 1951 the department had held the line and Congress had pushed past it. In 1952 the department proposed the larger figure itself, and Congress, cutting overall aid by about a fifth, left this one nearly whole.

None of this money was military, and that remained true for another decade. From Israel's founding through the early 1960s, American assistance to Israel was economic aid and loans only. According to one study of the aid relationship, Washington avoided direct arms sales because it did not want to alienate the Arab oil-producing states, and the first American military aid, itself in the form of loans rather than grants, came in 1962. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] The first step of the dashboard below sets out that period: economic aid and loans only, from 1948 to 1961, with arms withheld for fear of alienating Arab oil states. The grants of 1951 and 1952 belong entirely inside that step. The later steps, from the first military loan to the earmarks, the airlift of 1973 and the conversion of aid to grants, are told in [scene:iaa_s01|how the aid relationship grew].

The record allows the competing explanations of the first grant to be stated, each in its strongest form, without deciding between them. The case its sponsors made in public was strategic: Israel as a "bulwark" of democracy against Communist threats in the region, and "a dependable ally." [cite:rs_cw1_jta_douglas_taft_1951] The case Israel's government made was humanitarian and fiscal: hundreds of thousands of immigrants, the cost of defence, and its obligations to the refugee reintegration fund. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_eban_request] The State Department's position, as its own papers show it, was regional and balanced: a programme for the whole Near East, equal sums for Israel and the Arab states, the larger share for the Arab refugees, and resistance to the higher figures pressed on it by Israel's American supporters, including one who came at the President's suggestion. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mcghee_note] [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] Who gained is clear enough on paper: Israel's absorption budget, and in parallel the Arab states and the UN refugee programme. The cost fell on the American government's budget, and in 1951 it was carried over the objection of the department responsible for the region.

What the record does not settle should be said plainly. The sources cited here do not give the text of the fiscal 1952 appropriation, so the sum Israel finally received that year rests on two accounts, $65 million and about $68.8 million, that describe different stages. They do not reconcile Barda's figures with the Administration's. They do not say which states the $41.5 million in military grant authority of May 1951 was meant for, or why McGhee thought refugee money could be described to Feinberg as help for Israel. Nor do they show which argument moved the votes in Congress: the Cold War language of the sponsors, the immigrant arithmetic of the Israeli note, or the work of the advocates in the corridors. All three were present. The documents record that the grant passed, and how much it was, more clearly than they record why.
