What Was Blocked, and What Was Not

A brake is tested only when someone presses on it. Between January 2025 and October 2026 the courts, Congress and the states pressed on each of the five patterns of [scene:de31_five_patterns|the first section]. Some held, some gave way, and some held in law while the policy found another road. This section sets out the record by institution, dated, with status as of October 2026, and closes the part by testing the worst-case reading against it.

The courts: where they held

The largest single check came from the Supreme Court. On 20 February 2026, in Learning Resources v. Trump, it held by six votes to three that the emergency-powers law does not authorise tariffs, ending duties that had raised about $166 billion. [cite:us11_ks_ieepa] [cite:de31_gt_s122] When the administration replaced them with a 10 per cent global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a law that allows a temporary surcharge for balance-of-payments problems, the Court of International Trade held on 7 May 2026, by two to one, that the proclamation exceeded that authority. It gave relief only to the plaintiffs, two importers and the State of Washington, and the Federal Circuit paused even that while the government appealed. [cite:de31_gt_s122] In the removal cases the Court that overruled Humphrey's Executor drew a line at the Federal Reserve, and Governor Lisa Cook stayed in office while her case went on. [cite:de0_slaughter]

Lower courts held the walls between datasets in several places. A federal judge found that the tax agency had disclosed taxpayer information to immigration enforcement unlawfully about 42,695 times, and the appeals court in Washington upheld the block on 8 September 2026. [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] Judge Vince Chhabria in San Francisco first blocked Medicaid sharing in August 2025, then on 29 December 2025 allowed only basic identifying and contact fields, then paused the transfers after the January 2026 overshare. [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] [cite:de30_bl_medicaid] [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] The Justice Department's demands for states' full voter rolls had lost all thirteen trial-level decisions by 13 July 2026. [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] An injunction against mass layoffs at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau remained in force after the full appeals court refused to modify it on 19 June 2026. [cite:de20_orrick_cfpb]

The courts: where they did not

The courts also declined to stop much. The inspectors general won the argument and lost the remedy: Judge Reyes found the removals unlawful and reinstated no one, and the case was stayed until Slaughter; by late October 2026 it had reached the summary-judgment stage with no ruling on the merits. [cite:de31_wt_storch] [cite:de31_ch_storch] The pocket rescission stood on the Supreme Court's stay of 26 September 2025. [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid] Slaughter itself ran against the challengers. [cite:de0_slaughter] No court had blocked the civil-service schedule; suits in Washington and Maryland were pending. [cite:de30_afge_lit] On 28 September 2026 Chief Judge Boasberg refused to halt the TANF notice, calling the states' fears "vague, unsubstantiated predictions," and three days earlier the Supreme Court had let the citizenship-checking system linked to Social Security records resume. [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] [cite:de19_npr_save]

The courts also narrowed their own brake. On 27 June 2025, in Trump v. CASA, the Supreme Court held that universal injunctions, orders that protect everyone and not only the parties, "likely exceed the equitable authority that Congress has granted to federal courts." Before the ruling, district courts had issued 34 such injunctions against the administration in five months. The ruling left class actions open, and on 10 July 2025 a New Hampshire court used one to block the birthright-citizenship order for a whole class of children. [cite:de31_crs_casa] The brake was narrowed, not removed, and the narrowing had been urged by Justice Department officials of the first Trump administration in 2019 and 2020. [cite:de31_crs_casa]

Congress

Congress voted against the emergency tariffs more than once. On 29 October 2025 the Senate voted 50 to 46 to end the emergency behind the Canada tariffs, with Republicans Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins, Mitch McConnell and Rand Paul joining every Democrat; it had passed a similar resolution on Brazil the day before. [cite:de31_bnn_senate] House leaders adopted rules that let them keep such resolutions off the floor, but on 11 February 2026 the House voted 219 to 211, with six Republicans, to end the Canada emergency. Representative Don Bacon, a Republican, said, "We should defend our authorities"; the president's economic adviser answered that "The president will make sure they don't repeal his tariffs." [cite:de31_ap_house] Nine days later the Supreme Court settled the legal question. On 1 July 2025 the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip a ten-year bar on state laws regulating artificial intelligence from the budget bill, on an amendment led by two Democrats and a Republican, keeping the states' power to legislate. [cite:de31_markey_ai]

Elsewhere Congress did not act. The Saving the Civil Service Act, which would limit the president's power to reclassify posts, had not passed. [cite:de30_crs_lsb11412] No law reversed the pocket rescission or the removal of the inspectors general. And Congress removed one consumer brake itself, by majority vote under the Congressional Review Act, when it cancelled the overdraft rule in May 2025. [cite:de21_hk_cra]

The states

States were the plaintiffs in most of the data cases. Twenty sued over Medicaid sharing and won limits; about two dozen and the District of Columbia sued over the TANF notice and lost the first round; state election officials beat the voter-roll demands; and Washington State won relief from the Section 122 tariff for its own imports. [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] [cite:de31_gt_s122] Their reach is also their limit: a state can protect the records it holds and sue over federal ones, but cannot reinstate a federal officer or release federal funds.

The road around the brake

The tariffs show a pattern that the scorecard has to record. When the Supreme Court closed the emergency-powers route, the policy moved to a second statute, and when the second expired on 24 July 2026 after its 150-day limit, it moved to a third: the same day the administration imposed "Forced Labor Tariffs" of 10 to 12.5 per cent on goods from 60 economies under Section 301 of the Trade Act, said to reach 99 per cent of imports with exemptions. [cite:de31_cg_301] The brake held in law and the policy continued by another route, under statutes Congress had written. The inspectors general case showed the same shape: the court held the process unlawful and said the president could reach the same result by giving notice first. [cite:de31_wt_storch]

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis is the Sophia platform's, not a finding: immigration enforcement and government cuts as the occasion, a sovereign executive without a veto, the end state Curtis Yarvin's program describes ([scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]), as the destination. This part asked whether the brakes on such an executive are coming off.

Documented: in 2025 and 2026 orders, rulings and notices weakened veto points in all five patterns at once. A president may now remove members of the Federal Trade Commission at will, under a ruling that overruled the precedent protecting such commissions, and about 8,000 senior career officials by written notice; 17 inspectors general were removed in one night without the notice the law requires; about $4 billion that Congress had voted lapsed unspent; and tax, health and welfare records were opened to immigration enforcement. [cite:de0_slaughter] [cite:de30_npr_8000] [cite:de31_npr_igs] [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid] [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] Also documented, and running against a one-party reading: presidents of both parties used the same powers before, from Obama's removal of an inspector general to Biden's removal of a protected commissioner and his use of an emergency to cancel student debt. [cite:de31_govexec_walpin] [cite:de31_govexec_saul] [cite:de31_ag_nebraska] What is new is that the five moved together and at scale.

Probable: that many of the remaining brakes are procedural and can be met rather than overcome. The record points that way in two places: a judge said the president could remove the same inspectors general again after giving notice, and the tariffs moved from one statute to a second and then a third when courts and time closed each. [cite:de31_wt_storch] [cite:de31_cg_301] That rests on these two documented cases, not on any statement of intent.

Not supported: that the five patterns were drawn from the Dark Enlightenment's texts, or designed by Peter Thiel, Elon Musk or anyone else the book maps. No document links any of the orders, rulings or notices to them; the documented defenders of these changes argue from accountability and presidential control, and the one company that appears, Palantir, appears as a recipient of data, not as an author of rules. [cite:de30_govexec_sherk] [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] The rule holds: rhyme is not provenance. Not supported either, on this record: an executive without a veto. The Supreme Court struck down the largest use of emergency power and kept the central bank out of the removal power; both houses of Congress voted against a tariff emergency, with Republicans among the majorities; states won limits on Medicaid sharing and every voter-roll case decided at trial; and a court found 42,695 tax disclosures unlawful.
