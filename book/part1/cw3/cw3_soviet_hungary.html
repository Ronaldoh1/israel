Moscow's Notes and Budapest, November 1956

Two invasions ran through the same November week. In Egypt, Britain, France and Israel were fighting a war that the United States was trying to stop at the United Nations. In Hungary, the Soviet Union had intervened, and the United States was condemning it. The Office of the Historian of the State Department sets the two side by side: the same week that Washington pressed its allies to halt, it made a "strident condemnation of the Soviet intervention in Hungary." [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] The overlap shaped what each great power could say about the other's friends, and it explains why a Soviet government under condemnation for its own intervention could send notes threatening the invaders of Egypt, and why those notes were taken seriously in Washington.

The sources fetched for this book are thin on Hungary itself. They establish the American condemnation, and Peter Hahn's account adds two observations: that unrest in Hungary had distracted American officials in the weeks before the Suez war, and that, in their view, the turmoil left Soviet leaders "prone to impulsive behavior." [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The dates and course of the Hungarian rising and of the Soviet intervention are not in those sources, and this section does not supply them from elsewhere. What the record does show is how Hungary was used in the argument over the Middle East, by Washington and by Moscow.

The threat, 5–6 November. The editors of the State Department's documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States, gave the documents of 5 and 6 November a title that puts the Soviet threat at the centre: "Anglo-French Assault On the Canal Zone; the Threat of Soviet Intervention; Acceptance of a Cease-Fire." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3] According to Hahn, Soviet leaders threatened to "intervene in the fighting" and to retaliate against London and Paris with weapons of mass destruction, and intelligence reports of Soviet forces concentrating in Syria alarmed American officials. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The Office of the Historian records American concern about "the possibility that the Soviets would intervene to assist Nasser." [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] Whether the forces in Syria existed on the scale reported is not established by these sources; what is established is that American officials believed the reports and were alarmed by them.

Nikolai Bulganin, the Soviet premier, wrote to each of the three invading governments on 5 November. The letters were not identical, and the differences are the most revealing thing about them.

To Ben-Gurion he wrote that the Israeli government "is playing with the fate of peace, with the fate of its own people, in a criminal and irresponsible manner." Its policy, the letter went on, "will place a question [mark] upon the very existence of Israel as a State." The Soviet government had decided to advise its ambassador "to leave Israel and immediately go to Moscow." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin1] The note named no weapon. Its threat was to Israel's existence as a state, and its one concrete act was the recall of the ambassador.

To Guy Mollet, in Paris, Bulganin wrote that the Soviet Union was prepared to employ "all modern forms of destructive weaponry" to halt the intervention in Egypt. That phrase comes from the summary prepared by the Centre virtuel de la connaissance sur l'Europe (CVCE) of the letter as printed in the French diplomatic documents (Documents diplomatiques français 1956, volume III, pages 212–213); the full text was not fetched. [cite:rs_cw3_cvce_mollet] The letter to Eden, in London, was not fetched either, so its wording is known here only through what the Jewish Telegraphic Agency later reported of all three. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_23nov56]

That report, carried in the Detroit Jewish News on 23 November 1956, gave the demands common to the letters to Eden, Mollet and Ben-Gurion: immediate withdrawal, reparations to Egypt, and an objection to stationing the UN force anywhere but on both sides of the armistice lines. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_23nov56] The last point bore on Israel directly. Ben-Gurion had told the Knesset on 7 November that Israel would not agree to a foreign force in its territory or in any area it held; Moscow's position was that if a UN force stood anywhere, it should stand on Israel's side of the line as well. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov]

Ben-Gurion's first answer, 8 November. Ben-Gurion replied on 8 November. He expressed "surprise and sorrow at the threat against Israel's existence and well-being contained in your Note," and said that Israel's course "is not and will not be decided by any foreign factor." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin1] The reply was dated the same day on which Ben-Gurion told Eisenhower that Israel would withdraw from Sinai once satisfactory arrangements were made for the UN force, a retreat told in [scene:cw3_third_kingdom|the Third Kingdom and the retreat]. The two letters of 8 November therefore went in different directions: to Moscow, a refusal to be decided by any foreign power; to Washington, a conditional agreement to leave.

The American side drew the connection explicitly. On 8 November Assistant Secretary of State Rountree told Eban, for Israel, that Israel's withdrawal mattered for "Western-Soviet relations and the relationship between the United States and Israel," and that failing to comply "would endanger world peace." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d557] In Washington's framing the Soviet threat was a reason for Israel to leave quickly, not a pressure to be defied.

The second exchange, 15–17 November. Bulganin wrote again on 15 November, referring back to "my letter to you of 5 November" and demanding Israeli withdrawal, compensation to Egypt and the return of property. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin] Ben-Gurion's reply of 17 November is the fullest statement of Israel's case that the exchange produced. "It was, therefore, the elementary duty of our Government to take defensive measures," he wrote. "Any return to the Armistice Agreement means a return to murder, blockade, and boycott, directed against Israel." And he answered the charge of aggression against civilians directly: "We have not destroyed a single Egyptian town, nor have we caused harm to any civilian centre." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin] That last sentence was written after the killings at Khan Yunis on 3 November and Rafah on 12 November, which the Director of UNRWA recorded in a special report and which are told in this chapter's section on Khan Yunis and Rafah; the reader can set the claim beside that record. [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212]

The strongest form of Israel's case in these letters is the one Ben-Gurion made: the armistice regime of 1949 had not given Israel security from raids, from the closure of its shipping lanes or from economic boycott, and a demand to return to it was a demand to return to those conditions. The Soviet case, in its own terms, was that three states had invaded a fourth in defiance of the UN and should withdraw and pay for the damage. Each letter stated its writer's position; neither engaged the other's.

Hungary as an argument, 16 November. Washington used Hungary in its own diplomacy with the Arab world. On 16 November Eisenhower wrote to King Saud of Saudi Arabia. He cited "the ruthless suppression of the people of Hungary in their quest for freedom" as "indicative of the fate of any who become dominated by the Soviet Union," and asked the King to encourage Egypt to cooperate on "the fundamental problems which gave rise to the current crisis." [cite:rs_cw3_kingsaud_ike] Saud's own message, to which Eisenhower was replying, had spoken of "Israeli-French-U.K. aggression against Egypt." [cite:rs_cw3_kingsaud_ike] The exchange shows the American position at its most delicate: Washington agreed with the King that the invasion should end, and wanted him to see the Soviet Union, not the West, as the long-term danger to the region.

What ended the war? Two answers are given, and both have evidence. The first holds that the Soviet threat did it. Moscow's warnings, tinged with the language of nuclear weapons, and the reports from Syria made the war look like a path to escalation between the great powers; the cease-fire was accepted on 6 November, the day after the notes; and Israeli accounts give the Bulganin letter weight in the decision to withdraw. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin] The strongest version of this case rests on timing and on the State Department's own filing, which places "the Threat of Soviet Intervention" between the assault on the Canal and the acceptance of the cease-fire. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3]

The second answer holds that American pressure did it. The Office of the Historian and Hahn credit the cease-fire to American "political and financial pressure" and to the votes at the UN, which had begun on 2 November, three days before the notes. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] On this reading Washington treated the Soviet threat as a reason to end the war quickly, as Rountree told Eban, rather than as the cause of its end; and the financial and oil levers described in [scene:cw3_un_ceasefire|Washington against its allies] did the work of turning the cease-fire into a withdrawal. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d557] Both readings agree on what happened and in what order. They differ on weight, and the documents fetched here do not show what any of the three invading cabinets said in private about the Soviet notes when they decided.

The intelligence side of the Soviet–Israeli–American triangle in 1956, the Angleton years at the CIA, is told elsewhere in this library, in [scene:cia_s03|the Khrushchev speech] and Stage Two.

The gaps are specific. Bulganin's letter to Eden was not fetched, and the letter to Mollet is known only through a summary, so the exact form of the threat to London and Paris rests on Hahn's description and the CVCE's paraphrase. The Soviet troop concentrations in Syria are known here only as intelligence reports that alarmed American officials, not as a verified deployment. The course of events in Hungary is outside what these sources record. And whether Moscow ever intended to act on its threats is not something any document fetched for this book shows.
