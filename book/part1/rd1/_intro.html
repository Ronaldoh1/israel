This stage of the story opens in 1896, when a Viennese journalist named Theodor Herzl published Der Judenstaat, and closes in 1917, on the eve of the Balfour Declaration. In those two decades a political program on paper became a set of working institutions. There was a Congress with elected delegates, an executive and a council. There were national federations in America and Britain. There was a bank incorporated in London with a branch in Jaffa, and a fund that bought land in Palestine and never resold it.

The question here is simple: when, and how, was the project of a Jewish state conceived and given structure? Many people place its origin in the catastrophe of the 1930s and 1940s. The dates point the other way. By 1905 the movement had already weighed Argentina, a Sinai buffer at Suez and a British offer in East Africa. It chose Palestine through its own contested votes.

These years were also years of argument. The Bund, Orthodox rabbis, Ahad Ha'am and the official bodies of British Jewry all opposed or contested political Zionism, and the movement split over East Africa. Herzl, meanwhile, approached the Kaiser, the Sultan, Britain and the Tsar's minister in turn, seeking a charter from whichever power would grant one. Land was bought, mostly from absentee owners, and tenants who held no title paid the price. What follows traces how these pieces were assembled, and by whom.
