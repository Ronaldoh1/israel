The Asymmetry: Who the First Amendment Protects

Most Americans learn that the First Amendment protects free speech. Fewer learn whose speech it protects, and against whom. The answer the Supreme Court gives is precise. The amendment restrains government, and only government. Lawyers call this the state action doctrine: a constitutional right can be claimed only against "state action", meaning conduct by a government or by someone exercising government power, never against a private person or company acting on its own account. In June 2019 the Court restated the rule in plain terms: "the Free Speech Clause prohibits only governmental abridgment of speech." [cite:dt5_lii_halleck] This section follows that rule through three decisions and shows what it produces when the speakers in question are large companies on one side and their users on the other.

The rule: Halleck, 2019

The case was Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck, decided on 17 June 2019 by five votes to four. Manhattan Neighborhood Network, a private nonprofit, ran the public-access cable channels in Manhattan under a designation from New York City. Two producers whose access it suspended argued that because the channels were a public forum created under city authority, the network was bound by the First Amendment. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, writing for the majority, disagreed. Running a forum for other people's speech was not a power traditionally and exclusively reserved to government: "merely hosting speech by others is not a traditional, exclusive public function." The network was a private actor and could choose what it carried. Justice Sonia Sotomayor, for the four dissenters, argued that the city had handed the network its job, so that it was "a case about principals and agents." [cite:dt5_lii_halleck]

Halleck concerned a cable channel, not a social network, but its logic reaches every privately owned platform. A user removed from Facebook, X or YouTube has no First Amendment claim against the company, however important the platform has become to public debate. Most Americans do not know this. In a Pew Research Center survey in March 2026, 33 per cent correctly said the Constitution does not protect the press from interference by private companies, with almost no difference between Republicans (32 per cent) and Democrats (36 per cent). A larger share, 60 per cent, knew that platforms may legally restrict what users post. [cite:dt5_pew_fa2026]

Spending as speech: Citizens United, 2010

The same amendment that gives users no claim against a company gives the company a strong claim against the government. On 21 January 2010, in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the Court struck down the federal ban on independent election spending by corporations and unions. That part of the decision was five votes to four: Justice Anthony Kennedy wrote for himself, Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Antonin Scalia, Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas, while Justices John Paul Stevens, Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Stephen Breyer and Sotomayor dissented. The case began with Hillary: The Movie, a 2008 documentary made by the conservative nonprofit Citizens United. [cite:dt5_wiki_cu] The majority held that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption." The same decision upheld the law's disclaimer and disclosure rules [cite:ad15_lii_cu], and on that part the vote was eight to one, with Justice Thomas alone willing to strike them down. [cite:dt5_elb_thomas_alone] [cite:dt5_wiki_cu] The money that followed is the subject of [scene:dt4_the_case_law|Part Four].

Large technology firms use the spending right like any other company. Amazon, Meta and JPMorgan Chase each gave at least $1 million to President Trump's 2025 inaugural committee, which raised a record $239 million according to its filing with the Federal Election Commission. [cite:dt5_alarabiya_inaugural] On 23 September 2025 Meta launched the American Technology Excellence Project, a super PAC (a committee that may raise and spend unlimited sums so long as it does not coordinate with candidates) funded with "tens of millions" of dollars to fight state regulation of artificial intelligence and to back candidates of both parties it regards as friendly to the industry. A California committee had preceded it in August. [cite:dt5_techcrunch_metapac] Elon Musk funds America PAC while owning X. [cite:de11_ap_2026] None of this is unlawful, and no dataset links any firm's political spending to its moderation decisions. The point here is structural: the companies that decide what users may say are also among the largest political speakers.

Moderation as editing: Moody v. NetChoice, 2024

In 2021 Florida and Texas passed laws restricting how large platforms could remove or demote users' posts. [cite:dt5_dwt_moody] On 1 July 2024, in Moody v. NetChoice, all nine justices voted to vacate the lower-court judgments and send the cases back for a fuller analysis. Justice Elena Kagan's opinion, joined in full by the Chief Justice and Justices Sotomayor, Kavanaugh and Barrett and in large part by Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, went further and set out how the First Amendment applies to the platforms' main feeds. Justices Thomas, Alito and Neil Gorsuch concurred only in the judgment. [cite:dt5_scotusblog_moody] The majority treated a platform's choices about what to show, remove and rank as editorial judgments of the kind a newspaper makes. Government violates a platform's rights when it "interferes with such editorial choices," and a state's wish to change "the mix of speech" a platform carries is "not [a] valid, let alone substantial" interest. [cite:dt5_dwt_moody]

Taken together, the three decisions produce an asymmetry. A large company may spend without limit on independent political advocacy, because that is its speech. It may remove, demote or keep up whatever users post, because that is also its speech. A user whose post is removed has no constitutional claim against it, because the company is not the state. The government, for its part, can neither cap the company's independent spending nor tell it what to carry.

Two readings of the same design

That the asymmetry exists is a matter of record. Whether it is a defect is a judgment, and both readings have serious support. On the first, it is the First Amendment working as intended. Editorial freedom is what protects a newspaper, a cable channel or a website of any political leaning from a government that dislikes its choices; the Florida and Texas laws were written by officials who wanted to change what platforms carried, and the Court's answer would apply equally to a future legislature of the other party. On the second reading, the doctrine was built for a world of many newspapers and fits poorly a world in which a handful of companies host most public argument. Users who lose access to those services have no forum for appeal except the company itself, while the company's owners, as the next section shows, can change its rules overnight.

Public opinion points to a related distrust, split by party. In February 2024, 83 per cent of Americans said social media sites were likely to censor political viewpoints on purpose, including 93 per cent of Republicans and 74 per cent of Democrats. Forty-four per cent said technology companies favoured liberal views and 15 per cent conservative ones. A narrow majority, 51 per cent, wanted more government regulation of the companies, 60 per cent of Democrats and 45 per cent of Republicans. [cite:dt5_pew_tech2024]

The last step of that sequence is where users do have rights, and it is the subject of the third section of this part. Before it, the record of what the companies actually moderated needs to be set out, in every direction from which complaints have come. [scene:dt5_what_was_moderated|What was moderated]
