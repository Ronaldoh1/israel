Israel: Four School Systems, Several Stories

Israel's education ministry holds the pen closely, as an earlier part of this volume showed. But the ministry runs not one school system but four, and each teaches the country's founding differently.

The four streams

Israeli children attend one of four main types of school: state secular, state religious, independent ultra-Orthodox (Haredi), and Arab. The Arab schools teach in Arabic and replace the Bible examination with one on Muslim, Christian or Druze heritage. The Haredi schools concentrate almost entirely on religious study and teach little of the secular core. [cite:nat_wiki_edu_israel] The balance between them is shifting. A 2012 projection reported in the business daily Globes found that of about 370,000 additional pupils expected between 2001 and 2017, 43 percent would be Arab and 40 percent Haredi, with only 4 percent in state secular schools, so that the state Jewish system's share of all pupils would fall from 52 to 41 percent. [cite:nat_globes_pupils2012]

What the books say

The most cited critical study of Israeli schoolbooks is Palestine in Israeli School Books (2012) by the Israeli education scholar Nurit Peled-Elhanan. [cite:peled_elhanan_textbooks] Examining dozens of books published since the 1990s, she found Palestinians shown mainly as refugees, farmers or masked militants, maps that did not mark the West Bank as separate from Israel or that used the biblical names "Judea and Samaria," and a Jerusalem map with no Palestinian sites. One textbook said the flight of Palestinians after the killings at Deir Yassin had "solved a terrifying demographic problem" for the new state. Newer books, she noted, acknowledge "certain cases of expulsions," where older ones avoided the refugees altogether. [cite:nat_national_peled2012]

Her findings were challenged. Elie Podeh, a Hebrew University scholar of Israeli textbooks, said she had ignored "considerable improvements," including the removal of descriptions of Arabs as robbers or murderers. Another education professor said similar bias could be found in American books. [cite:nat_national_peled2012] The 2013 study of both systems, described earlier in this volume, found more extreme negative portrayals of the other side in Israeli state secular books than in Palestinian books, but too few in either to be statistically meaningful, and found that both erased the other on their maps. [cite:pen_forward_wexler2013]

The book that taught both stories

One experiment shows where the ministry draws its line. Israeli and Palestinian teachers, working with the Peace Research Institute in the Middle East and funded by Western governments, wrote a history book, Learning Each Other's Historical Narrative, that printed the Israeli and Palestinian accounts on facing pages with a blank column between them for pupils' notes. When the ministry's chief of pedagogy learned that about fifteen pupils at Sha'ar Hanegev High School, near Sderot, were reading it in an optional programme, he banned it and summoned the principal; the ministry said schools may use only approved books. The Palestinian education ministry banned the Arabic edition too. One of its Israeli authors, the historian Eyal Naveh, said: "It's not legitimate in a democratic state to ban books." Nearly all 2,000 printed copies went into a warehouse. [cite:nat_fwd_prime]

Both governments, in other words, rejected the same book for the same reason: it put the other side's story in front of their own pupils. That is the plainest illustration in this volume of how a centralized system holds the pen.
