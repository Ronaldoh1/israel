Leave, Childcare and Child Benefits

Family policy is where the two systems differ most in design. Israel built its family benefits into its national insurance system in the 1950s, as rights that follow residence and employment. The United States built most of its support for families into the tax code and the employer, with a federal floor that guarantees time off but not pay. This section compares the two on leave after a birth, the price of childcare and the money each state gives parents, with the years of each measure stated; the next section turns to abortion, fertility treatment and birth rates. [scene:us18_reproduction|Abortion, IVF and birth rates]

Leave after a birth

Israel's maternity insurance came into effect on 1 April 1954, as one of the first five branches of the National Insurance Law. [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] What it pays has lengthened in three steps. The paid allowance was 12 weeks (6 weeks for women with a shorter work record) until May 2007, 14 weeks (or 7) until the end of 2016, and 15 weeks (or 8) from January 2017. [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] The allowance replaces earnings rather than paying a flat sum: the National Insurance Institute (NII) takes the higher of the mother's average daily income over the last three or six full months before leave, up to a ceiling of NIS 1,752.33 a day as of 1 January 2026. [cite:us18_nii_matrates] In 2018 the average allowance was NIS 285 a day, and the average recipient had earned 83.8 per cent of the national average wage. [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] The labour law adds job protection around the paid weeks. A woman with twelve months at her employer may take 26 weeks of leave, of which 15 are paid by the NII and the rest unpaid, and she may not be dismissed during pregnancy or for 60 days after returning without a permit from the minister. [cite:us18_nbn_women]

Fathers came later and remain few. Since 1998 a father may take part of the paid leave if the mother returns to work; in April 2017 the minimum the father must take was cut from 21 days to 7, and the number of fathers paid rose from 545 in 2016 to 1,266 in 2018, or 9.5 for every 1,000 women paid. [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018]

The United States has no national paid leave. The Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993 gives eligible workers up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave for a birth or a family illness, but the leave is unpaid. [cite:us18_crs_leave] Pay depends on the employer or the state. In March 2023, 27 per cent of private-sector workers had paid family leave through their employer, about 17 percentage points more than in 2010; 13 per cent of workers in the lowest-paid quarter of jobs had it, against 44 per cent in the highest-paid quarter. [cite:us18_crs_leave] The Bureau of Labor Statistics found 90 per cent of civilian workers with access to unpaid family leave in 2023, and says it has no comparable paid-leave estimates for 2024 or 2025 because the survey's scope changed. [cite:us18_bls_leave] States have filled part of the gap. California started the first state programme on 1 July 2004; by March 2025 ten jurisdictions, including the District of Columbia, were paying benefits, and Delaware, Maine, Maryland and Minnesota had enacted programmes due to start paying during 2026. [cite:us18_crs_leave] Federal civil servants gained paid parental leave in the defence authorisation act of December 2019. [cite:us18_crs_leave]

Childcare

Part Fourteen showed that Israel made preschool free for three- and four-year-olds from the 2012–13 school year, after the 2011 protests, and that the saving to families was smaller than the law implied. [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the basket] [cite:us14_taub_preschool] The United States has no national free preschool, and the Labor Department's National Database of Childcare Prices shows what families pay instead. In 2022, across 2,512 counties, full-day care for one child cost between $6,552 and $15,600 a year, between 8.9 and 16 per cent of the county's median family income. [cite:us18_dol_childcare] The database covers 2008 to 2022, so it describes the period before and during the pandemic rather than 2026 prices. [cite:us18_dol_childcare] This book found no Israeli series on the price of care for children under three that is defined the same way, so no side-by-side figure is given.

Money for children: an allowance and a tax credit

Israel pays a monthly child allowance through the NII to every family with children, whatever its income. The Children Insurance Law took effect in 1959, at first as a fixed payment to large families only. [cite:us18_nii_children2018] From 1 January 2026 the allowance is NIS 173 a month for the first child, NIS 219 for each of the second to fourth, and NIS 173 for the fifth and later. [cite:us18_nii_childrates] In 2018 about 1.19 million families with 2.88 million children received it. [cite:us18_nii_children2018]

The allowance has been cut and restored with changes of government, and the cuts fell hardest on large families, which is why its history matters for poverty. Allowances were cut in 2002–2004; children born from June 2003 were paid the same rate as the first two children, whatever their birth order, until June 2009. [cite:us18_nii_children2018] The NII found that the cutback starting in 2002 raised child poverty by 5 percentage points between 2002 and 2005 and deepened the poverty of those already poor. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007] What the NII calls the Streamlining Law cut allowances again in August 2013, to NIS 140 for new children; after a new government took office in May 2015 the allowances were raised, and from January 2017 the state also deposits NIS 50 a month into a long-term savings account for each child, to which parents may add NIS 50 from the allowance. [cite:us18_nii_children2018] The 2018 rate table shows the old tiering still in force for older children: NIS 336–354 for fourth and later children, against NIS 150–189 for the first three. [cite:us18_nii_children2018]

The United States gives most of its child support through the income-tax system rather than as a payment. Its largest test came in 2021, when Congress raised the Child Tax Credit from $2,000 to $3,000 per child, and $3,600 for children under six, for one year. When the credit returned to its usual level in 2022, child poverty on the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts tax credits as income, rose from 5.2 per cent in 2021 to 12.4 per cent in 2022. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] Part of the change reflects the end of other pandemic measures in the same year; the measure itself does not separate them.

On the OECD's measure of public spending on families, the two countries sit far apart, but the measure needs care. The OECD's Social Expenditure Database (SOCX) counts cash benefits and services; on that basis the United States spent 0.77 per cent of GDP on families in 1980 and 0.60 per cent in 2023, and Israel 1.89 per cent in 2023, within a range of 1.74 to 2.49 per cent over the years the series covers. [cite:us18_socx_usa] [cite:us18_socx_isr] That series leaves out tax breaks, which is where most American support sits; the OECD's family database, which adds them, still put the United States below 1 per cent of GDP in 2021, against an OECD average of 2.35 per cent, with more than half of American spending going on services. [cite:us18_oecd_pf11] The OECD publishes Israel's figures from Israeli authorities' data. [cite:us18_oecd_pf11]

Who each design leaves out is different. Israel's allowance reaches every resident family with children, but its value has swung with coalition budgets, and the families that rely on it most, the largest and poorest, have taken the deepest cuts. [cite:us18_nii_survey2007] [cite:us18_nii_children2018] The American design pays most to families with enough earnings to use a tax credit, guarantees leave without pay, and leaves the price of care to the market; the 2021 experiment showed what a larger payment did to child poverty, and its expiry showed the reverse. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:us18_dol_childcare]
