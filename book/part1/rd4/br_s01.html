Britain Trained the Army That Would Fight It

The Jewish fighting force of 1948 is usually remembered as self-made, built in defiance of Britain. Defiance is part of the story. But much of the training, doctrine and leadership of that force came through British channels, offered when Britain needed Jewish fighters and withdrawn when it did not.

The first channel opened in 1938, at the height of the Arab Revolt. Captain Orde Wingate was a British intelligence officer and a devout Christian who embraced the prophetic vision of a Jewish return to the land. He proposed a joint unit of British soldiers and Jewish supernumerary police drawn from the Haganah, and the Jewish Agency eventually agreed. The Special Night Squads operated from Kibbutz Ein Harod from June to October 1938. About 75 Haganah volunteers served in them. Among them were Moshe Dayan and Yigal Allon, both future generals, and Pinchas Kopel, who would later found the Border Police. [cite:nli_wingate_hebrew] [cite:wiki_special_night_squads] [cite:israeled_wingate]

What Wingate taught was a method: small units, moving at night, raiding into Arab villages. Critics have argued that the method outlived him. The Israeli writer Uri Avnery traced a line of retaliation-raid doctrine from Wingate through the Palmach to the Israel Defense Forces. [cite:avnery_dayan_wingate] That is an interpretation offered by a critic, not a settled finding, but it identifies a real question about where the army's habits of fighting came from. The same British administration that sponsored the squads also arrested Haganah members caught training with weapons. Dayan himself was imprisoned in 1939. [cite:archiveshub_wingate]

The second channel opened under the pressure of the world war. Facing a possible Axis breakthrough in North Africa, Britain cooperated with the Haganah in forming the Palmach in May 1941. After El Alamein, that support receded. [cite:wiki_haganah_mirror] The pattern was the same as in 1938: cooperation when the threat was acute, distance once it passed.

The third channel came last and took longest. After lengthy negotiation, the British Army announced a Jewish Brigade Group, formed in 1944. It fought the Germans in northern Italy in March 1945. [cite:wiki_zvi_brenner] [cite:wiki_haganah_mirror]

Taken together, the three episodes describe a relationship that ran in both directions at once. Britain armed and trained Haganah cadres when it needed them against the Arab revolt and against the Axis, and jailed them when it did not. Those cadres became the Palmach, the Brigade, and the command of the army of 1948. From 1945 onward they would also fight Britain itself. The Haganah was never officially recognized, yet by 1947 it fielded about 10,000 mobilized men and 40,000 reservists. The officer corps that led them had, in large part, learned its trade in British service.
