From Persecuted to Patronised: Constantine's Conversion, 303–337

Within ten years the Christian churches of the Roman Empire went from being hunted by the state to being paid by it. Nothing else in this book explains as much about what followed. Before 312 the bishops of Rome, Alexandria, Antioch and Carthage led communities with no legal standing, no tax privileges and no claim on the emperor. By the time Constantine died in 337, those bishops had imperial money, imperial basilicas, legal immunities and a say in imperial law, and the emperor had a say in what they taught. This section sets out how that change happened, what the two contemporary witnesses report about Constantine's turn to Christianity, and what can and cannot be known about why he made it.

The Great Persecution as the starting point

The last and harshest state persecution of Christians began under Diocletian. On 23 February 303, the festival of the Terminalia, the praetorian guard levelled the church at Nicomedia, Diocletian's eastern capital. The Christian rhetorician Lactantius, who lived in the city, wrote that "the books of the Holy Scriptures were found, and they were committed to the flames." [cite:vt21_lact_dmp] The edict published the next day ordered the destruction of Christian scriptures, liturgical books and places of worship across the empire, banned Christian assembly and stripped Christians of legal rights. A second edict in the summer of 303 ordered bishops and priests arrested, and a fourth, in 304, required every inhabitant to sacrifice to the gods on pain of death. [cite:vt21_wiki_dioclpers]

This matters for Book Two for two reasons. First, the largest documented burning of Christian scriptures in antiquity was carried out by a pagan state against the church, not by the church against anyone. Second, the persecution left a wound inside the church: clergy who had handed over scriptures to the authorities were called traditores, "those who handed over", and the refusal of rigorists in North Africa to accept bishops ordained by them produced the Donatist schism, the first church dispute that Constantine would be asked to settle. [cite:vt21_wiki_dioclpers]

How many Christians were there? No census exists. The historian Keith Hopkins offered a tentative estimate of about six million by the year 300, roughly a tenth of the empire's population. [cite:vt21_wiki_dioclpers] The figure is an estimate, but it makes the point that Constantine joined a large minority, not a majority.

The persecution ended in stages. In 311 the dying emperor Galerius issued an edict that allowed Christians "again to be Christians" and to rebuild their meeting places, asking only that they pray to their God for the emperor and the state. [cite:vt21_lact_dmp]

Two accounts of one battle

Constantine was proclaimed emperor by his father's army at York after his father Constantius died there on 25 July 306. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] Six years of civil war followed among the rival claimants. On 28 October 312 he defeated his rival Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge outside Rome; Maxentius drowned in the Tiber. [cite:vt21_wiki_milvian]

There are two Christian accounts of what happened before the battle, and they do not match. Lactantius, writing around 315, says Constantine was told in a dream to mark "the heavenly sign" on his soldiers' shields, and describes the mark as the Greek letter Chi (X) with an upright line drawn through it: most read this as an early form of the Chi-Rho, the first two Greek letters of "Christ". He reports no vision in the sky. [cite:vt21_lact_dmp44] Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, gave no vision at all in his Ecclesiastical History. Only in his Life of Constantine, written after the emperor's death in 337, does he describe a cross of light above the sun around midday, carrying the words "Conquer by this", seen by Constantine "and his whole army", followed that night by a dream in which Christ told him to copy the sign. Eusebius says the emperor told him the story himself many years later "and confirmed his statement by an oath". He then describes the labarum, the battle standard topped with the Chi-Rho. [cite:vt21_vc1] [cite:vt21_wiki_milvian]

Historians weigh these differently. Some, such as John Julius Norwich, think the sky vision never occurred because no source mentions it until a quarter of a century later; others, following Peter Weiss, suggest Constantine saw a solar halo, a ring of light around the sun, which he first read in solar terms and later in Christian ones. [cite:vt21_wiki_milvian] What can be said firmly is that the earliest account is a dream, the fuller account is late and comes from the emperor through his admirer, and the two agree only that a sign associated with Christ was used in the campaign.

The public monuments are more cautious than either writer. The Arch of Constantine, dedicated by the Roman Senate on 25 July 315, credits the victory to the emperor acting "instinctu divinitatis", by the prompting of the divinity, without saying which divinity, and carries no Christian symbol. [cite:vt21_wiki_arch] His coins went on showing the sun god Sol for more than a decade, a point taken up in the next section. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol]

The Milan letter of 313

In early 313 Constantine met his eastern co-emperor Licinius at Milan. The policy they agreed survives as a letter Licinius issued in the East, dated by Lactantius to 13 June 313. It is usually called the Edict of Milan, though it was not an edict and was not issued at Milan. Its core sentence grants that "the Christians and all others should have liberty to follow that mode of religion which to each of them appeared best". Property taken from Christian congregations was to be returned "without money demanded or price claimed", with buyers compensated from the imperial treasury. [cite:vt21_lact_dmp48]

Two things in the text deserve attention. It is a grant of freedom to everyone, not an establishment of Christianity. And it treats the church as a corporate body able to own property, which later imperial gifts would build on. Christianity became the established faith of the empire only under Theodosius, in the 380s, as the third section of this part shows. [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Church and empire]

A baptism at the end

Constantine was not baptised until he was dying. In the spring of 337, ill near Nicomedia, he asked for baptism; Eusebius's Life has him say he had hoped to be baptised in the river Jordan, records that he wore white and would not put on the imperial purple again, and says he died at Pentecost. [cite:vt21_vc4] Eusebius of Caesarea does not name the bishop who baptised him. Other sources identify him as Eusebius of Nicomedia, the bishop who had sided with Arius in the dispute settled against Arius at Nicaea twelve years earlier ([scene:vt22_the_room|Part Twenty-Two]). [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] He died on 22 May 337. [cite:vt21_brit_constantine]

A late baptism was not unusual in the fourth century: many Christians delayed it because baptism was believed to wash away all earlier sins. It is still a fact that the emperor who convened Nicaea, built basilicas and legislated for bishops did so for 25 years as an unbaptised catechumen, and was finally baptised by a bishop of the party Nicaea had condemned. In 326 he had his eldest son Crispus executed and his wife Fausta was killed soon after; the reasons are not recorded in any reliable source. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine]

Why he did it: what the record allows

Older Sophia material frames the conversion as "a political calculation". That is one reading among several, and the record does not settle it. Jacob Burckhardt in the nineteenth century saw Constantine as essentially unreligious, using the church for power; Peter Brown called it "a very Roman conversion", a soldier's bargain with a god who gave victory; Peter Leithart argues he was "a sincere if a somewhat simple believer"; H. A. Drake holds that his personal views favoured Christianity while his public policy aimed at civic unity among Christians and pagans alike. [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol]

The documented acts point both ways. He gave the church money, buildings and legal privileges and took part in its councils. He also kept pagan forms in public: the unnamed divinity on the arch, Sol on the coinage until the mid-320s, the old title of chief priest of the Roman cults, and an early edict letting polytheists keep their rites. After he defeated Licinius in 324 he destroyed a few temples and stripped many more of their treasures to pay for his new capital, Constantinople. [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol] A careful reader can say that Constantine chose the Christian God as his patron and the Christian church as his partner, and that he did so as a Roman emperor who expected his god to deliver victory and unity. What he believed in private, the sources cannot show.
