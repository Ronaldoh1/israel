The Law: 427 to 1

The July 2025 memo announcing that no further files would be released provoked anger across the political spectrum, including from much of the administration's own base. [cite:ep9_abc_bondi_fired] On 15 July 2025 Representative Ro Khanna, a Democrat, introduced the Epstein Files Transparency Act, and Representative Thomas Massie, a Republican, filed a discharge petition to force it to a vote. The petition reached the 218 signatures it needed in November. The House passed the bill on 18 November 2025 by 427 votes to 1, the Senate passed it by unanimous consent, and the President signed it on 19 November. [cite:newsweek_transparency_act] [cite:efta_wiki]

The law is short and specific. It required the Justice Department to publish its Epstein records within thirty days. It allowed withholding on only a few listed grounds: protecting victims' identities, material showing child sexual abuse, images of death or abuse, properly classified material, and records whose release would harm an active investigation, which had to be narrowly drawn and temporary. And it forbade withholding anything for reasons of "embarrassment, reputational harm, or political sensitivity". [cite:ep8_govinfo_act] It required a list of every government official and public figure named in the records, and a public account of the grounds for each redaction.

What it did not contain was a way to enforce it. When Khanna and Massie asked the judge in Maxwell's case to appoint a monitor to oversee compliance, Judge Paul Engelmayer ruled in January 2026 that he had no authority to supervise the department's compliance with the Act, while calling their concerns "undeniably important and timely". [cite:ep9_fox_monitor] In July 2026 the same coalition introduced a second bill whose whole purpose is to give victims, state attorneys general and members of Congress the standing to sue to enforce the first.
