Inside the White House, 1945–1948

The Agency's other diplomatic track ran to Washington, and there it met a peculiarity of the Truman presidency. American policy on Palestine is often described as a State Department product, made on the merits by career diplomats. In the Truman years that description leaves out the building where the decisions were finally taken. Inside the White House a small number of aides and friends gave the President views and visitors that the State Department did not, and at least one of his most consequential statements was issued a month before a national election. Who had access, and when the President spoke, are both on the record. Why he spoke as he did is still argued.

The longest-serving of those aides was David K. Niles. He served in the White House from 1942 to 1951, first under Franklin Roosevelt and then under Harry Truman, and he was one of only two Roosevelt aides whom Truman kept on [cite:wiki_david_niles] [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. His brief was minority affairs. Niles was a committed Zionist, and he became an important channel through which American Zionists reached the White House [cite:wiki_david_niles] [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. That is a documented role, not an accusation: he had been entrusted with minority affairs, and his own commitment was to the Zionist cause.

Around Niles stood others. Max Lowenthal advised the President's special counsel, Clark Clifford, on Palestine [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. And outside the staff altogether there was Eddie Jacobson, Truman's personal friend and former business partner, who provided a separate, private channel to the President; his role, including the March 1948 meeting he arranged, is told in [scene:s08|Truman and the men around him]. What they offered was proximity: being in the room, or being able to get someone else into it.

The clearest test of that influence came in the autumn of 1946, after a conference on Palestine in London had adjourned; the conference and Britain's part in it are set out in [scene:rd5_bevin|Bevin and Palestine, 1945–1946]. On 4 October 1946, the eve of Yom Kippur and a month before the congressional midterm elections, Truman issued a statement on Palestine [cite:avalon_yomkippur_1946] [cite:nuremberg_yomkippur]. He regretted the adjournment of the London conference. He urged immediate, large-scale Jewish immigration into Palestine. And he said that the United States could support a solution along the lines of a viable Jewish state "in an adequate area of Palestine" [cite:avalon_yomkippur_1946] [cite:nuremberg_yomkippur].

The statement landed hard in London. The British Prime Minister, Clement Attlee, was furious, and Truman wrote to him that he "very much" regretted any embarrassment it had caused [cite:frus_truman_attlee_1946]. The apology concerned the embarrassment; the documents cited here do not record any change in the position itself.

The timing invited an explanation, and the New York Times printed one within three days. On 7 October 1946 James Reston wrote in that paper that the President "went against his advisers' foreign policy and chose to follow the promptings of those who were primarily interested in retaining Democratic majorities in Congress" [cite:palestinestudies_christison_truman]. On Reston's reading, the statement was electoral, and its date, a month before the midterms, was part of the evidence.

Truman rejected that reading publicly. In February 1947 he issued a statement calling the impression that partisan and local politics motivated American policy on Palestine "most unfortunate and misleading", and insisting that his position had been consistent since 1945 [cite:jvl_truman_feb1947]. On his own account, the statement of October 1946 restated a view he had held since his first months in office, not a new position adopted for an election.

Each side has real evidence. Reston's case rests on the calendar, which no one disputes, and on his reporting that the statement went against the foreign-policy advice the President had received. Truman's case rests on consistency: the October statement's call for immediate, large-scale immigration was, on his account, the position he had held since 1945, and a statement that restates an existing position is not by itself proof of a new electoral calculation. His own earlier words on the subject are gathered in [scene:tr_s01|Truman in his own words].

The two readings are not mutually exclusive, and the documents do not force a choice between them. Who had the President's ear, and when he chose to speak, are on the record. Whether electoral politics or conviction drove him is argued by named sources on both sides, and the answer can be both: a man may hold a view sincerely and still choose the most politically useful day on which to announce it.

The White House channel was the last link in a longer chain, told in the previous stage of this book. Two American Zionist campaigns of the 1940s, a rival and aggressive one led by the Irgun's delegation in the United States and a disciplined mainstream lobby, moved American opinion through advertising, local committees and Christian supporters into Congress and both parties' platforms. From there the pressure reached the White House, where Niles provided access and the Yom Kippur statement of 1946 put the President on record, and finally the United Nations, where the United States lobbied for the partition vote of November 1947. That chain shows how the pieces connected; it does not show that any one of them decided the outcome.

What the record does not settle is the weight of each influence. The sources here document Niles's access but not the content of his advice on any given day, nor whether he drafted or proposed the October statement. They record Lowenthal's role as an adviser to Clifford without detailing what he advised. And no document in this section records Truman saying, in private, that the midterms were or were not on his mind when he spoke on 4 October 1946. The accounts of motive therefore remain what they are: Reston's reporting on one side, the President's own public denial on the other.
