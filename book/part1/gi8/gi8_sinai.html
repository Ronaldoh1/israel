Sinai and Egypt: the Largest Return

Egypt sits at the edge of the "Greater Israel" debate for two reasons. One is a single phrase: the "river of Egypt" of Genesis 15:18, which maximalist readings take to be the Nile. The other is that Israel has twice held the whole Sinai Peninsula and twice given it back. The first time, in 1956–57, is covered in Part Three. [scene:gi3_sinai56|Part Three: the Sinai test] This section covers the second: the fifteen years of Israeli rule after 1967, the 1979 peace treaty, the completion of the withdrawal in April 1982, and the arbitration over Taba that followed. It is the counter-record at its largest, and it also leaves one territory, Gaza, explicitly outside its terms.

The verse and the border

Genesis 15:18 promises Abram's descendants the land "from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates"; some modern translations render the first river as the "Wadi of Egypt". [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] The historian Daniel Pipes, examining the claim that Israel aims at a Nile-to-Euphrates state, argued in 1994 that the river of Egypt most likely means the Wadi al-Arish in northern Sinai, following the medieval commentator Rashi, and that the narrower boundaries in Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 govern the general Genesis wording. He called the Nile-to-Euphrates idea a "groundless fantasy", while reporting that it had become conventional wisdom in Arab countries and Iran. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Part One sets out the textual debate. [scene:gi1_phrase|Part One: the phrase] For this section the point is narrower: no Israeli government document reviewed for this volume claims Egyptian territory west of the international border, and the one Egyptian territory Israel held for any length of time it returned under a treaty.

From occupation to treaty, 1967–1979

Israel captured Sinai in June 1967 and held it under a military governorate together with Gaza, the West Bank and the Golan. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation] Settlements followed in north-eastern Sinai, of which the largest was the town of Yamit. [cite:gi1_csm_sinai1982] After the 1973 war two disengagement agreements pulled Israeli forces back from the Suez Canal; the second, in September 1975, came with a US–Israel memorandum guaranteeing Israel's oil supply for five years, Sinai's oilfields being among the assets Israel gave up. [cite:ow1_jvl_moa1975]

The Camp David framework of 17 September 1978 had two parts. One concerned the West Bank and Gaza, providing for "transitional arrangements" of no more than five years and "full autonomy to the inhabitants" under an elected self-governing authority. [cite:gz_campdavid] The other led to the Treaty of Peace signed in Washington on 26 March 1979, witnessed by President Jimmy Carter. Its first article ended the state of war and stated: "Israel will withdraw all its armed forces and civilians from the Sinai." Article II fixed the permanent boundary as the recognised line between Egypt and the former mandated territory of Palestine, "without prejudice to the issue of the status of the Gaza Strip", and the parties recognised it "as inviolable". Annex I set an interim withdrawal within nine months of ratification and a final withdrawal "not later than three years" after it. [cite:gi8_avalon_egtreaty]

April 1982: the settlements removed

The handover ended on 25 April 1982. Yamit was evacuated and levelled before Egypt took it over. The defence minister who carried out the evacuation, Ariel Sharon, drew a line under it: "In Sinai, in Yamit, we have reached the red line of our concessions." [cite:gi1_csm_sinai1982] The sentence matters for what came after. A government led by the Likud, the party whose platform claimed the land "between the Sea and the Jordan", gave up the whole peninsula and removed civilian settlements from it; and the minister who did so signalled in the same breath that the land west of the Jordan was a different matter. Part Five follows the Likud platform, and Part Twelve sets the Sinai withdrawal beside the others. [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five: Likud in government] [scene:gi12_withdrawals|Part Twelve: the withdrawals]

Taba: the last 250 acres

One patch of coast remained in dispute. In January 1982, finalising the return, Israeli negotiators argued that Taba, about 250 acres on the Gulf of Aqaba some twelve miles south of Eilat, should stay in Israel, citing ambiguities in the 1906 description of the old Egyptian–Ottoman border; Israel built two hotels there. Under Article 7 of the treaty, which provides for conciliation or arbitration, Israel's cabinet accepted arbitration in principle on 13 January 1986. A three-member panel ruled for Egypt on 29 September 1988, and Israel evacuated Taba on 15 March 1989, with Israelis still allowed to enter without an Egyptian visa. [cite:gi8_enc_taba] The Taba case is small, but it is the plainest instance in this volume of an Israeli territorial claim beyond a treaty line being decided against Israel by an outside body and carried out.

Gaza and Sinai: the part the treaty left out

The treaty separated Gaza from Sinai, and that separation is where Egypt re-enters the Greater-Israel argument. Egypt did not ask for Gaza back, and Israel kept it. Ideas of moving Gazans into Sinai have a documented Israeli history before the treaty. The Egyptian outlet Mada Masr, drawing on the economist Sara Roy, writes that the original Allon Plan envisaged moving large numbers of Palestinians from Gaza to Sinai and the West Bank, that some 12,000 relatives of suspected guerrillas were held in detention camps in Sinai, and that families displaced in 1972 were moved to "Canada Camp" on the Egyptian side of the Rafah line, where some waited about twenty years to return. [cite:gz_mada_sinai] Those were measures of an occupation that then included Sinai; they do not show a claim to Egyptian territory, and with the 1982 return the camp found itself in Egypt. The Gaza Strip's own history under Israeli rule, and the 2005 withdrawal of its settlements, are told in Gaza, Documented. [scene:gz2_egypt|Gaza, Documented: Egypt and Gaza]

What the record shows

The Sinai record cuts against a reading of Greater Israel as a fixed programme pursued by every Israeli government: a Likud government signed away the largest territory Israel has ever held, removed its settlers, and later accepted an arbitration that cost it Taba. It does not settle the question for the land west of the Jordan, which the same government treated as outside the bargain, as Sharon said in 1982, and the treaty itself left Gaza aside. Egypt's position from 1979 has been that the treaty border is final. When Netanyahu's August 2025 remark about the "Greater Israel" vision was reported, Egypt's Foreign Ministry asked for clarification and said there was no route to peace "except through a return to negotiations"; that exchange is in Part Seven.
