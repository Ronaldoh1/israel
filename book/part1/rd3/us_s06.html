No State, No Currency: How the Money Moved

In July 1948 new banknotes reached Israel in secret. They had been printed in New York. It is usually assumed that a new state needs years to build its own financial system. Israel did not. When independence came in 1948, the institutions that would carry its money had been operating for decades, and its first banknotes had already been arranged, quietly, abroad.

Under the Mandate, the country's currency was British in origin and in management. In 1926 the British Colonial Secretary appointed a Palestine Currency Board. From 1 November 1927 it issued the Palestine pound, at par with sterling and divided into 1,000 mils [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. It was a currency managed from London. Its notes stayed in circulation until 18 August 1948 [cite:israelmint_pcb_notes].

The Zionist movement's own money ran through a separate channel, also anchored in London. The Jewish Colonial Trust had been chartered there in 1899, and its operating subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, was founded in 1902. The bank was based in London and owned through the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_israeli_pound] [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. For half a century these two institutions carried the movement's money. They were, in effect, a banking system without a state.

The decisive step came just before independence. Eliezer Hoofien, the Anglo-Palestine Bank's chairman, persuaded the American Bank Note Company in New York to print new notes [cite:israelmint_aplb_series]. Because the names of the state and its currency had not yet been decided, the notes were denominated in "Palestine pounds." According to Israel's state mint, they were deliberately left unmarked in two respects: they did not carry the words "legal tender," and the printer's name was omitted. The purpose, the mint states, was to avoid needing approval from the United States Secretary of State to print foreign currency [cite:israelmint_aplb_series].

After their secret arrival in July 1948, the notes became legal tender by law on 17 August 1948 [cite:jhom_anglo_palestine_notes]. The next day the British-issued Palestine pound left circulation. A currency printed abroad, with neither its printer's name nor its legal status on its face, had become the money of the new state.

The passage from movement bank to national bank was completed three years later. On 1 May 1951 the Anglo-Palestine Bank's assets passed to Bank Leumi le-Israel, and the currency became the Israeli pound [cite:wiki_israeli_pound].

The sequence answers practical questions: what currency the new state used, which banks held its money, and how it got money it could spend. The answer is that the financial system came before the state. A bank whose parent trust was chartered in London in 1899 had operated through the entire Mandate, and when the moment came, it was that bank's chairman who arranged the state's first currency. The same bank would also take part in one of the most contested arrangements of the 1930s, the transfer agreement with Nazi Germany, described in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement].
