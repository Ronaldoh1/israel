Did Money Buy the Politicians?

In mid-1948 Harry Truman's campaign was nearly broke, and the question of what money did to American policy on Palestine has been argued ever since. It is usually answered in one of two ways. Either Zionist money bought Congress and the White House, or money played no part at all. Both answers treat a bundle of separate claims as one. Pulled apart, the claims stand on very different evidence, and one of them cannot be settled either way.

The first claim is that there was organized political pressure. This is documented in detail. The record includes the local committees of the American Zionist Emergency Council, the movement's chief lobbying body in the United States; its letter and telegram campaigns; its book America and Palestine; the 1944 party platforms; and the Wagner–Taft resolution. Much of it survives in the movement's own files. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]

The second claim is that money changed a specific decision. This is documented once. The historian John Judis records that in mid-1948 Truman stopped pressing Israel over territory and refugees in part because his campaign needed $100,000 from Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann. The full case is set out in [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg].

The third claim is that members of Congress were systematically paid before 1948. This cannot be proved or disproved, because the records that would decide it were never required to exist. Until the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, the governing law was the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925. It required disclosure mainly from party committees working across several states, not from candidates. It carried few penalties. It left enforcement to Congress, which rarely enforced it. It was riddled with loopholes, and candidates' donors were largely hidden until 1971. [cite:wiki_fcpa_1925] [cite:sage_fec_history] [cite:crs_campaign_finance] Without disclosure records, any claim that particular legislators were paid is unsupported. So is any claim that they were not.

The fourth point is that other documented forces pushed the same way without money. They include the moral urgency of the Holocaust years; concentrations of Jewish voters in states such as New York; sincere Christian restorationism, the belief that the Jews should be restored to Palestine, going back to 1839; Henry Wallace's personal Zionism; and the 1922 Lodge–Fish resolution, which is credited to lobbying with no payment documented.

The opposing record belongs here too. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense opposed the policy in writing, and they were overruled. The pressures that beat them are documented as political. A financial one is documented only in the single case above.

The result is a finding with a visible gap in it. What can be shown is an organized, well-funded political movement with direct access to decision-makers, and one documented case of money shifting a decision. Wider claims of bought politicians are not in the record. That silence is itself a finding. It is evidence neither of innocence nor of guilt. It reflects a legal regime under which the evidence never had to be kept.
