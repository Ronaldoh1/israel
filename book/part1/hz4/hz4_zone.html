The Security Zone

When Israel pulled most of its army back from central Lebanon in 1985, it did not go home. It kept a self-declared "security zone" along the border, about nine miles deep, which covered roughly a tenth of the country. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] Human Rights Watch put the zone at about 850 square kilometres, with some 150,000 residents in 162 villages and towns. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] The zone lay south of the Litani River, not along or north of it as some later accounts, including this project's own earlier simulation, have it. It lasted fifteen years, from 1985 to May 2000; the phrase "eighteen-year occupation" counts from the 1982 invasion, and "twenty-two years" counts from the 1978 one. [cite:hz4_merip_2000]

Israel did not hold the zone mainly with its own soldiers. Human Rights Watch counted 1,000 to 1,200 Israeli troops in it, alongside 2,500 to 3,000 fighters of the South Lebanon Army (SLA), a Lebanese militia. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] That arrangement is the first documented fact this book sets against the thesis it is testing: that armed groups on Israel's borders serve as reasons for war rather than only as its targets. Whatever one makes of the thesis, the SLA is not an inference. It was a Lebanese armed force that Israel created, paid and supplied.

Who built the SLA. The militia began in the civil war, when Lebanon's army broke apart. Major Saad Haddad, a Greek Catholic officer with about 700 men at Marjayoun, joined other Christian officers and, coordinating with Israel, took control of areas near the border; in April 1979 he proclaimed a "State of Free Lebanon" from Metulla, inside Israel. [cite:hz4_olj_sla] Human Rights Watch reported that Israel helped establish this force in 1976 and later supplied it with advisers and equipment. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] Haddad renamed it the South Lebanon Army in May 1980; after he died of cancer in January 1984, a defected Lebanese army general, Antoine Lahad, took command in April 1984. [cite:hz4_olj_sla]

Who paid for it. The US Congressional Research Service described the SLA as "an allied militia that [Israel] fully funded and equipped." Israel paid its members about $500 to $800 a month, and the annual cost, with maintenance and infrastructure, was estimated at $35 million. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] A former Israeli general, Avraham Tamir, said "huge sums of money were invested in fortifying and equipping the SLA." [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] Its commanders were mostly Christian, but its ranks were not: CRS cited estimates of about 30 percent Shia, 13 percent Druze and 7 percent Sunni, with some observers putting the Shia share as high as half, and L'Orient-Le Jour reported that Shia recruits became the majority partly because the salaries drew men in hard times. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [cite:hz4_olj_sla] Israel's stated position throughout was that the SLA was an ally defending its own area and that Israel's aim was to protect its northern towns. [cite:hz4_jvl_rabin93]

Khiam. At Khiam, a town in the zone, the SLA ran a detention centre from 1985 until 2000. Amnesty International reported in May 1992 that about 200 people were held there with no legal basis, that the International Committee of the Red Cross had never been allowed to visit, and that since 1988 detainees had had no contact with their families; it said the evidence "suggests that Israeli officials have been directly involved" in interrogation and torture, at least until 1988. [cite:hz4_ai_khiam92] Human Rights Watch, which documented torture of detainees held without charge through the 1980s and 1990s, held Israel ultimately responsible as the occupying power. [cite:hz4_hrw_khiam99]

Israel's own account came in a September 1999 affidavit to its High Court. The defence ministry said Khiam was "under the responsibility" of the SLA and that its interrogators and jailers were Lebanese. It also admitted that officers of the Shin Bet, Israel's internal security service, met SLA interrogators several times a year, that they "cooperate with members of the SLA, and even assist them by means of professional guidance and training," and that Israel and the SLA consulted on arrests and releases. [cite:hz4_hrw_khiam99] Shin Bet documents released years later, after a petition by the Israeli lawyer Eitay Mack, include a 1987 report saying the SLA ran the prison with staff trained by the army and the Shin Bet, and that detainees were held without charge or detention orders for indefinite periods. [cite:hz4_mee_khiam] Israel maintains that it did not run Khiam. The record shows it paid the force that did, trained its interrogators and had a say in who was held.

The war between the Shia parties. Inside the community Hezbollah drew on, its first major armed contest was not with Israel but with Amal, the older Shia movement. From April 1988 to November 1990 the two fought in the south, the Bekaa and Beirut's southern suburbs; Amal was backed by Syria and Hezbollah by Iran. In April and May 1988 Amal drove Hezbollah out of most of its southern positions. [cite:hz4_wiki_brothers] A Damascus Agreement signed on 30 January 1989, supervised by Syria's foreign minister Farouk al-Sharaa and Iran's Ali Akbar Velayati, gave Amal responsibility for security in the south and let Hezbollah resume political and social work there. [cite:hz4_wiki_brothers] Casualty estimates for the conflict vary widely and none is authoritative. Fighting continued until late 1990.

Taif: the militia that was not a militia. The Taif Agreement, signed in Saudi Arabia on 22 October 1989 and ratified by Lebanon's parliament on 5 November, ended the civil war. It split parliament equally between Christians and Muslims and required all Lebanese and non-Lebanese militias to be disbanded and their weapons handed to the state within six months; it also called for "all requisite measures to liberate all Lebanese land from Israeli occupation", and the word "resistance" does not appear in it. [cite:hz4a_olj_taif] Parliament ordered the militias dissolved by 30 April 1991. [cite:hz4_wiki_taif] [cite:hz4a_nd_taif] Amal handed its weapons to the Lebanese army and the Druze PSP gave its arms to the Syrian army. "Hezbollah, however, neither surrendered its weapons nor entered any discussions on disarmament." [cite:hz4_lbc_arms] It was allowed to remain active, treated in practice as a "resistance force" against Israel's occupation of the south rather than as a militia. [cite:hz4_wiki_taif] [cite:hz4a_nd_taif] No clause of the agreement grants that status. Hezbollah and its allies argue that the militia clause never applied to it and point to successive governments' ministerial statements endorsing the resistance; its critics call that reading retroactive and say such statements have no binding force. [cite:hz4a_olj_taif] The exemption tied Hezbollah's weapons to Israel's presence. That link is why the withdrawal of 2000, and the argument over what remained occupied afterwards, mattered so much. [scene:hz4_withdrawal|The withdrawal and the Shebaa Farms]

Musawi and Nasrallah. On 16 February 1992 Israeli helicopters fired missiles into the motorcade of Abbas al-Musawi, Hezbollah's secretary-general, killing him, his wife, his five-year-old son and others. [cite:hz4_wiki_musawi] Israel described the strike as retaliation for the capture of Israeli servicemen; later reporting by Ronen Bergman and Dieter Bednarz said the original plan had been to abduct him for a prisoner exchange, and some Israeli officers warned that a more radical successor would follow. [cite:hz4_wiki_musawi] The successor was Hassan Nasrallah, who would lead the organisation into the wars that the rest of this book describes. [cite:hz4_wiki_musawi] A month later, on 17 March 1992, the Israeli embassy in Buenos Aires was bombed, in an attack claimed as revenge for Musawi's killing. [cite:hz4a_infobae_1992] [scene:hz4_abroad|Attacks abroad]

In August 1992, in the first post-war election, Hezbollah entered Lebanon's parliament for the first time, winning what the Christian Science Monitor called a "landslide" in the east. [cite:hz4_csm_1992] From then on it was both an armed force exempted from disarmament and a party in the state, the dual form the rest of this book follows.
