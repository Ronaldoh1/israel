The Transfer Agreement of 1933

On 25 August 1933, Zionist bodies and the Nazi government of Germany signed the Haavara, or Transfer, Agreement. It allowed German Jews who were emigrating to move part of their assets to Palestine in the form of German export goods. It helped roughly 60,000 people reach Palestine. It also undercut the worldwide anti-Nazi boycott, and it divided the Jewish world. It became the single most contested funding mechanism of the pre-state period.

The agreement had three parties: the Zionist Federation of Germany; the Anglo-Palestine Bank, acting for the Jewish Agency; and the Reich Economics Ministry [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. The mechanism was simple in outline. An emigrant deposited assets in an account in Germany. Those funds bought German export goods, which were shipped to Palestine. When the goods were sold there, the emigrant was repaid from the proceeds [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara].

The scale was considerable. The agreement was a major factor in the emigration of approximately 60,000 German Jews to Palestine between 1933 and 1939 [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. By the end of 1937, goods worth 77.8 million Reichsmarks had moved; by the outbreak of war, about 105 million. The Jewish Virtual Library records the total transfer as £8.1 million in Palestine pounds, roughly $40.4 million at the time, including £2.6 million provided by the Reichsbank [cite:jvl_haavara]. The war ended the program.

The Nazi state had its own reasons for signing. It wanted Germany Judenrein, empty of Jews. It also wanted to break the anti-Nazi boycott of 1933, which had mass Jewish support and which German officials saw as an economic threat [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. Haavara served both aims. It moved Jews out of Germany, and it moved German goods abroad in spite of the boycott.

That is why the agreement met opposition, and why some of it came from inside the Zionist movement. The Revisionist leader Ze'ev Jabotinsky, Rabbi Stephen Wise of the American Jewish Congress, and Abba Hillel Silver all argued that it sabotaged the boycott [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. They took their case to the Nineteenth Zionist Congress at Lucerne in August 1935, and they lost. The Congress voted by a large majority to continue the agreement and placed Haavara under the supervision of the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. The historian Edwin Black wrote that the agreement "tore the Jewish world apart."

The familiar versions of this history each keep only half of it. One presents Haavara as pure rescue; the other, as collaboration. The documents support both halves at once. Haavara moved some 60,000 people out of Nazi reach. It was also a commercial accommodation that the Nazi state wanted for its own reasons. And the movement's own Congress chose it knowingly, over the objections of some of its own leaders [cite:jvl_haavara]. The agreement later served as the model for a smaller arrangement with Czechoslovakia on the eve of the war [cite:jvl_haavara].

The deal also shows how institutions built before the state were put to use in the 1930s. The Anglo-Palestine Bank, the movement's London-based bank described in [scene:us_s06|how the money moved], was a party to the agreement on the Jewish Agency's behalf, and the Agency itself took over supervision after Lucerne. The machinery of the state-in-waiting was now handling the consequences of a regime whose legal program was taking shape in Germany at the same time.
