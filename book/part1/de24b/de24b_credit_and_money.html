Credit and Money: The Ledger Behind a Name

The last set of records the state can reach is financial: the credit files that private bureaus keep on almost every adult, the payments the federal government makes and the debts it says it is owed, and the reports banks and money transmitters must file about their customers. Some of these the government holds itself; others it can buy or compel. This section explains how each works, what changed in 2025 and 2026, and what joins are documented. Bank surveillance reports and account closures in both political directions are treated in [scene:de26_money_rails|the section on money rails]; this section adds the credit file, the Treasury's payment and debt machinery, and a reporting order aimed at the southern border.

The credit file

Three private bureaus, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, hold files on most American adults: accounts, balances, payment history, past addresses and employers. The Fair Credit Reporting Act limits who may obtain a full report to those with a "permissible purpose," such as a lender or, with consent, an employer. One section carves out the government: notwithstanding those limits, a bureau "may furnish identifying information respecting any consumer" to a government agency, meaning name, current and former addresses, and current and former places of employment. [cite:de24b_justia_1681f] That top block of a credit file, often called "credit header" data, is also sold onward. Thomson Reuters' people-search service CLEAR, renewed without competition for DHS from 31 March 2026, lists credit-bureau data among its sources, along with utility records and licence-plate reads. [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] The precedent for a bureau's data reaching immigration enforcement is documented: utility account records on more than 170 million people, held by an industry exchange and licensed to Equifax, flowed into CLEAR and were used by ICE until Equifax stopped supplying law enforcement in October 2021 under pressure from Senator Ron Wyden. [cite:de24_globe_utility]

The Treasury as the government's payment hub

Almost every federal payment, from tax refunds to Social Security to contractor invoices, is disbursed by the Treasury's Bureau of the Fiscal Service, which is why access to its systems was among the first disputes over the Department of Government Efficiency in early 2025 ([scene:de19_the_access|Part Nineteen]). On 25 March 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14249, "Protecting America's Bank Account Against Fraud, Waste, and Abuse." It requires payments to pass Treasury "pre-certification verification" before they go out, including checks that payees are not dead, through the Treasury's Do Not Pay system, and tells Treasury to "minimize administrative barriers to accessing and using data," including by waiving the Privacy Act's computer-matching requirements where it can. Agencies had 90 days to change their published records notices to allow disclosure to Treasury. The order excludes data "such as health records." [cite:de24b_eo14249] In August 2025 the budget office told agencies to add those disclosures and offered a four-year waiver of formal matching agreements, while keeping safeguards: a written agreement, a public notice with 30 days for comment, independent checking of any match, and notice to people found, with the right to contest. It also called Do Not Pay "poorly managed over the last four years." [cite:de24b_omb_m2532] On 22 September 2025 Treasury announced that Palantir would build a "common API layer" within the modernisation of the Internal Revenue Service; an API, or application programming interface, is a standard doorway through which one program asks another for data. No value or data scope was published. [cite:de27_treasury_api]

Debts collected from payments

The Treasury Offset Program (TOP) works in the other direction: it takes money owed to the government out of money the government pays. An agency owed a federal debt more than 120 days overdue must refer it; before referral it must send the debtor a letter at least 60 days in advance offering a chance to pay, agree a plan or dispute the debt. When a federal payment is about to go out, the payee's name and taxpayer number are compared with the debtor list, and the payment is cut. [cite:de24b_fs_top] A tax refund can be taken in full; Social Security retirement benefits and federal salaries up to 15 per cent, though disability and Supplemental Security Income payments are excluded. Unpaid child support and some state debts are collected the same way. In the year to September 2020 TOP and the IRS's parallel levy programme collected $10.4 billion. [cite:de24b_crs_top] The largest group exposed in 2026 was student borrowers: 9.3 million were in default on federal loans by 30 June 2026, owing $234 billion. [cite:us17_ci_defaults] The Education Department paused offsets and wage garnishment on 16 January 2026, and as of 19 October 2026 planned to resume garnishment that autumn, with no date set. [cite:ad7_natp_pause] [cite:ad7_ci_garnish] The debts at issue were real and the process had notice; what the machinery shows is how one flag in one database reaches every federal payment to a person.

A border-zone reporting order

Banks and money transmitters must already file a currency transaction report with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), a Treasury bureau, for cash dealings over $10,000. [cite:de26_aba_fincen] A "geographic targeting order" lowers that threshold in named places for a limited time. On 11 March 2025 FinCEN ordered money transmitters, currency exchanges and check cashers in 30 ZIP codes in Texas and California to report cash transactions from $200. Plaintiffs told the courts they lost 50 to 60 per cent of their customers in the one week it applied. On 13 July 2026 the Ninth Circuit upheld an injunction in southern California, holding that the order was likely a rule that needed public notice and comment and that FinCEN had not weighed its costs. [cite:de24b_orrick_gto] FinCEN reissued narrower versions; the order of 2 September 2026 sets a $1,000 threshold in ZIP codes of five Texas and three New Mexico counties, drops Arizona and California, requires identity checks, and runs to 1 March 2027. A Texas court's injunction covers only members of the trade association that sued. [cite:de24b_mofo_gto]

If it were connected

Graded as in [scene:de24b_benefits|the first section of this part]. Credit-bureau identifying data to immigration enforcement: documented, through the statute's government carve-out and through resellers whose products ICE buys. [cite:de24b_justia_1681f] [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] Agency records into Treasury's payment checks: documented as policy, through the order and the August 2025 memo. [cite:de24b_eo14249] [cite:de24b_omb_m2532] Palantir's Treasury API layer joined to its immigration systems at ICE: possible, with no document; the Treasury work's data scope is unpublished, and nothing shows a link. [cite:de27_treasury_api] Debt flags used to stop payments for non-financial reasons, such as a fine for protest conduct: not supported; the offset rules require a legally enforceable debt and prior notice. [cite:de24b_fs_top] Border cash reports into immigration investigations: probable, since DHS personnel already run hundreds of thousands of searches of FinCEN reports a year and the order targeted the border, but no use against a named person is documented. [cite:de26_aba_fincen] What stands in the way is mostly procedural law: the Privacy Act notices the memo still requires, the notice-and-comment rule that sank the first border order, and the offset statute's debt and notice requirements.

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis for this part is that records collected to give people help, schooling, work and credit can be turned into a single instrument of enforcement. Documented: civil records crossed to immigration enforcement in 2025 and 2026, from SNAP files run through SAVE in at least 27 states to a Medicaid file that reached Palantir's software by mistake; school-adjacent cameras answered 620 immigration searches in a month; continuous vetting checks the criminal and financial records of more than 3.8 million people; and clearances were revoked from officials the government accused of politicising intelligence. [cite:de24b_npr_snap27] [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] [cite:de28_74_flock] [cite:de24b_bu_cv] [cite:de24b_kvia_gabbard] Probable: TANF and SNAP records reaching DHS under notices already in force, and border cash reports feeding investigations. Not supported: a single score or file joining benefits, schooling, work and credit for any person, or any of these records used against a critic outside the clearance cases. [cite:de24b_bl_tanf]

The record also runs against the hypothesis in plain ways. Courts blocked the SNAP demand for 22 jurisdictions, paused Medicaid sharing after the overshare, struck down the law-firm orders and enjoined the first border order. [cite:de24b_bl_snapfeb] [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] [cite:de24b_gma_lawfirms] [cite:de24b_orrick_gto] Most of the machinery predates 2025 and was built under both parties: E-Verify in 1996, continuous vetting by 2021, the offset program and eligibility matching well before 2025. [cite:de24b_ev_manual] [cite:de24b_bu_cv] [cite:de24b_gao_21183] What changed is direction: data collected to verify eligibility began to move toward enforcement, and the brakes that held were statutory and specific.
