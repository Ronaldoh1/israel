The Vatican is the second city in this volume, and the hardest to see clearly, because the word "Vatican" names three things at once. It names a church of more than a billion members. It names the Holy See, the government of that church, which sent ambassadors and signed treaties for centuries before it owned a single acre as a modern state. And it names Vatican City, a walled territory of about 44 to 49 hectares that has existed as a state only since 1929. Claims about "the Vatican" and Israel usually slide between the three, and the slide is where most of the confusion starts.

This part keeps them apart. It begins with the machinery: how a treaty with Mussolini turned a papal palace into a state, what Italy still does for that state, how the pope governs it, how the Holy See runs its diplomacy and its budget, and how its money has been handled, from the Rothschild loans of the 1830s to the Vatican Bank's accounts for 2025. The aim is to show, as Part Two did for the City of London, exactly why this place does not work like Rome around it, or like Chicago, Atlanta or Madrid. Then the part turns to the question the volume asks of every city: what did the Vatican's institutions actually do for the Zionist movement and the State of Israel? It reads the theology first, then the record pope by pope from 1904 to 2026, then tests the strongest form of the claim that the Vatican's long refusal was a front. It ends with the verdict.
