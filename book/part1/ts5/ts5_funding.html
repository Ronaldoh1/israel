Who Paid for the Scofield Bible

Preparing the notes took Scofield several years, and friends paid so that he could give his time to the work. [cite:ts5_dunlap] Who did is a question with a documented answer and a widely circulated false one. The false answer, that a Jewish Wall Street lawyer or the Rothschild family financed the book in order to make American Protestants serve Zionism, is repeated in books, articles and viral videos, and until this volume it was printed in this library's own account ([scene:s05|Road to 1948]). This section sets out the documented record first, then traces the false claim to its sources and shows why it fails.

The documented backers

The men who supported the work were named by people who knew Scofield. Arno C. Gaebelein, one of the book's consulting editors, named four in his History of the Scofield Reference Bible (1943), pp. 49–50: Alwyn Ball Jr., John T. Pirie, John B. Buss and Francis E. Fitch; Charles Trumbull's authorised biography of 1920 names Pirie as well. [cite:ts4_sfiles] David Dunlap, writing for a Brethren history site and drawing on Gaebelein and Trumbull, identifies three of them. Pirie was a partner in Carson, Pirie and Scott, the Chicago department store; Ball was a New York real-estate broker; Fitch ran a printing business and published Scofield's correspondence Bible course in its early years. All three, Dunlap writes, "fellowshipped at so-called 'Brethren assemblies'" around New York, the Plymouth Brethren congregations that had grown from Darby's movement. [cite:ts5_dunlap] Buss is listed as of St. Louis. [cite:ts4_goeman]

Scofield himself credited two of them. The historian D. Jean Rushing, in her 2011 thesis on his life, writes that while "others in the conference circuit" contributed, Scofield acknowledged that Ball and Pirie "made it possible" for him to prepare the work. [cite:ts4_cyf] A further gift is recorded by the historian Ernest Sandeen, whose The Roots of Fundamentalism (1970, p. 191) is cited for a $1,000 contribution in 1908 from Lyman Stewart; this figure is reported here from a site that reproduces Sandeen's page and was not checked against the book itself. [cite:ts4_sfiles] Stewart, a co-founder of the Union Oil Company of California, was a prominent lay funder of conservative Protestant causes: in 1908 he co-founded the Bible Institute of Los Angeles, and he and his brother Milton paid $300,000 for the publication of The Fundamentals. [cite:ts5_wiki_lstewart]

The rest of the money came from sales. Oxford University Press published, printed and sold the book as a commercial title. [cite:ts5_dunlap] [cite:ts5_ia_1917] None of the named backers was Jewish, and none had any documented connection to the Rothschilds or to the Zionist movement. [cite:ts4_cyf] [cite:ts4_goeman] They were evangelical businessmen paying for a Bible whose theology they shared.

The Untermyer claim and where it came from

The competing story names Samuel Untermyer, a prominent New York corporate lawyer. Its only documented element is that both men belonged to New York's Lotos Club: Untermyer from 1894, Scofield from 3 March 1900, proposed by Horatio Fraser and seconded by John Elderkin. Part Four sets out the membership record ([scene:ts4_rumours|Part Four]). [cite:ts4_sfiles] The link to Untermyer begins with Joseph M. Canfield's hostile biography, The Incredible Scofield and His Book (1988). According to a reproduction of the pages, Canfield asked why "a godly man" would join "such a godless club", and then named Untermyer as the likely sponsor with the words "we assume" and "There must have been anticipation". [cite:ts4_sfiles] That is an inference, and it concerns club membership, not money.

Later writers turned the inference into a statement of fact. David W. Lutz, in an essay in the 2005 collection Neo-Conned Again, wrote that Untermyer used Scofield "to inject Zionist ideas into American Protestantism" and that wealthy Zionists "promoted and funded" his career; in October 2015 the Washington Report on Middle East Affairs published an article by Maidhc Ó Cathail repeating this, citing Lutz and Canfield without page references. [cite:ts5_ocathail] That article dates Scofield's admission to 1901 and calls Untermyer a member of the admitting committee; the club's book records the entry in 1900 and names an election committee of William Henry White, John Elderkin and William Jay Ives. [cite:ts4_sfiles] Neither Lutz nor Ó Cathail, on the record of their own citations, produced a letter, payment, meeting or document of any kind. [cite:ts5_ocathail]

The Rothschild variant

The stronger claim, that the Rothschilds paid for the Bible, is younger still. The research site Scofield-Files traces it through an internet mailing list (1999) and websites (2001–02) to Michael Collins Piper's The High Priests of War (2004), the first known text to say the Rothschilds funded Scofield, and from there into later books and posts. [cite:ts4_sfiles] Piper wrote for The Spotlight and American Free Press, papers backed by Willis Carto that Wikipedia, summarising the press record, describes as noted for "antisemitic and White separatist" themes; the Anti-Defamation League, the Simon Wiesenthal Center and MEMRI described him as a promoter of antisemitic conspiracy theories and Holocaust denial. [cite:ts5_wiki_piper] The story revived in 2024 after the singer John Rich, on Tucker Carlson's programme, linked Darby to "the Rothschilds", in Mike Rothschild's paraphrase. [cite:ts4_mrothschild] [cite:ts4_goeman] The writer Mike Rothschild, who studies conspiracy theories, reported that he could not find "any primary source that connects the Scofield Bible, Scofield himself, or Darby to the family", and noted that Oxford University Press is owned by the University of Oxford. [cite:ts4_mrothschild] Check Your Fact rated the claim false on 12 March 2025. [cite:ts4_cyf]

Why the claim fails

The claim fails on four separate grounds. First, the documented backers are named by contemporaries, including Scofield himself, and none of them is Untermyer or a Rothschild. [cite:ts4_cyf] [cite:ts4_sfiles] Second, the only Untermyer link is shared membership of a large social club, and Untermyer was neither Scofield's proposer nor his seconder. [cite:ts4_sfiles] Third, the dates do not fit the motive alleged: Untermyer's leading Zionist office, the presidency of Keren Hayesod, began in 1921 according to Scofield-Files, twelve years after the Bible appeared, and in the 1900s Scofield's circle was not pro-Zionist in any political sense; Gaebelein, his consulting editor, called Zionism in 1905 "a movement ... of unbelief" ([scene:ts3_niagara|Part Three]). [cite:ts4_sfiles] [cite:ts3_wiki_gaebelein] Fourth, the theology needed no outside author: it is Darby's system, taught at Niagara from 1878, and the notes themselves place Israel's restoration after Christ's return rather than calling for a Zionist programme ([scene:ts5_notes|the notes]). [cite:ts5_sac_deut30]

The claim's shape also matters, and it should be named plainly. It says that Jewish financiers secretly paid to change what Christians believe in order to serve Jewish political ends. That is one of the oldest antisemitic accusations, and its strongest version here was first put into print by a writer whom the leading monitoring bodies describe as an antisemitic conspiracy theorist. [cite:ts5_wiki_piper] [cite:ts4_mrothschild] That is not the reason it is rejected in this volume: it is rejected because the record contradicts it. It is named because it travels faster than the record, because it was printed in an earlier volume of this library, and because believing it hides the real history. The Scofield Bible was the work of an American evangelical network, paid for by its own businessmen and sold by a commercial publisher to a public that bought it in the millions. That network, not a hidden patron, is what later built the political alliances described in Parts Six and Eight ([scene:ts6_blackstone|Part Six], [scene:ts8_falwell|Part Eight]).
