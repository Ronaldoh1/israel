Founders, the CIA's Fund and a Block of Votes That Cannot Be Outvoted

Palantir Technologies sits at the point where the people of Book Two meet the machinery of Book Five. Peter Thiel co-founded it and chairs it; the Army, the Pentagon's intelligence programmes and Immigration and Customs Enforcement run on its software; and its chief executive has written a book arguing that Silicon Valley owes the nation its engineering. The library has already told the company's early history with American intelligence, from the CIA's venture fund through the NSA's XKEYSCORE to the Army's ten-year deal, in [scene:ow9_origins|The One-Way Mirror], and Thiel's part in founding it in [scene:de9_paypal_palantir_facebook|Part Nine]. This section does not repeat those accounts. It asks two narrower questions: who controls the company, and what, in plain terms, it sells.

The founding, and a year that will not settle

Palantir was founded in 2003 by Thiel with four others: Stephen Cohen, Joe Lonsdale, Alex Karp and Nathan Gettings. Karp, a former Stanford Law School colleague of Thiel's, was hired as chief executive in 2004 and has held the post ever since. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] It was incorporated in May 2003 with about $30 million in initial funding, most of it from Thiel and his fund. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] The name comes from the seeing stones of Tolkien's fiction, which show distant things to whoever holds them; what that name has been made to mean by critics and by the company's admirers is a matter of interpretation, set out under that label in [scene:de39_seeing_stones|Part Forty-Two].

Karp's own background is often misdescribed. He holds a doctorate in social theory from Goethe University in Frankfurt, which is routinely reported as having been written under the philosopher Jürgen Habermas. His supervisor, the sociologist Karola Brede, said in March 2026 that Karp was not Habermas's doctoral student: Habermas had asked her to meet the young American, the two men talked, and Karp drew on Habermas's idea of the "lifeworld," but the thesis, on aggression as a force that holds social groups together, was written "under my supervision." [cite:de27_lgc_brede] The correction matters only because the Habermas line is used, by admirers and critics alike, to make Karp a product of the Frankfurt School; the record shows a doctorate in Frankfurt and conversations with its most famous living thinker, not discipleship.

The CIA connection is real and small. In-Q-Tel, the agency's venture-capital arm, invested about $2 million. Fortune, in a 2016 profile, called it Palantir's "first outside investor." [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015] The year is less certain than it is usually made to sound. One widely used account puts the investment at the company's launch in 2004; other accounts give 2005; the library's [scene:ow9_origins|earlier volume] gives no year, and the date is best stated as 2004 or 2005. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] What the sources do agree on is the order of events: the CIA's fund came first, its money was modest, and until 2010, by Fortune's account, Palantir's "only customers were in intelligence, law enforcement, and defense." [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] The question of whether the agency shaped the product is answered in the library's earlier account: it helped fund and test software for its own analysts' work, and the relationship with the NSA ended in 2015 without a full contract. [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015]

From private start-up to S&P 500

For seventeen years the company stayed private. It moved its headquarters to Denver, Colorado, in August 2020 and listed its shares on the New York Stock Exchange by direct public offering on 30 September 2020, without the usual underwritten sale of new stock. S&P Global announced on 6 September 2024 that it would join the S&P 500 index; in November 2024 it moved its listing to Nasdaq, effective 26 November; and in February 2026 it announced plans to move its headquarters again, to Miami. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] The filing for the 2020 listing showed a company that had lost $580 million the previous year and drew nearly half its revenue from governments. [cite:ow9_ap_s1] By 2026 it was profitable and large, as [scene:de27_money_and_war|the third section] shows.

Who controls it: three classes of share and a voting trust

The most important fact about Palantir's governance is in its own filings, and it is not hidden. The company has three classes of common stock. Class A shares, the ones bought and sold on Nasdaq, carry one vote each; there were about 2.3 billion of them at 30 June 2026. Class B shares, which are not listed, carry ten votes each; there were about 101 million. And there is a small third class, Class F, about one million shares, which are not counted by number at all. [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026] In the company's words, "All shares of Class F common stock are held in a voting trust established by Stephen Cohen, Alexander Karp, and Peter Thiel." [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026]

A voting trust is a legal arrangement in which shareholders hand the voting rights of their shares to a trustee who votes them as the founders direct. The Class F shares are designed to carry whatever number of votes is needed to top up the founders' total. The quarterly report states that the arrangement "generally gives the Founders the ability to control up to 49.999999% of the total voting power" of the company's stock, so long as the three men and their affiliates together hold at least 100 million of the company's equity securities, the threshold that applied at 30 June 2026. [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026]

In plain arithmetic, a block of just under half of all votes cannot be outvoted unless almost every other vote is cast against it, and in practice many shareholders do not vote. The structure gives three men a durable say over the election of directors and over any change to the company's charter, however much of the company's economic value the public comes to own. Dual-class structures of this kind are common among founder-led technology companies, and they are lawful; investors who buy Class A shares are told of the arrangement in the filings. Palantir's version is unusual in one respect: the founders' share of votes is set by formula, up to a fixed ceiling, rather than simply by the number of shares they hold, so long as they stay above the ownership threshold. [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026]

Two points of precision follow. First, Stephen Cohen is the least discussed of the three people who control the company, though his legal position in the trust is the same as Thiel's and Karp's; public argument about Palantir tends to run through Thiel's politics and Karp's speeches, and leaves him out. Second, control of the company is not control of its customers. The voting trust decides who sits on Palantir's board; it does not decide what the Army or ICE does with the software they license, which is set by those agencies' own law, as the next section examines. Thiel's position, stated exactly, is co-founder, chairman, a large shareholder and one of the three founders who established the voting trust; Palantir's contracts are the company's, not his. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026]

What it sells, in plain terms

Palantir sells software, not data. Its products are platforms: programs that let an organisation pull records from many separate systems into one place, match the records that refer to the same person, place or object, and then search, map and analyse the result. [cite:de27_pal_notdata] There are four that matter here. Gotham, released in 2008, is the platform built for defence, intelligence and police customers. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] Foundry is its counterpart for companies and civilian agencies. Apollo is the company's system for delivering and continuously updating its software inside customers' own computer systems. And AIP, the Artificial Intelligence Platform launched in April 2023, lets customers put large language models, the kind of AI behind chatbots, to work on the data already inside Gotham or Foundry. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr]

The company's own description of its role is consistent: it says it does not collect, store or sell personal data, and that it acts for its customers as a "data processor, not a data controller," which in European data law means the party that handles data on another's instructions rather than the party that decides why it is held. [cite:de27_pal_notdata] Whether that description is the whole story, and what follows from software whose value lies in joining records, is the subject of [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|the next section].
