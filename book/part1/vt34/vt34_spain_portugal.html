Three Inquisitions: Spain, Portugal and Rome, 1478–1834

The medieval inquisition of Part Twenty-Eight was a set of papal commissions handed to friars. [scene:vt28_inquisition|Part Twenty-Eight] The early modern world produced something different: three standing tribunals with their own councils, archives, prisons and budgets. Two of them, in Spain and Portugal, were created at the request of kings, staffed by men the kings chose, and used for the kings' purposes as well as the Church's. The third, in Rome, was the pope's own, and it is the one that survives today under another name. Who acted matters to the question this part of the book asks, because the popular phrase "the Inquisition" folds crowns, bishops and popes into a single actor that the documents do not show.

Spain: a papal licence for a royal court

On 1 November 1478 Pope Sixtus IV issued the bull Exigit sincerae devotionis affectus, which allowed Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile to appoint inquisitors in their kingdoms. The first two, the Dominicans Miguel de Morillo and Juan de San Martín, were named on 27 September 1480, and at the first auto de fe, in Seville on 6 February 1481, six people were burned alive. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] In 1483 a Council of the Supreme and General Inquisition, the Suprema, was created, and on 17 October 1483 Tomás de Torquemada became Inquisitor General for all the Spanish kingdoms. Both the Inquisitor General and the members of the Suprema were named by the crown; historians describe the tribunal as an institution at the service of the monarchy. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq]

The papacy did not simply bless what followed. On 18 April 1482 Sixtus IV issued a bull condemning abuses by the inquisitors in Aragon, which the nineteenth-century historian Henry Charles Lea called "the most extraordinary bull in the history of the Inquisition". Ferdinand answered on 13 May 1482 by professing to doubt that it was genuine, and the pope suspended it; the next year he issued the bull naming Torquemada. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] The record shows a pope who protested and then gave way to a king, not a pope directing a king.

Who it was aimed at

Its first target was the conversos, Jews who had been baptised and their descendants, accused of practising Judaism in secret. On 31 March 1492 Ferdinand and Isabella issued the Alhambra Decree, ordering every Jew in their kingdoms to be baptised or leave by 31 July. Estimates of those expelled run from about 40,000 to 100,000; the decree was revoked, symbolically, only on 16 December 1968. [cite:vt34_wiki_alhambra] The decree was a royal act; its effect was to turn every Jew who stayed into a Christian under the tribunal's jurisdiction.

Muslims followed. A decree of 1502 ordered the Muslims of Castile to convert or leave, and the converts, the Moriscos, became the tribunal's second great category. After the Alpujarras revolt of 1568–1571 they were dispersed across Castile, and in 1609 Philip III ordered their expulsion; about 300,000 people left. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] Books went with the people. In 1499 Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, archbishop of Toledo and Isabella's confessor, had the library of the Granada madrasa carried into the Bib-Rambla square and burned in a public bonfire. [cite:vt34_wiki_madrasa] How many volumes burned is not reliably known, and some Nasrid royal manuscripts long assumed lost in that fire survive, because the last sultan took the palace library with him into exile in North Africa. [cite:vt34_cuni_nasrid]

The tribunal's later targets reached the Christian mainstream. Between 1558 and 1562, under Philip II, it broke up small Protestant circles in Valladolid and Seville, about 120 people, and its autos de fe of those years sent about 100 to their deaths. Across the tribunal's history most of those it labelled "Lutheran" were not Protestants at all, but people charged for anticlerical remarks, drunken mockery or irreverence. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] It tried the alumbrados, the "enlightened" contemplatives of Castile who taught that the soul could give itself directly to God, and sentenced them to long imprisonment, though no alumbrado was executed. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] [scene:vt29_mystics|Part Twenty-Nine: the mystics] And it controlled print: Spain issued its own indexes of forbidden books in 1551, 1559, 1583 and later, alongside Rome's. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] [scene:vt30_forbidden_arts|Part Thirty: the Index]

How many died

The numbers have been fought over for two centuries, and the honest answer is a range with names attached. Juan Antonio Llorente, the tribunal's former secretary, claimed in 1817 that 31,912 people had been burned. Modern counts begin with the summaries of cases (relaciones de causas) that regional tribunals sent to the Suprema: from those, Jaime Contreras and Gustav Henningsen counted 44,674 trials between 1540 and 1700, with 826 people executed in person and 778 burned in effigy. [cite:vt34_eretico_victims] Henry Kamen puts total executions at no more than about 3,000, some 2,000 of them in the first forty years; Joseph Pérez gives about 125,000 trials and fewer than 10,000 deaths; William Monter estimated 2,750. A recent synthesis by the reference work Ereticopedia, adjusting for the gaps in the surviving summaries, arrives at roughly 7,300 executions in person between 1480 and 1820, of which about 4,500 are confirmed, most of them before 1540. [cite:vt34_eretico_victims]

A Vatican-sponsored symposium has entered the argument too. When the proceedings of a 1998 Vatican symposium on the Inquisition were presented in June 2004, their editor, Agostino Borromeo of Rome's Sapienza University, said the Spanish tribunal had held about 125,000 trials and that about 1 per cent of defendants were executed, and that in Portugal 5.7 per cent of more than 13,000 people tried in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries were condemned to death. [cite:vt34_icn_borromeo] John Paul II's letter for the same volume repeated the Church's repentance for "intolerance and even the use of violence" in the service of truth. [cite:vt28_va_jp2_2004] The lower figures in this debate are not only the Church's: Kamen and Monter are secular historians working from the same archives. What no serious estimate supports is Llorente's thirty thousand, or the still larger numbers that circulate online.

Portugal: the same model, a later start

Portugal's Jews had been baptised by force. On 5 December 1496 King Manuel I, negotiating a marriage with the daughter of Ferdinand and Isabella, ordered them to convert or leave; in 1497 he stopped them leaving and had them baptised. [cite:vt34_wiki_manuel] Their descendants, the New Christians, were the main target when, after years of royal lobbying by John III, Pope Paul III issued the bull Cum ad nihil magis on 23 May 1536. The grand inquisitor was named by the pope but chosen by the king, usually from the royal family. The first Portuguese auto da fé was held in 1540; tribunals sat in Lisbon, Évora and Coimbra, and from 1560 in Goa. [cite:vt34_wiki_portinq] Fortunato de Almeida counted 31,457 sentences between 1536 and 1794, with 1,183 people executed in person and 663 in effigy; Lea's count is almost the same. [cite:vt34_wiki_portinq] The Marquis of Pombal abolished the purity-of-blood laws against New Christians in 1773–1774 and wrote the tribunal's last procedural rules, and the Constituent Cortes abolished it on 31 March 1821. [cite:vt34_wiki_portinq]

Rome: the pope's own tribunal, still in office

Rome's tribunal had a different founder and a longer life. On 21 July 1542 Paul III's bull Licet ab initio set up a commission of six cardinals, led by Gian Pietro Carafa, later Pope Paul IV, as the Sacred Roman and Universal Inquisition. [cite:vt34_eretico_rominq] [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] The Holy See's own history of the office says it was "initially a tribunal exclusively for cases of heresy and schism". [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] Its courts worked mainly in central and northern Italy; Sicily and Sardinia belonged to the Spanish Inquisition. [cite:vt34_eretico_rominq] Andrea Del Col estimates about 1,250 death sentences among the 51,000 to 75,000 cases judged in Italy after 1542; Giordano Bruno in 1600 and Galileo's trial in 1633 are its best-known cases. [cite:vt34_wiki_rominq] [scene:vt29_bruno_galileo|Part Twenty-Nine: Bruno and Galileo]

Pius X renamed it the Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office on 29 June 1908, and Paul VI renamed it the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith on 7 December 1965, with the stated aim of moving from punishing error to promoting the faith. [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] Since Francis's constitution Praedicate evangelium entered into force on 5 June 2022 it has been the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. [cite:vt34_wiki_rominq] [cite:vt6_vn_pe] The tribunal that judged Bruno is, institutionally, the office that ruled on Freemasonry in 2023. [scene:vt30_rosicrucians_masons|Part Thirty]

The end in Spain

The Spanish tribunal was abolished four times. Napoleon suppressed it in French-held Spain on 4 December 1808; the Cortes of Cádiz abolished it on 22 February 1813; Ferdinand VII restored it in 1814 and abolished it again on 9 March 1820 under a liberal revolt; after 1823 he did not revive it, and bishops set up local Boards of Faith instead. [cite:vt34_wiki_abolition] One of these, at Valencia, condemned the Catalan schoolmaster Cayetano Ripoll for teaching deism to his pupils; he was hanged on 26 July 1826, the last person executed in Spain for heresy. The protest that followed helped end the boards. [cite:vt34_wiki_ripoll] The final abolition came by a decree of 15 July 1834 from the moderate liberal government of Francisco Martínez de la Rosa, signed by the regent María Cristina. [cite:vt34_wiki_abolition] By then the Spanish and Portuguese tribunals had been carried overseas for almost three centuries, and the next two sections follow them there.
