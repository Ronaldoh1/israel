Money: The Fixes on Offer

Any fix for money in American elections starts from a ceiling set by the Supreme Court. In Buckley v. Valeo in 1976 the Court upheld limits on contributions to candidates, disclosure rules and voluntary public financing, but struck down limits on what candidates and independent groups may spend. [cite:dt4_wiki_buckley] In Citizens United in 2010 it struck down, 5–4, the ban on independent spending by corporations and unions, while upholding the disclosure rules by 8–1. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt5_elb_thomas_alone] Two months later a federal appeals court held that contributions to groups that only spend independently cannot be capped, and the super PAC, a committee that may raise and spend unlimited sums so long as it does not coordinate with a candidate or party, followed. [cite:dt4_volokh_speechnow] [cite:pm_wiki_superpac] These are constitutional holdings. Congress cannot reverse them by passing a law. The fixes therefore fall into two groups: those that work inside the ceiling, by adding money, light or friction, and those that would move the ceiling itself.

Matching small donors: New York City

The first group's best-tested example is in New York City. Its Campaign Finance Board matches contributions from city residents of up to $250 at a rate of eight public dollars to one private dollar, and 94 per cent of candidates in the 2021 primary took part. [cite:dt7_nyc_match] The idea is to change whose money matters: a $50 gift from a resident becomes worth more to a candidate than a much larger gift from someone outside the city.

The strongest evidence for the design comes from a study by Michael Malbin and Michael Parrott, published in 2017, which compared New York with Los Angeles. In New York City Council races, small donors per 100,000 residents rose from 176 under a one-to-one match in 1997 to 218 under a six-to-one match in 2009–2013, and their share of all the money candidates raised rose from 45 to 63 per cent. Small donors came from 73 per cent of the city's census block groups, against 43 to 57 per cent in Los Angeles. A comparison with state assembly races covering the same neighbourhoods found the New York gain statistically significant. [cite:dt7_malbin_parrott]

The same study is also the best evidence of the design's limits. Los Angeles adopted matching funds and saw no meaningful gain at first; small donors per 100,000 residents fell from 174 to 161, which the authors traced to its matching rate and residency rules. In both cities the mayoral candidates drew a smaller share of their money from small donors, and from less varied neighbourhoods, than council candidates did, which matters for anyone hoping to scale the model up to Congress or the presidency. [cite:dt7_malbin_parrott] And one way of making public financing competitive is closed: in 2011 the Supreme Court struck down, 5–4, Arizona's rule giving publicly financed candidates extra money when their privately funded opponents or outside groups spent more. [cite:dt4_wiki_bennett]

Vouchers: Seattle

Seattle tried a different design. Since 2017 each resident has received four $25 "democracy vouchers" per election cycle to give to local candidates who agree to fundraising and spending limits, paid for by a property levy of $3 million a year. Use has been modest: about 38,000 people, 6.76 per cent of voting-age residents, gave vouchers in 2019; 48,000 (7.59 per cent) in 2021; 30,649 (4.72 per cent) in 2023. Researchers from Georgetown and Stony Brook found that voucher users still skewed towards higher-income, white and older residents, but were "more representative of all Seattle voters than cash donors." [cite:dt7_cascade_seattle]

The program's critics made their case in public. Ari Hoffman, a former council candidate, called it "inefficient, underutilized, and fails to curb big money's influence," and opponents pointed out that political committees spent more than $2 million on the 2021 mayoral race regardless. [cite:dt7_cascade_seattle] Seattle's voters heard both arguments and renewed the levy, at $4.5 million a year for ten years, by 59.12 to 40.88 per cent on 5 August 2025. [cite:dt7_bp_seattle] [cite:dt7_cascade_seattle]

The national precedent cuts the other way. Public funding for presidential campaigns began in 1976. In June 2008 Barack Obama became the first major-party nominee to decline it entirely, and in 2012 neither he nor Mitt Romney accepted it. The share of taxpayers directing money to the fund on their returns fell from a peak of 28.7 per cent in 1980 to about 6 per cent in 2012, and the Congressional Research Service reported that its spending limits were "increasingly regarded as too low to permit effective campaigning." [cite:dt7_crs_presfund] A voluntary system works only if taking the money is worth the limits that come with it, and unlimited outside spending has made that bargain harder to offer.

Disclosure: the one door the Court left open

Disclosure is the reform the Court has most clearly allowed. "Dark money" is political spending by groups, usually nonprofits, that are not required to name their donors. In 2024 super PACs reported more than $1 billion from shell companies and such groups, and money from anonymous sources had grown from under $71.7 million in 2016 to $653 million in 2020. [cite:dt4_os_2024record] The DISCLOSE Act, which would require groups spending on elections to name their larger donors, failed in the Senate on 22 September 2022 by 49 votes to 49, short of the 60 needed to end debate. [cite:dt7_senate_disclose2022] It was reintroduced on 4 March 2026 with 186 sponsors, all of them Democrats; it would require organisations spending more than $10,000 on elections to disclose donors of more than $10,000 and would bar passing money between groups to hide its source. [cite:dt7_whitehouse_disclose2026]

Disclosure has critics outside the party that opposes it in Congress. In 2021, in Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, the Court ruled 6–3 that California's requirement for charities to file their major donors with the state was not narrowly tailored; Chief Justice Roberts wrote that the state "casts a dragnet for sensitive donor information." The challengers were backed by the ACLU, the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and the Human Rights Campaign, groups whose members have historically had reason to fear exposure. Justice Sotomayor warned in dissent of a "significant risk" that the ruling would "topple disclosure regimes that should be constitutional." [cite:dt7_wiki_afpbonta] The case concerned charity filings, not election spending, but it shows that donor privacy and donor disclosure both have defenders with a record behind them.

The revolving door

Federal lobbying reached a record of about $5 billion in 2025. [cite:us3_os_lobby2025] The main federal rule on officials who move into lobbying, the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007, passed 96–2 in the Senate and 411–8 in the House and extended the period in which former senators may not lobby their old colleagues from one year to two. [cite:ad15_wiki_hloga] The best evidence on what that period protects against comes from a 2012 study in the American Economic Review: lobbyists who had worked for a senator saw their revenue "suffer a 24 percent drop" when that senator left office, an effect that was immediate, lasting and larger for senior senators. [cite:dt7_aer_revolving] The finding supports the case that access is what is being sold. It also suggests the limit of a cooling-off period: it delays the sale of access without ending it.

The Court moves further

The ceiling has not stayed still. On 30 June 2026, in National Republican Senatorial Committee v. FEC, the Court ruled 6–3, in an opinion by Justice Kavanaugh, that federal limits on how much political parties may spend in coordination with their own candidates violate the First Amendment, overruling a 2001 precedent. Justice Kagan dissented, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson. [cite:dt7_pac_nrsc] Lawyers advising campaigns read the ruling as making parties more attractive to large donors, while leaving the rules for super PACs and nonprofits unchanged. [cite:dt7_skadden_nrsc] There are two readings of it. One holds that parties, which have names, platforms and voters, are more accountable channels for money than super PACs; the other holds that the ruling removes one more limit and adds a channel rather than replacing one. Both readings agree on the direction: since 1976 the Court has moved the ceiling up, not down.

Moving the ceiling

Only an amendment or a reversal by the Court itself can change that. An amendment needs two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-quarters of the states, and the Constitution has been amended 27 times, last in 1992. [cite:us2_annenberg_art5] The leading proposal, the Democracy for All Amendment, which would let Congress and the states set limits on election spending, failed a Senate vote to end debate 54–42 on 11 September 2014 and was reintroduced in March 2025 with about 40 Democratic and independent senators. [cite:dt5_senate_sjres19] [cite:dt5_duckworth_dfa] Public opinion is on its side across parties: in 2018 a University of Maryland survey found 66 per cent of Republicans and 85 per cent of Democrats backing an amendment to overturn Citizens United, and in 2023, 72 per cent of Americans told Pew there should be limits on campaign spending. [cite:dt4_time_umd] [cite:dt4_pew_money2023] The case against it is the one the Court made in 1976, that limiting spending "necessarily reduces the quantity of expression." [cite:dt4_wiki_buckley] In the meantime Montana's voters will decide on 3 November 2026 whether to bar corporations, unions and other entities from spending in state and local races; the Montana Chamber of Commerce says the measure "would prohibit Montana businesses and nonprofits from participating." [cite:dt5_sosmt_2026] [cite:dt5_bp_i194]

Set side by side, the money fixes show a pattern. The ones that add money for small donors have measurable effects where they have been tried, and they have been adopted by city voters. The ones that would subtract money from large donors run into the Court, and the Court's direction since 1976, most recently in June 2026, has been to remove limits rather than permit them. How much any of this would change policy depends on how much money changes policy, which [scene:dt4_what_money_buys|Part Four] found disputed.
