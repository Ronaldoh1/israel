The Huleh Crisis, 1951: Drainage, a Demilitarized Zone and Resolution 93

The armistice with Syria, signed in July 1949, had turned the ground Syria evacuated west of the international border into demilitarized zones; how that came about, under a ruler who seized power days before the talks opened, is told in [scene:rd6_syria_1949|Syria in 1949]. The agreement said who could keep troops where. It did not say who owned the zones, who could work their land, or who could decide what was built in them. Those questions came to the Security Council for the first time in the spring of 1951, over a drainage project in the Huleh area. The crisis is worth following closely, because almost every argument that ran along the Syrian front until 1967 was first made, on the record, between March and September 1951.

The work itself had begun earlier. By Israel's account, drainage had been going on since October 1950, with the knowledge of both Syria and the United Nations. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] The contractor named in the Council's later decision was the Palestine Land Development Company. [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_res93] Israel's ambassador to the United Nations, Abba Eban, described the aim of the project in terms of land: it would reclaim, he said, "10,000 acres from desolation and 25,000 acres from disease." [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_eban_huleh] Part of the work ran through the demilitarized zone, and that is where the dispute began.

Between 29 March and 6 April 1951 Syria lodged three complaints with the Security Council (S/2061, S/2065 and S/2074). It charged that Israel was violating the armistice of July 1949 in five ways: by draining and deepening the bed of the Jordan, by occupying the demilitarized zone, by firing on Syrian posts, by forcibly evacuating the zone's Arab inhabitants and demolishing their villages, and by bombing El Hamma and Syrian outposts. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Syria's reasons went beyond the incidents. Drainage, it argued, would give Israel a military advantage, would create more refugees, would require Syria to build new outposts, and would impair irrigation. Because most of the zone had been under Syrian occupation, Syria said, it should have been consulted before any work began. And it presented the Arab landowners in the zone not as bystanders but as people protecting their land from expropriation. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951]

Israel answered in its own complaint (S/2072). The work, it said, had been under way since October 1950 in plain view of Syria and the United Nations; it was lawful; and it needed no Syrian consent, because Israel held sovereignty over the zone. Israel also put forward an incident of its own. On 4 April 1951, it reported, Syrian forces entered El Hamma and attacked an Israeli police patrol, killing seven policemen and seriously wounding three. Israel's air action of 5 April was, in its account, a response to that attack. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951]

The two cases did not meet, because they began from different premises about the zone itself. If the zone was Israeli territory under special military restrictions, as Israel held, then a civilian drainage project inside it was Israel's business, and Syria's objections were interference. If the zone's status had been left open by the armistice, as Syria held, then work that changed the land and moved its inhabitants changed the facts the armistice had frozen, and Syria had a right to be consulted. Each government's complaint was consistent with its premise. The Council's task was to decide what the armistice permitted, and it did so without settling whose premise was correct.

A second front opened while the Council was deliberating. On 7 May 1951 Israel complained (S/2126) of an attack by Syrian irregulars on Tel el Mutilla, inside Israel. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Syria denied that its army had been involved (S/2360). The question of who had fought there was left to a later UN report, taken up below.

On 18 May 1951 the Security Council adopted Resolution 93 at its 547th meeting, by 10 votes to none, with the Soviet Union abstaining. [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_res93] The resolution did three things. It found that "Aerial action taken by the forces of the Government of Israel on 5 April 1951" was a violation of the cease-fire ordered by Resolution 54 of 1948. It endorsed the requests that the Palestine Land Development Company be told "to cease all operations in the demilitarized zone" until agreement was reached. And it decided that Arab civilians who had been removed from the zone "should be permitted to return forthwith to their homes," under the supervision of the Mixed Armistice Commission. [cite:rs_cw1_unispal_res93] The resolution, in other words, censured one act by name, stopped the work pending agreement, and ordered the people who had been moved to be let back.

Eban addressed the Council the same day, and his statement is the strongest form of Israel's case. He argued that the draft "does confer a veto power upon the very interests which are implacably opposed" to drainage: by requiring agreement before work could resume, the Council had handed the decision to the party that wanted the work stopped. He set the project's purpose against that outcome, the reclamation of "10,000 acres from desolation and 25,000 acres from disease." He objected that Israel's action of 5 April was censured while Syrian attacks were not. And he put the strategic stake plainly: whoever holds the Huleh area, he said, "clutches Israel at its throat." [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_eban_huleh] On Eban's reading, Israel was being asked to suspend a lawful civilian project on what it held to be its own territory, while the attacks on its police went uncensured.

Syria's case, stated with equal care, was that the project was not simply civilian and the territory not simply Israel's. Drainage changed the military balance along a line the armistice was meant to hold still; it removed inhabitants who were already at risk of becoming refugees; and it was carried out without consulting the state whose forces had held most of the zone before it was demilitarized. On that reading the Council had not given Syria a veto. It had restored the condition the armistice required: that changes in the zone be agreed, not imposed. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Both cases drew on the same agreement, and both governments read the same silence in it differently.

The question the resolution left open was sovereignty, and the UN's own officer answered it soon afterwards. In a memorandum of June 1951 the UN Chief of Staff concluded that neither Israel nor Syria held sovereignty in the demilitarized zone, and that the work should stop until the two governments agreed. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] That finding rejected Israel's claim of sovereignty outright. It did not accept Syria's either: Syria had not claimed sovereignty in the summary of its case, but a right to be consulted, and the Chief of Staff's conclusion that the work needed agreement was closer to Syria's position than to Israel's on the practical question, while denying both a sovereign title. The same memorandum reported a fact that complicated any compromise. The Arab landowners in the zone, he wrote, rejected every proposal to rent, sell or exchange their land. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Whatever the governments decided, the people who held the land had already said no to every arrangement that would have removed them from it.

Those people are the part of the story most easily lost behind the diplomacy. On 8 July 1951 the Chief of Staff reported that 785 civilians had been evacuated from the zone. The chairman of the Mixed Armistice Commission interviewed 632 of them, and about 260 chose to return. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] The sources describe them as the zone's Arab civilians and inhabitants; they do not record their citizenship, or what became of those who did not go back. The Council had ordered that they be permitted to return. The figures show that fewer than half of those interviewed took up that right, and the record cited here does not say why the others chose as they did.

The Tel el Mutilla complaint was settled on the evidence in September. A UN report of 23 September 1951 (S/2359) stated that the allegation of Syrian army participation in the fighting "could now be considered as having been proved." The proof it cited was Syrian: medal awards published in the Syrian Official Gazette. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Syria had denied army involvement, and its denial stands in the record beside the finding. The episode is one of the few in the crisis where a UN body reached a firm conclusion against a party's own account, and it ran against Syria, just as the resolution of May had run against Israel on the air action of 5 April.

The mechanism the crisis exposed is the one that governed the Syrian front for the rest of the period. The armistice gave the Mixed Armistice Commission the task of supervising the zone; it gave the Security Council the power to censure and to order; and it gave neither the means to enforce. Israel could carry out work on the ground and argue its legality afterwards. Syria could fire on that work and complain to the Council. The UN could find against either, and did against both in 1951, but a finding changed the situation only as far as the two governments chose to let it. The Arab inhabitants of the zone, who held the land and refused to part with it, had no seat in any of these bodies. They appear in the record as complainants through Syria, as a figure in a report and as the subject of an order.

What the record cited here does not settle should be stated plainly. It gives no casualty figure for the Israeli air action of 5 April, and no independent account of the El Hamma clash beyond Israel's own report. It does not show whether the drainage resumed, on what terms, or when. It does not record the citizenship of the evacuated civilians, or why most of those interviewed chose not to return. And it leaves the sovereignty question where the Chief of Staff placed it: Israel continued to assert sovereignty, Syria continued to insist on its say, and the UN's own officer held that neither owned the zone. The eighteen-year struggle that grew out of that unresolved question, over farming and building in the zones, over water and the Jordan's headwaters, is told for 1953–67 in [scene:sy_s02|the demilitarized zones and the water war].
