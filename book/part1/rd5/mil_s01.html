Three Militias Become One Army

On May 14, 1948, the new state of Israel inherited not one army but three armed organizations: the Haganah, the largest pre-state Jewish armed force; and the Irgun and Lehi, two underground militias. Turning them into a single national force took four months. At two points it took force.

The legal step came first. On 26 May 1948, twelve days after the declaration of independence, David Ben-Gurion's provisional government issued an order establishing the Israel Defense Forces. The order outlawed any other armed force and required the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi to merge into the new army. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] On paper, that settled the question. In practice, it set up a test.

The test arrived by sea in June 1948. The Irgun brought an arms ship, the Altalena, to the coast, carrying weapons it meant partly for its own units. Ben-Gurion refused to let the cargo bypass the new army. On 22 June, IDF units under the command of Yitzhak Rabin shelled the ship off the Tel Aviv beach. Sixteen Irgun members and three IDF soldiers were killed. The Irgun's separate units were then disbanded. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] Within a month of the army's creation, the principle was fixed: weapons arriving for the state would pass through the state's own forces. The shelling did not end the political career of the Irgun's commander, Menachem Begin. He became prime minister in 1977.

The second confrontation came in Jerusalem. On 17 September 1948, members of Lehi assassinated the United Nations mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte. The government declared Lehi a terrorist organization and arrested its members. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] Here too the story did not end with the arrests. In February 1949 the detained members were released under a general amnesty. One of Lehi's three leaders, Yitzhak Shamir, became prime minister in 1983. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination]

These events close a line that runs through this book's account of the road to 1948, from the paramilitary groups of the Mandate, Britain's rule over Palestine, to the army of a sovereign state. The pre-state undergrounds had carried out operations described in earlier chapters, among them the attack on Deir Yassin by the Irgun and Lehi with Haganah and Palmach support ([scene:ma_s01|the massacres of 1948]). After independence the question changed. It was no longer what these groups would do against the British or against Arab forces. It was whether the new state would tolerate armed organizations it did not control.

By September 1948 the answer was no. Within four months of its founding, the state had established its monopoly on armed force, by order, by shelling and by dissolution. Yet the sequel ran the other way. Within thirty-five years, the leaders of both absorbed undergrounds had held the office of prime minister. The record of these months is one of absorption rather than simple suppression. The state brought the militias under its control, by force where necessary, and over the following decades brought their leaders into its highest office.
