A Definition in Civil-Rights Law, 2016–2026

The second shield is a definition. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance adopted a "working definition" of antisemitism on 26 May 2016, accompanied by contemporary examples, several of which concern Israel. [cite:ow11_eo13899] Since 2019 American executive orders have directed federal agencies to use it when enforcing the civil-rights law that governs schools and universities, and a bill to write it into statute passed the House in 2024. The dispute over it is not about whether antisemitism should be fought. It is about whether a definition that includes examples about Israel will be used to treat criticism of Israel as discrimination. This section sets out the instruments in order, with the governments' stated purpose and the critics' case side by side.

The definition and its caveat. The definition is non-binding. The Anti-Defamation League, which supports its use, describes it as a guide for education and assessment, not a "charging authority," and says it does not support using it to create new categories of prohibited speech. Its text states that "criticism of Israel similar to that leveled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic." [cite:ow11_adl_ihra] According to the American Jewish Committee, the State Department has used a version of it in its international monitoring since 2006, and as of August 2024, 35 states and the District of Columbia had adopted or recognized it by proclamation, executive order or law. [cite:ow11_ajc_states]

Executive Order 13899, 2019. On 11 December 2019 President Trump signed Executive Order 13899, "Combating Anti-Semitism." It directs agencies enforcing Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which bars discrimination on the basis of race, colour or national origin in federally funded programmes, to "consider" the IHRA definition and its contemporary examples when assessing evidence of discriminatory intent. Title VI does not cover religion, and the order explains that Jews may be protected when discrimination against them is based on race, colour or national origin. It contains its own limit: agencies "shall not diminish or infringe upon" any right protected by the First Amendment, and each case requires a detailed analysis of the allegations. [cite:ow11_eo13899] The order's stated purpose is to enforce Title VI against antisemitic discrimination as vigorously as against other forms. [cite:ow11_eo13899] The critics' concern, set out by the American Civil Liberties Union, is that a definition with examples about Israel would lead universities, which risk federal funds, to suppress speech critical of Israel. [cite:ow2_wiki_aaa]

Executive Order 14188, 2025. On 29 January 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14188, "Additional Measures To Combat Anti-Semitism." It reaffirms the 2019 order, which it says the previous administration had "effectively nullified," and responds to campus protests after 7 October 2023. It requires agencies to report within 60 days on their authorities and on pending complaints, encourages the Attorney General to use federal civil-rights criminal law, and directs the Secretaries of State, Education and Homeland Security to recommend how universities can be familiarized with the immigration law's grounds of inadmissibility so that they can monitor and report foreign students and staff, which could lead to investigation and removal. [cite:ow11_eo14188] The last provision moved the issue from civil-rights enforcement into immigration law, and Part Six returns to it.

The Antisemitism Awareness Act. Congress has twice considered putting the definition into statute. On 1 May 2024 the House passed the Antisemitism Awareness Act, which would require the Education Department to take the IHRA definition into account in Title VI enforcement, by 320 votes to 91. The ADL and the Conference of Presidents of Major American Jewish Organizations supported it; the ACLU called the definition "overbroad" and warned it "could result in colleges and universities suppressing a wide variety of speech critical of Israel"; and opposition also came from some Republicans, among them Marjorie Taylor Greene and Matt Gaetz, who argued it could be used against teachings drawn from the New Testament. [cite:ow2_wiki_aaa] The House bill stalled in the Senate. [cite:ow2_jlink_aaa] Its successor, S.558, came before the Senate committee on health, education, labor and pensions on 29–30 April 2025. Senators offered amendments, including one by Bernie Sanders stating that people may "criticize the government of Israel for their policies without being antisemitic"; the committee's chairman, Bill Cassidy, said "supporting these amendments is an effort to kill this bill," and Rand Paul said "every one of the 11 examples is about speech." No vote was taken, and the bill remained in committee. [cite:ow2_jlink_aaa] The latest report consulted is from July 2025; as of October 2026 this volume has not found a record of a Senate vote, and the status should be checked before it is relied on.

The drafter's objection. The strongest criticism comes from the definition's lead drafter. Kenneth Stern, who drafted it in 2004–05 and directs the Center for the Study of Hate at Bard College, told NPR in March 2025 that the administration was "absolutely weaponizing antisemitism," that the approach amounted to "McCarthyism," and that it made students "less safe," while acknowledging that antisemitism exists on campuses. The White House replied that "the only people weaponizing antisemitism are the liberal activists." [cite:ow11_npr_stern] The ADL's position sits between the two: it supports the definition as a reference but not as a basis for new speech restrictions. [cite:ow11_adl_ihra]

The definition is therefore a shield of a particular kind. It does not protect Israel's government from criticism in law; its own text says such criticism, like that of any country, is not antisemitic. [cite:ow11_adl_ihra] [cite:ow11_eo14188] What the executive orders do is place examples concerning Israel inside the machinery of federal civil-rights enforcement, where the sanction is the loss of federal funds and, since 2025, where immigration status can be at stake. Whether that machinery protects Jewish students from discrimination or chills speech about Israel is the question the courts and Congress have not yet settled.
