How the Quarrel Reached the Bible

The indulgence controversy began as a dispute about a practice. It became a dispute about the canon because both sides went looking for the scriptural ground under the practice, and found that part of it lay in a book whose place in the Bible had been questioned for eleven centuries.

Indulgences for the dead presupposed purgatory, a state of purification after death from which the prayers and offerings of the living could help release a soul. The Catholic Church's own catechism today says that it "formulated her doctrine of faith on Purgatory especially at the Councils of Florence and Trent", and when it gives the scriptural basis for praying for the dead, it quotes the story of Judas Maccabeus, who "made atonement for the dead, that they might be delivered from their sin". [cite:cn_va_ccc1031] The passage is in the Second Book of Maccabees, one of the books that the Greek-speaking churches read and the Hebrew Bible does not contain, and that Jerome, working from the Hebrew in Damasus's commission, had doubted. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero] [cite:vt23_ccel_jerome_solomon]

Luther's revision of the canon proceeded in two stages. In his German New Testament of September 1522 he moved four books, Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation, to the end as disputed, and called the Letter of James "an epistle of straw" that had in his view no evangelical character. [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon] In his complete Bible of 1534 he printed Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, the two books of Maccabees and the additions to Esther and Daniel in a separate section headed "Apocrypha": books "useful and good to read", but not equal to scripture. [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible] The Second Book of Maccabees, with its prayer for the dead, went into that section.

Was Luther's judgment on Maccabees driven by the purgatory quarrel? The documents permit a careful answer, and it cuts both ways. His stated principle was older than the quarrel: he followed Jerome's Hebrew-based list rather than inventing a new one, as Jerome had doubted the same books in the 390s for scholarly reasons. [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon] [cite:vt23_ccel_jerome_solomon] At the same time, the disputed books were not neutral in 1534. One of them was the scriptural foundation most often cited for the practice that had begun the Reformation, and setting it apart removed that foundation from Protestant doctrine. The motive cannot be read from the documents. The effect can. The canon split along a line that ran through the economics of the afterlife.

The split hardened over the next three centuries, and money shaped its final form too. Lutheran confessions never formally defined a canon. The Reformed Westminster Confession of 1646 declared the Apocrypha "not being of divine inspiration" and "no part of the canon of the Scripture". [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon] [cite:vt14_wcf_ch1] What made the slim 66-book Bible the ordinary Protestant edition was a funding decision: in the 1820s the British and Foreign Bible Society stopped paying for Bibles that printed the Apocrypha. [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible] For most English-speaking Protestants today the shape of the Bible on the shelf was settled not by a council but by the budget of a missionary society.

The Protestant side therefore repeated, in its own way, the pattern of the earlier centuries. A list was revised by named men with authority over a community, for reasons partly scholarly and partly bound up with a conflict over money and power, and the revision was then fixed in practice by whoever paid for the printing. The Catholic side's answer, which followed within twelve years of Luther's Bible, was to make its own list a dogma that no Catholic could question. That answer was given by a council convened by a pope whose family is the subject of the next section.
