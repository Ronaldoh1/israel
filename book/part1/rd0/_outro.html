By the eve of the First Zionist Congress in 1897, much was already in place. The hope of return had moved from prayer to program: two rabbis had argued that human settlement could begin redemption, Moses Hess and Leon Pinsker had made it a political proposal, the Lovers of Zion had met at Kattowitz in 1884, BILU had sent its pioneers, and Nathan Birnbaum had named the idea "Zionism" in 1890.

The pressures were documented too: the pogroms after 1881, the May Laws of 1882, and the Dreyfus Affair that convinced Theodor Herzl assimilation could not guarantee safety. Most of those fleeing Russia chose America, not Palestine. Alternatives had been tried at scale, from Baron de Hirsch's Argentine colonies to Edmond de Rothschild's thirty settlements in Palestine, neither of them a statehood project. Opposition from the Bund, Reform Judaism, cultural Zionists and Orthodox rabbis was present from the start.

Around all of this stood older structures: a British state whose Parliament seated the City's Remembrancer, banking houses bound to governments and the papacy by contract, a Protestant restorationist lobby, and a Vatican that would refuse. Political Zionism entered that board. It did not build it.
