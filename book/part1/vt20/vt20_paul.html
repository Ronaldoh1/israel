Paul: Revelation, Tradition and the Quarrel with Jerusalem

Paul of Tarsus wrote the oldest surviving Christian documents, and he never met Yeshua during Yeshua's lifetime. Both facts are in the record, and both matter to Ron's question of how a movement around a Galilean teacher became an institution. This section sets out who Paul was, which letters are his, what he says about the source of his message, whom he actually met, and the one "council" he did attend, a meeting in Jerusalem around 48 to 50 CE, nearly three centuries before Nicaea.

Who he was and what he wrote

Paul was born around the turn of the era in Tarsus in Cilicia, described himself as "a Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee" [cite:vt20_audit_esv_phil3], and by his own account persecuted the early followers of Yeshua before joining them, a turn usually dated to about 31 to 36 CE. He "did not know Jesus during his lifetime", but he knew eyewitnesses, including Peter and James, from the mid-30s onward. Tradition holds that he was executed in Rome under Nero, probably between the fire of July 64 and 68; the Acts of the Apostles ends before his death, and no New Testament text describes it. [cite:vt20_wiki_paul] [cite:vt20_audit_wiki_fire64]

Thirteen letters in the New Testament carry Paul's name. Critical scholars accept seven as his own: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon, written around the middle of the first century. Most regard Ephesians and the three Pastoral letters (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus) as written in his name by later followers, and are divided on Colossians and 2 Thessalonians. The Letter to the Hebrews, traditionally counted as Paul's fourteenth, does not name its author, and most modern scholars do not think Paul wrote it. [cite:vt20_wiki_pauline] The figure "Paul wrote 14 of the 27 books", repeated in some Observe The System material, counts Hebrews; the defensible figures are thirteen letters claiming his name and seven accepted as his. The distinction matters for what follows, because the Pastoral letters are where the New Testament sets out qualifications for "the office of overseer" (in Greek episkopos, the word later rendered "bishop"), and they are among the letters least likely to be Paul's own. [cite:vt20_esv_1tim3]

Revelation, not acquaintance

Paul's own account of his message is in the opening chapters of Galatians. He insists that the gospel he preaches "is not of human origin": "I received it through a revelation of Jesus Christ." After that revelation, he writes, he "did not confer with any human being" and did not go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before him, but went into Arabia and returned to Damascus. [cite:vt20_nrsv_gal12] In 1 Corinthians he lists those to whom the risen Christ appeared, Cephas (Peter), the Twelve, more than five hundred at once, James, all the apostles, and last of all himself. [cite:vt20_esv_1cor15] Paul, in other words, grounds his authority in a vision, not in having followed Yeshua in Galilee.

The same letters also show him receiving and passing on what others taught. 1 Corinthians 15 opens: "I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received." [cite:vt20_esv_1cor15] His account of the Last Supper is introduced, "I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you." [cite:vt20_esv_1cor11] Paul rarely quotes sayings of Yeshua, and when he does he marks them: on divorce he gives a charge as "not I, but the Lord", and a few lines later adds his own ruling, "I, not the Lord". [cite:vt20_esv_1cor7] Scholars debate how to reconcile "not from any human being" in Galatians with "what I also received" in Corinthians. One common answer is that Paul distinguishes the core of his gospel, which he says came by revelation, from traditions about Yeshua's death, resurrection appearances and words, which he learned from others. The record allows that reading; it also leaves Paul as the clearest early example of a teacher whose authority rested on a claimed direct revelation, a point Part Twenty-Four returns to when the same kind of claim is made by teachers the churches later called heretics ([scene:vt24_who_they_were|The Evangelical Gnostics]).

Whom he met

Paul's independence did not mean isolation. "After three years", he writes, he went up to Jerusalem to visit Cephas and stayed with him fifteen days, and saw no other apostle except "James the Lord's brother". [cite:vt20_nrsv_gal12] This is one of the most important sentences in early Christian history: a first-hand statement, from the 50s, by a man who spent two weeks with Yeshua's leading disciple and met Yeshua's brother, the same James whom Josephus records being stoned in Jerusalem decades later ([scene:vt20_yeshua_record|the previous section]). The claim that Paul "never met anyone who knew Jesus" is therefore wrong. The accurate statement is narrower: Paul never met Yeshua before the crucifixion, and he says his gospel did not depend on those who had.

The Jerusalem meeting and the Antioch quarrel

Fourteen years later Paul returned to Jerusalem with Barnabas and Titus, a Greek convert, to lay his gospel before the leaders. He reports that Titus was not compelled to be circumcised; that the leaders "contributed nothing to me"; and that James, Cephas and John, "acknowledged pillars", gave him and Barnabas "the right hand of fellowship", agreeing that Paul would go to the Gentiles and they to the circumcised. They asked only that he "remember the poor", which he was eager to do. [cite:vt20_nrsv_gal12] This meeting is generally dated around 48 to 50 CE, and most scholars identify it with the gathering of "apostles and elders" that Acts 15 describes. In Acts' version the meeting issues a decree that Gentile converts need not keep the law of Moses but should abstain from food offered to idols, from blood, from the meat of strangled animals and from sexual immorality; scholars debate the decree's origin, and Paul's own account does not mention it. [cite:vt20_wiki_jerusalem]

When Ron asks about "Paul and the council deciding what Christianity would be", this is the council Paul took part in. It decided a real and lasting question: that non-Jews could join the movement without becoming Jews. It did not set a creed, a canon or a priesthood, and it was a meeting of a small Jewish sect's leaders, not of bishops summoned by an emperor. Nicaea, the council usually meant by that phrase, met in 325, about two and a half centuries after Paul's death, and is the subject of [scene:vt22_the_room|Part Twenty-Two].

The agreement did not hold smoothly. Paul reports that at Antioch he opposed Cephas "to his face" because Cephas, who had been eating with Gentile believers, withdrew from them after "certain people came from James". Paul's argument there is the core of his theology: a person is justified "not by the works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ". [cite:vt20_nrsv_gal12] The record therefore shows a movement with at least two centres of authority within twenty years of the crucifixion, Jerusalem under James and Paul's Gentile churches, disagreeing openly over who could eat with whom and what the law required. Only Paul's side of the Antioch quarrel survives in his own words.

What Paul did and did not found

Paul is often called the second founder of Christianity, and in one respect the label fits: his letters are the earliest Christian texts and his Gentile mission shaped who the movement's members would be. The record does not support the stronger claim that Paul invented a hierarchy or a system of obligatory payments. His letters address communities meeting in houses, name women and men as fellow workers, and describe his collection for Jerusalem as explicitly voluntary; those details belong to [scene:vt20_first_communities|the next section]. The structures later read back into his name, a single bishop over each city, appear first in other writers, beginning with Ignatius of Antioch, and in the Pastoral letters that most scholars think he did not write.
