The Crown's Tribunal

On 1 November 1478 Pope Sixtus IV issued a bull allowing Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile to appoint inquisitors in their kingdoms. The first two were named in September 1480, and at the first auto de fe, in Seville on 6 February 1481, six people were burned alive. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] In 1483 a royal council, the Suprema, was created and Tomás de Torquemada became Inquisitor General. Both were named by the crown; historians describe the tribunal as an institution at the service of the monarchy. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] The pope protested once. On 18 April 1482 Sixtus IV condemned abuses by the inquisitors in Aragon; Ferdinand replied that he doubted the bull was genuine, and the pope suspended it. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq]

Its first target was the conversos, Jews who had been baptised and their descendants, accused of practising Judaism in secret. On 31 March 1492 the monarchs' Alhambra Decree ordered every Jew in their kingdoms to be baptised or leave; estimates of those expelled run from about 40,000 to 100,000. [cite:vt34_wiki_alhambra] The decree was a royal act, and its effect was to bring every Jew who stayed under the tribunal's jurisdiction. Muslims followed: a decree of 1502 ordered those of Castile to convert or leave, and in 1609 Philip III expelled the converts, the Moriscos; about 300,000 people left. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] In 1499 the archbishop of Toledo had the library of the Granada madrasa burned in a public square. [cite:vt34_wiki_madrasa]

The tribunal reached the Christian mainstream too. It broke up small Protestant circles in Valladolid and Seville between 1558 and 1562, and most of those it labelled "Lutheran" across its history were people charged for anticlerical remarks or irreverence. It tried the alumbrados, contemplatives who taught that the soul could give itself directly to God, and it issued its own indexes of forbidden books. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] Philip II carried it to the Americas: Lima in 1570, Mexico City in 1571, Cartagena in 1610. In 1571 he excluded the Indigenous peoples from its jurisdiction, so its work there fell on Spaniards, people of mixed descent and Africans; in Mexico, one count gives 51 executions over its life. [cite:vt34_wiki_mexinq] [cite:vt34_wiki_peruinq] The most famous book-burning in the Americas, of 27 Maya codices at Maní in 1562, was the work of a Franciscan friar acting on his own authority, not of the tribunal, and other churchmen stopped him. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] The full account is in [scene:vt34_the_americas|The Vatican Architecture: the Americas].

How many died is the question most often asked and most often answered badly. The tribunal's former secretary, Juan Antonio Llorente, claimed in 1817 that 31,912 people had been burned; later counts do not support it. [cite:vt34_eretico_victims]

Most of the deaths came in the first decades, before 1540. [cite:vt34_eretico_victims] The tribunal was abolished four times between 1808 and 1834; after 1823 bishops set up local Boards of Faith in its place, and one of them, at Valencia, condemned the schoolmaster Cayetano Ripoll for teaching deism to his pupils. He was hanged on 26 July 1826, the last person executed in Spain for heresy.
