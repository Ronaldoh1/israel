The Church, the Land and the Revolution

The Ethiopian church was not only the keeper of a Bible. It was one of the great landholders of the empire, and its wealth was bound up with the throne that had granted it. When the throne fell in 1974, the church's land and its independence went with it, and its patriarch was killed. This section follows that last transfer of power over the Ethiopian church, carried out not by a foreign army but by Ethiopia's own revolutionary state.

How much land the church held is disputed, and the dispute is itself revealing. The church traditionally claimed about one-third of Ethiopia's land; the Library of Congress country study judges that its actual ownership "probably never reached this figure", and gives estimates of between ten and twenty per cent of cultivated land. [cite:et_cs_land] [cite:et_wiki_landreform] Land rights in the empire included gult, a right granted by the monarch or provincial rulers that allowed its holder to collect tribute and, until 1966, labour service from the peasants who worked the land. [cite:et_wiki_landreform] Rights of this kind tied income from the land to grants from the throne, so the bond between altar and crown described in Part Two had an economic side as well.

The revolution broke it. Haile Selassie was deposed on 12 September 1974. [cite:et_jstor_rasta] On 4 March 1975 the military government, the Derg, nationalised all rural land without compensation, abolished tenancy and gave each peasant family the right to use a plot of no more than ten hectares. [cite:et_cs_land] The country study is blunt about the consequence: "The Ethiopian Church lost all its land", and its clergy came to depend on stipends from the Derg. [cite:et_cs_land] [cite:et_wiki_landreform] An institution that had kept its books for sixteen centuries, through Jesuits, Fascists and the British Army, now depended for its daily bread on the state.

Control of the church's leadership followed. For sixteen centuries its head had been a bishop sent from Alexandria. That changed only in the twentieth century: an agreement of 1948 led to the consecration of the first Ethiopian bishop as head of the church in 1951, and in 1959 the Coptic pope granted the Ethiopian church its own patriarch, Basilios, installed on 28 June 1959. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] His successor, Theophilos, was enthroned on 9 May 1971. [cite:et_wiki_theophilos]

After the revolution Theophilos consecrated three bishops, among them the future patriarch Paulos, without the government's approval. The Derg seized him; his Wikipedia biography records that he believed "he was entitled to make all decisions concerning the church independent of the authorities". [cite:et_wiki_theophilos] He was held in solitary confinement, escaped briefly in disguise and was recaptured. In 1979 he was taken with other prisoners to a former palace and "strangled by an electrical wire", and buried face down in a trench. [cite:et_wiki_theophilos] His successor, Takla Haymanot, was elected under the Derg's direction. The Coptic Patriarchate refused to recognise the election, and ties between the Coptic and Ethiopian churches were severed. [cite:et_wiki_theophilos]

The Derg's treatment of the church completes a sequence that runs through this book. A Roman emperor tried to choose the bishop of Aksum in the fourth century; a Jesuit patriarch tried to take the church in the seventeenth; a Fascist governor killed its monks in the twentieth; a British museum holds its sacred tablets; and finally its own state took its land and killed its patriarch for consecrating bishops without permission. In every case the struggle was over the same thing: who would control the institution that kept the eighty-one books. The books themselves survived all of it, because the church that kept them, stripped of its land and its leader, kept reading them.
