Skyhawks and Dimona, 1966: Two Tracks Side by Side

In February 1966, the month Damascus changed hands, Washington took its next decision on arms for Israel. By then the Johnson administration was running two policies toward Israel at once, and the record of that year shows them more clearly than any before it. On one track Washington was becoming Israel's supplier of weapons: tanks in 1965, and in February 1966 the first American combat aircraft. On the other it was trying, through visits of American scientists to the reactor at Dimona, to satisfy itself that Israel was not building a nuclear weapon. The two tracks met in the same documents. The conditions attached to the aircraft sale included a nuclear pledge and an inspection clause, and Rusk told Eban in the same year that the nuclear question was a "matter of fundamental significance" to the relationship. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d298] This section follows both tracks through 1966, and corrects several details in the way the story is often told.

When the arms relationship began. A common account holds that American arms sales to Israel began only after 1967, as a reward for the victory of the Six-Day War. The dates do not support it. The Kennedy administration approved the sale of Hawk missiles to Israel in 1962, while it was pressing Israel hard over Dimona: arming Israel conventionally while working to stop it going nuclear. [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] [cite:state_frus1964_68v18] That earlier decision belongs to Stage Four and Stage Five and is weighed with the case for the alliance in [scene:wa_s07|the strongest case for the alliance]. One description of it needs correcting here, because it is often repeated. The Hawk is a surface-to-air missile, an air-defence system for shooting down aircraft; it was not offensive weaponry, and calling it "the first US sale of offensive weaponry" misdescribes it. What the record establishes is the date, five years before the war. The terms of that sale mattered later, because in February 1966 McNamara offered aircraft on "the Hawk terms." [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_feb1966] No source consulted for this section explains, in a single statement of strategy, why the same administration pursued nuclear restraint and conventional supply together; the decisions are documented more fully than any unified rationale connecting them. [cite:state_frus1964_68v18]

Six decisions, 1964–1966. Between Kennedy's death and the spring of 1966 the American position moved, step by step, from refusing to sell tanks to selling aircraft: no direct sale but help in Europe in early 1964, German M48s with American kits in October, tanks for Jordan in February 1965, the memorandum of 10 March, and the direct tank deal of 29 July, when Rusk was still "strong against any US sale" of the 24 planes the memorandum had held out. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185] [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] That sequence is told in full in [scene:cw6_tanks|the first direct tank sale].

This also corrects a second common detail, that "M-48 Patton tanks" were "delivered in 1965." [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] The record is more particular. Forty German M48s had reached Israel when the German route was closed; the American direct sale of 210 M48s was agreed in July 1965 for delivery over two years or more, and the delivery dates themselves are not in the sources consulted for this chapter. [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] The M48s came first through Germany and then by direct American sale.

The White House meeting of 12 February 1966. The aircraft promised in March 1965 were decided eleven months later. On 12 February 1966 Johnson met Rusk, McNamara, Vance, Bell, Hare and Komer on "Israel/Jordan matters." The proposal before them was to sell Israel 48 aircraft centred on the A-4E Skyhawk attack aircraft, and to offer Jordan "36 F–104s or F–5s." Israel had also asked for the A-6, and McNamara ruled it out: it cost about four times as much as the A-4E, it was short because of Vietnam, and Israel would have had only a one-squadron force of it. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d270] Johnson told McNamara to tell the Israelis the decision was final. He proposed trading the numbers of aircraft and the sums of aid for Israeli help in keeping Congress quiet about both sales, Israel's and Jordan's, and said Congress was pressing him; he had already spoken to twelve congressmen, and to Eban. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d270] On one reading, the memorandum shows a president who treated domestic politics as part of the arms decision and expected Israel's government to help manage it.

McNamara and Eban. The same day McNamara put the offer to Israel's foreign minister, Abba Eban. According to the memorandum of conversation drafted by Townsend Hoopes, he offered 24 A-4Es with an option on 24 more, at about $26 million for the first 24, on the Hawk terms: 10% down, 3.5% interest and ten years to pay. [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_feb1966] The offer carried five conditions. Israel was to go on relying mainly on Europe for its aircraft; to reaffirm its undertaking "not to be the first power in the Middle East to manufacture nuclear weapons"; to accept periodic inspection of Dimona; not to use American aircraft as nuclear carriers; and to keep the deal entirely secret until Washington decided how to make it public. [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_feb1966] Three of the five conditions concerned nuclear weapons. In this document the two tracks were not parallel; they were written into one transaction.

The deal Komer reported. Late in February Komer told Johnson that Israel had accepted. The United States would sell "48 A4Es (about $50 million)." Israel would quietly support the sale of 36 second-hand F-104s to Jordan, for cash, at 12 a year from 1968; it would reaffirm its pledge not to go nuclear "unless others do"; and it would stop pressing for more aircraft for several years. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d273] The Jordanian half of the bargain had its own clock. Hussein had asked for a quick answer, saying that otherwise he would have to accept MiGs when Arab leaders met on 14 March 1966. Johnson approved going ahead. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d273] The money moved in two directions at once: on credit to Israel, for cash from Jordan, with each sale made to keep the other side from buying elsewhere, and Soviet aircraft as the alternative Washington was trying to forestall in Amman.

One printed summary of these events is wrong and should not be followed. The State Department historians' summary of the volume says that "agreements were reached for the sale of 48 F–4s to Israel and 36 F–104s to Jordan." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_s] The documents printed in the same volume, the record of the 12 February meeting and Komer's report, and the McNamara–Eban memorandum, all name the A-4E Skyhawk, 48 aircraft. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d270] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d273] [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_feb1966] The summary's "F–4" appears to be an error. The F-4 Phantom was a later decision, approved in 1968, after the war. [cite:jvl_1968_phantom_sale]

The aircraft did not arrive before the war. The first four Skyhawks were unloaded at Haifa port on 29 December 1967, according to the Israeli Air Force as reported by Ynet. [cite:rs_cw6_ynet_skyhawk] Other accounts give their delivery simply as 1968. [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] Either way the point about chronology stands: the sale was decided in February 1966, sixteen months before the June war, though the aircraft flew for Israel only after it.

The inspection track: what "inspection" meant. The Dimona visits that sat beside these sales had begun under Kennedy, and the record of the Johnson years shows what they could and could not see. On 5 December 1963 Israel invited American representatives to visit between 10 and 15 January 1964. In mid-January three American scientists spent one day, about eleven hours, at Dimona, and reported that "all significant facilities at this site were inspected." They judged it "an ambitious project" with a research purpose, and estimated that without outside help Israel could not produce a first device until two or three years after deciding to. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen]

The next visit was negotiated over terms. In October 1964 Eshkol asked to postpone it until after Israel's elections, then set 30 January 1965. On 4 January 1965 he rejected the American conditions of at least two full days, full access and independent measurements, insisting that the team come as "invited guests," not "inspectors." [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] The distinction was the whole question. An inspector has rights; a guest sees what the host shows. The January 1965 team, Ulysses Staebler of the Atomic Energy Commission, Floyd Culler of Oak Ridge and Clyde McClelland of ACDA, reported on 5 February 1965 that "nothing [at the site] suggests an early development of weapons program," and recommended visits "at intervals not to exceed one year." [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen]

Not every American official believed the reports. In January 1965 Henry Kissinger met Israeli officials and scientists, and on 1 February told the American embassy that he had "a strong belief that Israel is already embarked on a nuclear program," naming Shimon Peres as "the strongest champion" of nuclear weapons as an absolute deterrent. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:rs_cw6_cns_duplicity] The embassy's science attaché, Robert Webber, rejected the theory that Dimona was a bluff on 9 April 1965, and on 18 November 1965 said the reactor made "no sense except as the basis for a weapons project." Burr and Cohen call him a rare exception among Johnson's officials. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:rs_cw6_cns_duplicity]

The visit of 1966. A three-man American team visited Israel's atomic sites from 31 March 1966. Its report, forwarded to McNamara by the Director of the Defense Intelligence Agency on 4 May, found "no evidence that Israel is producing or intends to produce nuclear weapons material." Dimona was running at much reduced power, and there was no chemical separation plant there. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d289] The same report recorded what the team could not settle. It could not account for 80 to 100 tons of uranium concentrate that Israel had bought from Argentina; it could not determine whether a small separation plant existed elsewhere; and it judged that a concealed one could escape detection. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d289] The two American documents disagree on the visit's length. The State Department telegram of 11 June 1966 dates it 31 March–1 April 1966; the DIA memorandum of 4 May dates it 31 March–4 April. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d298] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d289] The sources consulted do not resolve the difference, which matters because the length of a visit was exactly what Eshkol and Washington had argued about.

The demand of 11 June 1966. Washington then asked for more. On 11 June 1966 the State Department instructed its embassy to ask for visits every six months, with full access to facilities and records. Its reasoning was arithmetical. A reactor of Dimona's size, run to produce weapons-grade plutonium, could discharge a fuel load about every six months; run for peaceful purposes, about every two years. A visit once a year could therefore miss a weapons cycle entirely. Rusk had told Eban that the issue was a "matter of fundamental significance" to the relationship. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d298] The FRUS editors record that Johnson's wider effort to secure inspection of all Israeli nuclear facilities by the International Atomic Energy Agency failed, and that Rusk warned in 1966 that Israel would "lose U.S. support" if it went nuclear. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_d]

What the pledge said. The comparison shows why the wording has been argued over. In the memorandum of 10 March 1965 Israel reaffirmed that it "will not be the first to introduce nuclear weapons into the Arab-Israel area"; the FRUS editors date that reaffirmation to 11 March 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] Eleven months later McNamara's condition spoke of not being the first power in the Middle East "to manufacture" nuclear weapons, and Komer reported a pledge not to go nuclear "unless others do." [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_t] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d273_t] To "introduce" is not the same act as to "manufacture," and a pledge conditional on others is not the same as an unconditional one. The records do not show whether the American officials who used the different phrases meant them as the same undertaking, or whether the Israelis who accepted each of them understood them alike.

What every team missed. The most important fact about the visits was established only decades later. William Burr and Avner Cohen, publishing declassified records through the National Security Archive in November 2020, write that Dimona's underground reprocessing plant was hidden from every American team, and that none of the nine Atomic Energy Commission teams that visited between 1961 and 1969 knew of it. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:nsarchive_duplicity_1964_65] The National Security Archive's summary adds that CIA reporting was never shared with the inspection teams; that by late 1966 CIA reports claimed a fully assembled bomb was weeks away, a view the State Department did not accept; and that Eshkol refused a nuclear test in order to preserve his understandings with the United States. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_israel_findings] The same summary reports Cohen's finding that Israel had improvised two nuclear devices and placed them on operational alert in late May 1967; that rests on one scholar's research and is reported here as his finding. [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_israel_findings]

This corrects a third detail in the common account. It is often said that there were six American visits to Dimona between 1964 and 1969, increasingly staged, with staged control rooms shown to the visitors, and the 2020 release is cited for it. [cite:nsarchive_duplicity_1964_65] The sources consulted for this chapter do not contain either the count of six or the staged control rooms. Burr and Cohen give nine AEC teams across 1961–1969, not six across 1964–1969, and what they document is concealment, a plant kept hidden and access limited by the host, rather than staging. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] The difference is not trivial. A staged visit implies a performance mounted for the visitors; a concealed plant and an "invited guests" visit imply something the record does support: a host who set the terms of what was seen, and visitors who accepted them.

Were the visits a real check? Two readings of the evidence stand. On the first, the visits did what they were designed to do: the teams of 1964, 1965 and 1966 each reported no evidence of a weapons programme, and the DIA wrote in May 1966 that no convincing intelligence contradicted them. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d289] [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] On this view, Washington had no documentary basis at the time for acting on suspicion against the findings of its own scientists. On the second, the visits were too constrained to check anything: Burr and Cohen document Israeli limits on time and access and the concealment of the reprocessing plant from every team, and Kissinger and Webber judged at the time that a weapons programme was under way. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:rs_cw6_cns_duplicity] The title Burr and Cohen gave their collection, "Duplicity and Self-Deception," names both halves of their reading: concealment by the host, and an American government that did not share its own intelligence with its own inspectors. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_burr_cohen] [cite:rs_cw6_nsa_israel_findings]

A trade, or two policies? By the late 1960s the shape of the relationship was set in a way that lasted: the United States did not force genuine nuclear transparency, and the conventional military and diplomatic relationship deepened. [cite:state_frus1964_68v18] It is tempting to describe this as a bargain, nuclear ambiguity tolerated in exchange for arms. The documents of 1966 come close to it: McNamara's offer made a nuclear pledge and Dimona inspection conditions of the Skyhawk sale. [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_feb1966] But the conditions ran the other way from the bargain the phrase implies. Washington offered aircraft in return for nuclear restraint and inspection, not in return for silence about a bomb, and it pressed for more inspection, not less, four months later. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d298] No document consulted for this book states an explicit policy of tolerating nuclear ambiguity in exchange for the arms relationship. The pattern is observable in the outcomes across the period; it is not proved as a stated trade by any single record. [cite:state_frus1964_68v18] The later consequences, including the American legal mechanisms that were never applied to Israel's arsenal, are told in [scene:cia_s16|the Dimona surveillance loop].

What the record does not settle. Several things remain unsettled by these sources. The dates of the 1966 visit differ between two American documents of the same spring. The delivery dates of the American M48s are not in the record consulted here. The relation between the three wordings of Israel's nuclear pledge, introduce, manufacture, or go nuclear "unless others do," is not explained by any document in which an official sets them side by side. Whether the 80 to 100 tons of Argentine concentrate were ever accounted for is not stated. And the claim that by May 1967 Israel had two improvised devices on alert rests on one scholar's research.
