Defence Spending Over Time, and Who Pays for Iron Dome

Both states spend more on their militaries than most of their peers, but the weight of that spending has moved in opposite directions over four decades. This section sets out the burden of defence on each economy from the 1980s to 2025, using each country's own series and SIPRI's common measure, and then asks a narrower question that is often asked loosely: who pays for Israel's missile defences, Iron Dome above all. The aid itself is set out in Part One and, in more detail, in an earlier volume. [scene:us1_aid|Part One: what the aid is] [scene:gi16_aid|The aid in detail]

Israel: from a fifth of GDP to a twentieth, and back up

In the mid-1980s Israel spent more than 20 per cent of its GDP on defence, with inflation above 400 per cent a year before the 1985 stabilisation plan, which an American grant of $1.5 billion over two years helped carry. [cite:us1_jvl_melnick] The burden then fell steadily. On the total-cost measure used by Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics, which adds costs outside the defence budget, it was 10 per cent of GDP in 1993, 8.2 per cent in 2000, 7.8 per cent in 2005 and 7.0 per cent in 2009. [cite:us1_inss_even2010] By the Bank of Israel's count, defence spending averaged 5.3 per cent of GDP in 2015–2019 and was 4.2 per cent in 2022, its lowest point; then the wars reversed the trend, to 5.2 per cent in 2023, 8.4 per cent in 2024 and 7.8 per cent in 2025. The central bank assumes about 5.5 per cent a year for 2026–2030. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] SIPRI, measuring on its own common definition, put Israel's military spending at $48.3 billion in 2025, 7.8 per cent of GDP and 120 per cent above 2016 in real terms. [cite:us19_sipri_fs2025] The Knesset's 2026 budget gave defence about $44.8 billion. [cite:gi16_bd_budget2026] These series do not use the same definition, and the points are shown separately rather than joined into one line.

The United States: Cold War peaks, a long decline

American defence outlays, on the Office of Management and Budget's measure, were 9.4 per cent of GDP in 1968, at the height of the Vietnam War, 4.9 per cent in 1980, 6.2 per cent in 1986 at the peak of the Reagan build-up, 5.2 per cent in 1990, 3.7 per cent in 1995 and 3.0 per cent in 2000. Defence took 27.6 per cent of federal outlays in 1986 and 16.3 per cent in 2000. [cite:us19_crs_budget2001] On SIPRI's measure the burden was 3.4 per cent of GDP in 2016 and 3.1 per cent in 2025, when American military spending reached $954 billion, a third of the world's total and 11 per cent above 2016 in real terms. [cite:us19_sipri_fs2025] The 2026 war with Iran came on top: Central Command put its cost at $43.6 billion as of 3 September 2026. [cite:ow7_ap_cost2026] That sum, for one American war over about six months, is close to Israel's entire defence budget for 2026, though the two figures count different things. [cite:gi16_bd_budget2026]

Two points follow. The first is that Israel's burden, even at its post-2023 level, is well below what it carried in the mid-1980s, while America's is close to its post-Cold War low of about 3 per cent; the ratio between the two burdens has narrowed from more than three to one in the mid-1980s to about two and a half to one in 2025. The second is the aid's weight within Israel's effort. The regular American grant, about $3 billion a year in 2011 and $3.8 billion under the 2016 agreement, was about a quarter of Israel's security outlays in 2011 by the Israeli economist Gazit's estimate; by 2025–26 the CRS put the military financing at "approximately 14%" of the defence budget and INSS at "roughly 15%". It was "about half a percent" of GDP by INSS's estimate, about 0.6 per cent in 2025 by this book's arithmetic. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] Israel's own taxpayers and borrowing carry the rest; the Bank of Israel counts the war's gross budget cost at about NIS 352 billion for 2023–2026. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] [scene:gi16_costs|What the wars cost]

Iron Dome: the told version and the record

A common claim in American debate holds that the United States pays for Iron Dome. The record is more specific. Israel developed Iron Dome on its own and initially retained the proprietary rights, and it was first fielded in 2011. [cite:us19_crs_md] [cite:ow1_csis_imd] American funding began in fiscal year 2011 with $205 million. By the Congressional Research Service's tables, Congress appropriated $6.08 billion for Iron Dome from 2011 to 2025, $3.83 billion for David's Sling, $3.71 billion for the two Arrow systems since 2006 and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser, $14.8 billion in all for 2006–2025; the Arrow programme alone has received $4.75 billion since 1990. [cite:us19_crs_md] The Missile Defense Agency's obligations, a different measure that runs from earlier years, total $16.1 billion through 2025, of which $5.7 billion came in 2024 alone: the regular $500 million plus the April 2024 supplemental's $4 billion for Iron Dome and David's Sling and $1.2 billion for Iron Beam. [cite:us19_crs_md] [cite:hz12b_hac_onepager]

The 2016 agreement fixes the regular sum at $500 million a year from 2019 to 2028, divided roughly into $300 million for research and development, which Israel matches, and $200 million for procurement and co-production, at least half of which must be spent in the United States. [cite:us19_crs_md] The American share of Iron Dome production was 30 per cent by 2023 testimony, and on 9 October 2026 a Raytheon–Rafael joint venture delivered the first Tamir interceptors built in Camden, Arkansas. [cite:us19_crs_md] [cite:ow8_rtx_camden] The flow also ran the other way: the US Army bought two Iron Dome batteries in 2019, and in October 2023 the administration transferred them back to Israel under an arrangement a defence official described as a lease through a foreign military sale. [cite:ow7_twz_irondome] [cite:us19_crs_md] Congress has backed the programme by wide margins; in July 2025 the House rejected, by 422 votes to 6, an amendment to strip the $500 million. [cite:us1_ji_greene] The industrial side of this partnership is told in Part Twelve. [scene:us12_defence|Part Twelve: defence industry]

The record therefore supports a narrower statement than the common one. The United States has paid for a large share of Iron Dome's batteries and interceptors since 2011, and for just under half of Arrow's development costs, while Israel designed Iron Dome, initially held its technology rights, matches the research money and pays for the rest of its defence. A growing part of the American money is spent in American factories. [cite:us19_crs_md] When the threat came from Iran rather than from rockets, the United States also defended Israel with its own systems and paid for that from its own budget: an estimated 100 to 150 THAAD interceptors in June 2025, which Costs of War priced at $1.8 to 1.95 billion. [cite:ow7_cnn_thaad2025] [cite:hz12_cow_2025] [scene:ow7_iran|Defending Israel, fighting Iran] Both governments face their elections with these sums live: Israel votes on 27 October 2026, the United States on 3 November, and talks on the agreement that follows 2028 are covered in Part Twenty.
