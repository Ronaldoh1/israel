Semitic Is Not a Synonym for Jewish

Much of the modern argument over Israel turns on a single word, and that word has a history of its own. It runs in three stages: a linguist's term from the eighteenth century, a nineteenth-century coinage aimed at Jews, and a twenty-first-century dispute over whether the word should reach political criticism of a state.

The root is Shem, in Hebrew שֵׁם, one of the sons of Noah in the genealogy of Genesis 10. In 1781 the German scholar August Ludwig von Schlözer coined the term "Semitic" (semitisch) for the family of languages that the biblical genealogy traced to Shem. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] It classified languages, not races or religions. It worked the way "Romance languages" does: a family label, named after Rome, that covers French, Spanish and Italian without implying that their speakers are one people. Schlözer grouped Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Ge'ez, Akkadian and Phoenician together. Through most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word kept that classroom sense, alongside "Indo-European" and "Sino-Tibetan." Words change meaning over time; that is normal. What matters is tracking how and when this one changed.

The family today includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Amharic, Tigrinya, Maltese, Ge'ez, Syriac and Mehri, as well as extinct Akkadian. By the word's original meaning, all their speakers are Semitic peoples, among them Arabs, Jews, Ethiopians, Assyrians, Chaldeans and Maltese. This is a textbook definition, not a political claim. Peer-reviewed population genetics points the same way for the Levant, finding substantial shared ancestry among many of the region's populations. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] [cite:haber_2013_bronze]

That applies directly to Palestinians. They speak Arabic, a Semitic language. According to the population genetics literature, including Nebel and colleagues in 2000 and Behar and colleagues in 2010, they descend from populations that have lived in the region continuously since antiquity, overlapping substantially with the ancestral pool of Levantine Jewish populations. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] On linguistic and genetic grounds alike, Palestinians fit the definition of a Semitic people. That fact does not resolve any particular dispute between Israelis and Palestinians. It settles something narrower: the conflict cannot coherently be described as Semites against anti-Semites.

The word "antisemitism" took on its political meaning in 1879. That year Wilhelm Marr, a German journalist and agitator who was a Lutheran, not a Jew, published Der Sieg des Judenthums über das Germanenthum (The Victory of Judaism over Germandom) and founded the League of Antisemites. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] He made "antisemitismus" the name of his movement, a euphemism for Judenhass, Jew-hatred, meant to sound scientific and modern rather than crude and religious. Moshe Zimmermann's 1986 biography, Wilhelm Marr: The Patriarch of Anti-Semitism, documents that the choice was deliberate. The adjective was older. In 1860 the Jewish scholar Moritz Steinschneider had written of "antisemitic prejudices" to attack Ernest Renan's claim that Semitic peoples were inferior. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] Nothing in Marr's writing or his league's activity shows any concern with Arabic speakers or any other Semitic-language population. From Marr onward, antisemitism meant hatred of Jews, and it still does. That settles what the word means. It does not settle how any particular modern claim should be judged.

The familiar story ends with that settled meaning. The documents go on. The contested shift came much later. The working definition of antisemitism adopted in 2016 by the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA) carries 11 attached examples, 7 of which describe criticism of Israel or Zionism as antisemitic under certain conditions. In effect, it turned the word toward a new target: political criticism of a state and a political movement. [cite:ihra_definition] On March 25, 2021, more than 200 scholars, including Omer Bartov, Christopher Browning, Michael Rothberg and Noah Feldman, signed the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism. It kept the core definition but stated that boycott of Israel, criticism of settlements and criticism of Zionism as an ideology are not in themselves antisemitic. [cite:jerusalem_declaration] A popular version of the same move circulates in op-eds and online: the claim that opposing Israel denies Jews their right to national self-determination and is therefore discriminatory. It appears in pro-Israel commentary (a 2019 formulation by Bari Weiss is among the most cited), in advocacy organizations' own materials, and in critiques of them. [cite:currentaffairs_snappy_hasbara]

The Jerusalem Declaration has serious critics of its own. Dr. Dana Barnett of the Begin-Sadat Center for Strategic Studies, and researchers writing for NGO Monitor, have argued that its exceptions could shelter antisemitic framing rather than guard against it. Those exceptions permit comparisons of Israel to settler colonialism or apartheid and treat BDS, the boycott movement, as ordinary non-violent protest. [cite:besa_jda_barnett] [cite:ngomonitor_jda_bogus] Credentialed scholars hold each position in print. Some say the IHRA definition reaches too far into legitimate criticism; others say the Jerusalem Declaration reaches too far in sheltering the real thing. This book does not resolve that dispute.

What the etymology does is clear the ground. Once "Semitic" is understood as a family of languages and their speakers, the conflict appears as what the documents show it to be: a political dispute between a state, a political movement and a displaced population, all of them Semitic in the word's original sense.
