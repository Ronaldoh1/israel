Aliyah Bet: The Clandestine Fleet

When Britain capped legal Jewish immigration to Palestine, the Zionist movement organized its own. Aliyah Bet, "immigration B," was the name for the clandestine immigration of Jews to Palestine in defiance of British limits. In 1938 the Jewish leadership created a body to run it: the Mossad LeAliyah Bet, a branch of the Haganah charged with coordinating the voyages. Revisionist groups ran voyages of their own. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:wiki_aliyah_bet]

Postwar Jewish immigration to Palestine is often pictured as the spontaneous flight of desperate refugees acting alone. The passengers were indeed desperate, and they were survivors with nowhere else to go after the world's governments had closed their doors at Évian. But the movement that carried them was organized, and organized on a large scale.

After the war, with Britain's quota standing at about 18,000 a year, clandestine immigration became the main route into the country. Its overland stage was the Brihah, Hebrew for "flight," a network run with Mossad emissaries, partisans and youth movements. It moved nearly 200,000 survivors out of displaced-persons camps in Germany, Austria and Italy to ports in Italy, France, Yugoslavia and Greece. Inside the camps, Zionist youth movements supplied the passengers. [cite:hsb_aliyah_bet_exodus] [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps]

From those ports the ships sailed. They included a fleet bought and crewed largely by American volunteers. In all, the ships made about 142 voyages. More than 90 percent of them were intercepted by the Royal Navy. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:wiki_aliyah_bet]

Britain needed somewhere to put the people it caught. From August 1946 it interned intercepted immigrants in camps on Cyprus, and by 1948 it was holding about 52,000 people there. The last camp did not close until February 1949. [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps] The camps were meant to deter further voyages. In July 1947 the Exodus 1947, carrying about 4,500 people, was boarded in international waters, and its passengers were sent back not to Cyprus but to camps in Germany. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum describes that decision as an admission that the Cyprus deterrent had failed. [cite:ushmm_cyprus_camps] [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet]

The cumulative numbers are substantial. By 1948 well over 100,000 people had taken this route, more than 70,000 of them Holocaust survivors. Total immigration during the Mandate, legal and illegal together, came to about 480,000. [cite:ushmm_aliyah_bet] [cite:jvl_aliyah_bet]

Both halves of the picture are true at once, and neither cancels the other. The people on the ships were refugees with no other place willing to take them. The operation that moved them functioned like a state in waiting, with its own intelligence service, its own logistics, its own fleet and its own system of recruitment. Every ship the Royal Navy intercepted also became a public argument against the Mandate. The human cost of that confrontation, at sea and in harbor, is the subject of the section on [scene:s07b2|the ships].
