Bringing In the Non-Zionists: The Enlarged Jewish Agency, 1924–1929

American quotas, by one account, turned Jewish emigrants toward Palestine in the mid-1920s. American Jewish money was harder to steer. It is often assumed that American Jewish leadership was Zionist from the start, and that the money which built the Jewish national home flowed from a settled consensus. In the 1920s that was not so. Much of the wealthiest American Jewish leadership was non-Zionist, and a non-Zionist relief body ran its own appeals in competition with the Zionist fund [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. Bringing those leaders and their money inside the movement's institutions took Chaim Weizmann the better part of five years. When it was done, in Zurich in August 1929, the Jewish Agency had become a body in which Zionists and non-Zionists sat in equal numbers, and the institution that would govern the Yishuv until 1948 rested on a funding base that reached well beyond committed Zionists.

The problem first showed itself as a fight over fundraising. The American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, a non-Zionist relief body chaired by the banker Felix Warburg, ran its own campaigns. The Zionist Organization of America saw them as a rival to the Keren Hayesod, the movement's permanent fund, and the two clashed. To protect the Keren Hayesod, the ZOA created a United Palestine Appeal under Rabbi Stephen Wise [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. Two appeals were now asking the same American Jewish public for money, one Zionist and one not.

The stakes were large, because the United States had already become the movement's main source of funds. From April 1921 to December 1929 the Keren Hayesod raised $20.1 million worldwide, about $370 million in today's dollars. More than half of it, $11.0 million, came from the United States [cite:jta_kh_decade] [cite:bls_cpi]. Over the fund's first twelve years, the United States gave about half of the £4.82 million raised [cite:jta_kh_12yr]. The money paid for the core of the movement's work in Palestine: before 1948 the fund's mandate covered immigration, settlement, defense, water and public works [cite:jvl_kh].

The country breakdown shows how lopsided the base was. After the United States came South Africa with $1.5 million, Germany with $0.98 million, Romania and Britain with $0.88 million each, Poland with $0.82 million and Canada with $0.74 million [cite:jta_kh_decade]. No other country gave even a seventh of the American total. And this was the Zionist fund alone; the non-Zionist Joint Distribution Committee under Warburg raised its money separately [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. The longer-run story of who paid for the pre-state enterprise, through 1948, is told in [scene:gf_s01|the Global Funding Ledger].

Weizmann's answer was to stop competing with the non-Zionists and bring them in. From December 1924 to February 1925 he was in the United States, launching the annual Keren Hayesod campaign and negotiating an expanded Jewish Agency with Louis Marshall, president of the American Jewish Committee [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. The offer was institutional: the non-Zionists were to be brought inside the Jewish Agency itself, as partners in its governance, rather than courted as donors to a rival appeal.

The negotiation took more than four years. It ended at Zurich in August 1929. There the Sixteenth Zionist Congress ratified the agreement, and the Council of the enlarged Jewish Agency met, with Albert Einstein, Léon Blum and Herbert Samuel present [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14] [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14_pdf].

The Council's membership was split equally between Zionists and non-Zionists. Louis Marshall became chairman of the Council and Lord Melchett its associate chairman. Felix Warburg, who had chaired the rival relief appeal, became chairman of the Administrative Committee. And the Keren Hayesod was recognized as the Agency's main financial instrument [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14] [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14_pdf]. The man who had chaired the rival relief body now held a chair inside the Agency, beside the Zionist fund.

Weizmann's papers record what he believed he had won. Marshall and Warburg assured him that his "financial troubles are over" [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14] [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14_pdf]. Whether they were is another matter, and the sources here do not show how much non-Zionist money actually flowed through the enlarged Agency in the years that followed.

The partnership lost its chairman almost at once. Marshall died weeks after Zurich, in September 1929. One summary of these events places his death in the same month that the riots in Palestine began; the violence in Jerusalem, Hebron and Safed in fact began in August 1929, weeks before he died, and is told in [scene:s07a4|Hebron, 1929].

The enlargement was not only an internal Jewish matter. The Shaw Commission, sent to investigate the August violence, listed the enlargement of the Jewish Agency among the immediate causes of the outbreak, beside the incidents at the Wall [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d]. The two descriptions of Zurich sit uneasily together. For Weizmann, by his papers' account, it brought the assurance that his financial troubles were over. The Commission, by contrast, placed it beside the Wall incidents among the immediate causes of the violence. The record supports both: it was an act of Jewish communal organization, and a British inquiry judged it to have helped bring on the outbreak of 1929. The sources here do not record the Arab leadership's own statements about Zurich.

The importance of 1929 lies in what the Agency became afterward. By that year the body that would run the pre-state Yishuv had brought wealthy American non-Zionists inside its governance, widening its funding base well beyond committed Zionists, nineteen years before 1948. The record leaves several things open. It does not say how much non-Zionist money the enlarged Agency actually received, or how Warburg's Administrative Committee used it. It does not show how far the non-Zionist half of the Council shaped the Agency's political decisions, as distinct from its finances. And the structure was tested at once: within weeks of Zurich, the Agency faced the crisis of August 1929 and lost the chairman around whom the partnership had been built.
