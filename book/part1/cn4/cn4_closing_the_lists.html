Closing the Lists: Athanasius, the Monks and the Councils of Africa

The first list that matches today's New Testament exactly was written by a man who spent much of his career as a fugitive. Athanasius became patriarch of Alexandria in 328, the head of the Egyptian church and one of the most powerful bishops in the East. He was also banished repeatedly from 336 onwards, as the emperors' favour swung between his Nicene party and its opponents, and after his banishment by Constantius II in 356 he lived for a time hidden in the desert of Upper Egypt. [cite:cn_brit_athanasius] At the time of his death, on 2 May 373, he held his see again. [cite:cn_brit_athanasius] His list was an act of a patriarch who had learned that authority must be asserted constantly or lost.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter to the churches of Egypt announcing the date of Easter. In his letter of 367, the thirty-ninth, Athanasius used the occasion to set down the books of scripture. He named twenty-two books of the Old Testament and then the twenty-seven books of the New, the same twenty-seven found in every major Bible today. "These are fountains of salvation", he wrote. A second group, including the Wisdom of Solomon, the Teaching of the Apostles and the Shepherd of Hermas, was not in the canon but could be read by new converts. The books called apocryphal he dismissed as "an invention of heretics". [cite:vt23_na_festal39] [cite:vt14_ect_festal39]

The letter's audience matters as much as its list. Egypt was full of monasteries, and the monks read widely. Athanasius was telling them, with all the authority of the patriarch, what not to read. The letter warns; it does not order books destroyed, and no record shows that it was followed by a search of monastic libraries. [cite:vt23_na_festal39] [scene:vt14_canon|Athanasius's letter, read closely] But it has been linked, in a debate that is still open, to one of the most famous finds in the history of religion.

In 1945 farmers digging near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a sealed jar holding thirteen leather-bound codices: Coptic translations of Greek texts, including the Gospel of Thomas, the Apocryphon of John and the Gospel of Truth that Irenaeus had attacked. The latest dated scrap of paper used to stiffen the covers is from 348, so the books were made around 350 and buried in the following decades; the circumstances of the burial remain unknown. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi] Hugo Lundhaug and Lance Jenott have argued that the codices were produced and read by Christian monks, most likely of the Pachomian monasteries nearby, citing the scraps in the covers and the notes of the scribes. [cite:vt25_lj2015] Nicola Denzey Lewis and Justine Ariel Blount have argued the opposite: that the scraps have no single monastic source, that Athanasius's letter was aimed at other opponents, the Arians and the Meletians, and that the codices may have been grave goods belonging to a wealthy private owner. [cite:vt25_lewisblount2014] The popular story, that monks buried the books to save them from the patriarch's order, is one hypothesis among several, not a documented fact.

In the Latin West the lists were closed by councils in North Africa. Synods at Hippo in 393 and Carthage in 397 adopted a list of books, and the Code of Canons of the African Church, issued at Carthage in 419, preserves it with a telling final instruction: the list was to be sent to "our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface", the bishop of Rome, and to other bishops, so that they might confirm it. [cite:vt23_na_carthage] The African bishops had done the work. They asked Rome, among others, to agree. Forty years after Irenaeus's line of bishops had become imperial law, the request shows how far Rome's standing as the reference point of the Latin churches had advanced, even where it had not drafted the list itself.

The closing of the lists, then, was not one act but a convergence. A patriarch of Alexandria, writing between exiles, told his monks which books were "fountains of salvation" and which were inventions. A bishop of Rome, installed after a street battle, commissioned the Latin text. African councils drew up the Western list and asked Rome to confirm it. Each step was taken by an office that had acquired, in the previous half-century, wealth, legal power and the emperor's support. The excluded books were not burned by decree. They were left off the lists of the institutions that now controlled the churches, the scriptoria and the schools, and outside those institutions few people had the means to copy them.
