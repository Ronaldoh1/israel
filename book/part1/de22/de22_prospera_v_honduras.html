Próspera against Honduras

Próspera is the one exit project in this book that became a functioning place with residents, businesses and its own rules, and it is also the one that collided most directly with the state around it. It sits on the island of Roatán, off Honduras's Caribbean coast, under the ZEDE law of 2013 described in [scene:de22_seas_and_zones|the previous section]. This section sets out how it is governed, on the evidence of its own charter; what its nearest neighbours said about it; how Honduras repealed the law it was built on and then had its highest court declare that law void; and how the company answered with an investment arbitration claim first priced at more than $10 billion. Each side's stated position is given beside the other's, and the status of every proceeding is given as of October 2026.

The company and the charter

Erick Brimen, an entrepreneur who became its chief executive, formally applied for a charter city in Honduras in 2017; construction of the first buildings began in 2021, and by 2024 Próspera was reported to have about 2,000 residents. [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] Its organiser is Honduras Próspera, a company incorporated in Delaware and funded by venture investors, which reported raising more than $120 million by 2024, including from funds backed by Thiel, Andreessen and Sam Altman. [cite:de22_charter] [cite:de22_chequeado] Among those funds is Pronomos Capital, whose founder says he created it in 2018 "with financing from Peter Thiel"; that places Thiel's money one step removed from Próspera, and no document shows a direct investment by him. [cite:de22_chequeado] On 21 January 2025 Próspera announced a "strategic investment" by Coinbase Ventures, the venture arm of the cryptocurrency exchange, with North Island Ventures and BoostVC; no amount was disclosed. [cite:de22_coinbase]

The zone's founding charter is dated 23 August 2018, and an amended text was published in 2026. [cite:de22_charter] It sets up a nine-member Próspera Council. Residents choose four trustees and the company, as "Promoter and Organizer," chooses four; a technical secretary chairs it. Two of the resident seats are reserved for landowners until the zone reaches urban population density. Ordinary business passes by a majority, but resolutions, prior approval of statutes and regulations, and amendments to the charter itself need two-thirds of the whole council: six votes of nine. [cite:de22_charter] The company's four trustees can therefore block any of those measures on their own. Earlier reporting described the same arrangement as giving the company an effective veto. [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] Residents aged 18 and over vote, one each; "e-residents," who register remotely, do not; and residents gain the power to repeal rules by referendum only once the population passes 10,000. [cite:de22_charter] Amendments also go to the Committee for the Adoption of Best Practices, a body appointed under the Honduran ZEDE law, which approves them unless it objects within 45 days. [cite:de22_charter] [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] Services can be contracted to a "general service provider" for up to 50 years, though not policing or courts, and disputes go to the zone's own court subject to binding private arbitration. [cite:de22_charter] Honduran criminal law continues to apply. [cite:de22_wiki_prospera]

The neighbours: Crawfish Rock

The first part of Próspera was built beside Crawfish Rock, a small community on Roatán. On the evening of 18 September 2020 a meeting between company executives and residents ended in a confrontation with municipal police. The next day Próspera "strongly" rejected accusations of any intent or authority to expropriate land, published a unanimous resolution of its council that the project would "in no case" benefit from expropriation, said all land purchases were voluntary, and said its first 58-acre development lay "far" from Crawfish Rock. [cite:de22_heraldo_2020]

Residents told a different story to reporters. In 2022 Luisa Connor, president of the community association, told The Guardian: "We didn't even know what a Zede was," and "They deceived us big time." The fear was expropriation: drawings of later phases on Próspera's website appeared to include the centre of the village, and the ZEDE law contained a clause allowing land to be taken. Próspera's representatives said they had informed the community in June 2019, citing a resolution signed by 49 residents that described the project as "a real estate and community development project," and promised not to expropriate. [cite:de22_guardian_cr] In 2023 Connor told CorpWatch that "the city will grow like a tumor," and another community leader, Venessa Cárdenas, said residents had a right to be consulted and were not. [cite:de22_corpwatch] Próspera's charter now requires compensation of twice the greater of market value or loss for any taking, and its chief executive backed a national ban on expropriation in July 2023; disputes over groundwater, sewage and shared roads continued to be reported in 2025. [cite:de22_charter] [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] Both accounts are on the record: the company's written commitments, and residents' statements that they agreed to something they did not understand.

The repeal and the ruling

President Xiomara Castro called for the zones to be abolished. [cite:de22_stab_ruling] On 21 April 2022 Congress repealed the ZEDE law unanimously. [cite:de22_cc_repeal] The repeal of the matching constitutional articles needed ratification by a later session, which never came, so the existing zones went on operating legally for more than two years. [cite:de22_cc_ruling] On 20 September 2024 the Supreme Court, in a ruling signed by its fifteen magistrates, declared the whole ZEDE framework unconstitutional from its inception: the 2013 constitutional reforms, the ZEDE law, its investor guarantees and a 2021 resolution on special jurisdiction. [cite:de22_cc_ruling] Castro called the ruling "patriotic, historic, fair, and legal," and wrote that it meant not "selling off our territory piecemeal or privatizing our sovereignty." [cite:de22_cc_ruling] [cite:de22_stab_ruling] The court said it would rule separately on zones already operating. [cite:de22_stab_ruling]

Próspera had warned the day before that a retroactive ruling would "have no legitimacy," and that "there is nothing to gain from such a ruling": it would unsettle investors and workers, harm Honduran banks with loans to the zones and damage relations with the United States. [cite:de22_prospera_stmt] Another zone's secretary called the ruling "abusive." The government's commissioner on the ZEDEs, Fernando García, said it would work with investors who "act in good faith." [cite:de22_cc_ruling] Próspera kept operating; its technical secretary, Jorge Colindres, said in 2026 that the ruling had cost 1,500 jobs and that the zone counted 974 jobs in March 2026. [cite:de22_heraldo_2026]

The dispute was also fought in Washington. The State Department raised concerns in July 2022 that the repeal could breach the Central America free-trade agreement, CAFTA-DR, and a House committee report of 14 June 2024 urged Honduras to let the zones continue. [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] In January 2023 Próspera welcomed a bill from Representative Paul Gosar to widen sanctions for expropriation, with Brimen saying there should be "more serious consequences for expropriation and threats of expropriation." [cite:de22_prospera_gosar] On 12 January 2024 Representative Steven Horsford, a Nevada Democrat, urged in the Congressional Record that visas be denied to Honduran officials who advocated expropriating American investments, citing Próspera and its "50-year legal stability guarantee." [cite:de22_crec_horsford] Bloomberg reported that Brimen had given $5,000 to Horsford's campaign in 2023; that is a sequence on the record, not evidence that the donation caused the statement. [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] On the other side, 32 members of Congress wrote to President Biden that corporations had "weaponized" investor-state dispute settlement. [cite:de22_corpwatch]

The arbitration

Próspera's main answer was a claim against Honduras at the International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID), the World Bank body that hears investor-state cases. The request was filed on 19 December 2022 by Honduras Próspera Inc., St. John's Bay Development Company and Próspera Arbitration Center under CAFTA-DR, and registered as case ARB/23/2. [cite:de22_po6] [cite:de22_italaw] The claim was for up to $10.775 billion if the investment were lost entirely, on the basis of a 50-year legal stability guarantee. [cite:de22_eyn_2022] Reporters put that at about a third of Honduras's economic output; the economy minister, Pedro Barquero, said the country could not pay it without diverting money from development, and Brimen said Honduras had been unwilling to settle. [cite:de22_ct_econ] [cite:de22_eyn_2022] [cite:de22_eyn_2023]

Honduras fought on two fronts. It notified the World Bank on 24 February 2024 that it was leaving the ICSID Convention, with effect from 25 August 2024; a letter from 85 economists praised the withdrawal as a "critical defense of Honduran democracy." [cite:de22_icsid_den] [cite:de22_ct_econ] The withdrawal did not stop the case, which had been filed while Honduras was a member. Inside the case, Honduras challenged the tribunal and lost; on 26 February 2025 the tribunal rejected its preliminary objection, and on 19 March 2026 it refused to split off six further objections, including an essential-security defence and a claim that there was no protected investment, ruling that they would be heard with the merits. [cite:de22_po6] In their memorial of 15 October 2025 the claimants asked for restitution of their assets or, in the alternative, $1.63 billion in damages; the alternative claim is about 85 per cent below the $10.775 billion first claimed. [cite:de22_po6] [cite:de22_eyn_2025] [cite:de23_riotimes_icsid] Honduras's Attorney General's Office attributed the reduction to the state's defence and called the claim "unjust and inadmissible," built on "speculations without legal basis." [cite:de22_eyn_2025]

Castro's successor, President Nasry Asfura, re-signed the ICSID Convention on 6 March 2026; Honduras deposited its ratification on 17 July and rejoined on 16 August 2026, presented as a signal of legal security for investors. [cite:de22_heraldo_2026] [cite:de22_icsid_rat] Honduras then faced fourteen pending ICSID cases. Colindres said Próspera hoped the case could reach "a conciliation." [cite:de22_heraldo_2026] Status as of October 2026: the arbitration is pending on the merits, no hearing date or award has been published, and Próspera continues to operate.

What the case shows

Three points are documented. A private company holds, under its own charter, a blocking vote over the rules of the place where some 2,000 people live, and residents' fuller powers are deferred until the population reaches a size it has not approached. [cite:de22_charter] [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] When an elected government and a unanimous court revoked the law beneath it, the zone did not leave; it relied on rules made by states, a trade treaty and a World Bank tribunal, and on pressure from members of the United States Congress. [cite:de22_po6] [cite:de22_crec_horsford] And the money it asks of Honduras, once the claim was set out in full, became restitution or, in the alternative, $1.63 billion in damages, against the $10.775 billion first claimed; the case was pending on the merits as of October 2026. [cite:de22_po6] [cite:de22_eyn_2025] The texts imagined exit as leaving the state behind. The one working example depends on the oldest instruments of state power, treaties and tribunals, to hold its ground, which is the problem [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight] found in the theory: a charter binds only as far as someone outside it will enforce it.
