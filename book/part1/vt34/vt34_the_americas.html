The Americas: Tribunals, Bishops and the Burning of Books

When people say "the Inquisition burned the Maya books", they compress three different things into one. The Spanish crown did set up tribunals of the Holy Office in its American kingdoms, at Lima, Mexico City and Cartagena. But from the 1570s those tribunals had no jurisdiction over the Indigenous peoples, who made up most of the population. The campaigns that destroyed Indigenous books, images, shrines and ancestral remains were run by friars and bishops, under their own authority, and sometimes against the wishes of other churchmen and of the crown. This section separates the actors, sets out what was destroyed and what was kept, and records the counter-record: that Mesoamerican rulers had burned books before the Spanish came, and that friars and Indigenous scholars together preserved much of what survives.

A burning in Texcoco, 1539, and the exemption that followed

Before any tribunal existed in Mexico, the first bishop, the Franciscan Juan de Zumárraga, held inquisitorial powers himself. In 1539 he tried Don Carlos Ometochtzin, a noble of Texcoco and grandson of the poet-king Nezahualcoyotl, for returning to the old gods and for sexual offences; Don Carlos admitted the sexual charges and denied the religious ones, and he was burned at the stake on 30 November 1539. [cite:vt34_wiki_ometochtzin] Recent scholarship reads the case as much a family and political quarrel as a religious one. Spanish authorities disapproved, Zumárraga was reprimanded, and the case led to inquisitorial jurisdiction over Indigenous people being withdrawn, on the reasoning that they were too recently converted to be judged as Old Christians were. [cite:vt34_wiki_ometochtzin] [cite:vt34_wiki_mexinq]

When Philip II created the American tribunals, he made that exemption law. The Lima tribunal was established on 9 January 1570 and the Mexico City tribunal on 4 November 1571, under Pedro Moya de Contreras; in 1571 Philip II excluded the Indians from the Inquisition's jurisdiction, leaving them, in the Mexican tribunal's case, within its reach only through printed matter in their languages. [cite:vt34_wiki_peruinq] [cite:vt34_wiki_mexinq] [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The tribunals' work fell instead on Spaniards, people of mixed descent and Africans. In Mexico the historian Luis González Obregón counted 51 executions over the tribunal's life; crypto-Jews of Portuguese origin were its most frequent capital victims, among them nine members of the Carvajal family burned on 8 December 1596. [cite:vt34_wiki_mexinq] The Lima tribunal's best-known act was the Auto General de Fe of 1639 against Portuguese New Christians accused of a "great complicity". [cite:vt34_muse_lima] The Cartagena tribunal, established in 1610 for New Granada and the Caribbean, tried 56 people for witchcraft in its first fifty years, 52 of them of African descent; one of them, Paula de Eguiluz, born enslaved in Santo Domingo, was sentenced on 30 November 1624 to 200 lashes and two years' penance in a hospital, and later to perpetual imprisonment. [cite:vt34_utex_cartagena] The Mexican and Peruvian tribunals were abolished by the Cortes in 1813, briefly revived, and gone by 1820. [cite:vt34_wiki_mexinq] [cite:vt34_wiki_peruinq]

Maní, 1562

The most famous burning in the Americas was not an act of the Holy Office. In May 1562 idols and human bones were found in a cave near Maní, in Yucatán. Diego de Landa, head of the Franciscans in the newly created province, began an investigation that, according to Spanish legal records summarised in Archaeology magazine, questioned 6,300 Maya by July, many under torture; more than 4,500 people were punished, at least 157 died, 32 were permanently maimed and dozens took their own lives. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] At the auto de fe on Sunday 12 July 1562, 27 codices were burned, along with 64 effigies and 114 sets of exhumed human remains. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] Landa wrote that the books held "nothing in which there were not to be seen superstition and lies of the devil", and "we burned them all, which they regretted to an amazing degree". [cite:vt34_popar_mani] An excavation in 2015 in front of Maní's town hall found thousands of deliberately smashed incense burners, many made at Mayapán, the first physical evidence of the event. [cite:vt34_arch_mani]

Other churchmen stopped him. Francisco de Toral, the first bishop of Yucatán, arrived that year, and ended the campaign in October 1562. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] Landa was sent back to Spain to answer for running an inquisition he had no right to hold; the Council of the Indies condemned his actions, but a commission absolved him in 1569, and after Toral's death he returned as bishop. [cite:vt34_wiki_landa] In Spain, around 1566, he wrote the Relación de las cosas de Yucatán, which records the Maya calendar, festivals and a key to their script. Its "alphabet" was wrong in its premise, but in the 1950s Yuri Knorozov used it to show that Maya writing was largely syllabic, the breakthrough that opened the inscriptions. [cite:vt34_wiki_landa] The man who burned the books also left the tool that let the stones be read.

More than a hundred codices are thought to have been destroyed across the colonial period, and at most four Maya books survive. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] Three are in Dresden, Madrid and Paris. [cite:vt34_bas_codices] The fourth, long called the Grolier Codex, was declared authentic by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History in 2018, dated to between 1021 and 1154 and renamed the Maya Codex of Mexico; it is an almanac of the movements of Venus. [cite:vt34_wiki_cmm] What survives, then, is astronomy, divination and ritual calendar, the same matters of writing, calendar and religion that Landa himself recorded in his Relación; that is the kind of knowledge the fires consumed. [cite:vt34_arch_mani]

Mexico: what Zumárraga did, and what Itzcoatl did

Zumárraga is often credited with a great bonfire of Aztec books at Texcoco. The evidence is thin. In 1881 the Mexican historian Joaquín García Icazbalceta argued that most destruction came before Zumárraga arrived, that no Spanish chronicler mentions his burning books, and that the one burning described by the Texcocan historian Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxochitl was carried out by Tlaxcalan allies of the Spanish in 1520. [cite:vt34_wiki_icazbalceta] Icazbalceta wrote as a defender; the charge remains unproven rather than refuted, and the destruction of temple archives during and after the conquest is not in doubt.

The Mexica themselves had destroyed books. Sahagún's Nahua informants recorded that Itzcoatl, ruler of Tenochtitlan from 1427 to 1440, had the historical paintings burned, saying it was "not wise that all the people should know the paintings", so that a new history centred on Huitzilopochtli could replace them. [cite:vt34_wiki_itzcoatl] Control of the record was a practice of Mesoamerican states before it was a Spanish one.

The largest single preservation also came from a friar. From 1559 the Franciscan Bernardino de Sahagún gathered testimony from elders at Tepepulco and Tlatelolco, working with Nahua scholars, Antonio Valeriano, Martín Jacobita, Alonso Vegerano, Pedro de San Buenaventura and others; the result, completed between 1575 and 1577, is the twelve-book General History of the Things of New Spain, the Florentine Codex: 2,446 pages and 2,472 illustrations, in Nahuatl and Spanish, covering the gods, the calendar, divination, omens, rhetoric, the heavens, medicine, plants and the conquest from the Mexica side. [cite:vt34_whe_florentine] In 1577 Philip II ordered such manuscripts on Indigenous religion and history confiscated; the codex reached Florence, and is in the Laurentian Library. [cite:vt34_whe_florentine] The crown that wanted the knowledge collected also wanted it out of circulation.

Peru: the extirpation of idolatries

In the Andes the knowledge was kept in knotted cords, the quipus, and in sacred places and ancestors. The Third Council of Lima (1582–1583), under Archbishop Toribio de Mogrovejo, ordered bishops to destroy completely the quipus that recorded non-Christian rites, calling them "pernicious instruments", while encouraging Christians to use quipus for confession and memorising doctrine. [cite:vt34_wiki_lima3] The council also issued a catechism in Spanish, Quechua and Aymara. [cite:vt34_wiki_lima3] The order targeted religious content, not the quipu as such, and what the destroyed cords recorded is not known.

From 1609 the archdiocese of Lima ran formal campaigns, the "extirpation of idolatries", in three waves, 1609–1621, 1625–1626 and 1646–1670, with occasional cases into the mid-1700s. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] They began with the priest Francisco de Ávila's trials in Huarochirí; the Jesuit Pablo José de Arriaga's manual, La extirpación de la idolatría del Pirú, was printed at Lima in 1621 [cite:vt34_wiki_arriaga]; Archbishop Pedro de Villagómez directed the last and largest wave. Visitors questioned villages, punished native priests with whipping, exile and, in some cases, death, and burned the huacas and the mummified ancestors, the mallquis, that families kept and honoured. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The historian Kenneth Mills called it "perhaps the most sustained religious persecution" of Indigenous people in colonial Spanish America; Nicholas Griffiths, noting how irregularly the campaigns came, reads them as "aberrations, not a commonplace phenomenon" of colonial rule. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The same campaign produced its own record: the Huarochirí Manuscript, compiled under Ávila and used in his prosecutions, preserves the beliefs it was meant to help suppress, and Arriaga's manual is itself an eyewitness description of the rites it set out to end. [cite:vt34_wiki_arriaga] [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The suppression of the Andean sun cult, the Coricancha and the Taki Onqoy movement belongs to the next part. What the record shows

The documented findings are these. Friars and bishops, not the Holy Office, destroyed Indigenous books, images and shrines in New Spain and Peru, by their own authority, often with torture, and church councils ordered religious quipus destroyed. The crown kept the Inquisition away from Indigenous people after 1571 and also confiscated the ethnographies its friars produced. Other churchmen, Toral above all, resisted the worst of it. And Indigenous people kept their practices alive, in hidden shrines, in households and inside Catholic forms; Mills's work shows Andean religion adapting rather than disappearing. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] Part Thirty-Five follows that adaptation in the cult of Guadalupe. [scene:vt36_guadalupe_tonantzin|Part Thirty-Five]

Interpretation, not finding: inner knowledge in the burned books

The Sophia platform reads the codices, quipus and ancestor shrines as carriers of an inner knowledge as well as an outer one: calendars that tuned human life to the sky, divinatory counts that were also disciplines of attention, and rites of ascent and return comparable to the mystery schools of [scene:vt33_what_was_lost|Part Thirty-Two]. In this reading, the friars' bonfires removed a technology of consciousness, not only a body of records. The surviving books support the first half: they are astronomical and ritual. They do not by themselves show a doctrine of consciousness, and the surviving Nahua and Maya testimony comes to us filtered through the friars who collected it. The strongest counter-reading is that of the communities themselves, many of whom kept, adapted and still practise these calendars and rites, so that what was lost was a library, not the knowledge's last carriers.
