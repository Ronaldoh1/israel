Paris, 1919: A Conditional Signature and a Shelved Report

Faisal carried his case to Paris, where in 1919 the powers that had won the war met to settle the peace, and the future of the former Ottoman provinces was among the questions before them. For Palestine, two records from that year cut against the story usually told about it. One was a document whose condition, by two accounts, did not reach the delegates. The other was a report that reached them and was set aside. Together they bear on a question that is still argued: did the governments that decided Palestine's future know what its inhabitants wanted?

The common answer is that they did, and that the inhabitants, through their leaders, agreed. At the peace conference, it is said, the Arabs accepted a Jewish homeland, and the powers acted on what the people of the country wanted. The documents of 1919 make that answer hard to sustain in either half.

The first record is the agreement signed in London on 3 January 1919 by Emir Faisal and Chaim Weizmann, in which Faisal accepted Jewish immigration into Palestine and the Balfour Declaration. Its articles, its history and the long argument over what it meant are told in full in [scene:rd2_faisal|the section on the Faisal–Weizmann Agreement]. What matters for the conference is a single feature of the text. Beside his signature Faisal added a condition in Arabic: he would concur only if the Arabs obtained their independence as he had demanded in his memorandum. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text] According to two accounts, the Zionist Organization submitted the agreement to the peace conference without that caveat. [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] [cite:israeled_faisal_weizmann] When the independence Faisal had named did not come, the condition failed. For decades the document would be cited as evidence of Arab consent. The consent it recorded was conditional, and by these accounts the condition was missing from the copy the conference received.

Palestinian Arabs were not only spoken for in Paris; they also spoke. The First Palestine Arab Congress met in Jerusalem in January 1919, timed to coincide with the conference. [cite:rs_mca_ifpo] According to the Jewish Virtual Library, it opened on 27 January and cabled the conference to oppose the Balfour Declaration. [cite:rs_mca_jvl_congress] That movement and its demands are the subject of [scene:pp_s01|the section on the Palestinian national movement].

The second record came from the American delegation. President Wilson sent a commission of his own, the King–Crane Commission, to find out what the people of the region wanted. It toured from June to July 1919. In that time it received about 1,875 petitions, 72 percent of them hostile to the Zionist plan. [cite:britannica_king_crane] [cite:jewishmag_king_crane] Its findings on Palestine were blunt. The non-Jewish population, the commission reported, made up nearly nine-tenths of the whole and was "emphatically against the entire Zionist program." It recommended the program's "serious modification." [cite:britannica_king_crane] [cite:jewishmag_king_crane]

The commission's work matters because of what it was. It was not a petition from one side or a protest from a congress. It was the attempt of one of the victorious powers, acting through its own appointees, to measure opinion in the territory being disposed of. The League of Nations Covenant, drafted that same year, made the wishes of such communities a principal consideration in the selection of the Mandatory, a story told in [scene:blg_s01|the section on Article 22 and the mandate system].

The report reached the peace conference in August 1919 and was shelved. [cite:wiki_king_crane] [cite:palquest_king_crane] It was not published for more than three years. Its first appearance in print came in December 1922, in Editor & Publisher, by which time Britain and France had established their mandates. [cite:wiki_king_crane] [cite:palquest_king_crane] Wilson himself may never have seen it. [cite:wiki_king_crane]

The sequence is the point. The opinion of the population was measured in the summer of 1919. The decisions about the territory were taken in 1920 and 1922. The measurement was made public only after the decisions were final.

Who shelved the report, and why, is the question the record leaves open. Some members of the commission believed that the State Department and Zionists were responsible for suppressing it. [cite:wiki_king_crane] [cite:palquest_king_crane] That attribution is theirs, and the sources gathered for this book do not establish who made the decision to hold it back or on what grounds. What they do establish is narrower and firmer: the report existed in August 1919, it said what it said, and it was not public until December 1922.

Two cautions keep this account honest. The first is that the commission's findings were a record of opinion, not a ruling; it recommended modification of the Zionist program, and the powers were free to disagree, as they did. The second is that the shelving of a report is not by itself proof of a plan to suppress it. A report can be set aside for many reasons, and the documents cited here do not say which applied. The record here shows the outcome, not the motive.

Still, the familiar claim that the powers acted on what the inhabitants wanted does not survive the documents. The Arab consent most often cited from 1919 was given on a condition, and by the accounts cited here the condition was missing from the copy the conference saw. The population's opinion was measured by an American commission, and the measurement was kept out of public view until the decisions it might have affected had been made. Whether the governments in Paris knew what the people of Palestine wanted is not, on this evidence, an open question. It has a documented answer from 1919. What the record does not settle is why that answer was set aside.
