The Uncomfortable Record: Lehi's Offer to Germany and the Killing of Lord Moyne

The familiar account of the war years holds that every Zionist faction stood with the Allies against Nazism and set itself against terrorism. For the movement's militant fringe, the documents tell a more uncomfortable story, and two episodes in particular are often left out of the telling. Both belong to Lehi, the underground that Avraham Stern led out of the Irgun so that it could keep fighting Britain.

The first episode survives on paper. A document dated 11 January 1941, found after the war in the files of the German embassy in Ankara, records an offer from Lehi to Nazi Germany. It was carried by Naftali Lubenchik to the German diplomat Werner Otto von Hentig in Beirut, then under Vichy control. In it Lehi proposed to "actively take part in the war on Germany's side" in exchange for German support for a Jewish state [cite:academickids_lehi] [cite:marxists_ankara_document]. What the German files do not contain is equally important. There is no record of any German response. Nothing in the surviving record shows that Berlin ever took it up.

The second episode took place at the end of the war, and it had consequences. On 6 November 1944 two Lehi members, Eliyahu Hakim and Eliyahu Bet-Zuri, shot Lord Moyne outside his residence in Cairo. Moyne was Britain's Minister Resident in the Middle East and a close friend of Winston Churchill. Lehi held him responsible for turning back refugee ships; what the immigration cap meant at sea is traced in [scene:s07b2|the ships]. Hakim and Bet-Zuri were hanged in March 1945 [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:jewishpress_moyne].

Churchill's papers, held at the Churchill Archives Centre, show the effect. When a request came for a greeting to Chaim Weizmann on his seventieth birthday, Churchill wrote "no message" on it. He also warned against pressure from Zionists and American Jews for clemency for the two assassins [cite:churchill_archives_moyne].

The mainstream movement's response was unambiguous. The Zionist Executive and the mainstream youth movements denounced the Irgun and Lehi as "traitors," and the Haganah had already acted against the underground groups before the assassination [cite:daat_moyne]. On this point the familiar account holds: the movement's leadership did condemn the underground at the time.

The record does not end there, however. Yitzhak Shamir, one of Lehi's leaders, later became prime minister of Israel, and he had Hakim and Bet-Zuri reburied on Mount Herzl, in the national cemetery [cite:daat_moyne] [cite:executedtoday_moyne]. Both halves of this history are documented. The movement's leadership condemned the fringe at the time. The state it founded later raised one of that fringe's leaders to its highest office and gave the two assassins a place in its national burial ground. A history that keeps only the first half flatters the record; one that keeps only the second distorts it. The documents require both.
