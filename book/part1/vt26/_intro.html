Part Twenty-Six leaves the Nag Hammadi jar for three texts that are often wrongly placed in it. The Gospel of Mary came to Berlin in 1896 in a codex bought in Cairo; the Gospel of Judas surfaced in Middle Egypt in the 1970s and was published only in 2006; Pistis Sophia has sat in a London library since 1785. Each is laid out in the same way: the manuscript and its date, the text's outline in order, its teaching in plain words with a few short quotations, the points where it cuts against the emerging church (a woman teaching the apostles and Peter rebuked for doubting her; the twelve portrayed as priests sacrificing their own followers; Wisdom's fall and return, with Mary again out-speaking Peter), and what bishops, popes and councils actually said or did about it, with dates. The record that results is uneven, and the part says so: one of the three texts was named and condemned by a bishop around 180, the other two by no surviving authority at all, and the institution's most consequential act concerned not a text but the reputation of Mary Magdalene.
