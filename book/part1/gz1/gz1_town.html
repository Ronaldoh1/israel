A Town on the Coast Road

Before there was a Gaza Strip there was Gaza, a town and the district named after it, and the strip that bears its name today is the small part of that district the Egyptian army still held when the war of 1948 ended. To understand what was lost and what remained, this section sets out the town and its district as they stood under the Ottoman Empire and the British Mandate: their place on the map, their people, and the small Jewish presence the record shows among them.

An Ottoman district on a frontier. From the sixteenth century Gaza was part of the Ottoman Empire. [cite:gz_wiki_history] When the Ottomans reorganized southern Palestine in 1872 they made Jerusalem an independent district, a mutasarrifate, and Gaza became one of its districts, alongside Jaffa and Hebron. [cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] Gaza's position on the coast road between Egypt and Syria made it a frontier town, and in 1906 the Ottoman and British empires fixed the boundary between Ottoman territory and British-controlled Egypt. [cite:gz_wiki_history] The treaty of 1979 confirmed that line between Egypt and former Mandate Palestine as Egypt's permanent border. [cite:gz_treaty1979]

The battles of 1917. The First World War brought the front to Gaza's edge. The British Egyptian Expeditionary Force attacked the Ottoman defences there in March and again in April 1917 and was defeated both times. It broke through further east at Beersheba at the end of October, fought the Third Battle of Gaza on the night of 1 to 2 November, and occupied the town on the morning of 7 November, after the Ottoman garrison had withdrawn in the night. Gaza had had about 40,000 inhabitants before the war; the British bombardment that preceded its capture severely damaged or destroyed most of its housing. [cite:gz_wiki_gaza3] Five days earlier, in London, the British government had issued the Balfour Declaration, as [scene:dv2_parent_test|the volume on the three parents] sets out. From 1920 to 1948 Britain governed Gaza as part of Mandatory Palestine. [cite:gz_wiki_history]

The people of the district. The Mandate government's last official population estimates, the Village Statistics of 1945, shows the Gaza subdistrict with 137,180 people: 133,040 Muslims, 2,890 Jews and 1,250 Christians. The town of Gaza itself had 34,250 inhabitants, of whom 33,160 were Muslims, 1,010 Christians and 80 Jews. [cite:gz_wiki_subdistrict] The subdistrict stretched well north and east of the present strip; it included the town of al-Majdal, beside the site of ancient Ashkelon, [cite:gz_oup_morris_majdal] and dozens of villages on the coastal plain whose fate in 1948 is told in the next section. [cite:gz_wiki_subdistrict]

Gaza's Jews. A small Jewish community had lived in Gaza at many points in its history. In its last period it was renewed in the second half of the nineteenth century, mostly by Sephardi traders, and had close to a hundred members before the First World War, when the Ottomans expelled most Jews of foreign nationality and the community effectively lapsed. Jews returned slowly after the British conquest; in 1926 the community's secretary counted 51. In the riots of 1929, Gaza's Jews barricaded themselves in a hotel near the police station and were then taken, with the help of some local Arabs and the British police, to the railway station and by train to Lod. The National Library of Israel calls it "the end of the Jewish community in Gaza." [cite:gz_nli_jews] The 1945 estimates nonetheless list 80 Jews in the town; the sources consulted do not say who they were. [cite:gz_wiki_subdistrict]

Kfar Darom. One Jewish settlement stood inside what became the strip. On 6 October 1946 a group of new immigrants set out from Be'erot Yitzhak to found Kfar Darom, south of Gaza town, on land that had been a Jewish-owned orchard since 1930 and had been destroyed during the Arab revolt of 1936 to 1939. [cite:gz_wiki_kfardarom] It was part of the Jewish Agency's plan of eleven new outposts in the Negev. [cite:gz_wiki_history] Its history is short and returns twice in this volume: it was besieged and abandoned in 1948, and rebuilt as an Israeli settlement after 1967, only to be evacuated again in 2005. [cite:gz_wiki_kfardarom]

The picture this record gives is of a district with an overwhelmingly Arab population, a historic town at its centre, and a small Jewish presence, a few dozen people in the town and one outpost inside the later strip. Within two years almost all of that would change: most of the district would be inside Israel, most of its villages would be empty, and the remaining sliver around Gaza town would hold more than three times as many people as before.
