What Gnosis Meant

The Greek word gnōsis means knowledge, but not knowledge of facts. It is the knowledge one has of a person, or of oneself: acquaintance, recognition, insight. The teachers whom later writers called Gnostics claimed that salvation came through this kind of knowing. A person was saved not by holding the right beliefs about God, or by performing the right works, but by recognising where he or she had come from and where he or she was going. The Gospel of Truth, a sermon from the school of Valentinus written in Greek between about 140 and 180, pictures ignorance as a nightmare and knowledge as waking from it; the truth is a "living book" written in the heart, and those who know come to rest in the Father. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth]

The idea set the Gnostics against the developing church in a way that went beyond any single doctrine. Irenaeus of Lyon, the most detailed of their opponents, reported that the Valentinians divided humanity into three kinds. The material people were bound to perish. The "animal" or psychic people, the ordinary members of the church, could be saved by faith and good works. The spiritual people, the Valentinians themselves, would be saved "because they are spiritual by nature", and the teachers called the members of the bishops' churches "animal". [cite:vt24_ah1_06] Whether or not Irenaeus reported the scheme fairly, his alarm is understandable. If the most advanced Christians needed knowledge rather than obedience, then the bishops' authority reached only the second rank of believers.

The rites reflected the same idea. Irenaeus describes ceremonies called "redemption" that varied from group to group: a spiritual marriage, a baptism in the name of the unknown Father, an anointing with oil and water. Some anointed the dying so that their souls would be "incapable of being seized or seen by the principalities and powers", and taught them words to say on the way up, beginning "I am a son from the Father". Others rejected rites altogether and held that knowledge itself was the redemption. [cite:vt24_ah1_21] The Gospel of Philip, found at Nag Hammadi, lists five mysteries, "a baptism and a chrism and a eucharist and a redemption and a bridal chamber", and states the goal in five words: "You saw Christ, you became Christ." [cite:vt24_gphil]

Elaine Pagels, whose book The Gnostic Gospels won the National Book Award in 1980, drew a political conclusion from this contrast. The orthodox doctrine of bodily resurrection, she argued, "serves an essential political function": it legitimised the authority of the men who claimed to succeed the apostles who had seen the risen Christ. Gnosis, in her phrase, was "the theological justification for refusing to obey the bishops". [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Her thesis was widely read and widely criticised. Henry Chadwick found it lacking in "full rigour", and other scholars objected that she read modern politics into ancient theology. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] The argument this book makes does not depend on her reading. It rests on what both sides said: that the Gnostic teachers claimed a knowledge that did not pass through the bishops' office, and that the bishops regarded that claim as a threat.

The Gnostics did not, for the most part, reject the church outright. Many attended its services, read its scriptures and regarded themselves as its most advanced members. That was precisely the problem. A rival church can be named and excluded. A teaching that claims to stand above the ordinary church while living inside it challenges the whole structure of authority. The fight that followed, set out in Part Two, was less about what happened in the beginning of the world than about who could teach what it meant.
