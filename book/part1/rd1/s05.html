The Scofield Reference Bible, 1909

In 1909 Oxford University Press published a King James Bible with one man's notes printed beneath the text on every page. A common view holds that Christian support for Israel comes straight from ancient scripture, read plainly. The verses involved are ancient. The framework that links them to a modern political return of Jews to Palestine, and that made such a return a matter of prophecy for millions of American Protestants, has a more recent and more traceable origin. Its main vehicle was this annotated Bible. The research this book draws on argues that before Scofield the framework was "substantially absent" from American Protestant tradition, and that afterward it became a mainstream evangelical framework. Over the following century, on this argument, it produced a political constituency of roughly 30 to 40 million Christian Zionists, which supported the Zionist project in America before American Jewish support consolidated. [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] That is a strong claim. The record supports parts of it more firmly than others, and this section sets out which parts are which.

The ideas did not begin with Scofield. The earlier volume of this library traces [scene:bc_s05|the Christian restorationists] who came before him. The more immediate source was John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), an Anglo-Irish minister who founded the Plymouth Brethren and developed dispensationalist premillennialism in the 1830s. Darby visited America six times between 1862 and 1877 and spread the framework among American Protestants decades before Scofield's Bible existed. [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] That qualifies the claim of absence. Before 1909 the framework was known in America and was held by some of its Protestants. What it lacked was a place in the mainstream. According to the research, standard nineteenth-century American Protestantism was largely amillennialist or postmillennialist. It did not treat a modern political return of Jews as an eschatological necessity, and it did not treat the Church as a separate dispensation from Israel.

The man who carried Darby's framework into the American mainstream had an unusual biography for the purpose. Cyrus Scofield was born in Michigan in 1843, and his family moved to Tennessee. He served in the Confederate Army during the Civil War, from 1861 to 1865. In 1873 President Ulysses S. Grant appointed him United States Attorney for Kansas. He held the post for less than a year and resigned amid financial irregularities. In 1877 he was convicted of forgery in St. Louis and served a prison sentence, which the shorter record describes as brief. In the late 1870s he abandoned his first wife and children and moved to Texas. He was twice divorced. In 1882 he was ordained a Congregationalist minister without formal seminary training and began pastoral work in Dallas. He was not a scholar of Hebrew or Greek. Between 1901 and 1907 he wrote the annotations. He died in 1921, and the book stayed in print after him. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology]

The standard source for this biography, including the forgery conviction, is Joseph M. Canfield's The Incredible Scofield and His Book (1988), which has served as the reference work for some four decades. Canfield also documents where the project's support came from. The research summarizes him as finding that the promotion of Scofield's work, and the money behind the annotation project, came in significant part from figures connected with early American Zionist advocacy. Among them was Samuel Untermyer, a Wall Street lawyer who later became a prominent Zionist. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] That is a documented association. It is not evidence that these backers caused the book's success or wrote its theology. The theology came largely from Darby, and Scofield's work would have found some readers in any case. The research's own account of what produced the book's scale points elsewhere: Oxford's distribution, and what it calls the funding architecture that put the Bible "into every seminary, Sunday school, and pulpit in the American South and Midwest." The material does not say how much the backers gave or how their support was arranged. Without those details, no conclusion beyond the association can be drawn. The biographical record is also no argument against the theology. Scofield's personal history and the framework's later influence are separate facts, each to be checked on its own terms.

The book's form did as much work as its content. Each page carried the King James Version, the standard English Protestant translation of 1611, at the top. Scofield's notes filled a substantial part of the bottom. A reader did not open a Bible and then consult a separate commentary. The commentary was already in place on the page, printed under Oxford's imprint and bound in the same covers as the text. According to the research, the notes did three things. First, they divided biblical history into seven "dispensations," periods in which God deals with humanity in different ways. Second, they introduced a pre-tribulation rapture, the belief that the faithful will be taken up before a time of catastrophe. The research calls this a modern doctrine that is not found in the biblical text. It dates its origin once to the eighteenth century and elsewhere to Darby in the 1830s. Third, they presented the political return of Jews to Palestine as a prerequisite for the Second Coming of Christ. That last point turned a territorial political program into an article of prophecy. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] Scofield had systematized Darby's framework and put it into the vocabulary of American Protestants. The decisive step was placing it in the margins of the Bible ordinary readers already used.

The research describes the effect of that placement as cumulative. Over years of daily reading, text and note merged in the reader's memory, until Scofield's reading became simply "what the Bible says." A claim is hard to examine if the reader never registers it as a separate claim. The label on the book stayed the same, the Bible, while what the reader received under that label now included one man's interpretation. The Reference Bible sold millions of copies through the twentieth century. On the research's account, a mid-century American evangelical who studied the Bible seriously was likely to meet Scofield's notes as the frame for reading it. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] The second edition of 1917 added references to the Balfour Declaration, which had just been issued.

Oxford's part is the step most easily misread. Oxford University Press is one of the two oldest and most prestigious academic publishers in the English-speaking world; Cambridge University Press is the other. [cite:wiki_oxford_university_press] Its name gave a marginal framework an authority it could not have earned on its content alone. A self-published set of the same notes would probably have reached a small readership. Under Oxford's imprint, bound into the scriptural text, it reached millions. The research calls this a transfer of legitimacy, not a conspiracy, and the Press's governance supports that reading. Oxford University Press has never been privately owned. It is a department of the University of Oxford. Since the seventeenth century it has been governed by the Delegates of the Press, 15 sitting academics appointed by the university's Vice-Chancellor, who met fortnightly to approve every title. There was no owner or publishing family whose interests could explain the decision, and no single person positioned to ask why a new theological framework should be bound into scripture. The book went through the same committee process as any other title. Because the underlying text was technically just the King James Version with added footnotes, no special theological review was triggered. [cite:wiki_oxford_university_press] The record shows a gap in review. It does not show that anyone used that gap deliberately, and it does not show any promotional strategy by the Press. The Press did not set out to shape American policy in the Middle East, but its imprint gave the notes an authority its editors had not intended to confer. The Press has never withdrawn the book. Later editions, the New Scofield of 1967 and the Scofield Study Bible III of 2002, were revised but kept the core dispensationalist framework. The research counts 117 years of continuous publication.

The research then sets out what the doctrines meant politically, as later Christian Zionist teaching drew them out. Two of the verses are ancient. Genesis 12:3 reads "I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse," and Genesis 15:18 gives the land promise. The research's argument concerns how Scofield's framework applied them to a modern state.

On the research's reading, none of these doctrines had been part of mainstream American Protestant theology before Scofield placed them there. The research summarizes the change this way: the framework was standard by 1950 and taken for granted by 2000. It also argues that the framework leaves no theological role for the people already living in Palestine. Palestinian Christians, members of a community older than most European churches, become an anomaly. Palestinian Muslims become obstacles to the fulfillment of prophecy. These are arguments about the logic of a doctrine, and they should be read as arguments. They are not a census of what each believer holds.

The research also makes a theological critique, and it is presented here as one side of a dispute. Evangelical Protestantism teaches salvation through Christ alone. Scofield's scheme runs a separate track for national Israel, in which an ethnic nation and a literal grant of land carry weight of their own in the history of salvation, alongside the work of Christ. The sequence the notes require runs as follows. Israel is dispersed. Israel is regathered, in unbelief. The regathered nation passes through the Tribulation. Only then does Israel turn to Christ. The Second Coming follows, Christ establishes a millennial kingdom, and Israel stands at its center. The critique fixes on the fourth step. In a tradition of salvation by grace, it asks, why must a people be regathered in unbelief and pass through catastrophe before conversion? On this reading, the notes never reconcile the two tracks; they simply print them on the same page. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] [scene:s04fa|The Uganda counterfactual] tests the same sequence against a different geography. The material does not give the replies of dispensationalist theologians to this critique. A reader should treat it as a contested theological argument, not a settled finding.

A separate passage is not history at all, and is labeled here as interpretation. One symbolic reading, put forward in this library's opening volume, holds that the confusion dissolves if "Israel" is read not as land but as IS-RA-EL, a "consciousness architecture." In that reading IS stands for Isis, the receptive or lunar principle, RA for the solar or radiant principle, and EL for the unifying divine force. Of the three, only EL is real Hebrew etymology. On this reading "Israel" names a spiritual condition rather than a nation, and Scofield's notes performed a literalizing move, turning a symbol into a flag, a border and an ethnic deed. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] This is offered as a key for thinking, not a documented claim. The documentary case stands without it.

What followed belongs to later volumes and is summarized here only in outline. Dallas Theological Seminary, founded in 1924, became an institutional home for training pastors in the framework. Between the 1920s and the 1960s, according to the research, Scofield's theology became standard in Southern Baptist and other evangelical denominations and in Bible colleges of the South and Midwest. Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth (1970), the best-selling nonfiction book of the 1970s, carried it to a mass readership. In 1979 Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority. Tim LaHaye and Jerry Jenkins's Left Behind novels, published from 1995 to 2007, sold more than 65 million copies. In 2006 John Hagee, pastor of Cornerstone Church in San Antonio, published Jerusalem Countdown and founded Christians United for Israel (CUFI). [cite:hagee_jerusalem_countdown] CUFI claims more than 10 million members, a self-reported figure. It holds an annual Washington Summit and appears in FEC records giving to pro-Israel candidates. [cite:cufi_org] Membership claims of this kind cover a wide range of engagement and do not mean that every member holds the same views. The alliance between these organizations and Israeli leaders is told in [scene:bwm_src_s15|the Christian Zionist network].

The size of the constituency rests on survey figures, and they should be stated exactly. The research cites a 2019 Pew Research Center survey. According to that survey, about 25 percent of American adults identify as evangelical Protestant, and 75 to 80 percent of white evangelical Protestants express strong support for Israel as a biblical requirement. A plurality of white evangelicals were found to support Israel more strongly than American Jews do, 42 percent against 27 percent. The material does not say exactly what question produced those two figures. A related source puts white evangelicals at approximately 25 to 30 percent of the electorate. [cite:pew_evangelical_israel_support] The research also cites a 2022 University of North Carolina Institute of Politics survey, which found white evangelicals to be the largest single religious group supporting unconditional American aid to Israel. The figure of 30 to 40 million Christian Zionist voters is an estimate the research derives from these shares. [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] It is not a count, and the range is wide.

Two limits keep the account honest. First, nothing in it was hidden. Scofield published openly, Oxford's decisions were public, and Christian Zionist organizations argue their positions in public. To read the chain as a conspiracy would be a category error. Scofield published in 1909, the Balfour Declaration came in 1917, and the state was declared in 1948. He was one factor among many, and nothing here claims that he or Oxford brought the State of Israel about. The research does use the word "manufactured" for the constituency. What the documents support is narrower: a publishing event put a specific framework into wide circulation, and institutions carried it forward for decades.

Second, this is a dispute within Christianity, not a case against Christianity. Mainline Protestant denominations, including the United Methodist, Episcopal, Presbyterian (PC-USA), Evangelical Lutheran and United Church of Christ churches, largely do not embrace Christian Zionism. Roman Catholic tradition does not endorse it. The research describes Orthodox tradition as rejecting it explicitly in one passage, and speaks of "most Orthodox tradition" in another. In 2009 Palestinian Christian leaders from across denominations signed the Kairos Palestine document, which calls Christian Zionism a "heresy" from within Christian tradition. The research names the Palestinian Christian Sami Awad, the mainline Protestant scholar Gary Burge and the evangelical Shane Claiborne as public figures whose positions show that the framework can be contested inside the tradition. They show that holding it is not required of Christians. The Christians who reject Scofield's framework do so on grounds within their own traditions.

The record establishes the book, its publisher, its author's history, its notes and their place on the page, and the later institutions that carried the framework. It leaves open how far the Reference Bible itself, as distinct from Darby's earlier preaching, the Bible colleges and later popular writers, caused American Christian support for Zionism. It does not show the size or terms of the money behind the annotations. It also does not show what Oxford's Delegates discussed when they approved the book. The constituency figures are survey estimates. The research's account of later American policy, which it partly traces to this constituency, is examined in later volumes against the full record of who decided what.
