The Aid: What It Is, What It Weighs and What It Frees Up

Any comparison of the American and Israeli systems runs into the aid sooner or later, so this book states it precisely at the start. Under a memorandum of understanding (MOU) signed on 14 September 2016, the United States committed $38 billion over its fiscal years 2019 to 2028: $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing (FMF) grants and $5 billion for missile defence, or $3.3 billion and $500 million a year. The agreement runs to 30 September 2028. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] Counting every kind of aid since 1946, the Congressional Research Service (CRS) puts the total at about $174 billion in current dollars, or about $298 billion in 2024 dollars. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Part Fifteen of the Greater Israel volume sets out the mechanics and the emergency money that followed October 2023 in full; this section keeps to what a comparison of the two systems needs. [scene:gi16_aid|The aid in detail]

Military money, spent mostly in America

Three facts govern what the aid can and cannot do. It is military money. The economic grants that Israel received for decades, about $1.2 billion a year through the 1990s, fell to $482 million in 2005 and $44 million in 2008, according to CRS data; none are listed from 2020. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] It is paid early: since 1991 Congress has required Israel's FMF to be disbursed as a lump sum in the first month of the fiscal year, into an interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] And it is spent mostly in the United States. Under the 2007 MOU Israel could spend up to 26.3 per cent of its grant on equipment made in Israel, a practice called offshore procurement; the 2016 agreement phases this out, "ending entirely in FY2028". [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] The White House said at signing that ending offshore procurement and fuel purchases would move up to $1.2 billion a year more into American-made goods. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] Researchers at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies (INSS) concluded in February 2026 that the aid now goes almost entirely to procurement in the United States. [cite:gi16_inss_aid] The missile-defence money pays for systems built partly in each country. [scene:ow8_coproduction|Co-production]

Forty years of a shrinking weight

The aid's dollar amount has changed less than its weight. CRS figures put total American aid to Israel at about $3.7 billion in 1985, $3.5 billion in 1990, $4.0 billion in 2000, $3.1 billion in 2010 and $3.8 billion in 2025, in the dollars of each year; the exception was 2024, when the wartime supplemental lifted the total to about $12.5 billion. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] Over the same years Israel's economy grew more than twentyfold in dollar terms, from $28.9 billion in 1985 to $610.8 billion in 2025, by the World Bank's count. [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp] Set side by side, the two series give the ratio this book uses throughout. On the book's own arithmetic, US aid in a given American fiscal year equalled 12.7 per cent of Israel's GDP in 1985, 5.6 per cent in 1990, 2.9 per cent in 2000, 1.3 per cent in 2010, 1.0 per cent in 2019 and 0.6 per cent in 2025. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp] The calculation divides fiscal-year aid by calendar-year GDP, both in current dollars, so each figure is approximate; it agrees with INSS's estimate of "about half a percent" for 2026 and with the 1.5 per cent that the Israeli analyst Gazit gave for 2011. [cite:gi16_inss_aid] [cite:gi16_cato_bandow]

Israel's own defence burden has moved on a different path, and the measures are not identical across the years, which the table below marks. By the economists Rafi Melnick and Yosef Mealem's account, defence spending fell from "more than 20% of GDP in the mid-1980s" to under 10 per cent by the late 2000s. [cite:us1_jvl_melnick] Shmuel Even of INSS, using the total cost of defence including costs outside the budget, put it at 10 per cent of GDP in 1993, 8.2 per cent in 2000 and 7.8 per cent in 2005. [cite:us1_inss_even2010] The Bank of Israel records 4.2 per cent in 2022, then 8.4 per cent in 2024 and 7.8 per cent in 2025 as the wars that began in October 2023 drove spending up. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] Measured against that spending, the aid's share has also fallen: about 24 per cent of security outlays in 2011 by Gazit's estimate, "approximately 14%" of the defence budget by the CRS in 2025 and "roughly 15%" by INSS in 2026. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] Against the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute's figure of $48.3 billion for Israel's military spending in 2025, the $3.8 billion is about 8 per cent, by this book's arithmetic; the gap from the CRS and INSS figures comes from different years and definitions of the budget. [cite:ir13_sipri_2025]

1985: when the aid did support the civilian economy

The over-time view changes the answer to the question of what the aid frees up. In 1985 the argument was at its strongest, because the aid was then partly civilian and large against a small economy. Israel had inflation "of over 400%" before its Economic Stabilization Plan of July 1985, and Melnick and Mealem write that the plan's success "was supported by the U.S. grant in the amount of $1.5 billion (over a two-year period)", while American loans for defence purchases were converted into grants. [cite:us1_jvl_melnick] CRS data show economic aid of $1.95 billion that year beside $1.7 billion in military grants. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] Part Eleven sets that plan beside the American economic turn of the same years. [scene:us11_turn|Part Eleven: the turn]

What the aid frees up now, stated precisely

Today the aid is military money that Israel does not have to raise itself, and that is the whole of the mechanism. Each dollar of FMF buys an American weapon that Israel would otherwise have had to buy with its own shekels, delay or do without; to the extent Israel would have bought it anyway, the grant leaves that much of its own budget free for other uses, military or civilian. Economists call this fungibility, and Washington once acted on it: loan guarantees approved in 1992 and 2003 were reduced by amounts equal to Israel's settlement spending, by a net $773.8 million in 1994–97 and by $289.5 million in 2003 and $795.8 million in 2005, according to CRS data. [cite:gi16_jvl_loans] Israel's Finance Ministry counts the grant as income in its budget: of the 2026 defence budget of about $44.8 billion, passed on 30 March 2026, about ₪22 billion is "expenditure contingent on income, including the U.S. aid grant". [cite:gi16_bd_budget2026]

Three limits apply to that statement. The money freed is at most the amount of the grant, now about 0.6 per cent of Israel's GDP, and nothing in the record ties it to any particular Israeli programme, civilian or military. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp] The requirement to buy in America has a cost of its own, which Gazit put at about $600 million a year in 2011, a view the United States government has not endorsed. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] And the dollars are spent mainly with American contractors, so the aid is at once a transfer to Israel's defence budget and a purchase from American industry. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] INSS argues that the aid's real value to Israel now lies less in the money than in access to American systems and the symbol of the alliance, and that it also gives Washington leverage over Israeli policy. [cite:gi16_inss_aid]

Status as of October 2026

The arrangement may not outlast 2028. Israel's Defence Ministry announced on 5 June 2026 that talks had begun on a framework to replace the MOU, with the aim to "gradually transition from aid to a completely reciprocal partnership". [cite:ow1_mod_mou2026] Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said in a CNBC interview that month that he wanted the wind-down to "start now", until the aid reaches "coming to zero". [cite:gi16_ji_netanyahu] No new agreement had been reported concluded by the latest reporting this book found, and the talks will continue past Israel's general election of 27 October 2026. [cite:ow1_wiki_nextmou] [cite:gi6_aje_dissolve] Critics argue that folding support into defence procurement would leave "no line item to block".
