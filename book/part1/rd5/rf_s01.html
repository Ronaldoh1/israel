Who Objected at the Time

It is sometimes said that no one in the United States questioned the campaign for a Jewish state, or the money raised for it, until much later. The record of the 1940s says otherwise. Objections were raised while the decisions were being made, by an anti-Zionist Jewish organization, by a mainstream Jewish body, by a future intelligence officer and a former college dean, by a foreign parliament, and in 1948 by Hannah Arendt, Albert Einstein and others who signed their names to public protest. They were made in public and published. They were also outweighed. This section sets out who objected, what each said, and how large or small each voice was.

The American Council for Judaism. The most direct objection came from Jews who rejected Zionism on religious grounds. The American Council for Judaism was founded in 1942 and led by Lessing Rosenwald, with Rabbi Elmer Berger. It argued that Judaism was a religion, not a nationality, and it opposed partition. [cite:djn_1948_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman] In 1948 it went further and objected to funds raised by the United Jewish Appeal being used "for political and foreign governmental purposes." [cite:djn_1948_acj] The complaint touched the same charitable campaigns whose 1948 proceeds are traced in this chapter's account of Golda Meyerson's American tour: in the Council's view, charity was being put to political ends abroad.

How representative was it? On this the sources agree more than they differ. The Council's own historian documents it as a small minority that the mainstream Jewish organizations opposed, and Zionist sources describe it as a fringe group. [cite:kolsky_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman] Its argument mattered less for its numbers than for its content: it showed that the claim to speak for American Jews as a whole on Palestine was contested from inside the community.

The American Jewish Committee. A larger and more established body took a different path. At the congressional hearings of 1944, the American Jewish Committee, a mainstream organization, proposed an international trusteeship for Palestine instead of a Jewish commonwealth. [cite:djn_1944_hearings] This was not anti-Zionism of the Council's kind. It was an alternative arrangement offered by a leading Jewish organization, and it shows that support for a Jewish state was not yet a settled position among American Jewish institutions in the middle of the war. The same 1944 congressional resolutions drew protest abroad: Iraq's parliament formally objected to them. [cite:djn_1944_marshall]

Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, had argued before the Anglo-American Committee in 1946 for a single binational state shared by Arabs and Jews, an argument told in [scene:aac_s01|the Anglo-American Committee]. He did not stop there. In May 1948, the month of independence, Magnes met Truman to press for a federation under trusteeship. [cite:rs_rd5_manne_2026]

Kermit Roosevelt and the Gildersleeve committee. In 1948 Kermit Roosevelt Jr. published a 24-page pamphlet, "The Partition of Palestine: A Lesson in Pressure Politics." [cite:ptsem_kr_pamphlet] Its title states its argument: that the partition decision had been produced by political pressure. Roosevelt was later the CIA's field commander in Iran's 1953 coup. [cite:w_kermit_jr] With Virginia Gildersleeve, the former dean of Barnard College, he formed the Committee for Justice and Peace in the Holy Land, which warned that "extreme Zionist pressure" risked disrupting national unity. [cite:mosaic_cia_zionism] [cite:w_kermit_jr] These objections echoed, from outside government, the warnings that the State Department, the Joint Chiefs and the Defense Secretary had made from inside it the year before.

Arendt: "There Is Still Time." The fullest argument came in May 1948. Hannah Arendt's essay "To Save the Jewish Homeland: There Is Still Time" appeared in Commentary that month, on pages 398 to 406. [cite:rs_rd5_arendt_commentary] In the historian Robert Manne's account, Arendt argued that Jewish near-unanimity for a state, reached before any settlement with the Arabs, would bring war, and that even a victorious Jewish state would remain small, surrounded by hostile neighbors and absorbed in its own defense. She proposed instead a gradual binational federation, built on mixed Jewish-Arab local councils under United Nations trusteeship with American and British support, and wrote that "It is still not too late." She condemned the Irgun's attacks, including Deir Yassin. [cite:rs_rd5_manne_2026]

Arendt worked closely with Magnes, and their proposals ran in parallel: a federation, under trusteeship, in place of partition. In 1952 she called Magnes "the conscience of the Jewish people." Manne also reports that she later estimated the Arab "evacuation" of 1948 at 500,000, a figure that sits among the many estimates of the refugee exodus discussed elsewhere in this chapter. [cite:rs_rd5_manne_2026] Her essay is arguably the fullest statement of the dissenting case at the time. As her title said, her aim was to save the Jewish homeland; her case was that a state won by war against its neighbors would leave it small, besieged and absorbed in self-defense.

The Einstein–Arendt letter. The last objection of the year was aimed not at partition, which had happened, but at what one wing of the new state's politics had become. A letter dated 2 December 1948 and published in the New York Times on 4 December, signed by 28 people including Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and Sidney Hook, protested Menachem Begin's visit to the United States. It described his Tnuat Haherut, the Freedom Party, which had grown out of the Irgun, as bearing "the unmistakable stamp of a Fascist party." [cite:rs_rd5_einstein_nyt] The Irgun's own path from underground army to political party, after the Altalena and the absorption of the militias into one army, is told in [scene:mil_s01|from three militias to one army].

The letter named Deir Yassin directly. It said that on 9 April "terrorist bands" had attacked a "peaceful village, which was not a military objective," and "killed most of its inhabitants (240 men, women, and children)." [cite:rs_rd5_einstein_nyt] The figure of 240 is the one in circulation in 1948. Later research has revised the count of the dead at Deir Yassin, and the current state of that evidence is set out in [scene:ma_s01|Deir Yassin, the Hadassah convoy and Tantura]. The letter is evidence of what its signers believed and said in December 1948, not a settled casualty figure.

Raised, published, outweighed. These objections differed in their grounds. The Council for Judaism objected in principle to Jewish nationhood; the American Jewish Committee to the form a solution should take; Roosevelt and Gildersleeve to the political pressure behind the decision; Arendt and Magnes to partition by war rather than federation by agreement; and the signers of December 1948 to a specific party and its record. None of them prevailed. Partition was voted, the state was proclaimed and recognized, and the fundraising the Council objected to continued. Formal action on foreign-agent questions came only much later, with the 1962 order under the Foreign Agents Registration Act and the Fulbright hearings of 1963, told in a later volume.

What the record does not settle is how much support these positions had beyond their authors. The sources measure the Council for Judaism only in words, a small minority in its own historian's account and a fringe in Zionist accounts, and give no membership figures. They say nothing about how many Americans read Roosevelt's pamphlet or Arendt's essay. What they do establish is that the objections existed, in print, in the years the decisions were made, and that several of the most pointed came from Jewish organizations and Jewish writers.
