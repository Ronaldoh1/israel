ICE's Data Contracts, Vendor by Vendor

Immigration and Customs Enforcement does not run one surveillance system. It buys many services from many companies, each sold for a stated purpose, and the question for this book is what they add up to. This section lists the documented contracts by what they supply, with the date, the administration that signed it and the value as reported. The figures are not added together: some are ceilings, some obligations, some the cost of a single licence, and they run over different years. The agency's overall budget growth, $75 billion for ICE in the budget act signed in July 2025, is set out in [scene:us6_asylum|US vs Israel]; Palantir's part is in the previous part and in the library's [scene:ow9_ice|One-Way Mirror].

The platform: Palantir

Palantir has worked for ICE since 2014, when the agency's criminal investigators, Homeland Security Investigations, began using its Investigative Case Management system under a contract first reported at $41 million; renewals followed in 2019 and 2022. [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] [cite:ow9_eb_icm2022] In April 2025 ICE paid $29.9 million for ImmigrationOS, a platform to prioritise people for arrest and track departures; in February 2026 the Department of Homeland Security signed a five-year purchase agreement worth up to $1 billion; and on 5 May 2026 ICE ordered $86.3 million of work under it to modernise its Enforcement and Removal Operations systems. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:ow9_sa_dhsbpa] [cite:de27_hg_ero] Its ELITE tool gives each target's address a "confidence score." [cite:ow9_fortune_elite] Records from 2014 to 2022 released under freedom-of-information law showed agents being trained to search the student-visa database SEVIS through the case system, planning searches of airline passenger and border-crossing data, and able to import phone extractions; the same records said the people-search service CLEAR "is not connected to Palantir tools." [cite:de28_guardian_icm]

People data: LexisNexis and Thomson Reuters

The second layer is the commercial people-search business, which compiles names, addresses, phone numbers, vehicles, property and court records into searchable profiles. LexisNexis Risk Solutions has supplied its Accurint service under a contract signed in February 2021, under the Biden administration, with about $24.5 million obligated, which ran to 31 August 2026; ICE users ran more than 1.2 million person searches in seven months of 2021. On 10 August 2026 ICE gave notice of a sole-source bridge contract of about $6.7 million, whose work statement requires connections to ICE systems "such as but not limited to" the Palantir platform, PenLink and ICE's own analytics. [cite:de28_bu_lexis] Thomson Reuters supplies CLEAR, which draws on licences, vehicle registrations, credit data, property and court records and, per a DHS justification, utility data; its DHS contract was renewed without competition from 31 March 2026. Separately it supplied licence-plate-reader data under a $22.8 million contract from May 2021 to May 2026. Thomson Reuters says CLEAR excludes immigration status, call logs and bank data, and that Palantir is not a CLEAR customer. [cite:de28_lawsites_tr]

Phone location: Babel Street and Penlink

The third layer is location data from phone apps, sold through advertising networks and resold to government. ICE bought Babel Street software, whose location product is called Locate X, through a reseller in September 2018 for $1.1 million and directly in September 2019 for $1.5 million, under the first Trump administration. [cite:de28_epic_babel] In September 2023 DHS's inspector general found that ICE, Customs and Border Protection and the Secret Service had bought and used such "commercial telemetry data" without completing the privacy assessments the law requires, and recommended that ICE stop until they were done. [cite:de28_oig_23_61] In autumn 2025 ICE returned to the market with a $2.3 million contract with Penlink, a Nebraska company, covering Tangles, a tool for searching the open web and social media, and Webloc, a database of phone movements. Both products came from Cobwebs Technologies, an Israeli firm Penlink bought in 2023; ICE bought licences, not the company. Penlink says its tools "exclusively use publicly or commercially available data." [cite:de28_ffp_penlink] What Webloc can do, and the ICE legal analysis saying it can be searched without a warrant, are described in [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Six]. [cite:de25_404_webloc]

Social media: ShadowDragon and Zignal

ICE bought ShadowDragon's SocialNet, which compiles information about people from social media and other sites, through resellers: $289,500 from July 2020, under the first Trump administration, and $602,056 from August 2021, under Biden. [cite:de28_epic_shadowdragon] In September 2025 it became public that HSI had bought $5.7 million of licences over five years for Zignal Labs, through the reseller Carahsoft, for "real-time data analysis for criminal investigations"; Zignal says it analyses more than eight billion posts a day, and a 2025 company pamphlet claimed it gave "tactical intelligence" to operators in Gaza. Zignal did not respond to questions. [cite:de28_lever_zignal]

Phones, faces and spyware

The remaining tools act on a person directly. Cellebrite's phone-extraction devices have been bought by ICE since at least March 2017, and a May 2026 DHS forecast planned a five-year contract worth up to $100 million covering phones and drones. [cite:ow6_ipt_cellebrite] [cite:de28_fs_cellebrite] Clearview AI's face-recognition service was bought by HSI on 5 September 2025, $3.75 million up front and about $9.2 million with options, for child-exploitation cases and assaults on officers. [cite:de28_bu_clearview] Paragon's Graphite spyware was bought for $2 million in September 2024, under Biden, paused for review, and reactivated in August 2025; the contract was closed out in January 2026, and in May DHS said ICE had "no relationship" with Paragon while declining to say whether it still had the capability. [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] [cite:de28_npr_paragon] These are examined in [scene:de25_spyware_and_phones|Part Twenty-Six] and [scene:de26_faces_drones_satellites|Part Twenty-Eight].

Two corrections to common accounts follow from the list. A widely repeated line says ICE bought the Israeli company Cobwebs; it did not. Penlink bought Cobwebs, and ICE bought licences from Penlink. [cite:de28_ffp_penlink] And the stack is not the creation of one administration. Babel Street and ShadowDragon were first bought under Trump's first term, LexisNexis, the plate-reader data, a second ShadowDragon order and Paragon under Biden, and Palantir under Obama; what changed after 2025 was the volume, the new tools and the money. [cite:de28_epic_babel] [cite:de28_bu_lexis] [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019]

If it were connected

Each vendor sells one thing; the worst case is what they do joined. Documented: Palantir's case system has drawn on SEVIS, passenger and border-crossing data and phone extractions (2014–22 records), and Medicaid records reached Palantir in January 2026. [cite:de28_guardian_icm] [cite:de27_npr_medicaid] Probable: LexisNexis people-search data flowing into the Palantir platform, because the 2026 work statement requires the connection. [cite:de28_bu_lexis] Possible, with no document: Penlink's phone-location trails, Zignal's social-media alerts and Clearview's face matches feeding Palantir, so that a face in a protest photograph could be matched to a name, the name to an address with a confidence score and the address to a phone's movements on one screen; the join would need only an API and a work order, since all are ICE's own licences, and what stands in the way is agency policy, the privacy assessments the inspector general found missing in 2023, and the courts. [cite:de28_oig_23_61] Not supported: CLEAR feeding Palantir, which both 2014–22 records and Thomson Reuters say does not happen. [cite:de28_guardian_icm] [cite:de28_lawsites_tr] The law that governs these purchases is the subject of [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|the next section].
