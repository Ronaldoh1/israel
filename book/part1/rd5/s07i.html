UNSCOP and the Empty Chair

The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine, known as UNSCOP, is often described as an impartial international body that investigated the problem and arrived independently at its recommendation. The documented record of how its hearings worked complicates that picture. The committee did not weigh two prepared cases against each other. It heard one, at length, while the other side stayed away.

UNSCOP was established by the General Assembly in May 1947, at Britain's own request, after London had handed the question to the United Nations. Its eleven members were drawn from Australia, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Guatemala, India, Iran, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden, Uruguay and Yugoslavia. [cite:unscop_ecf] On its face it was a genuinely international body spanning several continents, not a proxy for the United States or Britain.

Its work, however, was boycotted entirely by Palestinian Arab representatives. As a result, the committee heard testimony almost exclusively from Zionist representatives. [cite:unscop_ecf] The Arab Higher Committee for Palestine refused to take part because it regarded the whole framework as illegitimate. In its view, any United Nations body that set out to decide Palestine's future without first affirming the self-determination rights of the existing majority was biased before it began. The Jewish Agency, by contrast, engaged fully. It provided detailed logistical input, including its own proposed itinerary for the committee's site visits in Palestine.

The Agency's case was presented by David Ben-Gurion, testifying as its head. He told the committee explicitly that an independent Jewish state was the only acceptable political outcome. [cite:unscop_testimony] The map the Zionist side presented was substantially the 1937 Peel Commission map, with the Galilee, the Negev and West Jerusalem added. [cite:unscop_testimony] It was not a new proposal so much as an extension of an earlier one, and it came after more than a year in which partition had been the Agency's avowed policy, as described in [scene:s07h|the previous section on the Agency's campaign]. One historical account describes the Jewish Agency's presentation as overwhelming the committee "with data, logic, and cogent analyses of every central and tangential issue," while the boycotting side presented nothing for the committee to weigh against it. [cite:unscop_testimony] The imbalance did not arise because the Arab position was weak. It arose because that position was never formally placed before the committee for comparison.

The limits of this point should be stated carefully. The record does not show that Arab perspectives were absent from every channel open to the committee. UNSCOP traveled through the region and could observe conditions directly, and it would be wrong to claim that its members heard nothing about Arab objections. What the record does establish is more specific: no formal, prepared Arab case was presented to UNSCOP to set against the Jewish Agency's. That is a narrower claim than saying the process was rigged, and it is the one the evidence supports.

UNSCOP issued its report on September 3, 1947. It was not unanimous. A seven-member majority recommended partition into a Jewish state and an Arab state joined by an economic union. A three-member minority proposed a federal state instead. [cite:unscop_wiki] The report, produced after hearings shaped by the boycott, became the direct basis for the revisions made by the United Nations Ad Hoc Committee and for the General Assembly vote of November 29, 1947. [cite:unscop_wiki]

The empty chair, then, was a choice made by the Arab Higher Committee on grounds of principle, and the consequence of that choice was that the record on which partition was built contained one side's data, maps and arguments. Between the report and the vote, the decisive pressure would shift to Washington, where the government's own experts were warning against the course the President was moving toward.
