The American Machine: Hadassah, Brandeis and the ZOA

The usual account holds that American support for Zionism became organized and significant only after 1948, or at the earliest after the Holocaust. The groundwork was in fact laid inside the United States a generation earlier, years before there was a British Mandate to build under. None of it was based in Palestine. It was domestic American institution-building.

The first piece was Hadassah. Henrietta Szold founded it in New York on 24 February 1912. Sources count between 38 and 122 founding members. In 1913 the organization sent its first two nurses to Palestine, for maternity care and the treatment of trachoma. [cite:ushmm_youth_aliyah] Hadassah built a distinct fundraising channel, financed by American Jewish women and focused on medical and settlement infrastructure. It ran alongside the male-led political and financial institutions of the movement, the Jewish Colonial Trust, the Jewish National Fund and later the ZOA, rather than through them. [cite:hadassah_wiki] The channels were not sealed off from one another: Hadassah became a founding member of the ZOA in 1918. Szold's own record extended far beyond the founding. She later directed Youth Aliyah, founded in 1933, which the US Holocaust Memorial Museum credits with bringing more than 14,000 unaccompanied children to Palestine and Britain between 1933 and 1945. [cite:ushmm_youth_aliyah] Operations of that scale involved real hardship for the children concerned, a fuller story than the arc from founding to rescue can capture.

The second piece was a person. Louis Brandeis, who joined the US Supreme Court in 1916, turned to active Zionist leadership after a 1914 meeting with Jacob de Haas, Herzl's former secretary. He led the American movement until 1921. In 1917 he met Balfour, and he served as a conduit between the British government and President Wilson while the Balfour Declaration was being drafted. He also worked with the Rev. William Blackstone, whose Blackstone Memorial of 1916 was aimed at influencing Wilson. [cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] Nahum Goldmann later argued that without Brandeis, "the Balfour Declaration would probably never have been issued." That is one historian's assessment of cause, not a settled fact.

Two kinds of claims about Brandeis need to be kept apart. That he had direct access to the President during the drafting is documented by multiple historians and is not in dispute. Why he chose to take up Zionist leadership is a different question, and historians disagree. Some, cited critically by outlets such as Mondoweiss, argue that the conversion was partly a matter of career strategy. [cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] No primary source settles his motives, and this book leaves the question open, as the historical debate does.

The third piece was organizational. In 1918 the Federation of American Zionists, Young Judaea, Hadassah and other bodies merged into the Zionist Organization of America. Brandeis was honorary president and Julian W. Mack president. The new organization reported 147,551 members that year. [cite:zoa_wiki]

The merger did not produce lasting unity. Brandeis and his followers favored private economic investment over national fundraising on the World Zionist Organization's model. At the 1921 Cleveland convention they split away, and Louis Lipsky became president. [cite:zoa_wiki] The movement's best-known American figure thus lost a fight over what kind of institution American Zionism should be, within three years of its founding.

The internal conflict does not change the larger picture. By 1918, thirty years before statehood, American Zionism had a national women's fundraising organization, its most influential advocate seated on the Supreme Court with access to the President, and a merged national body with more than 147,000 members. The American side of the movement's support was, like its land pipeline in Palestine, built early and built on purpose.
