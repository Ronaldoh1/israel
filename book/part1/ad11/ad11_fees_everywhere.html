Fees Everywhere

A tax is set by a legislature, applies to everyone in the same position and appears in a published schedule. A fee is a charge for a particular service or event: a booking, a missed payment, a reconnection, a court appearance. Fees are not unusual or improper in themselves; a toll for a bridge or a fee for a passport charges the user of a service rather than every taxpayer, which is often the fairest arrangement. This section looks at three places where fees behave differently from that model: where they are hidden until the end of a purchase, where they are the enforcement end of a household falling behind on a bill, and where a local government uses them to fund itself. Fees charged by banks and card issuers, overdraft and late fees above all, are covered in [scene:ad9_fees_and_unbanked|Part Nine] and are not counted again here.

The price you are shown and the price you pay

The practice called drip pricing works by advertising a lower number than the one the buyer will finally pay. Mandatory service, resort, cleaning or processing charges are added screen by screen, so that the full price appears only at checkout, after the buyer has compared offers on the advertised figure and invested time in the purchase. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the federal consumer-protection agency, voted 4–1 on 17 December 2024 to adopt a rule against it for two industries, live-event tickets and short-term lodging, with Commissioner Andrew Ferguson casting the dissenting vote. The FTC projected that the rule would save consumers up to 53 million hours a year of searching for true prices, a saving it valued at more than $11 billion over ten years. [cite:ad11_upi_fees] That figure is the value of time saved, not the total of fees charged; no official estimate of total hidden-fee revenue across the economy exists, and figures circulating in the tens of billions are projections, not measurements.

The rule took effect on 12 May 2025. It requires a business that advertises a price in those industries to show the total price up front, including mandatory fees, at every stage of the purchase, including the resale market for tickets. It does not ban any type of fee or cap its amount. [cite:ad11_ftc_fees2025] Government taxes, shipping and optional extras may be shown separately, but must be disclosed before payment, and each violation can draw a civil penalty of up to $51,744. [cite:ad11_btlaw_fees] The rule has been enforced: on 9 April 2026 the FTC announced a $10 million settlement with the largest ticket-resale platform in the country, whose filings name StubHub, over allegations that it did not show all-in prices at every stage and deliberately delayed compliance to gain an edge in sales of high-demand tickets. The company neither admitted nor denied the allegations. [cite:ad11_hc_stubhub] As of October 2026 the rule remains in force for those two industries only; hidden fees elsewhere, from apartment rentals to restaurant surcharges, fall under the FTC's general power against deceptive practices and under state law.

Falling behind on the lights

For a household that cannot pay a utility bill, the cost of falling behind is not only a late charge but the loss of the service. Until recently no one counted how often that happened nationwide. In April 2026 the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) published its first survey of residential disconnections, covering utilities that serve 93.5 per cent of electricity customers and 96.1 per cent of gas customers. In 2024 utilities sent 122 million final disconnection notices and carried out 15.1 million shutoffs, 13.4 million of electricity and 1.7 million of gas, and recorded 12.6 million reconnections. [cite:ad11_iej_shutoffs] The figures count events, not households, so one family disconnected twice appears twice, and the summary used here comes from the Initiative for Energy Justice, an advocacy group, reporting the EIA's numbers. They show a large enforcement system operating on household arrears, most of it reversed within the year when the household pays. Late-payment and reconnection charges, deposits and protections against shutoffs in extreme heat or cold are set state by state by utility regulators, and vary widely.

Fines and fees as a source of revenue

Courts fine people for traffic, parking and municipal-code violations and, in many places, add fees to cover the cost of the court, the prosecutor, probation or collections. The clearest documented case of a government relying on these charges to fund itself is Ferguson, Missouri. After the police shooting of Michael Brown in August 2014, the US Department of Justice investigated the city's police department and municipal court and published its findings on 4 March 2015. [cite:ad11_doj_ferguson]

The report traced the money. Fines and fees brought in $1.38 million of the city's $11.07 million in general-fund revenue in fiscal 2010, about 12 per cent. The court collected $2.46 million in fiscal 2013, more than the $2.11 million budgeted, and the city budgeted $3.09 million of $13.26 million for fiscal 2015, about 23 per cent. City officials pressed the police for more: in March 2010 the finance director warned of a shortfall "unless ticket writing ramps up significantly," and in March 2013 asked whether the department "could deliver 10% increase." The court issued over 9,000 warrants in 2013, mostly for minor offences such as parking, traffic and housing-code violations, and added charges each time a defendant missed a court date or a payment. The Department's finding was that "Ferguson's law enforcement practices are shaped by the City's focus on revenue rather than by public safety needs." [cite:ad11_doj_ferguson] The arithmetic of the shares: $1.38 million divided by $11.07 million is 12.5 per cent; $3.09 million divided by $13.26 million is 23.3 per cent.

Ferguson was not unique, but neither was it typical. A national review of local audits by the magazine Governing in 2019 found nearly 600 jurisdictions drawing more than 10 per cent of their general-fund revenue from fines and forfeitures, and at least 284 drawing more than 20 per cent; in Georgetown, Louisiana, the share was 92 per cent in 2018. The same review found that for most local governments fines are under 5 per cent of general revenue, and that the median among the 50 largest cities was 2 per cent. The heavy reliance is concentrated in small towns, often with busy highways passing through them, and the review called its counts low estimates because many governments do not report fines separately. [cite:ad11_governing_fines]

Unpaid court debt reaches beyond the courtroom through the driving licence. A driver whose licence is suspended for an unpaid fine must choose between not driving to work and driving illegally, which can add a new offence. According to the National Conference of State Legislatures (NCSL), nearly 11 million people have faced debt-based suspensions; a New Jersey study found that nearly 45 per cent of suspended drivers lost their jobs; and a 2019 Brennan Center report found jurisdictions spending more than 42 cents on hearings and jail for every dollar they collected through these suspensions. [cite:ad11_ncsl_dl] The penalties that follow from unpaid court debt, including jail, bail and charges in prison, are the subject of [scene:ad14_justice_costs|Part Fourteen].

Where it works as promised

Each of these mechanisms has drawn a correction, and the corrections have come from both parties. The FTC's fee rule was adopted under one administration and took effect and was enforced under the next, against a major company. [cite:ad11_ftc_fees2025] [cite:ad11_hc_stubhub] Since 2017, at least 25 states and the District of Columbia have passed laws, largely bipartisan, to curb or end licence suspensions for unpaid debt; California, Colorado, Idaho, New Mexico and Virginia no longer suspend for these reasons, and California's on-time collection rate rose 8.9 per cent in the year after it stopped, which suggests the suspensions were not needed to make people pay. [cite:ad11_ncsl_dl] Ferguson itself eliminated the added charges for missed appearances after the investigation. [cite:ad11_doj_ferguson] What remains is narrower than a system of extraction by fee: a federal price-disclosure rule that covers two industries, utility shutoffs counted nationally for the first time, and a minority of local governments that still fund a large share of their budgets from the people their courts fine.
