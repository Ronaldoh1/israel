The French Reactor, 1955–1960

The war of October and November 1956, told in [scene:cw3_war|the Suez war], ended for Israel in a forced withdrawal from Sinai and Gaza. It also left behind something that outlasted the withdrawal: an agreement with France that gave Israel a reactor able to produce plutonium. The story of how that agreement was reached, signed, built and then nearly undone runs from 1955 to the end of 1960. It is a story told almost entirely from French recollections, Israeli memoirs and the work of a small number of American and Israeli scholars, because the governments involved said nothing about it at the time and have said little since. That limits what can be stated with certainty, and the limits are part of the record.

Two tracks: the open one. Israel's nuclear program began on two tracks at once, one public and one secret. On 12 July 1955 Israel signed a general "Atoms for Peace" agreement with the United States, the second country to do so. In the same year, according to William Burr and Avner Cohen's documentary collection for the National Security Archive, Ben-Gurion launched a secret feasibility initiative and delegated it to Shimon Peres. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The public track produced a small American research reactor. On 20 March 1957 Israel signed a contract with the American firm AMF for a 1-megawatt pool reactor at Nahal Soreq. Construction began in January 1958, the final contract was signed on 19 March 1958, and the reactor reached criticality in a public ceremony in June 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] Soreq was everything that the second track was not: small, American, announced and celebrated.

Israel did try the American route for something larger first. In July–September 1956 Ernst David Bergmann asked the United States for a reactor and heavy water. The Atomic Energy Commission was willing, but only under stricter safeguards, and Israel dropped the probe. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The episode matters for what followed. A reactor from Washington would have come with conditions on how it was used and who could see it. Israel looked elsewhere.

The request in Paris, September 1956. It looked to France. The fullest account of the first request comes from Bertrand Goldschmidt, of France's atomic energy commission, the Commissariat à l'énergie atomique (CEA), in an interview that Avner Cohen recorded in Paris on 15 June 1993. It is a recollection given almost four decades after the event, and Cohen himself notes that Goldschmidt often said he did not remember. [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt] With that caution, Goldschmidt recalled attending the meeting in September 1956 at which Peres asked for French help. The request had two parts. The first, a reactor, was "relatively easy to agree upon." The second, a plant to extract plutonium from the reactor's spent fuel (a reprocessing plant), was "quite difficult." [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt]

The difficulty lay inside the CEA. According to Goldschmidt, the high commissioner, Francis Perrin, hesitated for weeks over the reprocessing plant until Pierre Guillaumat urged Goldschmidt to persuade him. Perrin later accused Goldschmidt of "extorting" his signature. [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt] The point is worth holding on to. The two halves of the request were not equivalent. A research reactor could be presented as science. A separation plant was the step that turned a reactor's output into material for a weapon, and the man at the head of France's atomic agency did not want to sign for it. That he was brought round, by his own colleague's account, under pressure from above, shows that the decision was made by a small circle within the French state and against reluctance inside it.

France agreed in the autumn of 1956. According to the Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library, it agreed at that stage to supply an 18-megawatt (thermal) research reactor. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] The Nuclear Weapon Archive, a privately compiled reference last revised in 1997, calls the Suez Crisis "the genesis of Israel's nuclear weapons production program" and dates a secret meeting of Golda Meir, Peres and the French ministers Pineau and Maurice Bourgès-Maunoury to 7 November 1956. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] The Archive is a reported source and gives no document for the meeting, so its date rests on its own authority. Its framing, that the war and the nuclear agreement were bound together, is consistent with the timing in the other sources: the request came in September, the war in October, and the agreement was developed over the following year. What those sources do not establish is what was said in any one meeting.

The agreements of 1957. What France offered grew between 1956 and 1957. The Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library both state that a revised agreement, which they date to 3 October 1957, provided for a 24-megawatt reactor, not 18, and that its cooling and waste systems were designed for three times that power. The chemical reprocessing plant, the "quite difficult" half of Peres's request, was covered in protocols that were not written down. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] Those three features, a reactor larger than first offered, systems built for far more than its rated power, and a separation plant agreed only in unwritten form, are the core of what the documentary record can say about the deal.

A fuller version comes from a single reported source. Rebecca M. Miller, writing in The National Interest, describes the 1957 package as three confidential contracts: one for a 24-megawatt heavy-water reactor, a loan of 385 tonnes of natural uranium, joint nuclear-weapons research and production, and backing for a plutonium extraction plant. [cite:rs_cw4_natinterest] That account goes further than the others in one respect that matters, since it names weapons research as a term of the agreement. It is attributed here to its author and has not been confirmed from the other sources gathered for this section, which speak of a reactor and a reprocessing plant but not of a weapons clause.

The signature and its date. When the 1957 agreement was signed is itself disputed, and the dispute is revealing rather than trivial. The Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library give 3 October 1957. Burr and Cohen's briefing book gives 3 October or 30 October 1957. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] A second set of sources tells a different story, in which the date on the document is not the date it was signed. According to the Center for Israel Education, Prime Minister Maurice Bourgès-Maunoury dated his approval 30 September but signed on 1 October, the day his government fell. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_backdate] Goldschmidt, in his 1993 interview, said the document was backdated by one day because Bourgès-Maunoury had lost a no-confidence vote, and that his successor, Félix Gaillard, was "totally unaware" of it on taking office. [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt]

Peres told the same story from the Israeli side. In his memoir he recalled watching Bourgès-Maunoury, after his government had fallen, sign the letter authorizing the reactor sale and date it the previous day. "I asked no questions," he wrote, and he called it "the most generous display of friendship I had ever known." [cite:rs_cw4_peres_memoir] The two accounts are therefore of different kinds. One gives a calendar date taken from the agreement's face, and the sources that hold it differ among themselves by four weeks. The other is the testimony of two men who said they were present, one French and one Israeli, and who agree that the date on the paper was a fiction meant to place the signature inside the life of a government that had already ended. The second account, if accurate, means that the commitment was made by a prime minister without the authority to make it, and that the next French government inherited it without knowing. The sources gathered here cannot settle which date is right, and this section gives none as certain.

Building in secret. The reactor was built at Dimona, outside the inspection system of the International Atomic Energy Agency, under Colonel Manes Pratt. At the peak of construction about 1,500 Israelis and some French workers were employed. The largest components, such as the reactor tank, passed through French customs described as parts of a desalination plant bound for Latin America. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] When the ground was broken is reported differently: the Nuclear Weapon Archive dates it to early 1958, while the American scientists who visited the site in 1961 were told that ground was broken in 1959. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] Neither date is confirmed by a primary document in the sources gathered here.

A heavy-water reactor needs heavy water, and here the record reaches beyond France. On 25 February 1959 Israel and Norway signed an agreement under which Norway, through the seller NORATOM, sold Israel 20 tons of heavy water, shipped via the United Kingdom. Richard Kerry, the American political officer in Oslo, warned that disclosure could embarrass Norway. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The Federation of American Scientists separately reports that the French Air Force secretly flew up to four tons of heavy water to Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] The two reports are not alternatives. Both may be true, and they describe two different channels, one commercial and documented in American records, the other military and reported by a single scholarly source.

The mechanism, then, ran through a few hands. In Israel, Ben-Gurion authorized the program and delegated it to Peres; Bergmann had made the failed American approach; Pratt built the site. In France, the decision was made at the level of ministers and the leadership of the CEA, and by the account of a man who was there it was pushed through over the reluctance of the agency's own head. The customs declaration and the absence of inspection kept it from anyone else. No legislature in either country, and no international body, had any part in it. Who paid for the construction is not stated in the sources for this section; the question of where Dimona's money came from was raised by American officials in December 1960 and is taken up later in this chapter.

De Gaulle's review, 1960. Under Charles de Gaulle the agreement came under review. In May 1960, according to the Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library, France pressed Israel to disclose the project and accept international inspection, threatening to withhold the reactor's fuel. De Gaulle offered to sell Israel fighter aircraft in exchange for halting work on the reprocessing plant. The Jewish Virtual Library adds that de Gaulle feared a scandal at a time when France's position was already strained by the Algerian war. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear]

The historian Shlomo Aronson puts the matter more sharply. He writes that de Gaulle "secretly advised Israel that he had decided to abrogate the 1957 accord," at the moment in May 1960 when Pinhas Lavon was reviving the affair that bore his name, and that this crisis was concealed and became widely known only years later. [cite:rs_cw4_aronson_ch6] The coincidence of dates meant that Ben-Gurion faced the threatened loss of the French reactor and the reopening of the Lavon affair in the same weeks; the political half of that story is told in the section on the Lavon affair reopened.

The compromise. The crisis was resolved by a compromise. France would supply the uranium and components already ordered and would not insist on inspections. Israel, for its part, would state that it had no intention of making atomic weapons, would not reprocess plutonium, would reveal the reactor, and would finish it without French help. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] The Nuclear Weapon Archive dates the agreement to November 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist]

What the compromise changed is disputed. On one reading, held by the Federation of American Scientists and the Nuclear Weapon Archive, it was a real change: Israel pledged no weapons and no reprocessing, agreed to reveal the reactor, and work on the plutonium plant halted. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] On the other, the Jewish Virtual Library says that little changed in practice. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] The second reading draws support from later work by Cohen and Burr, who document a concealed underground separation plant at Dimona; that evidence belongs to the American visits of the Kennedy years and is weighed in the sections that follow. The strongest form of the first case is that France did withdraw its help on the separation plant, which by Goldschmidt's account had always been the hard part, and that Israel made formal commitments it had not made before. The strongest form of the second is that the reactor, its oversized systems and the uranium already ordered all remained, that France gave up any claim to inspect, and that Israel was left to finish the work alone and unwatched.

The case for and the one voice against. Israel's leaders did not explain the program in public in these years, so its rationale has to be read from what was done rather than from what was said. The section on the Trident, later in this chapter, gives a former Israeli intelligence officer's account of Ben-Gurion's grand strategies, among which a nuclear deterrent stood beside a great-power ally and alliances beyond the Arab states. The strongest Israeli case, in those terms, is that a small state that had just been forced out of Sinai by both superpowers, and that had been refused a reactor without American conditions, sought a means of security that did not depend on anyone else's guarantee. The strongest case against was made at the time by Israelis themselves. According to Aronson, the only public voice of internal controversy over Dimona was the Public Committee for Nuclear Disarmament in the Middle East, led by Yeshayahu Leibowitz and Eliezer Livne. [cite:rs_cw4_aronson_ch6] The sources gathered here record the committee's existence and its leaders but not its arguments in its own words, and this section does not supply them.

What the record does not settle. Several things remain open. The exact date on which the 1957 agreement was signed cannot be fixed from these sources; the candidates are 30 September (as dated), 1 October (as signed, by the backdating accounts), 3 October and 30 October. The terms of the agreement are known in outline but not from its text: the reprocessing protocols were unwritten by design, and the fuller three-contract version with its weapons clause rests on one reported source. When construction at Dimona began is reported as early 1958 and as 1959. What the 1960 compromise actually changed at the site is disputed between sources that say the separation plant was halted and sources that say little changed. And the date of de Gaulle's message, as Aronson describes it, is given only as May 1960.

The familiar account is that Israel's nuclear program was a single secret decision, hidden from everyone. The record is more specific and more divided. It shows an open American track and a secret French one running side by side; a French decision taken by a few ministers and officials over the objection of the head of France's own atomic agency; a signature that, by the testimony of two men present, was dated to a government that had already fallen; and a French president who, within three years, tried to take back the most sensitive part of what his predecessors had given. How the United States learned of the reactor is told in [scene:cw4_dimona_discovery|how Washington found Dimona]. Kennedy's escalating confrontation with Israel's government over Dimona belongs to 1963 and is told in [scene:s11b|the 1963 letters].
