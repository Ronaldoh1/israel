Services Rendered: NILI, the Mule Corps and the Jewish Legion, 1915–1918

A common account treats the Balfour Declaration as a gift: a promise Britain handed over in November 1917 without receiving anything in return. The wartime record complicates that account. While the Declaration was being argued over in London, a ring of Jews in Palestine was spying on the Ottomans for British intelligence, and Jewish volunteers were serving in British uniform, first at Gallipoli and later in the campaign for Palestine itself. Among those volunteers was David Ben-Gurion. These services gave the Zionist movement standing with the British at the moment the cabinet was deciding. What the record does not show is that they bought the Declaration, and the difference matters.

The intelligence work began in 1915. A ring of about 40 members, based mainly in Zichron Ya'akov and Hadera, formed around the Aaronsohn siblings and Avshalom Feinberg. It took the name NILI. Its members gathered Ottoman military information and passed it to British intelligence, in part through a vessel run by the forerunner of MI6.[cite:wiki_nili] [cite:jc_nili_shindler] The CIA's own journal has reviewed its role.[cite:cia_review_spies_palestine] NILI worked inside an Ottoman province at war, in a period when, as the previous section shows, the Ottoman authorities were expelling foreign nationals, hanging Arab nationalists and, in April 1917, emptying Jaffa; the risk to its members was real.

Among its members was Aaron Aaronsohn, a noted agronomist. In 1916 he left Palestine to brief the British in person. He influenced British officials including Sir Mark Sykes and William Ormsby-Gore, both of whom were later pivotal in persuading the cabinet to issue the Balfour Declaration.[cite:jc_nili_shindler] This is the most direct documented line in this material between wartime service and the Declaration's authors: a man who had organized espionage for Britain inside Ottoman Palestine was talking, in person, to the officials who would press the cabinet. The line runs through influence on officials, not through any bargain on the record.

The Ottomans broke the ring in October 1917, a month before the Declaration was issued. Sarah Aaronsohn, one of the siblings at its center, took her own life after days of torture.[cite:wiki_nili] [cite:jc_nili_shindler]

The military service had begun earlier still, and it grew out of the wartime expulsions told in [scene:rd1_wwi|Palestine at war]. From Jews exiled to Egypt the British Army formed the Zion Mule Corps in 1915.[cite:rs_rd1_yishuv_wwi] Its 737 volunteers, largely refugees expelled from Palestine, served at Gallipoli that year.[cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion] Men driven out of the country by one side of the war were serving the other.

In 1917–18 the service became formal and larger. Five battalions of the Royal Fusiliers, numbered the 38th to the 42nd and known together as the Jewish Legion, fought in Allenby's Palestine campaign. One battalion trained in Nova Scotia, and it included David Ben-Gurion.[cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion] He had been expelled by the Ottomans in 1915 and had gone to Alexandria and then New York.[cite:rs_rd1_bghouse] That battalion's road back to Palestine therefore ran across the Atlantic.

The Legion was not the project of a united movement. Much of the British Zionist leadership opposed forming it. Chaim Weizmann and Ze'ev Jabotinsky were the exceptions who supported it.[cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion] Weizmann was by then, with Nahum Sokolow, one of the movement's de facto leaders in London, because the Zionist Organization, whose head offices were in Germany, had declared itself neutral.[cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] [cite:weizmann_papers_v9] That neutrality is a useful corrective. The organization as a body did not offer Britain its services. Individuals and groups did, sometimes against the wishes of their own leaders.

Put together, intelligence from inside Ottoman Palestine and soldiers in British uniform gave the movement standing with Britain at the moment the cabinet was deciding on the Declaration. The record places these services alongside other sources of British goodwill: Weizmann's wartime work as a chemist, and the restorationist sympathies documented in earlier chapters of this book. None of these, on the evidence gathered here, was the single cause. The Declaration's drafting, its addressee, the opposition to it inside the cabinet, and Britain's other wartime undertakings in the region belong to Stage Two and are told in [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration].

The familiar claim that the Declaration was given for nothing does not survive the record: a spy ring, a mule corps and five battalions were given to Britain's war effort, and one of the spies reached the officials who would argue the case in cabinet. But the opposite claim, that the Declaration was a payment for services, does not survive it either. The sources document influence, standing and timing. They do not document a bargain. The timing also cuts both ways. NILI collapsed in October 1917, before the Declaration was issued, and the Legion's battalions fought in 1917–18; the sources do not date that service closely enough against the cabinet's decision to weigh it. The record gathered here cannot measure how much weight any of these services carried in the cabinet's final decision, and it says so.
