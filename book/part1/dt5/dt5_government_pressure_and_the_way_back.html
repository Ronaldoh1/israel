Government Pressure, and the Way Back

Users have no First Amendment claim against a platform acting on its own account. They do have one when government is behind the decision. The Supreme Court has long held that an official may not do indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly: threatening a private intermediary into suppressing someone else's speech is itself government action. The framework dates from Bantam Books v. Sullivan (1963), and the Court restated it in 2024. [cite:dt5_scotusblog_vullo] This section sets out that test and then applies it, on the same footing, to the documented pressure of both the Biden and Trump administrations. It ends with the other half of the asymmetry: money.

The test: criticise, but do not threaten

Maria Vullo headed New York's Department of Financial Services under Governor Andrew Cuomo. After the Parkland school shooting in 2018 she urged the banks and insurers she regulated to weigh the reputational risk of doing business with gun-promotion groups such as the National Rifle Association. The NRA sued. On 30 May 2024 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously, in an opinion by Justice Sotomayor, that the NRA had plausibly alleged a violation. Vullo was "free to criticize the NRA and pursue the conceded violations of New York insurance law," but she could not "threaten enforcement actions" against regulated companies "to punish or suppress the NRA's gun-promotion advocacy." [cite:dt5_scotusblog_vullo] The line the Court drew runs between persuasion, which officials of any party may use, and coercion backed by regulatory power, which none may.

Four weeks later the Court decided Murthy v. Missouri, in which two states and several social media users argued that federal officials had pressured platforms to suppress their posts. On 26 June 2024 the Court ruled six to three, in an opinion by Justice Amy Coney Barrett, that the plaintiffs lacked standing to obtain an injunction; Justices Alito, Thomas and Gorsuch dissented. [cite:de11_scotus_murthy] The ruling did not decide whether the officials' contacts crossed the Vullo line. It decided only that these plaintiffs had not shown that they personally faced ongoing harm a court could stop.

The Biden-era record

The fullest account from inside a platform came after the case. In a letter to Representative Jim Jordan, released on 26 August 2024, Mark Zuckerberg wrote that in 2021 senior Biden administration officials "repeatedly pressured our teams for months to censor certain COVID-19 content, including humor and satire." He added: "I believe the government pressure was wrong, and I regret that we were not more outspoken about it." He also wrote that Meta itself made the final decisions to remove content. [cite:dt5_wrap_zuck] The White House replied that, "when confronted with a deadly pandemic, this Administration encouraged responsible actions to protect public health and safety," and that it believed companies should make "independent choices about the information they present." [cite:dt5_inquirer_wh] On his first day back in office, 20 January 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14149, which directs that no federal officer "engages in or facilitates any conduct that would unconstitutionally abridge the free speech" of Americans and orders the attorney general to "investigate the activities of the Federal Government over the last 4 years." [cite:dt5_eo14149]

The Trump-era record

The second Trump administration's own record soon raised the same question. On 15 September 2025 the late-night host Jimmy Kimmel said in his monologue that the MAGA movement was trying to cast the accused killer of the activist Charlie Kirk as "anything other than one of them." Two days later Brendan Carr, chairman of the Federal Communications Commission, which licenses broadcast stations, said on a podcast: "We can do this the easy way or the hard way." Within hours the station groups Nexstar and Sinclair pulled the show from their ABC stations, and ABC suspended it. Nexstar had a $6.2 billion purchase of Tegna awaiting the commission's approval. The show returned on 23 September. Some of the sharpest criticism came from Republicans: Senator Ted Cruz said the threat was "right out of Goodfellas," and Senator Rand Paul said Carr had no business threatening licences. Carr said Disney had "made the business decision" and denied making a threat. He was questioned by the Senate Commerce Committee in December 2025 and by a House subcommittee in January 2026. [cite:dt5_wiki_kimmel]

Earlier, on 24 July 2025, the commission approved the merger of Paramount, owner of CBS, with Skydance. Skydance committed to appoint a CBS News ombudsman to review complaints of bias for at least two years and affirmed that the company would have no diversity, equity and inclusion programmes. The approval followed Paramount's $16 million settlement of the president's lawsuit over the editing of a "60 Minutes" interview with Kamala Harris. Carr said, "It is time for a change." Commissioner Anna Gomez dissented, saying she "cannot support this order." [cite:dt5_cbs_paramount]

The Vullo test applies to both episodes in the same terms. An official who says a platform's choices are harmful is speaking; an official who ties those choices to a pending licence, merger or enforcement action may be coercing. Murthy left the Biden-era contacts untested on the merits, and the Kimmel episode led to hearings in both houses of Congress. Each administration rejects the charge that it coerced anyone. [cite:de11_scotus_murthy] [cite:dt5_wiki_kimmel] [cite:dt5_inquirer_wh] What the record establishes is narrower and more durable: officials of both parties, holding regulatory power over the companies concerned, pressed them about what to carry, and the companies moved.

The way back on money

The other half of the asymmetry, corporate spending, is harder to change, because Citizens United is a constitutional ruling. Congress cannot reverse it by ordinary statute; it can legislate in the space the Court left open, chiefly disclosure, which the Court upheld eight to one. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt5_elb_thomas_alone] That leaves three routes, each with its own record.

The first is a constitutional amendment, which needs two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-quarters of the states. [cite:dt5_bp_art5] The Democracy for All amendment, which would let Congress and the states set "reasonable" limits on raising and spending money in elections, reached the Senate floor in 2014 and failed to clear a filibuster, 54 votes to 42, on 11 September 2014. [cite:dt5_senate_sjres19] Senator Jeanne Shaheen reintroduced it in March 2025 with about forty Democratic and independent cosponsors. [cite:dt5_duckworth_dfa] The second route is a later Supreme Court overruling the decision, which requires a case and a majority willing to discard precedent. The third is a state law built to test the decision's limits. Montana's Initiative 194, which qualified on 17 August 2026, would define the powers of corporations, unions, nonprofits and other "artificial persons" so as to exclude spending on candidate elections, parties and ballot issues, on pain of losing the privilege of doing business there; news and editorial content are exempt. [cite:dt5_sosmt_2026] It is on the ballot on 3 November 2026, and the result is not yet known. Montana voters approved a ban on corporate election spending in 2012, which was overturned in 2013. The Montana Chamber of Commerce opposes the new measure, saying it "would prohibit Montana businesses and nonprofits from participating in the political process." [cite:dt5_bp_i194]

The test

Two claims from the Dark Enlightenment bear on this part. Thiel's, from 2009, is: "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible." [cite:de1_cato_thiel] Yarvin's is that prestigious institutions of media and academia, which he calls the Cathedral, act "as if they were a single organizational structure" to set the bounds of acceptable opinion. [cite:de1_tablet] [cite:de4_land_de]

Yarvin's claim, as a description of platform moderation, is not supported by this part's facts. The record shows companies taking opposite positions on the same slogan, reversing their own decisions, and changing their rules when owners or political conditions changed. That is concentrated power, documented, but not a single structure. Thiel's claim fares better than the book's thesis would like. It is documented that elected governments of both parties pressed private companies over lawful speech, and that some of the sharpest objections to the FCC's conduct came from the president's own party. Majorities and their officials are a real threat to free expression, which is the core of his worry. Against him, the protections that held were themselves democratic products: a Supreme Court applying a written Constitution, a unanimous ruling against a New York official, congressional questioning of the FCC chairman. The reform side has its own weak point: every route to limiting corporate spending gives legislatures or voters new power over political spending by groups, which is the ground on which the Montana Chamber of Commerce opposes I-194. [cite:dt5_bp_i194] On the evidence, the claim that democracy is incompatible with freedom is not supported; the narrower claim that democratic officials will use regulatory power against speech they dislike is documented, for both parties.
