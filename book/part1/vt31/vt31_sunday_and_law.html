Sunday, Precept and the Law of Obligation

The second half of the thesis concerns obligation: that the institution made attendance, confession and obedience compulsory, under spiritual penalty, where the inner-tradition texts told their readers to lay down no rule. As with the tithe, the institutional side can be traced in its own documents, from a Spanish council around 300 to the Catechism of 1992 and a German bishops' decree of 2012. The other side is harder to state, because the Gnostic texts are not one body of law. Some reject rules outright, some reinterpret them, and at least one sets out a demanding code of its own. This section lays out both records and does not smooth either.

From gathering to duty

Christians met on Sunday long before any law required it: the Didache and Justin describe the weekly gathering as a custom, not a command ([scene:vt20_first_communities|Part Twenty]; [scene:vt24_how_they_lived|How They Lived]). [cite:vt21_justin67] [cite:vt20_na_didache] The first penalty for staying away appears in Spain. Canon 21 of the Council of Elvira, held around 300–309, rules that anyone living in a city who fails to come to church for three Sundays (tres dominicas ad ecclesiam non accesserit) is to be kept from communion for a short time, "so that he may be seen to be corrected". [cite:vt31_elvira21] The Catholic Encyclopedia reads the canon as a brief excommunication meant to correct, not expel. [cite:vt31_ce_sunday] It is a local council's rule for city-dwellers, and its penalty is mild, but the logic is set: attendance is owed, and absence is punished by exclusion.

The state then added its own weight. On 7 March 321 Constantine ordered magistrates, city people and workshops to rest on "the venerable day of the Sun", leaving farm work free, as Part Twenty-One sets out ([scene:vt21_sun_and_title|Sun, Sunday and the Chief Priest's Title]). [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] The Council of Laodicea in the later fourth century told the faithful to abstain from work on the Lord's day "as far as possible", and the Apostolic Constitutions of the same period prescribed both hearing Mass and resting, attributing the rule to the apostles. [cite:vt31_ce_sunday] In Gaul, the Council of Agde of 506, presided over by Caesarius of Arles (the same bishop who first theorised the tithe), required lay people to receive communion at Christmas, Easter and Pentecost. [cite:vt31_ce_agde] [cite:vt31_schaff_agde] Caesarius also insisted that the faithful stay for the whole Mass rather than leave after the readings. [cite:vt31_ce_sunday] From the eighth century, local councils forbade servile work, markets, lawsuits and public oaths on Sunday, and Sunday law took roughly its modern shape. Not every step pointed toward strictness: the Council of Orléans in 538 rejected attempts to apply the Jewish Sabbath code to Sunday. [cite:vt31_ce_sunday]

Lateran IV: the minimum fixed, the penalty named

The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 set the rule that still underlies Catholic practice. Every adult was to confess all sins to his or her own parish priest "at least once a year" and to receive the Eucharist at least at Easter. Anyone who failed was to be "cut off from the Church" while alive and "deprived of Christian burial" at death. [cite:vt31_lateran4] The same council also ordered tithes paid before secular taxes. [cite:vt31_lateran4] In medieval terms this joined three things into one system of membership: a priest one had to confess to, a payment one had to make, and a sanction (exclusion in life and in burial) for those who did not.

The law today

The 1983 Code of Canon Law calls Sunday "the primordial holy day of obligation" and states that on Sundays and holy days "the faithful are obliged to participate in the Mass" and to avoid work that hinders worship and rest. [cite:vt31_cic1246] The Catechism repeats the obligation and attaches its weight: "Those who deliberately fail in this obligation commit a grave sin", unless excused for a serious reason such as illness or the care of infants, or dispensed by their pastor. [cite:vt31_ccc2181] It lists five precepts of the Church, which it describes as a necessary minimum of prayer and moral effort: Mass on Sundays and holy days, confession at least once a year, communion at least at Easter, keeping the holy days, and the days of fasting and abstinence. Beside them stands the duty to provide for the Church's material needs. [cite:vt31_ccc2043] The Catechism also asks the state to help: public authorities should guarantee time for rest and worship, and Christians "should seek recognition of Sundays and the Church's holy days as legal holidays". [cite:vt31_ccc2181]

The practice of German Catholics shows how far law and life have parted. In 2025 about 6.8 per cent of the country's 19.2 million registered Catholics attended Sunday Mass. [cite:vt31_cwr_de2025] Few of the rest are disciplined for missing Mass. The penalties that are enforced in practice concern membership and money.

Leaving, and what it costs

Under the 1983 Code, "an apostate from the faith, a heretic or a schismatic incurs a latae sententiae excommunication", an exclusion that takes effect automatically, without a trial. [cite:vt31_intratext_1364] The late Roman state once backed such lines with civil penalties. Theodosius I legislated harshly against Christians who abandoned the faith, and Manichaeans lost the right to make or benefit from wills. [cite:vt31_wiki_theod_apost] Today the sharpest case is German. In September 2012 the German bishops issued a decree, approved by the Holy See after three years of talks, on Catholics who formally leave the Church at a state registry, which is the act that ends church-tax liability. Such a person may not receive the sacraments except a blessing before death, may not work for church institutions such as schools and hospitals, may not serve as a godparent, and may be refused a church funeral if there is no sign of repentance. The decree says that "one cannot partly leave the Church". [cite:vt31_nss_2012] [cite:vt31_npr_2012] On 26 September 2012 the Federal Administrative Court in Leipzig ruled against the canon lawyer Hartmut Zapp, who had sought to stop paying the tax while remaining a Catholic. [cite:vt31_nss_2012] The bishops' stated position, given by their president Robert Zollitsch, was that the church is a community of faith that co-exists alongside the legal order and the two "cannot be separated". The reform group We Are Church replied that the decree worked like an excommunication though it was not formally one. [cite:vt31_npr_2012] The tithe and the precept, separated in the Code, meet again here: in Germany, leaving the tax roll is treated as leaving the Church.

What the other texts say about rules

Against this law of obligation the thesis sets a handful of sayings, set out in full for the second and third centuries in [scene:vt24_how_they_lived|How They Lived]. In the Gospel of Mary the departing Saviour tells the disciples to lay down no rules beyond his own and to make no law "like the lawgiver" ([scene:vt26_gospel_of_mary|Part Twenty-Six]); Thomas 14 rejects fasting, prayer and alms as outward duties. [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt24_gthom2] The Gospel of Philip ties freedom to knowledge: "He who has knowledge of the truth is a free man", and "Ignorance is a slave. Knowledge is freedom." [cite:vt31_gphil_free]

The same texts also complicate the thesis. Thomas 27 asks its readers to keep the Sabbath "as a Sabbath", and Ptolemy's Letter to Flora keeps the Ten Commandments, reinterpreting fasting and the Sabbath rather than abolishing them; both are discussed in Part Twenty-Four. [cite:vt24_gthom2] [cite:vt24_ect_flora] Pistis Sophia, a later text, ends with a long code of renunciation, listing sins its hearers must give up. [cite:vt26_mead_ch102] What these texts reject is a law imposed from outside and enforced by an office. They do not reject discipline as such.

Two things should be kept apart when the two records are set side by side. One is a body of law that can be dated, enforced and appealed. The other is a set of sayings whose communities left no rulebook, so there is no way to show they never made rules of their own. On the documents alone, the institution built a law of obligation step by step, and several inner-tradition texts tell their readers not to. Whether those readers lived without rules is the question the next section takes up.
