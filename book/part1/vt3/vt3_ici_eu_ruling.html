The Property-Tax Case

The second money question that the 1929 settlement leaves in Italian public life is a tax not paid. It is often reported as "the Church's ICI," after the municipal property tax (imposta comunale sugli immobili) that Italy levied until 2011. The phrase is too narrow in one direction and too broad in another. The exemption at issue was not in the Lateran Pacts, whose tax exemptions cover only the basilicas, palaces and institutions listed in Articles 13 to 16 of the Treaty. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] It was in Italian tax law, and it covered all "non-commercial entities": not only parishes, dioceses and religious institutes, but bodies active in social assistance, health, education and sport, including amateur sports clubs. [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012] Church bodies were among those exempted, which is why the case has been reported as a Church case; the beneficiaries were a wider class.

The Commission's decision, 2012

The problem lay in a 2006 amendment. It extended the exemption to property where non-commercial entities carried on activities "not exclusively of a commercial nature," which opened it to buildings partly used for paying activities such as schools, clinics and accommodation. [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012] Competitors complained to the European Commission, which opened a formal investigation under the state-aid rules of the European Union treaties (case SA.20829). [cite:vt3_ec_dec2012]

The Commission decided on 19 December 2012. It found that the ICI exemption, as it applied from 2006 to 2011, was new state aid and "incompatible with the internal market." [cite:vt3_ec_dec2012] It found no aid in the replacement tax, the IMU, which from 1 January 2012 limited the exemption to premises where non-commercial entities carried on non-economic activities, nor in a separate income-tax provision that the Commission said "does not confer any selective advantage on ecclesiastical institutions or amateur sports clubs." [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012] [cite:vt3_ec_dec2012] Then it declined to order repayment. Italy had argued that it could not identify, years after the fact, which properties had been used for which activities, and the Commission accepted that recovery was "objectively impossible." [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012] The competition commissioner, Joaquín Almunia, stated the principle the decision rested on: non-profit bodies play an important social role, but when they compete with commercial operators they should not gain an undue advantage. [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012]

The courts, 2016 and 2018

Two of the competitors went to court: Scuola Elementare Maria Montessori, a private school, and Pietro Ferracci, who ran a bed and breakfast. On 15 September 2016 the EU General Court held their actions admissible but dismissed them (cases T-220/13 and T-219/13). [cite:vt3_curia_pr2018] The school and the Commission appealed.

On 6 November 2018 the Court of Justice gave judgment in Joined Cases C-622/16 P to C-624/16 P. It agreed that competitors could bring the action. On recovery it reversed the lower court. Repayment, the Court held, is the logical consequence of a finding of unlawful aid, and it can be treated as "objectively and absolutely impossible" only if the Commission shows, after a scrupulous examination, both that the difficulties are real and that no alternative method of recovery exists, including partial recovery. [cite:vt3_curia_pr2018] The Commission had relied only on gaps in the land register and the tax databases. The Court annulled the part of the 2012 decision that declined to order recovery. [cite:vt3_curia_pr2018] It upheld the finding that the IMU exemption was not state aid. [cite:vt3_curia_pr2018] Commentators drew the rule out plainly: difficulties caused by a state's own poor records do not make recovery impossible. [cite:vt3_lexxion_2018]

The recovery, 2023 to October 2026

The Commission took a new decision on 3 March 2023. It ordered Italy to recover the unpaid ICI for 2006 to 2011 on property used for economic activities, case by case; property used for non-economic purposes was excluded. It accepted that the Italian authorities faced real difficulty in identifying beneficiaries but held that this did not rule out at least partial recovery. [cite:vt3_eunews_2023] [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] It did not state an amount.

Italy legislated the procedure eighteen months later, in Article 16-bis of Decree-Law 131 of 16 September 2024, converted into law on 14 November 2024. [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] The implementing decree of the prime minister's office, due by 14 January 2025, was late. [cite:vt3_avv_ici2025] It was signed on 23 December 2025. [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] Its scope is narrow. It applies only to entities whose IMU and TASI returns for 2012 or 2013 showed liabilities above 50,000 euros a year; nothing is due where the aid stayed within the EU's limits for small amounts of aid or counts as compensation for services of general economic interest; where the effective local rate cannot be identified, an average rate of 5.5 per thousand applies; and compound interest runs from the date the aid was received. [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] Activities of religion and worship are non-economic by definition, so no recovery applies to them. [cite:vt3_avv_ici2025]

The deadlines have moved twice. The declaration was first due by 31 March 2026. [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] [cite:vt3_io_ici2026] A further decree of 26 March 2026, published on 31 March, extended it to 30 September 2026. [cite:vt3_ff_proroga] As of 21 September 2026 the declaration was due by 30 September and payment by 30 October 2026, with sums over 100,000 euros payable in four quarterly instalments. [cite:vt3_ff_sept2026] As of 21 October 2026, no figure for the number of declarations filed or the sums recovered had been published, and the payment deadline had not yet passed. [cite:vt3_io_ici2026] The only widely quoted estimate of the sums at stake is one attributed to ANCI, the association of Italian municipalities, of roughly 100,000 properties and 4 to 5 billion euros; it is not an official figure, and the threshold and exclusions in the 2025 decree mean the amount actually due may be well below it. [cite:vt3_money_anci] [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025]

Who benefits today, and what it costs

Set side by side, the four sections of this part allow a plain accounting. The Holy See still holds what it gained in 1929: a sovereign territory, protected properties in Rome and Castel Gandolfo, and the settlement of its claims, none of which the 1984 revision reopened. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] [cite:vt3_l121_1985] The Church in Italy, a different subject from the Holy See, receives the largest recurring flow: about 1.06 billion euros a year through the eight per thousand, distributed by its bishops' conference among clergy pay, worship and charity, on the strength of the choices of about 27 per cent of taxpayers. [cite:vt3_teleborsa_8x2026] [cite:vt3_cei_ripart2026] Italy, for its part, no longer pays priests from its budget, no longer has a state religion, and in 2012 closed the property-tax exemption for economic activity from that year on. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_ec_ip2012]

What remains unpaid is the ICI for 2006 to 2011, owed by a class of non-commercial entities of which Church bodies are a part. Italy argued in 2012 that the sum could not be recovered, was told by the Court of Justice in 2018 that it could, and as of October 2026 was still waiting for the first payments under a procedure designed to collect only from the largest debtors. [cite:vt3_curia_pr2018] [cite:vt3_dpcm_2025] [cite:vt3_ff_sept2026] Italy's stated position throughout was that the records needed to identify the debtors did not exist; the Commission's stated position since 2023 is that partial recovery is possible all the same. [cite:vt3_ec_dec2012] [cite:vt3_eunews_2023]
