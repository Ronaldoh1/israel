What Americans Believe, and What the Data Show

Fifteen parts of this book have set the promise beside the record, mechanism by mechanism. This part asks a different question: why the promise keeps its hold on people whose own lives, in many of the ways measured so far, do not match it. The answer has three parts. Belief in the Dream has in fact fallen, and fallen far, in the polls. What remains of it leans on beliefs about mobility that the data do not support, or support only for some groups. And there is a well-studied set of habits of mind that lead people to read the outcome of a structure as a verdict on someone's character.

How much belief is left

The question "does the American Dream still hold true?" has been asked often enough to show a direction, though not by one pollster with one wording. In 2012 the Public Religion Research Institute found 53 per cent of Americans saying the Dream still held; by 2014 it found 42 per cent. [cite:ad16_colpol_apnorc] The Wall Street Journal and NORC, the research centre at the University of Chicago, asked whether the Dream "still holds true," "once held true but not anymore" or "never held true." In 2023, 36 per cent chose the first answer, 45 per cent the second and 18 per cent the third. [cite:ad16_axios_wsj23] In July 2025 the same poll found 31 per cent, 46 per cent and 23 per cent; only a quarter said they had a good chance of improving their own standard of living, the lowest since the question was first asked in 1987, and 78 per cent were not confident that their children's generation would be better off. [cite:ad1_wsjnorc25] In April 2026 the AP-NORC poll, with a margin of error of 2.6 points, found 34 per cent saying the Dream (defined as "if you work hard, you'll get ahead") still holds and half saying it once did. [cite:ad1_apnorc26] [cite:ad16_colpol_apnorc] The wording differs across these polls, so the drop from 53 to about a third is a direction, not a precise measure. The direction is the same in every series.

A longer series with a single wording comes from Gallup, which every January asks whether Americans are satisfied with "the opportunity for a person in this nation to get ahead by working hard." Satisfaction stood at 76 per cent in 2001 and 77 per cent in 2002, fell to 53 per cent in 2012 after the financial crisis, recovered to 72 per cent in January 2020, and dropped to 56 per cent in 2024. [cite:ad16_gallup_opp24] In 2026 it was 52 per cent, with Republicans at 85 per cent and Democrats at 24 per cent, a 61-point gap, the widest Gallup has recorded on the question. [cite:ad16_brookings_opp26] A partisan gap that wide shows the answer measures mood about the country as much as any household's own experience.

When people are asked about their own lives the picture is less bleak. Pew Research Center asked the same question in December 2022 and April 2024: had respondents "achieved the American Dream," were they "on your way," or was it "out of reach for you"? The answers barely moved: 30, 37 and 31 per cent in 2022; 31, 36 and 30 per cent in 2024. [cite:ad16_pew_top24] Two-thirds, in other words, still placed themselves inside the Dream or on the road to it. In the 2024 survey, 64 per cent of upper-income adults said the Dream was still possible against 39 per cent of lower-income adults, a 25-point gap. Fifteen per cent of Black adults and 19 per cent of Hispanic adults said they had already achieved it, against 39 per cent of white adults, but 48 and 47 per cent said they were on their way. [cite:ad16_pew_2024] Those gaps follow the gaps in income and wealth documented in [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|Part Twelve] and [scene:ad13_black_americans|Part Thirteen]; they are not traits of any group.

What people believe about mobility

The Dream as taught is a claim about mobility: that a child born poor can, with effort, rise. [scene:ad3_absolute_mobility|Part Three] measured it. Economists distinguish absolute mobility, whether children earn more than their parents did at the same age, from relative mobility, whether children move up the ranking. The relative measure is usually stated with quintiles: the population is split into five equal groups by income, and the question is how often a child born in the bottom fifth reaches the top fifth.

The economists Alberto Alesina, Stefanie Stantcheva and Edoardo Teso asked about 4,700 Americans, and about 7,900 people in Britain, France, Italy and Sweden, to estimate exactly that. Americans guessed that 11.7 in 100 children from the bottom fifth would reach the top fifth; the benchmark the authors used was 7.8. That is an overestimate by half (11.7 ÷ 7.8 = 1.5). Their guess of how many stay at the bottom, 32.2, was close to the actual 33.1. Europeans erred the other way, underestimating the chance of rising and overestimating the chance of being stuck. [cite:ad16_ast_aer] When the researchers showed respondents pessimistic information about mobility, both left- and right-leaning Americans became more pessimistic, but only those on the left became more supportive of policies aimed at equal opportunity; those on the right changed none of their policy views. [cite:ad16_richmond_ast]

Psychologists have found the same overestimate with other methods. Michael Kraus and Jacinth Tan reported in 2015 that Americans substantially overestimated both education and income mobility; a pre-registered replication by Kraus with 763 adults found the effect again, larger when people judged mobility for "people like me," and larger among those who saw themselves as higher in social class and among conservatives. [cite:ad16_kraus_frontiers] Shai Davidai and Thomas Gilovich, surveying more than 3,000 adults, found that Americans overestimate upward mobility and underestimate downward mobility, and that poorer respondents and conservatives believed in more mobility than richer ones and liberals. [cite:ad16_cornell_dg]

The finding is not unanimous, and the dissent matters. Alexander Chambers, Lawton Swan and Martin Heesacker asked 410 adults to estimate movement between quintiles and found that they underestimated mobility; 56 per cent said it had declined over forty years, when relative mobility had in fact stayed roughly stable, and only 15 per cent answered correctly. Liberals underestimated most. [cite:ad16_jr_chambers] The difference lies partly in what is asked. Relative mobility has held steady for decades; absolute mobility, the share of children out-earning their parents, fell from about nine in ten for children born in 1940 to about half for children born in the 1980s. [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] A respondent who says mobility has fallen is wrong about ranks and right about living standards. What the studies agree on is that most people's picture of mobility is inaccurate, and that the direction of the error follows politics.

Why structure gets read as character

Several lines of research try to explain why people, including people the system has not served well, keep a picture of it as fair. The oldest is the "just-world" idea, from the social psychologist Melvin Lerner: people want to believe that others get what they deserve, because the alternative, a world in which effort and outcome come apart, is frightening. The economists Roland Bénabou and Jean Tirole built that idea into a formal model in 2006. People, they argued, are motivated to believe that effort is rewarded, partly because the belief itself keeps them working; societies can settle into an "American" pattern, with widespread just-world belief and modest redistribution, or a "European" one, with more pessimism about effort and a larger welfare state. [cite:ad16_bt_nber] In their account the belief is not simply false consciousness: it changes how hard people work, and therefore what they earn.

System-justification theory, developed by John Jost and colleagues from 1994, goes further. It holds that people are motivated to defend the social arrangements they live under, and its strongest form predicted that the disadvantaged would sometimes defend those arrangements more than the advantaged, because "those who suffer the most also have the most to explain, justify, and rationalize." In one 2003 study, lower-income respondents were more likely than higher-income ones to say large income differences were needed to make people work hard. [cite:ad16_caricati] That strong form has not held up. Mark Brandt's 2013 test, using large surveys in the United States and many other countries, found no support for it: people of higher status defended the system as much as, or more than, people of lower status. [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt] [cite:ad16_caricati] The weaker claim, that people in general lean toward seeing their system as legitimate, survives; the claim that the poor are its most loyal defenders does not.

A third line concerns meritocracy, the belief that rewards follow talent and effort. The sociologist Jonathan Mijs, using about 25 years of International Social Survey Programme data, found that "the more unequal a society, the more likely its citizens are to explain success in meritocratic terms," and that they give less weight to family wealth and connections. [cite:ad16_mijs] The philosopher Michael Sandel argued in The Tyranny of Merit (2020) that politicians of both parties promised that a college degree would secure success, and that the creed "leads to hubris among the winners and humiliation for those who lose out." He pointed to selective colleges, where about 70 per cent of students come from wealthy families and about 3 per cent from the bottom fifth. [cite:ad16_duke_sandel] [scene:ad7_college_cost_pell|Part Seven] showed how college became the main ladder and its price; Sandel's point is about what happens to the people for whom it was not.

The last mechanism is the simplest. Success stories are told by those who succeeded. Of every 100 private business establishments opened in March 2013, about 80 were still operating a year later, about 51 after five years and about 35 after ten; 65 had closed. [cite:ad16_bls_survival] The founders who are interviewed, profiled and quoted come from the 35, and the lesson drawn from their stories (persistence pays) is drawn from a sample with the failures removed. This is survivorship bias: a conclusion that looks like a law because the counter-examples are no longer visible.

Where the belief is right

None of this means the belief is simply an error. Effort does change outcomes: [scene:ad2_what_holds|Part Two] documented that a full-time, year-round worker had an official poverty rate of 1.6 per cent, that the children of immigrants have climbed faster than the children of the American-born for more than a century, and that Hispanic Americans move up strongly across generations. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer] [cite:ad2_oi_race_hisp] The 35 businesses that survived ten years are real, and their owners mostly did work hard. Bénabou and Tirole's model, and the evidence behind it, suggest that belief in reward for effort is partly self-fulfilling: people who hold it work more. [cite:ad16_bt_nber] The case against the belief is narrower than "the Dream is a lie." It is that the belief overstates how often effort alone moves a person from the bottom to the top, and that this overstatement makes the structures documented in earlier parts harder to see.

Interpretation, not finding: the belief as the system's working part

The Sophia platform reads the persistence of the belief as the central mechanism of the Dream itself: a story told so that people keep working inside a system that takes more from them than it returns, and blame themselves rather than the design when it does not pay. In this reading the overestimate of mobility, the meritocratic explanation of inequality and survivorship bias are not separate quirks but one function. The strongest counter-readings are three. First, Brandt's finding that the disadvantaged do not defend the system more than others cuts against the idea that belief is installed in those it harms; belief is highest among those it has served. Second, belief has fallen sharply over two decades, after the financial crisis and again since 2020, which suggests people revise it in light of experience rather than hold it fixed. Third, a belief that raises effort can raise incomes, as Bénabou and Tirole argue, so its effects are not only extractive. [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt] [cite:ad16_gallup_opp24] [cite:ad16_bt_nber]
