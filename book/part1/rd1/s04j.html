Who Bought the Land, From Whom, and at What Cost

A common belief holds that the British government sold Palestinian land to outside Zionist buyers, and that this happened with little scrutiny or resistance. The documented mechanism was different in almost every respect. The land was bought in private transactions, mostly from absentee landowners; the British Mandate regulated the process rather than selling; and resistance was real, organized and, in at least one case, fought through the courts for years.

The story of the largest single block of land begins before Zionism. In 1872 the Ottoman government itself sold the Jezreel Valley, Marj ibn Amir, to the Sursock family of Beirut, wealthy Greek Orthodox Christians. Between 1901 and 1925, and mainly in the 1920s, the family sold roughly 60,000 to 75,000 dunams to Zionist purchasing organizations for a fraction of the land's later value. These Sursock Purchases alone account for approximately 22 percent of all Jewish land acquired in Palestine through 1948, and 58 percent of all land bought specifically from absentee landlords.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:sursockhouse_rothschild_purchases] This was a private family selling private property, not a government transferring state land.

Three organizations did the buying and holding. The Jewish National Fund held purchased land as inalienable national property. The Palestine Land Development Company did the negotiating, led by Yehoshua Hankin, who had worked with the Sursock family since 1891, personally negotiated the Sursock deal and most of the major Jezreel Valley acquisitions, completed the first significant purchase there in 1910, and became head of the company in 1932. A third, parallel body, the Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, founded in 1924 by Edmond de Rothschild, became the largest single Jewish landowner in Palestine.[cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] Under the property law of the time these purchases were legal. The point is not illegality but who bought, and how.

The question of how buyers knew where to buy has a bureaucratic answer. In 1907 the Zionist Organization sent Arthur Ruppin, a German-trained lawyer and economist, on a pilot study of colonization in Palestine. In 1908 he opened the Palestine Office in Jaffa and founded the Palestine Land Development Company as its purchasing arm. He had identified the strategic importance of the Jezreel Valley as early as 1907, years before the Sursock deal closed, and became known as "the father of Jewish settlement in Palestine." Several independent sources, including a peer-reviewed article in History of European Ideas and a Haaretz retrospective, also document that Ruppin believed the realization of Zionism required "racial purity" among Jews, regarded Ashkenazi Jews as racially superior to Sephardi and Yemenite Jews, and was influenced by the racial-theory literature that later influenced Nazi race science.[cite:wiki_arthur_ruppin] [cite:haaretz_ruppin_retrospective] [cite:tandfonline_ruppin_weltanschauung] These views shaped whom his office prioritized for settlement. They are documented about him as an individual; they do not show that everyone involved in settlement shared them. Both facts about Ruppin, the institutional achievement and the racial ideology, belong in an accurate account.

The money ran on two tracks. The Jewish Colonial Trust was the overall financial instrument, and its subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, opened in Jaffa in 1902, financed purchases directly and ran a network of credit unions offering long-term agricultural loans to settlers. Herzl's own framing was that "Settlers in the Land of Israel need credit rather than charity." Keren Hayesod, from 1920, raised donations for settlement infrastructure.[cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust] [cite:palquest_wzo_funding] By the early Mandate period, land bought from absentee owners went for roughly six Palestine pounds per dunam.[cite:emory_zionist_land_acquisition] Financing was not always planned in advance: agents such as Hankin sometimes bought first, at personal financial risk, and sought reimbursement afterward. The Trust's ownership was wide, with more than 100,000 subscribers by 1899, but its first 100 shares were "founders' shares" carrying voting rights equal to all the others combined, guaranteeing the founding leadership permanent control.[cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] The structure lasted: as late as 1969 the Trust controlled 88.3 percent of Bank Leumi's voting ordinary shares while holding 23.5 percent of its capital.

What made sale and eviction a single lawful act was Ottoman land law. It distinguished mulk, private freehold, from miri, state-leased land treated in practice as owned by whoever held the registered rights. Tenant farmers who had worked land for generations under hereditary arrangements, paying tithes to absentee landlords, typically held no registered title. When the landlord sold, they had no ownership claim to assert.[cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] [cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws] The gap predated Zionism; it was used, not invented.

Legal obstacles did exist. From 1881–82 the Ottoman government barred foreign Jews from settling in Palestine specifically; by March 1883 only Ottoman Jewish citizens could buy land there; and in 1892 Sultan Abdul Hamid II required imperial approval even for them. Scholars describe these rules as real obstacles rather than an absolute prohibition, and by 1918 total Jewish-held land stood at approximately 418,000 dunams, around 2 percent of the territory.[cite:wiki_jewish_land_purchase_full] The restrictions were circumvented in three documented ways: purchases by Ottoman Jewish citizens later transferred to Zionist bodies; European passport holders invoking the capitulations; and, in 1890s State Department correspondence, Russian Jewish emigrants naturalizing in America and returning to claim United States consular protection, in a case involving Simon Ben Nachman Lowenstein. American diplomats reported the Ottoman position that the government "does not regard it to its interest to have too large a number of any race or nationality established there."[cite:state_gov_1892_naturalization] Individual presence was tolerated; organized colonization toward territorial concentration was what the Ottomans sought to prevent. The research behind this book found no record of any formal consultation with Palestinian Arab or Old Yishuv leadership before organized purchasing began.

The cost fell on tenants. In the largest single group of Sursock-related purchases, 1,746 Arab farming families, 8,730 people, were evicted. Owed nothing in law, they were paid roughly $17 per person as a goodwill settlement. Across all Sursock-linked purchases, an estimated 20 to 25 villages were depopulated. Some in the Arab community regarded selling to Jewish buyers as a serious betrayal, and in at least one case negotiations stalled for years because the sellers did not want to be seen as responsible for the evacuation.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] These figures describe the best-documented case, not every transaction.

Resistance reached the courts. In 1929 the Jewish National Fund bought 30,800 dunams at Wadi al-Hawarith at auction from absentee landlords. The Bedouin Palestinian tenants refused to leave, and Awni Abd al-Hadi represented them through a multi-year legal battle. The final eviction order came on June 15, 1933, and the Istiqlal Party, the Palestine Communist Party and the Congress of Youth called strikes and issued protests.[cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith] Many sales met less opposition, but resistance was not absent.

Britain's role was inconsistent. Between 1920 and 1940 it enacted five laws to protect tenant cultivators from eviction, all of which failed in practice through loopholes. In 1938 its authorities let Zionist officials copy land-registration and taxation records of Arab ownership to facilitate further purchases.[cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] The same period also saw British immigration restrictions cut sharply the other way. Britain was not the seller, but neither was it neutral.

Between the sellers and the buyers stood people like Avraham Albert Antébi, a Sephardi figure active in Jerusalem from 1897 to 1916 whose loan fund, the Caisse de Prêts, served both the established Old Yishuv and new settlers, and who dealt with Arab landowners and Ottoman officials alike. Research on his correspondence shows the existing Jewish communities neither uniformly supportive of the purchases nor absent from them, but entangled with every side of the transactions.[cite:shofar_antebi_sephardim]
