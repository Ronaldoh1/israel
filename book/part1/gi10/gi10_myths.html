Two Myths: The Inscription and the Stripes

The third class is made of claims for which there is no supporting evidence, or for which the documented origin is something else. Two have travelled furthest: that the Knesset bears an inscription promising Israel the land from the Nile to the Euphrates, and that the two blue stripes on Israel's flag stand for those rivers. Both have been repeated by heads of state and in the founding charter of an armed movement. Both are myths. This section sets out who made each claim, when, and what the record shows.

The Knesset inscription

The earliest dated version found for this book is a newspaper error that was corrected. On 21 January 1980 the Christian Science Monitor ran a story, "Egyptians dispute Sadat bid to give Nile water to Israel," which attributed the slogan "From the Nile to the Euphrates" to the Israeli parliament building. A week later the paper printed a correction: "No such inscription is on the building." [cite:gi10_csm_1980]

The claim did not stop there. Daniel Pipes, in his 1994 survey of the Nile-to-Euphrates charge, recorded that Syria's president Hafiz al-Asad, his defence minister Mustafa Tlas and Iran's president had all said that the words "The Land of Israel from the Euphrates to the Nile" were carved over the Knesset's entrance, citing a report in Al-Jazira of 17 January 1982. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Yasser Arafat added a twist in a speech to the Arab League's Jerusalem Committee on 9 April 1990: the sign, he said, "remained for ten years. They were advised to remove it, but now they have put it back up." [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Pipes observed that no one had ever seen it, and that a visitor who toured the building without finding it was told "it was removed in anticipation of his visit." [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny]

Pipes was a partisan writer, and a finding that rests on him alone would be weak. This one does not. The 1980 correction came from a newspaper with no stake in the dispute, which checked its own story and withdrew the claim. [cite:gi10_csm_1980] The Knesset's own description of its ceremonial Chagall State Hall lists three tapestries, twelve floor mosaics and a wall mosaic, on themes from the Bible, Jerusalem and the return of exiles, with no map and no border inscription. [cite:gi10_jvl_chagall] No photograph of the inscription and no date of its supposed removal or replacement appear in any source found for this book. The claim that it was removed before each visit is the kind that cannot be tested, because it explains away every failure to find it. The verdict is myth: there is no evidence that the inscription exists or ever existed.

The flag's stripes

In an interview with Playboy published in September 1988, Arafat said that the two horizontal blue lines on Israel's flag represent the Nile and the Euphrates, "and in between is Israel." [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The same year, the Covenant of the Islamic Resistance Movement, Hamas, issued on 18 August 1988, stated in Article 32: "After Palestine, the Zionists aspire to expand from the Nile to the Euphrates." [cite:hm_avalon_charter] The covenant does not mention the flag; it is quoted here as Hamas's own stated position, and its treatment of the "Zionists" as a single actor is the collective attribution this volume does not make. The flag reading has since been repeated in many places. [cite:gi10_wiki_flag]

The flag's history is documented, and it predates the State of Israel by half a century. Israel's Ministry of Foreign Affairs states that "the blue stripes on the Zionist flag were inspired by the stripes on the tallit (prayer shawl)," the shawl worn in Jewish prayer, and that Zionist tradition credits the design to David Wolffsohn, with the flag adopted by the Zionist movement at Basel. [cite:gi10_mfa_flag] Wolffsohn's own account reads: "The talith (prayer shawl) with which we wrap ourselves when we pray: that is our symbol." [cite:gi10_wiki_flag] Blue and white had been proposed as Jewish colours in Ludwig August Frankl's poem "Juda's Farben" in 1864, and the settlement of Rishon LeZion carried a blue-and-white flag with a Star of David in 1885. [cite:gi10_wiki_flag] The Provisional Council of State voted unanimously on 28 October 1948 to adopt the Zionist flag as the flag of the state. [cite:gi10_mfa_flag] Its proclamation specified two dark sky-blue stripes 25 cm wide on a white field and a Star of David between them; it said nothing about rivers. [cite:gi10_wiki_flag]

The design therefore came from a prayer garment, decades before there was a state with borders to claim. The verdict is myth. Some Arab writers have said the same: the Arab writer Saqr Abu Fakhr, in Al-Hayat in November 1997, listed the Nile-to-Euphrates belief among popular misconceptions about Jews that the evidence refutes. [cite:gi10_wiki_flag]

Why the distinction matters

The myths share a structure. Each takes an object that every visitor to Israel can see, a parliament, a flag or a coin, and attaches to it a territorial meaning that no Israeli law, platform or official statement gives it. Each was repeated by leaders of states and movements then at war or in conflict with Israel, and each claims to have found a secret plan written into the state's public symbols. None survives a check of the object itself. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] [cite:gi10_csm_1980] [cite:gi10_mfa_flag] [cite:gi10_boi_coins]

They also do damage to the question this volume asks. The documented evidence for territorial maximalism in Israeli politics is substantial, and it lies elsewhere: in the 1977 Likud platform's statement that between the sea and the Jordan "there will only be Israeli sovereignty", in the 2022 coalition guidelines' claim of an "exclusive and inalienable right to all parts of the Land of Israel", in the annexation motions of 2025 and the maps a prime minister showed at the United Nations ([scene:gi5_likud|Part Five], [scene:gi6_votes|Part Six], [scene:gi7_maps|Part Seven]). [cite:gi5_jvl_likud1977] [cite:gi5_jl_guidelines] [cite:wb_toi_71_13] That evidence concerns the West Bank, Gaza and the Golan, not the Nile. An argument built on an inscription no one has seen can be dismissed in a sentence, and the documented record dismissed with it. Keeping the three classes apart is what lets the real documents be weighed on their own terms.
