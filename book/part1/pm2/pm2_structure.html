Four Bodies, One Network: How AIPAC Is Built

AIPAC is not one organization but four, each a different kind of legal entity, each allowed to do things the others may not. Seen from outside, they share a name, a headquarters and a mission. In law they are separate, and the separation is what lets the network work through every channel described in Part One. This section sets out the four, what each is permitted to do, how large each is according to its own filings, and what is known and not known about where their money comes from.

AIPAC itself. The parent body is registered as a 501(c)(4) "social welfare" organization in Washington. That status allows it to lobby without limit, but gifts to it are not tax-deductible. [cite:pm_pp_aipac990] Its annual tax returns, which are public, give a measure of its size that does not depend on anyone's estimate. In the fiscal year that ended in September 2016 it reported revenue of $105.9 million; in 2019, the last year before the pandemic, $133.9 million; in 2020, $96.6 million; in 2022, $73.5 million; and in 2023, $79.4 million. In the fiscal year ending September 2024, the year after the attacks of 7 October 2023, its revenue reached $156.4 million, the highest in the series, and in 2025 it reported $122.5 million, with net assets of $177.6 million. [cite:pm_pp_aipac990] Its chief executive for most of those years, Howard Kohr, was its highest-paid officer, with reported compensation of about $1.49 million in 2025. [cite:pm_pp_aipac990] AIPAC had 376 employees in 2022, and in 2025 it reported more than five million members and seventeen regional offices. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac]

The foundation. The second body is the American Israel Education Foundation, a 501(c)(3) charity, tax-exempt since 1989. [cite:pm_pp_aief990] Because it is a charity, gifts to it are tax-deductible and it may not lobby, but it may pay for educational travel, and under the congressional ethics rules described in Part One it can sponsor trips that AIPAC, as an employer of lobbyists, could not. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] It shares AIPAC's offices, board members and part of its logo, and its donors are redacted from its public tax filings. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] It is large in its own right: revenue of $59.2 million in fiscal 2024 and $61.1 million in 2025, and net assets of $220.3 million, more than AIPAC's own. [cite:pm_pp_aief990] What it spends its money on is described in Part Seven.

The PAC. The third body is AIPAC PAC, created on 15 December 2021. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] Like every PAC it may give a candidate no more than $5,000 per election, but its main work is the earmarking channel described in Part One: it steers its members' individual donations to the candidates it endorses. [cite:pm_sludge_jeffries] By its own account, more than 10,000 AIPAC members gave over $53 million to candidates through it in the 2024 cycle, which it described as more than any other PAC. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024]

The super PAC. The fourth body is the United Democracy Project, formed on 3 January 2022. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] It makes the independent expenditures, for and against candidates, that account for the largest sums in this volume. Its money comes from AIPAC itself and from individual donors, whose names it must report; in its first cycle AIPAC gave it $8.5 million, the WhatsApp co-founder Jan Koum $2 million, and four other donors $1 million each. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] Its spokesman has described its purpose as building "the broadest bipartisan pro-Israel coalition in Congress possible." [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022]

The meeting. One more element of the structure is not a legal entity but a gathering. For decades AIPAC's annual Policy Conference in Washington was among the largest political events in the capital. Nearly 20,000 delegates attended in 2016, and about 18,000 in March 2019, including 4,000 students; Israel's prime minister addressed the 2019 conference by satellite. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] [cite:pm_aljaz_pc2019] The last in-person conference was held in 2020; AIPAC replaced the 2021 conference with a virtual event and cancelled the 2022 conference, citing the pandemic. [cite:pm_jstar_pc2022] The conference had been the network's most public face. Its end, as Part Three shows, coincided with the network's shift toward its least public channel, the super PAC.

What the structure shows, and what it hides. The four bodies divide the work along the lines the law draws. The 501(c)(4) lobbies, and its donors do not appear in its public filings. The 501(c)(3) pays for travel with tax-deductible gifts whose givers are redacted. The PAC reports its contributions and itemizes, by name, every earmarked gift over $200 to the Federal Election Commission. The super PAC reports its donors too, but can pass money to other committees whose names say nothing about Israel. The result is a network in which some parts are among the most transparent in American politics and others among the least, and in which the public record is fullest for the money given to candidates and thinnest for the money behind the trips and the lobbying.
