The Lines of 1949

From 1949 to June 1967 Israel had no agreed borders, only armistice lines. Those eighteen years are the first test of the question this volume asks: once there was a state, did its governments claim land beyond the lines it held, and who did? The record of the period has three parts. The agreements themselves said the lines were not borders. Israel's governments treated them as borders in practice and proposed them, with small changes, as permanent frontiers. And the claim to the whole Land of Israel, including both banks of the Jordan, was carried in the Knesset by one named opposition party, Herut. How the Green Line was drawn on the Jordanian front is told in [scene:wb1_line|The West Bank, Documented: the Green Line], and the Gaza line in [scene:gz1_war|Gaza, Documented: the war and the line]. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map] [cite:gi3_jvl_herut]

Four agreements, one formula

The United Nations mediator Ralph Bunche negotiated four armistice agreements in 1949. Egypt signed first, at Rhodes on 24 February. Its Article V says the demarcation line "is not to be construed in any sense as a political or territorial boundary." [cite:rs_rd6_arm_egypt_text] Lebanon signed at Ras en Naqoura on 23 March. Its line "shall follow the international boundary between the Lebanon and Palestine," and forces were to withdraw to it within ten days. [cite:gi3_avalon_lebarm] Israel withdrew from 13 villages in Lebanese territory that it had occupied during the war. [cite:gi3_wiki_armistice] Jordan signed at Rhodes on 3 April. Its Article II states that no provision would prejudice the rights, claims and positions of either side in a final settlement. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text]

Syria signed last, on 20 July, near Mahanayim. Syria withdrew from most of the ground it held west of the old international border, and those strips became demilitarized zones. [cite:gi3_wiki_armistice] The agreement says the line and the zones "are not to be interpreted as having any relation whatsoever to ultimate territorial arrangements." The armed forces of both sides were to be "totally excluded" from the zones, and the agreement looked to "the gradual restoration of normal civilian life" there. [cite:gi3_un_syrarm] Each agreement, in other words, froze a military position and left sovereignty open. Israel's Foreign Ministry describes the territory inside the lines as "about 78% of the Mandate area" and the agreements as interim, meant to last until peace treaties replaced them. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map]

What Israel's government said

Israel's government of the time wanted the lines confirmed, not moved outward. Foreign Minister Moshe Sharett called them "provisional boundaries," and at the Lausanne conference of 1949 Israel proposed them, with minor changes, as the basis for permanent frontiers. [cite:gi3_wiki_armistice] Fifty-seven years later, at a memorial ceremony for Ben-Gurion on 27 November 2006, Prime Minister Ehud Olmert quoted a line he attributed to Ben-Gurion's speech in the 1949 Knesset debate on the armistice agreements: faced with a choice between "the entire land of Israel without a Jewish State" and a Jewish state without it, "we chose a Jewish State without the entire land of Israel." [cite:gi3_jvl_olmert2006] That wording comes through Olmert; the Knesset record of 1949 was not read for this book. The historian Benny Morris, as quoted by Martin Kramer, gives a two-sided summary: Ben-Gurion in 1948–49 wanted borders wider than the 1947 partition lines, but he "also refrained from supporting the conquest of the whole Land of Israel." [cite:gi3_kramer_bgborders]

The Syrian channel shows the same thing from another side. In 1949 Syria's new military ruler, Husni al-Za'im, proposed a summit with Ben-Gurion, a peace agreement and the resettlement of 300,000 Palestinian refugees in north-eastern Syria in return for American aid. Privately he told the American minister, James Keeley, that he would share Lake Tiberias and give Israel its western half. Ben-Gurion refused to meet him until Syria pulled its forces back to their pre-war positions: "There will be no discussions on peace or cooperation until those borders are adjusted." [cite:gi3_majalla_zaim] The refusal cost a chance at talks that might have led somewhere, and historians still argue about how real that chance was. What Ben-Gurion demanded was a Syrian withdrawal to the old Mandate frontier. He made no claim on Syrian territory.

Who claimed more

The party that did claim more was Herut, founded by Menachem Begin in June 1948 as the political heir of the Irgun. Until it merged into the Gahal bloc in 1965, according to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, Herut "maintained that the State of Israel should contain both banks of the River Jordan." [cite:gi3_jvl_herut] Its slogan was "Two banks of the Jordan River." It opposed the armistice agreements with the Arab states and refused to recognize the Kingdom of Jordan. [cite:gi3_wiki_herut] In the first Knesset election, in January 1949, it won 14 of 120 seats with 11.5 per cent of the vote, and it came fourth. [cite:gi3_wiki_herut] [cite:gi3_jvl_herut] The claim was the Revisionist one described in [scene:gi2_bothbanks|Part Two: both banks of the Jordan]. Between 1949 and 1967 it was the stated programme of an opposition party, not the policy of any government.

Jordan's parliament used the same language for the opposite claim. On 24 April 1950 it voted for "complete unity between the two banks of the Jordan, the eastern and western, and their union in one single state," annexing the West Bank. [cite:rs_rd6_kramer_annexation] According to a Library of Congress study, only Britain and Pakistan recognized the union, and it was condemned within the Arab League as serving Hashemite ambitions. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] Egypt held the Gaza Strip without annexing it and never gave its inhabitants Egyptian citizenship. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The annexation and its reception are covered in [scene:wb2_annex|The West Bank, Documented: the annexation].

Pressure at the edges

The lines were not quiet. On the Syrian front the dispute was over the demilitarized zones, which the agreement had left without a sovereign. On 18 November 1953 Moshe Dayan, then deputy chief of staff, told the State Department's Henry Byroade that "Israel claims sovereignty only over Jewish lands in the Demilitarized Zone." He said that "someone must keep the area alive" and that Syria wanted the border "on the east water line of the Jordan River," while Israel did not want Syria to have direct access to the river. Byroade replied that the United States "cannot stand idly by and watch the United Nations machinery fall apart." [cite:gi3_frus_dayan1953] Israel's cultivation and water works in the zones led to repeated clashes and to Security Council condemnation, as in Resolution 171 of April 1962, told in [scene:cw2_banat_yaacov|the Banat Ya'qub canal dispute] and [scene:cw4_borders|the armistice lines 1960–1962]. [cite:rs_cw4_unsc171]

On the Jordanian and Egyptian fronts the conflict was over people crossing. Morris, as reported by Avi Shlaim, estimated that 90 per cent or more of the crossings of 1949–56 were motivated by economic and social concerns, such as returning to homes, fields, property or relatives, rather than by political aims, and that between 2,700 and 5,000 infiltrators were killed, the great majority of them unarmed. Israel's reprisal raids included Qibya in October 1953, where 69 Jordanian villagers were killed. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994] These were fights over the lines. They were not campaigns to move them, with one exception, Sinai in 1956, which is the subject of [scene:gi3_sinai56|the next section].

What the period shows

The documented facts are these. The agreements denied that the lines were borders. Israel's governments sought to make them borders, at Lausanne and after. The claim to both banks of the Jordan was stated by Herut, which won 11.5 per cent of the vote in 1949, and not by any government. [cite:gi3_wiki_armistice] [cite:gi3_jvl_herut] The pattern that can be shown over time is pressure at the margins, in the Syrian demilitarized zones above all, where Israel claimed the Jewish-owned land and worked it against Syrian and UN objection. [cite:gi3_frus_dayan1953] Whether Labor's acceptance of the lines was a settled choice or a pause is a matter of argument. Olmert's Ben-Gurion quotation supports the first reading. Morris's wider-borders observation, Herut's programme and the Sinai campaign that followed support the second. [cite:gi3_jvl_olmert2006] [cite:gi3_kramer_bgborders]
