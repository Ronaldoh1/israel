Workers, Settlers and a Status Left Open, 1967–1987

After the campaign of 1971 the occupation of Gaza settled into a form that lasted until the first uprising. Three things defined it: the strip's workers were drawn into the Israeli economy, Israeli settlements were planted on its land, and the strip's political future was discussed by others and left unresolved. This section takes them in turn.

Labour. From 1967 Israel tied the economy of the occupied territories to its own. Moshe Dayan described the territories as "a source of factors of production, especially unskilled labor." Between 1967 and 1990, between 35 and 40 percent of the employed Palestinian labour force worked in Israel, most of it in low-paid jobs. [cite:gz_carnegie_labor] The figure is for the West Bank and Gaza together. From the early 1990s Israel required those workers to hold permits subject to security clearance. [cite:gz_carnegie_labor]

Settlements. The first plans for Israeli settlement in the strip came within a year of its capture: in 1968 Yigal Allon proposed two military Nahal outposts in central Gaza, and in 1970 a Nahal outpost was built near the site of Kfar Darom, the village abandoned in 1948; it became a civilian settlement in 1989. [cite:gz_wiki_gushkatif] The settlements grew into the bloc known as Gush Katif, which numbered 17 settlements and about 8,600 residents before 2005. [cite:gz_wiki_gushkatif] By 2005 there were 21 settlements in the strip with about 9,000 settlers, among about 1.3 million Palestinians; the historian Jean-Pierre Filiu calculated that each settler had about 400 times the land and 20 times the water allotted to a Palestinian farmer. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation] The settlements are followed to their evacuation in Part Three.

A future discussed by others. When Egypt and Israel made peace, Gaza's fate was set aside. The Camp David framework of 17 September 1978 provided for "transitional arrangements for the West Bank and Gaza for a period not exceeding five years", under which "a self-governing authority" freely elected by the inhabitants would replace the military government and give them "full autonomy", and Israeli forces would withdraw and redeploy to specified security locations. [cite:gz_campdavid] The autonomy was never established. The peace treaty of 26 March 1979 fixed Egypt's border with Israel at the old line between Egypt and Mandate Palestine "without prejudice to the issue of the status of the Gaza Strip." [cite:gz_treaty1979] Egypt recovered Sinai; the strip it had governed for most of two decades stayed under occupation, and its status, which the armistice of 1949 had left open, was left open again. In 1982 Israel transferred the administration of civilian life in the territories from the military government to a new Civil Administration. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation]

By the mid-1980s, the strip's people lived under military rule, with no citizenship, no elected government and no settled status, many of them dependent on work in the country that occupied them, living beside Israeli settlements. In December 1987 the uprising that became known as the intifada began in the Jabaliya camp. [cite:gz_wiki_history] Part Three begins there.
