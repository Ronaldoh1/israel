The Secret Services Are Built, 1948–1952

While the new state's diplomats were declaring that Israel would belong to neither camp, its government was building the instruments through which it would deal with both in secret. Israel's intelligence community was not founded in a single act. It was assembled over four years, from the summer of 1948 to 1952, out of one pre-state body that was broken up and three services that took its place, and then drawn together under a coordinating institute that answered to the prime minister. The order in which this happened explains a feature of the system that has outlasted every reorganization since: the foreign intelligence service was placed, from its first written charter, directly under the head of government.

The starting point was the dissolution of the Haganah Intelligence Service on 30 June 1948, a month after the Israel Defense Forces were established. According to the Jewish Virtual Library, three services took its place. Military intelligence, known as Aman, went to the army. Operations abroad went to the Foreign Ministry's Political Division. Internal security went to a service under Isser Harel, which became the Israel Security Agency, the Shin Bet. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_isa] The division followed a plain logic: the army would know the enemy's armies, the Foreign Ministry would gather political intelligence outside the country, and a separate service would watch for threats inside it.

The internal service was given legal standing early and kept out of public view for years. The Jewish Virtual Library states that the Security Service's existence was anchored in law on 18 February 1949, that its existence was not made public until 1957, and that a statute defining its responsibilities was passed only in February 2002. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_isa] This rests on a single secondary source, and the account available here does not say what form the 1949 anchoring took or which body adopted it. What it does show, if accurate, is a span of fifty-three years between the service's legal recognition and a law setting out what it was permitted to do, and eight of those years in which its existence was not made public.

Three services meant three separate chains, and within eighteen months the government moved to coordinate them. The instrument was a secret directive from the prime minister to the Foreign Ministry, dated 13 December 1949. It stated that "an institute is being established to co-ordinate state intelligence agencies." Reuven Shiloah, the Foreign Ministry's adviser for special projects, was to organize and head it. He was to report to the prime minister, while his office sat inside the Foreign Ministry for administrative purposes. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_mossad_directive]

The directive is the founding document of the body later known as the Mossad, and its arrangement deserves a close reading. The new institute was housed in one ministry and answerable to another office. The Foreign Ministry provided the desk; the prime minister held the line of command. The directive also set the money. It asked for about IL20,000 for 1950–51, of which IL5,000 was for special operations that required the prime minister's prior authorization. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_mossad_directive] A quarter of the first budget, in other words, was reserved for activity that could not begin without the personal approval of one man.

Shiloah came to the post with a record in both intelligence and diplomacy. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, he had formed the state's political intelligence service in 1948 and taken part in the armistice talks at Rhodes and the Lausanne talks of 1949. [cite:rs_cw1_ej_shiloah] He came to the institute as both a diplomat and an organizer of intelligence, and its arrangement, housed in the Foreign Ministry and answerable to the prime minister, mirrored that double role.

The institute took time to become what the directive described. GlobalSecurity.org records its earlier names, the Central Institute for Coordination and later the Central Institute for Intelligence and Security, and says that bureaucratic conflict delayed its becoming fully operational for more than a year. [cite:rs_cw1_globalsecurity_mossad] The source does not name the parties to that conflict. What the record does show is the order that broke the deadlock. On 2 March 1951 Ben-Gurion ordered Shiloah to set up a "Directorate" to take all overseas intelligence operations under its wing. [cite:rs_cw1_ej_shiloah] GlobalSecurity.org dates a reorganization to 1 April 1951. [cite:rs_cw1_globalsecurity_mossad] Between them, these two steps moved operations abroad, which in June 1948 had gone to the Foreign Ministry's Political Division, under a single body reporting to the prime minister.

The same year brought a failure. GlobalSecurity.org notes the exposure of an Israeli spy ring in Baghdad in 1951. [cite:rs_cw1_globalsecurity_mossad] The source gives the fact without detail, and the material gathered here does not establish what the ring was doing, who ran it, or whether its exposure played any part in the changes of that spring or in the change of leadership the following year. Those connections may exist, but they cannot be asserted from this record.

Shiloah's tenure ended in 1952. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica he retired that year and was replaced by Isser Harel, and he went on to serve as minister at Israel's Washington embassy in 1953–57. [cite:rs_cw1_ej_shiloah] The Mossad's own list of its heads gives Shiloah for 1949–1952 and Harel for 1952–1963. [cite:rs_cw1_mossad_gov_history] Harel's appointment completed the structure. He already headed the internal service. GlobalSecurity.org says Ben-Gurion made him, as the "Memuneh", responsible for both the Mossad and the Shin Bet; it does not give the date of that step. [cite:rs_cw1_globalsecurity_mossad] The internal and the foreign services, separated in 1948, now had one man over them, and that man answered to the prime minister.

The timeline below sets the steps in order. The dates come from five sources of different kinds: the text of the 1949 directive itself, the Mossad's own list of heads, a reference encyclopedia, and two secondary websites. Where only a secondary website gives a date, the date is attributed to it.

The mechanism that emerges is one of concentration. In June 1948 three services reported, by their nature, to three different authorities: the army, the Foreign Ministry, and whoever oversaw Harel's internal service. By 1952 the foreign service answered by its founding directive to the prime minister, its special operations needed his prior approval, and the man who ran it also ran the internal service. Who could act was clear. Who could review the action was not: the sources gathered here name no body outside the prime minister's office, whether a committee of the Knesset or a court, with a role in approving or examining the services' work in these years, and the internal service operated without a statute defining its responsibilities for its first half-century.

That founding arrangement has a long afterlife. A later account of the Israeli security system describes the Mossad as reporting only to the prime minister, with no Knesset committee able to reach its operational detail. [cite:wiki_mossad] The directive of 13 December 1949 already set that line of command, which suggests it was part of the design from the start rather than a later drift. What the Mossad did with that independence in later decades, and the findings of a UN commission of inquiry in September 2025, belong to later volumes and are told in [scene:s19h|the accountability vacuum]. [cite:un_coi_sept2025] The first contacts between these new services and American intelligence, and Shiloah's place in them, are taken up in [scene:cw1_cia_liaison|the start of the CIA liaison].

Several questions remain open on this record. The legal basis of the internal service in February 1949, its secrecy until 1957 and the 2002 statute all rest on one secondary source. The nature of the bureaucratic conflict that delayed the institute, and the parties to it, are not documented here. Nor is the relation, if any, between the Baghdad exposure of 1951 and the reorganizations of that year or Shiloah's retirement in 1952. The 1949 directive survives as a text; how the IL5,000 for special operations was spent, and what operations Ben-Gurion approved, are not in the record available to this book.
