Most of it is, and by 2028 nearly all of it will be, but "all" and "always" go too far. The 2016 agreement pledged $38 billion for FY2019–2028 and phases out "off-shore procurement," so that military financing is spent on American goods and services [cite:israeled_mou_text] [cite:crs_aid_israel_2016]. About 80% of the roughly $23 billion in post-October 7 aid has gone to American weapons makers such as Boeing, Lockheed Martin, RTX and General Dynamics [cite:aid_costsofwar].

The limits matter. For four decades Israel alone was allowed to spend up to roughly 26% of its aid on its own arms industry, benefiting named firms such as Israel Aerospace Industries and Elbit Systems; that exception is only now winding down to zero by 2028 [cite:spencerguard_38billion_israel] [cite:crs_us_foreign_aid_israel_2023]. For missile defense, the agreement sets a goal of at least 50% US co-production, not all of it [cite:israeled_mou_text]. Israel also receives its aid as a lump sum within 30 days, placed in an interest-bearing Federal Reserve account [cite:internationalpolicy_israel_security_partnership].
