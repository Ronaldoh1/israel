The Schools: Bible Institutes and Dallas Theological Seminary

A theological system lasts when institutions teach it to people who will teach it to others. Darby's dispensationalism reached North America through travelling preachers and summer Bible conferences, and the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909 fixed it in the margins of a printed text; both stories are told earlier in this volume. [scene:ts3_niagara|Part Three: the Bible conferences] [scene:ts5_edition|Part Five: the 1909 edition] This section follows the next step, the schools. Two kinds mattered: the Bible institutes, which trained lay workers, missionaries and pastors in short practical courses, and one graduate seminary, Dallas Theological Seminary, which made dispensationalism the core of a full theological education.

The Bible institutes

The first Bible institutes were founded in the 1880s: Nyack, in New York, in 1882, and the school in Chicago that became Moody Bible Institute in 1886. Writing in Christianity Today in February 1961, the vice-president of one such school counted "more than 200 Bible institutes and colleges" then in existence, and listed "the imminent return of Jesus Christ" among the beliefs they proclaimed. [cite:ts7_ct_bibleinst] That phrase is the movement's own summary of its teaching, not a measure of how many students held a particular view of Israel; the article does not name dispensationalism, and the schools varied.

Moody Bible Institute is the clearest case of a school that built its own channels of distribution. The evangelist Dwight L. Moody founded it in 1886 as the Chicago Evangelization Society, and it took his name after his death in 1899. Its publishing arm began in 1894 as the Bible Institute Colportage Association, a book-distribution society, and became Moody Press in 1941. Its radio station, WMBI, went on the air in 1926 and is described as the first non-commercial Christian radio station in America; the Moody network now owns 36 stations and supplies programmes by satellite to more than 700 outlets. The institute reported 2,341 students in 2025 and is described as dispensational in theology. [cite:ts7_wiki_moody] A school with its own press and its own radio network did not depend on denominations or commercial publishers to reach people: that is the institutional fact that matters for this volume's question, more than any one teacher's lectures.

Dallas Theological Seminary

Dallas Theological Seminary opened in 1924 as the Evangelical Theological College. Its founders were Lewis Sperry Chafer, his brother Rollin T. Chafer, and the Anglican scholar W.H. Griffith Thomas, who was to teach theology but died before classes began. [cite:ts7_wiki_dts] The seminary's own history says it began with 13 students and graduated its first class in 1927. [cite:ts7_dts_story] Chafer's systematic theology took, in the seminary library's description, a "premillennial, dispensational interpretation of the Scriptures", and the seminary remains non-denominational and committed to premillennialism, dispensationalism and biblical inerrancy; it is known as the school that did most to popularize dispensationalism. [cite:ts7_wiki_dts]

The figures for its size depend on what is counted. The seminary reported 1,377 full-time-equivalent students in 2025, and more than 15,000 alumni serving in 97 countries in 2014. [cite:ts7_wiki_dts] Its own website speaks of "more than 16,000 DTS alumni" and about 2,600 current students, and offers programmes in Arabic, Chinese and Spanish. [cite:ts7_dts_story] The two student numbers are not in conflict: one counts full-time equivalents, the other people enrolled, many of them part time or online. Either way, a school that opened with 13 students now has alumni in most of the world's countries.

What the seminary teaches about Israel, in its own words

The point of the system for this volume is its distinction between Israel and the church. In a podcast published by the seminary's Hendricks Center on 8 October 2024, the theologian Craig Blaising of Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary, who earlier taught at Dallas, described dispensationalism as reading Scripture so that Israel and the church stay distinct, and said that "Israel stays in the plan of God all the way to the end"; the tradition, he and the Dallas professor Glenn Kreider explained, has "progressive" and "traditional" forms that differ on how separate the two peoples are. [cite:ts7_dts_hendricks] That is a statement about the people of Israel in God's plan, made in theological terms. It is not, on its own, a position on the borders of the modern state, on settlements or on American aid, and the seminary's published account does not take one.

The alumni list is not a political map

Dallas alumni include some of the best-known American preachers of the past half-century: Chuck Swindoll (1963), David Jeremiah (1967), Andy Stanley (1985) and Tony Evans; in a 2009 LifeWay Research study, three Dallas alumni were among the ten preachers Protestant pastors named as most influential. [cite:ts7_wiki_dts] Such names are sometimes listed as evidence of a Christian Zionist network. Having studied at Dallas is not evidence of any political activity on Israel. A pastor belongs in the record of Christian Zionism for what he has documentably said or done about Israel, and that record is taken name by name in later parts. [scene:ts8_cufi|Part Eight: CUFI] [scene:ts12_officials|Part Twelve: officials]

One Dallas graduate, however, changed the scale of the whole system. Hal Lindsey took a Master of Theology at the seminary in 1962 and worked with the student ministry Campus Crusade for Christ until 1969. [cite:ts7_wiki_lindsey] He held that "the establishment of the State of Israel in May 1948 is the fulfillment of this major prophecy", the regathering of the Jewish people. [cite:ts7_wiki_lindsey] The book he published the following year took the seminary's prophetic scheme out of the classroom to a mass readership. The next section measures how far it went. [scene:ts7_media|Part Seven: the mass market]

The finding is narrow and solid. Between the 1880s and the 1960s, Bible institutes and one seminary in Dallas gave dispensationalism something a reading of prophecy alone could not: degree programmes, faculty, a publishing house, a radio network and thousands of graduates. What these institutions taught about Israel was a theology of a people in a divine plan. The step from that theology to support for particular policies of a particular state was taken later, by named organisations and named people, and is documented, or not, in Parts Eight to Twelve.
