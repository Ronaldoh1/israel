Zionism and Judaism: A Movement and a Religion

Judaism is a religion and a set of ethnic and cultural lineages thousands of years old. Zionism is a political movement with a place and a date of birth: Basel, Switzerland, in August 1897, convened by Theodor Herzl, a secular journalist from Vienna [cite:basel_program]. The two are often spoken of as one thing, so that opposing the movement is taken to mean opposing a people. The documents of the period do not support that equation. The line between the religion and the movement was drawn by Jews themselves: by the movement's founder in his own diary, by the largest American Jewish denomination twelve years before the movement existed, by a mass Jewish labor party founded the same year, by a cultural Zionist who warned against the political version, and by the largest Hasidic dynasty in the world, which still teaches its position today.

That the line exists does not settle every argument built on top of it. Many Jews, religious and secular, have understood and still understand their support for Zionism as part of their Jewish identity, and nothing in this record shows them wrong to do so. What the record settles is narrower: the distinction between the religion and the political project was not invented by outside critics. It was argued inside Jewish life from the start.

Herzl's founding. Herzl was not a rabbi or a religious scholar. He was an assimilated Viennese Jew, secular in outlook, a correspondent for the Neue Freie Presse, whose turn to politics was a response to the antisemitism he witnessed while covering the Dreyfus Affair. He set out his program in Der Judenstaat in 1896 and convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel from 29 to 31 August 1897 [cite:herzl_judenstaat] [cite:herzl_diaries]. Afterward he wrote in his diary: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State." The verb was his, written privately rather than for public consumption, and on this reading it describes a beginning: a political project that had not existed as such before Basel [cite:herzl_diaries].

The same diary shows that the territory was not fixed in advance. Herzl gave serious consideration to Argentina and to Uganda as candidate territories before Palestine was settled on [cite:herzl_diaries]. At its founding, political Zionism treated location as an open, practical question rather than a foregone theological conclusion. Two limits apply to this evidence. It documents the founder's own outlook and process, not the outlook of everyone who joined him; the movement drew religious and secular support alike from very early on. And it does not mean that Palestine was arbitrary to the movement's members, only that its founder weighed alternatives in writing. The rabbinic argument over whether Jews may return to the land by political means at all is a separate and older debate, told elsewhere in this library [scene:oaths_s01|the Three Oaths debate], and the Congress itself has its own section [scene:s04|the Basel Congress of 1897].

Reform Judaism, 1885. Twelve years before Basel, in November 1885, American Reform Judaism adopted the Pittsburgh Platform. Its words are explicit:

We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine, nor a sacrificial worship under the sons of Aaron, nor the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state.

This was not a reply to Zionism, which did not yet exist as an organized movement. It was an independent religious position, adopted publicly as the founding statement of what was then the largest Jewish denomination in America, that already disagreed with what would be proposed more than a decade later [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885]. It is difficult to reconcile with the simpler story in which Judaism naturally wanted a return to Zion and Zionism merely organized that existing desire. The platform remained mainstream American Reform's position for over half a century, until the Columbus Platform of 1937 softened it, and substantial Reform opposition to political Zionism continued after that. On this evidence, American Jewish institutional support for the political Zionist state is a development of the period after the Holocaust, not an ancient religious commitment [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885]. The platform documents the position of 1885. Reform Judaism's institutional stance shifted considerably over the twentieth century, and the 1885 text is no guide to its views today.

One reading finds a concession in the platform's wording. To declare that "we consider ourselves no longer a nation" is to acknowledge that the idea of Jewish nationhood had been there to set aside. On that reading, the question that divided the 1880s and 1890s was less whether Jews were a people than what that peoplehood required.

Ahad Ha'am, 1891. That question was argued most sharply from inside the Zionist camp. Asher Zvi Hirsch Ginsberg (1856–1927), who wrote as Ahad Ha'am, "One of the People," was a committed Zionist in the cultural sense: he wanted a Jewish spiritual and cultural center in Palestine [cite:wiki_ahad_haam]. In 1891, six years before Basel, he published "Truth from Eretz Israel," a firsthand report on the country. "We are used to believing abroad that Palestine is nowadays almost totally desolate," he wrote, "but in fact this is not the case. It is difficult to find in the land any tillable land which is not already tilled" [cite:wiki_ahad_haam]. He recorded the existing Arab population and warned that a political approach that did not account for it would produce conflict. His opposition was to the political, state-building version of Zionism that Herzl went on to champion, and to its handling of the people already there, not to Jewish attachment to the land. His essay establishes that the population, and the risk, were documented from within the movement's own intellectual circle before the political project took shape. It does not establish that his alternative, a cultural center rather than a state, would have been more workable in practice.

The Bund, October 1897. Two months after Basel, in Vilna, Jewish workers founded the Algemeyner Yidisher Arbeter Bund, the General Jewish Labor Bund in Lithuania, Poland and Russia [cite:wiki_bund]. It organized across the Russian Pale of Settlement on socialist and Yiddishist lines and rejected the Zionist proposal of national return to Palestine. Its watchword was doikayt, "hereness": Jewish cultural and political life was to be built where Jews already lived, with Yiddish as its vernacular and national-cultural autonomy inside the existing states as its goal. The Bund, in other words, also spoke of Jews as a nation. It disagreed with the Zionists about where that nation's future lay.

The Bund was not a debating society. The historian Henry Tobias, in The Jewish Bund in Russia: From Its Origins to 1905, documents it as a genuine mass movement with real weight in the wider Russian and Polish socialist and labor movements; for a significant stretch of the early twentieth century, more organized Jewish political energy in Eastern Europe ran through the Bund than through the Zionist movement [cite:wiki_bund]. The sources differ on its size. One account gives a membership of more than 400,000 across the Russian Empire by 1917, larger than the Zionist movement in Eastern Europe and the largest Jewish political organization in the Empire in the decade before the Balfour Declaration; another describes it in the early twentieth century as having tens of thousands of members [cite:wiki_bund]. Either figure makes it a major current, not a fringe.

Its eclipse came from outside, not from losing an open argument with Zionism. Its membership and infrastructure were devastated by the Holocaust, and its remaining structures in the Soviet sphere were suppressed as Stalin moved against independent Jewish political organizations [cite:wiki_bund]. Most of its members, or their children, died in the Holocaust before the State of Israel was declared; their opposition to Zionism did not save them. The earlier fate of the Bund in Soviet Russia, dissolved by its Bolshevik allies, is told in the next section [scene:s00b3|Jewish dissent across every lineage]. What the Bund's existence settles is specific. It does not show that the Bund's position was the authentic Jewish position any more than Zionism's was; both were real, competing currents in Jewish political life. It does show that equating "Jewish" with "Zionist" was contested by Jews, at scale, from the movement's first year, and it means the record cannot support the statement that "the Jewish people wanted Israel" as a description of that era.

Satmar, 1958. The religious case against political Zionism received its most substantial modern statement ten years after the state was founded. In 1958 Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, the Satmar Rebbe, published VaYoel Moshe, the central Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) theological argument against political Zionism [cite:wiki_satmar]. It rests on the Three Oaths of the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Ketubot 111a, a rabbinic tradition holding that the Jewish people swore not to reclaim the land by force or rebel against the nations before the messianic era. In this reading, a state established by human political and military effort is not the fulfillment of Jewish religious hope but a violation of a specific religious commitment. Its objection is to the means and the timing: sovereignty in the land by human political force before the arrival of the Messiah.

Satmar numbers roughly 130,000 people in the United States alone, concentrated in Williamsburg, Kiryas Joel and Monsey, with further communities in Israel, London, Antwerp and Montreal. It is the largest single Hasidic dynasty in the world, and it still teaches this position [cite:wiki_satmar]. That is the documented position of a specific, identifiable tradition. It is not the majority position of Orthodox or Haredi Judaism in general, and it should not be generalized into one.

Set side by side, the five positions show how many different answers Jews gave in these decades to the same questions: whether the Jewish future lay in Palestine, and by what means.

The strongest case for the other side. Read fairly, these documents also contain the materials of the Zionist argument. Jewish peoplehood was not a Zionist invention: the Bund organized Jews as a nation seeking cultural autonomy, the Reform rabbis of 1885 had to declare that they were "no longer a nation," and Ahad Ha'am, whose quarrel was with Herzl's methods, still placed the Jewish cultural center in Palestine. Satmar's objection is to the timing and the means of a return. And Herzl's turn to politics was a response to antisemitism he witnessed at first hand. On this reading, Zionism was one answer among several to a question that Jews widely shared, and one that drew religious and secular support from its early years; in America, the institutional support it later won came after the Holocaust. Nothing in the record contradicts that reading. What the record contradicts is the stronger claim that Zionism and Judaism are the same thing, so that the one cannot be opposed without opposing the other.

Why the distinction carries weight. The line between a political movement founded in 1897 and a religion and set of lineages far older is one that the founders themselves recognized, that Reform recorded in 1885, that the Bund built a mass movement on in 1897, and that Satmar still teaches in 2026 [cite:basel_program] [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] [cite:wiki_bund] [cite:wiki_satmar]. It continues in contemporary Jewish life in Judith Butler's Parting Ways, in Peter Beinart's The Crisis of Zionism and in the organization Jewish Voice for Peace [cite:butler_parting_ways] [cite:beinart_crisis_zionism] [cite:jvp_org]. When the two are collapsed into one, criticism of a documented political project and the state it produced comes to read as an attack on a people and a religion. That conversion is the slide this book calls the swizzle, which the section on the word "Semitic" first names. The Jewish critique of Zionism is not an attack from outside; it is a Jewish tradition older than the state.

A test of the accusation. One argument made in this book's research takes Herzl's alternatives as a thought experiment. Had the state been built in Uganda or Argentina, could criticism of it plausibly have been called "antisemitic"? The historically dominant local languages of Uganda are Bantu languages, Luganda among them; Argentina's are Spanish and Indigenous American languages such as Quechua, Guaraní and Mapudungun. Neither population speaks a Semitic language. Palestine's population spoke Arabic, which is one [cite:wiki_bantu_languages] [cite:wiki_languages_argentina] [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology]. Since "Semitic" was coined in 1781 as a term for a language family that includes Hebrew, Arabic and Amharic together, and was never a synonym for "Jewish," the argument holds that the conflation is no more valid in Palestine than it would have been anywhere else; it simply found a surface hook there, through a coincidence of geography. The argument has firm limits. It explains a pattern in how a rhetorical move spread; it is not a claim that the move is correct anywhere. Nor does it address whether some criticism of Israeli policy is antisemitic in the settled sense of the word, which is hostility to Jews and which the following sections treat on its own terms.

The same research names an inconsistency in how the word's root is used. The correct point that Palestinians speak a Semitic language, and share substantial ancestral overlap with Levantine Jewish populations according to the genetic studies of Nebel (2000) and Behar (2010), is often deployed to argue that criticism of Israeli policy cannot coherently be called antisemitic [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000]. On the word's linguistic terms, that argument is accurate. But the same breadth of the word rarely appears when the subject is the displacement of some 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians in 1948, or the conditions in Gaza documented later in this library, including a formally confirmed famine. The research does not propose that "antisemitism" be applied to harm done to Palestinians. Its position is that the word has always meant hatred of Jews specifically and never fit that harm in either direction. Its point is that Palestinian displacement and casualties are documented on their own terms and deserve the same seriousness, whichever way the "Semitic" argument is being pointed.

What the record does not settle. The documents establish a distinction and a debate; they do not decide the debate. They do not show that any one of these positions was the authentic Jewish one, or how Jews outside these organizations and platforms would have answered the national question. The sources differ on the Bund's size, and the record does not show how the Bund and the Zionist movement would have fared against each other had the Holocaust and Soviet repression not destroyed one of them. Reform's 1885 position shows where a major denomination stood in that year, not where it stands now. And the existence of a Jewish tradition of dissent does not show that Zionism's supporters are wrong to experience their support as Jewish. It shows that the distinction itself is real, documented, and older than the state.
