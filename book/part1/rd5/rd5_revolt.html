The Jewish Resistance Movement, 1945–1946

For fourteen months after the Second World War, the three Jewish armed organizations in Palestine fought the British together. The Haganah and the two other organizations, the Irgun and Lehi, whose origins are told in [scene:s07e2|the section on the Irgun and Lehi], had been separate commands. In the autumn of 1945 they joined forces for a time. The arrangement they made has a name, the Jewish Resistance Movement, and a beginning and an end that can be dated. What it cost in lives cannot, at least from the source available here.

That source needs to be named before anything else is said. The account of the movement used in this section comes from the Lehi heritage association, which keeps the memory of one of the three organizations. It is written from Lehi's own perspective, and it gives no casualty totals for the movement's operations. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] Every operational detail below is therefore Lehi's account of events, reported as such. British records, Haganah records and independent scholarship on these operations are not part of the material this book has verified for this period.

The agreement. In early September 1945, according to the heritage account, four men agreed to form a united Jewish Resistance Movement: Moshe Sneh and Yisrael Galili for the Haganah, Natan Yellin-Mor for Lehi, and Menachem Begin for the Irgun. The Haganah took part through its Palmah. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] The structure matters. Three separate commands kept their own organizations and their own leaders; what they agreed was to act against the British in concert. As the ending of the movement would show, the Haganah's part in it could be switched off by the Jewish Agency and the Zionist Congress; the Irgun's and Lehi's could not.

The timing also matters. The agreement came in the weeks after a new Labour government took office in London on 26 July 1945. [cite:rs_rd5_britannica_bevin] Its Foreign Secretary, Ernest Bevin, did not set out his policy on Palestine until 13 November 1945, when he announced a joint Anglo-American inquiry and said that Jewish immigration would continue only "at the present monthly rate" in the meantime. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_bevin_1945] The first coordinated operations came almost two weeks before that statement.

The Night of the Trains, 1 November 1945. Operations began on the night of 1 November 1945, remembered as the "Night of the Trains." At the railway station at Lydda, Lehi and Irgun fighters destroyed three engines and damaged a train and a signal box; some police and railway workers were killed or injured. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] The heritage account does not give their number or their names. The same night, a Lehi bomb at the Haifa refinery went off early and killed the operation's commander, Moshe Bar-Giora. The heritage account itself calls that operation a failure. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance]

The targets of that first night were infrastructure: the railway and the oil refinery. The source that records the deaths and injuries of police and railway workers at Lydda does not count them. That silence is the first of the gaps in this record.

The Night of the Bridges, 17 June 1946. Between the two nights, the Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry heard evidence and reported, in April 1946, and the British Prime Minister, Clement Attlee, tied the admission of the 100,000 refugees it recommended to the disarmament of the Jewish and Arab armed groups (see [scene:aac_s01|the Anglo-American Committee]). [cite:w_aac] The resistance movement's answer, in the sense that it was the next major operation the heritage account records, came on 17 June 1946, the "Night of the Bridges." The Palmah attacked eleven bridges across Palestine. The heritage account gives no casualty figure. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] Where the first night had been carried out by Lehi and the Irgun, this one was the Haganah's own operation, through the Palmah.

Britain's answer and the summer of 1946. Twelve days later, on 29 June 1946, Britain launched Operation Agatha, a raid by roughly 17,000 troops and police that arrested more than 2,700 people, including senior Jewish Agency officials, and seized documents from the Agency's Jerusalem headquarters. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] On 22 July 1946 the Irgun, commanded by Begin, bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, killing 91 people. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] Both events are told in the sections that follow, beginning with [scene:s07e3|Operation Agatha].

The halt. In early August 1946 the Jewish Agency's leadership met in Paris, and there, according to the heritage account, the decision was taken to halt armed action until the Zionist Congress met. The Congress, meeting in Basel, decided on 9 December 1946 to halt the armed struggle. After that, in the Lehi account's words, the fight "fell to Lehi and IZL", that is, to Lehi and the Irgun. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance]

The mechanism of the ending is the clearest thing in the record. The movement had been an alliance of three commands, but only one of them, the Haganah, answered to the Jewish Agency and the Zionist Congress. When those bodies decided to stop, the fight, in the Lehi account, passed to Lehi and the Irgun; their decisions were not the Congress's to make. The decision in Paris was a political one, taken by the leadership that also had a diplomatic course to pursue that same August (see the Agency's partition proposal, told in its own section). The decision in Basel confirmed it. The united movement thus ended by a political decision on the Jewish side, not by British arms, and the armed campaign against Britain went on without the Haganah.

Two ways of reading it. The movement is remembered in two registers. In the Lehi account, it was a "united armed offensive against the British," and the decisions in Paris and Basel left the fighting to Lehi and the Irgun. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] Read from the British side and from the side of those killed or injured at Lydda, the same operations were attacks on railways, a refinery and bridges in which police and workers died. The two readings do not disagree about what happened on the nights the source records. They disagree about what to call it, and the source available here belongs to one side of that disagreement.

What the record does not settle. Much remains open. The heritage account gives no casualty totals for the movement's operations, no number for the police and railway workers killed or injured at Lydda, and no casualty figure for the Night of the Bridges. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] It does not say whether the King David Hotel bombing was carried out under the united movement's coordination or by the Irgun alone, and the material verified here does not settle that question. It does not record the reasons given in Paris for the halt, or whether the bombing of 22 July weighed in that decision; the sequence is plain, with the bombing coming about two weeks before the Paris meeting, but the source draws no causal line, and neither does this book. A full account of the movement would need British military and police records and the Haganah's own papers, set against the Lehi account. Until that work is done, the dates and the targets are as firm as this record gets.
