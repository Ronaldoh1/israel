Black Americans: The Promise Measured Against the Policies That Shaped It

The taught version of the American Dream makes no exception by race: the same work brings the same home, the same security and the same better life for the next generation. For Black Americans the record can be tested more fully than for almost any other group, because the federal government both measured the outcomes and wrote down many of the rules that shaped them. This section sets out where Black households stand on income, poverty, wealth, homeownership, education and health in 2025 and 2026; what the largest study of mobility by race found; which federal policies are documented as having excluded Black workers and families, and where the intent behind them is disputed; and what economists argue about why the wealth gap is so much wider than the income gap. The gaps described here are outcomes with documented causes, not characteristics of the people who live them. Terms such as median, wealth and relative mobility are defined in [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two].

Where the record stands

The Census Bureau's 2025 figures, released in September 2026, put the official poverty rate for Black Americans at 17.3 per cent, 2.2 times the rate for non-Hispanic white Americans; under the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts benefits and necessary expenses, it was 20.1 per cent. Black child poverty was 24.1 per cent against 8.5 per cent for non-Hispanic white children. [cite:us8_jbhe_2025] The long-run direction is downward: Black poverty stood at 41.8 per cent in 1966, and reached a record low of 17.1 per cent in 2022. [cite:us8_pf_1966] [cite:us8_jbhe_2025] Median Black household income was $59,980 in 2025, up 4.8 per cent on the year, though the Census Bureau found the difference between Black and white income growth not statistically significant. [cite:us8_be_income] [cite:us8_census_2025]

Wealth tells a different story. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances put the median net worth of Black families at $36,800 and of white non-Hispanic families at $332,100, a ratio of about 9 to 1 (332,100 ÷ 36,800 = 9.0). The same survey put median family income at $95,300 for white non-Hispanic and $49,400 for Black families, a ratio of about 1.9 to 1 (the survey's family income differs from the Census household figure above). [cite:us8_scf2025] [cite:ad2_scf_t1] [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|Part Twelve] explains how much confidence a single survey round deserves for a group of this size. Homeownership, the asset most families hold, was 45.4 per cent for Black households and 74.5 per cent for non-Hispanic white households in the second quarter of 2026, a gap of 29.1 points; in 1960 it was 27 points (38 against 65 per cent). [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] [cite:ad6_stateline_gap] In 2024, 29.6 per cent of Black adults aged 25 and over held a bachelor's degree, against 38.6 per cent of white adults. [cite:ad13_jbhe_ba] Life expectancy at birth in 2024 was 74.8 years for non-Hispanic Black Americans and 78.9 for non-Hispanic white Americans, and in 2023 the infant mortality rate was 10.93 deaths per 1,000 births for non-Hispanic Black infants against 4.48 for non-Hispanic white infants. [cite:ad13_nchs_lt2024] [cite:us16_cdc_imr2023]

What the mobility data show

The most detailed study of mobility by race, by Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Maggie Jones and Sonya Porter, linked the tax and census records of 20 million children to their parents. It found that Black and American Indian children have substantially lower rates of upward mobility than other groups. A Black child whose parents were in the bottom fifth of the income distribution had a 2.5 per cent chance of reaching the top fifth, against 10.6 per cent for a white child from the same starting point. Black children raised in top-fifth families were almost as likely to fall to the bottom fifth as to stay at the top, while white children from the top fifth were nearly five times as likely to stay as to fall. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum]

The gap is a gap among men. Black women earned slightly more than white women raised at the same parental income; Black men earned substantially less than white men, and Black boys had lower outcomes than white boys in 99 per cent of the census tracts studied. Twenty-one per cent of Black men born to the lowest-income families were incarcerated on any given day. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] The authors tested the explanations most often offered and found that parents' marital status, education and wealth, and differences in ability, explained very little of the gap between children whose parents earned the same; the gaps persisted for children raised in the same tract and even on the same block. What did predict smaller gaps were neighbourhood conditions: low poverty, low measured racial bias among white residents, and a high share of Black fathers present in the area. Fewer than 5 per cent of Black children grew up in areas with poverty below 10 per cent and more than half of Black fathers at home, while 63 per cent of white children grew up in the comparable conditions for white families, and Black boys who moved to them earlier in childhood earned more and were less likely to be incarcerated. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:us8_oi_race]

The policies behind the gap, and the dispute over intent

Three federal programmes of the 1930s and 1940s did more than any others to build the middle-class security the Dream describes: Social Security, federal mortgage insurance and the GI Bill. Each is documented as reaching Black Americans far less than white Americans. The extent and the intent are argued over separately, and this section keeps them apart.

The Social Security Act of 1935 excluded agricultural and domestic workers from old-age insurance. The exclusion was proposed by Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau in testimony to the House Ways and Means Committee on 5 February 1935, on the grounds that the Bureau of Internal Revenue doubted it could collect payroll taxes from farms and households. [cite:ad13_dewitt] Its effect by race is documented in the 1930 Census: about 65 per cent of employed Black workers (3,564,044 of 5,503,535) worked in those two sectors, against 27 per cent of white workers. Because there were many more white workers overall, 74 per cent of the people excluded were white and 23 per cent Black. Coverage was extended to farm and domestic workers with a regular employer in 1950 and to the rest in 1954. [cite:ad13_dewitt]

Whether the exclusion was racially motivated is contested. Scholars including the political scientist Robert Lieberman, who wrote that old-age insurance was "founded on racial exclusion", and the historian Linda Gordon, who called the exclusions "deliberate and mainly racially motivated", argued that race drove the choice of which workers to leave out. Larry DeWitt, a historian at the Social Security Administration, examined the legislative record in 2010 and concluded that the racial-bias allegations "do not hold up under detailed scrutiny": the exclusion came from Treasury and the planning staff on administrative grounds, and was accepted by both committees with little debate. [cite:ad13_dewitt] The historian David Kaiser cited DeWitt's account in 2023 against the claim that Southern chairmen forced the exclusion, and the writer Adam Hochschild, who had repeated that claim, replied that he found DeWitt convincing. [cite:ad13_nyrb_kaiser] On the record, the disproportionate effect is confirmed; the racial motive is contested and rests on inference.

Housing and the GI Bill are covered in full in [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six]. In brief: the Federal Housing Administration's 1938 Underwriting Manual recommended deed restrictions prohibiting occupancy "except by the race for which they are intended". [cite:ad6_fha_manual] Economists dispute how much the better-known HOLC "redlining" maps themselves mattered, since the HOLC had made 90 per cent of its loans before the maps were drawn, but the same researchers judge the FHA's own lending lines "likely far more impactful". [cite:ad6_chifed_rose] [cite:ad6_ahm_holc] The GI Bill was administered locally: of 3,229 VA-guaranteed home, business and farm loans made in Mississippi in 1947, two went to Black veterans. [cite:ad6_katznelson] For education, Sarah Turner and John Bound found that the bill raised college attainment for Black men born outside the South but widened the Black–white gap among Southern-born men. [cite:ad6_turnerbound]

Why the wealth gap is wider than the income gap

Economists agree that the wealth gap is far larger than the income gap; they disagree about how much of it comes from present-day earnings and how much from history. Robert Barsky, John Bound, Kerwin Charles and Joseph Lupton found in 2002 that differences in earnings account for roughly two-thirds of the Black–white wealth gap, once the comparison is made without assuming a fixed statistical relationship between income and wealth. [cite:ad13_barsky] On that reading the wealth gap runs largely through the income gap, which itself has the documented causes set out above. William Darity, Darrick Hamilton and their co-authors argued in 2018 that the usual individual remedies do not close it: Black households headed by a college graduate held less wealth than white households headed by someone without a high-school diploma, and white households with an employed head held more than ten times the wealth of comparable Black households. [cite:ad13_fortune_darity] Ellora Derenoncourt, Chi Hyun Kim, Moritz Kuhn and Moritz Schularick traced the gap from 1860 to 2020 and found that, because of the starting point under slavery, convergence would be distant even under equal conditions since Emancipation; actual convergence stalled after 1950, and since the 1980s the gap has widened again as capital gains have gone mainly to white households, who hold more of the assets that rose. [cite:ad13_dkks] The three findings are not exclusive: earnings explain much of the gap among families today, while the long-run studies explain why equal earnings now would not close a gap built over generations. Inheritance, one channel between the two, is measured in [scene:ad12_inheritance|Part Twelve].

Where it works as promised

Parts of the record move in the direction the taught version predicts. Black poverty fell by more than half between 1966 and 2025. [cite:us8_pf_1966] [cite:us8_jbhe_2025] A 2024 study by Chetty and co-authors of children born between 1978 and 1992 found that Black children's adult earnings rose at every level of parental income, and that the earnings gap between white and Black children from low-income families fell by 30 per cent; over the same cohorts the gap between white children from rich and poor families widened by 30 per cent. The authors trace both changes to shifts in parents' employment within communities, carried through social ties. [cite:ad2_changing_opp] [cite:ad3_changing_opp] Black women raised at a given income earn as much as or slightly more than white women. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] These findings show that the gaps respond to changes in the conditions around children, which is what one would expect of outcomes with documented causes and would not expect of fixed differences between groups.

Interpretation, not finding: exclusion as design

The Sophia platform reads the record above as evidence that the Dream was built with a boundary: that the programmes which turned wages into homes, pensions and degrees were drawn, by design, around most Black workers, and that the gap of 2026 is that boundary's compound interest. In this reading the 1935 exclusions, the 1938 manual and the local administration of the GI Bill are one architecture rather than three accidents.

The strongest counter-readings are documentary. DeWitt's account of the Social Security exclusion attributes it to tax-collection limits that also excluded millions of white workers, and the coverage was extended within two decades. [cite:ad13_dewitt] Fishback and his co-authors show that the most famous redlining maps came after most HOLC lending. [cite:ad6_chifed_rose] The narrowing of the low-income gap for children born since 1978 shows the boundary moving. [cite:ad2_changing_opp] What the documents establish is disproportionate exclusion in specific programmes, with racial terms written explicitly into the FHA's manual; a single design across all of them is an interpretation the record does not settle.
