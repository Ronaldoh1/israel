What Israel Has Done Inside the American System

The title of this volume describes an asymmetry: a relationship in which, as the critics put it, Israel can see into the American state and the American state cannot see into Israel's. That claim has two halves, and this section takes the first: the documented record of Israeli intelligence activity directed at the United States. It is limited to cases established by a court, by released American government documents, or by named reporting with the government's response beside it. It sets apart what is unresolved, and it records the cases in which the United States did the same to Israel, because a mirror that reflects both ways is not one-way.

Pollard, 1985. The one case proved in court is that of Jonathan Pollard, a civilian analyst for US Navy intelligence. FBI agents arrested him on 21 November 1985 as he left the grounds of the Israeli embassy in Washington; he pleaded guilty on 4 June 1986 to conspiracy to deliver national defence information to a foreign government and was sentenced to life on 4 March 1987. [cite:ow13_wiki_pollard] The CIA's damage assessment of October 1987, declassified in 2012, found that he had passed about 800 documents and some 1,500 messages; his Israeli handlers, of a Defense Ministry science-liaison bureau known as LAKAM, tasked him chiefly on Arab states, Pakistan and the Soviet Union, and "never expressed interest in US military activities, plans, capabilities, or equipment." [cite:ow3_nsa_pollard] Israel apologised in December 1985 and again publicly in March 1987, [cite:ow13_csm_1985] [cite:ow13_upi_1987] but described the operation as unauthorised until May 1998, when Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu acknowledged that Pollard had been an Israeli agent. Released in 2015, Pollard arrived in Israel on 30 December 2020, after his parole ended, and Netanyahu met him at the airport. [cite:ow13_wiki_pollard] The FBI's side of the case is told in [scene:ow5_spies|Part Five], and the CIA's in [scene:ow3_alarm|Part Three].

NUMEC, 1965: unresolved. The most serious allegation, that highly enriched uranium missing from a nuclear-fuel plant in Apollo, Pennsylvania, reached Israel's weapons programme, has never been resolved. The editors of the National Security Archive's 2016 collection of the documents find "strong but not incontrovertible evidence that a diversion did occur"; the Atomic Energy Commission said it found no evidence of theft; the CIA has released none of its internal operational documents; no one was charged. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] The case is told in full in [scene:fis_s07a|the Cold War volume]. It belongs here only as what it is: an open question, not a finding.

What the NSA's own documents say. Documents taken by Edward Snowden and published by The Intercept in 2015 show how American signals intelligence regarded its Israeli partner. A 2008 NSA document said that Israel "also targets us," and that a National Intelligence Estimate had ranked Israel the third most aggressive intelligence service against the United States; a 2013 budget document listed Israel among hostile services and cyber threats. Netanyahu's office answered: "The state of Israel does not conduct espionage against the United States or Israel's other allies." [cite:ow_td_spying2015] A 2007 NSA history of the relationship, published by The Guardian in 2013, said that "balancing the Sigint exchange equally between US and Israeli needs has been a constant challenge" and that in the previous decade "it arguably tilted heavily in favor of Israeli security concerns." [cite:ow4_alternet_guardian] [scene:ow4_memorandum|Part Four] sets out the 2009 memorandum under which the NSA shares raw intelligence with Israel's Unit 8200, and [scene:ow4_both_ways|who watches whom].

The Iran talks, 2015. In March 2015 The Wall Street Journal reported that Israel had spied on the American negotiations with Iran over its nuclear programme and passed what it learned to members of Congress in an effort to defeat the agreement; Netanyahu's office denied it, Israeli officials said the details had come from French officials, and American officials said their objection was to the sharing with Congress rather than to the spying itself. [cite:ow13_npr_wsj2015] In December 2015 the same newspaper reported the other side of the glass: the NSA had kept intercepting Netanyahu's communications after President Obama limited the monitoring of allied leaders in January 2014, keeping him off the protected list for a "compelling national security purpose," and the intercepts showed Israel's campaign in Congress against the deal. The National Security Council said there was no surveillance "unless there is a specific and validated national security purpose." [cite:ow4_aj_wsj2015]

Devices near the White House, 2019. In September 2019 Politico reported, from three former senior officials, that cell-phone surveillance devices found near the White House and other sensitive sites in Washington had been linked by FBI and DHS forensic analysis to Israeli agents, and that the administration issued no public rebuke. Israel called the report a "blatant lie"; President Trump said he found it hard to believe. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The report rests on unnamed officials and has not been confirmed by any released document; the FBI's handling of such warnings is followed in [scene:ow5_counterintel|Part Five].

Pegasus and American diplomats, 2021. In 2021 NSO's spyware, sold under Israeli Defense Ministry licence, was used against at least nine State Department employees working on Uganda. The Israeli embassy said such use would be "a severe violation" of the export licence, and NSO cut off the customers involved; Reuters could not determine who launched the attacks, and NSO's tools are operated by the governments that buy them. [cite:ow10_reu_state] Part Ten tells the story.

2026. In June 2026 NBC News, citing unnamed officials, reported that the Defense Intelligence Agency had raised its counterintelligence threat assessment for Israel to "critical," out of concern that Israel was monitoring senior American officials involved in Middle East decisions. The report rests on unnamed officials, and the assessment is not public. Israel's embassy called it "completely false," and a White House official said the story was false. [cite:ow13_nbc_dia] [cite:ow5_nbc_dia]

What the record shows, and what the United States gets. The documented cases are few, spread over sixty years, and of different kinds: one court-proven espionage case, one unresolved nuclear question, intelligence assessments that rank Israel among the services targeting the United States, and several reported operations that Israel denies. The record also shows the United States collecting on Israel's leaders. [cite:ow4_aj_wsj2015] Set against these cases is what Washington says it receives: intelligence from Israeli sources, joint cyber operations against Iran's nuclear programme, and missile-defence systems developed together, set out in [scene:ow1_returns|Part One] and [scene:ow8_coproduction|Part Eight]. [cite:ow3_sw_sanger] The relationship is not one-way in what each side tries to learn about the other. Where it is one-way, the next section shows, is in what each side can verify about how the other uses what it is given.
