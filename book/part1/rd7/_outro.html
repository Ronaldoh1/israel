Read as a network, the record of the road to 1948 shows a founding that depended on a few things nothing else could replace: an organized movement to pursue it, armed groups to hold it, and two great sources of money at either end of the period, Edmond de Rothschild's in the first decades and the American appeals' in the last. Britain supplied the framework under which the settlers came; the United States, and the bloc that followed it, carried the vote and the first recognition. Most single leaders could be replaced, because the institutions they built carried on. Herzl gave an existing network a program and an organization; that network, built on settlement money from the 1880s, counted for more than any one founder. How much of the founding depended on the Rothschilds turns on what is assumed to fall with them: on the documented record the chance falls to about 30 percent without them, and lower only under assumptions the record does not support. Legitimacy, the moral and public case that made the founding hard to refuse, also counted: without the survivors' case as the record ties it to Truman and the UN, the chance falls by about seven points, and that is a floor. Central banks, by contrast, barely appear; the only one whose money reached the movement was the Reichsbank, through the Transfer Agreement.

The map is open. Every figure here can be checked against the ties behind it, and any reader can remove any other combination of nodes and see what follows.
