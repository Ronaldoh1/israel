The Insurance Machine: Prices, Denials and Middlemen

Between a patient and a doctor's bill stand several businesses that the taught version of the Dream never names. An insurer collects premiums and decides which claims to pay. A pharmacy benefit manager, or PBM, runs the drug side of most plans: it builds the list of covered drugs, negotiates with manufacturers and pays pharmacies. A hospital or physician group sets its prices in negotiation with insurers. This section follows a dollar through each of them, using the regulators' and researchers' own data, and sets each finding beside the industry's stated position. It separates two kinds of friction: designed friction, meaning checks built into the system on purpose, such as reviewing whether a treatment is needed; and captured friction, meaning documented practices that move money in ways the design did not intend or that regulators have challenged.

How a claim is paid or denied

A claim is the bill a doctor or hospital sends to the insurer after care. Before some services, the insurer requires prior authorization: the doctor must ask permission in advance, and the insurer decides whether it will pay. The best public data on denials come from plans sold on HealthCare.gov, the federal marketplace, which must report them to the government. KFF's analysis of 2024 data, published on 24 March 2026, found that insurers denied 19 per cent of in-network claims, about 85 million of 451 million, and 37 per cent of out-of-network claims. Rates varied widely between insurers, from 3 per cent to 36 per cent of in-network claims. Most denials were not explained in clinical terms: 36 per cent of denial reasons were listed as "other", 25 per cent were administrative, 13 per cent were for excluded services, 9 per cent for missing prior authorization or referral and only 5 per cent for lack of medical necessity. [cite:ad8_kff_denials24]

Patients rarely contest. Consumers filed at least 262,982 internal appeals, fewer than 1 per cent of denied claims, and insurers upheld 66 per cent of them; at least 5,881 went on to an independent external review. KFF cautions that the data cannot show which services were denied, that some denied claims are later paid after resubmission, and that denials made before care, through prior authorization, are not included. [cite:ad8_kff_denials24] A denial in these figures is therefore not always a final refusal; it is often a bill sent back for correction. What the data establish is the scale of the paperwork and how little of it patients challenge.

Medicare Advantage, the private-plan option within Medicare, gives the clearest picture of prior authorization. In 2024 its insurers made 52.8 million prior authorization decisions and denied 4.1 million requests in full or in part, 7.7 per cent, up from 6.4 per cent in 2023. Only 11.5 per cent of denials were appealed, but 80.7 per cent of appeals were fully or partly overturned. [cite:ad8_kff_ma_pa24] In rough numbers, 4.1 million × 11.5 per cent is about 470,000 appeals, of which about 380,000 succeeded, while about 3.6 million denials were never contested. Traditional Medicare uses prior authorization far less: 628,243 reviews in fiscal 2024, though with a higher denial rate of 22.9 per cent, and from 1 January 2026 a pilot called WISeR added prior authorization for selected services in six states. [cite:ad8_kff_ma_pa24]

The industry's stated position is that prior authorization exists to "ensure patients receive care that is safe, evidence-based and as affordable as possible", in the words of the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association. [cite:ad8_bcbsa_pa] On 23 June 2025 the Health and Human Services Secretary and the head of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services announced a voluntary pledge by insurers covering 257 million people: to show reductions in the services that need prior authorization by 1 January 2026, to honour existing approvals for 90 days when patients switch plans, to answer 80 per cent of complete electronic requests in real time by 2027, and to adopt a common electronic standard by 1 January 2027. [cite:ad8_healio_pa] A federal rule already requires Medicare Advantage and other public-programme plans to decide standard requests within seven days from January 2026 and to publish their approval statistics. [cite:ad8_kff_ma_pa24] Status as of October 2026: the insurers' associations say the first commitments took effect on 1 January 2026; in KFF's 2026 employer survey, 7 per cent of large firms said the number of services needing authorization had risen in the past year. [cite:ad8_bcbsa_pa] [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026]

The drug middlemen

Three PBMs dominate the drug side of American insurance. The Federal Trade Commission's interim staff report of 9 July 2024 found that the three largest, CVS Caremark, Express Scripts and Optum Rx, processed nearly 80 per cent of the roughly 6.6 billion prescriptions filled in 2023, and the six largest more than 90 per cent. Each is owned by a company that also owns a large health insurer and pharmacies, and the pharmacies affiliated with the big three took nearly 70 per cent of specialty drug revenue. The Commission voted 4 to 1 to publish; Commissioner Melissa Holyoak dissented, calling the report incomplete, and Commissioner Andrew Ferguson cautioned that much of it rested on public comments. [cite:ad8_ftc_pbm1]

The second interim report, of 14 January 2025, followed specialty generic drugs used for cancer, HIV and other serious conditions. It found that the big three's affiliated pharmacies earned $7.3 billion above the estimated cost of acquiring these drugs between 2017 and 2022, plus $1.4 billion from spread pricing, which is the practice of charging the employer or plan more than the PBM pays the pharmacy. [cite:ad8_ftc_pbm2] Of specialty generics dispensed by those pharmacies in 2020–2022, 63 per cent were marked up by 100 per cent or more over the national average acquisition cost, and 22 per cent by 1,000 per cent or more. The PBMs' trade association, the Pharmaceutical Care Management Association, questioned the purpose of a second interim report and said PBMs are "the only entities in the supply chain dedicated to lowering the cost of prescription drugs". [cite:ad8_pcma_resp]

A rebate is a payment from a drug manufacturer to a PBM in return for placing its drug on the plan's list. The Commission's separate case over insulin, filed in the autumn of 2024, alleged that the three PBMs' rebating practices artificially inflated insulin list prices, steering patients toward higher-cost drugs that paid larger rebates.[cite:ad8_ftc_insulin] [cite:ad8_bp_optum] Status as of October 2026: Express Scripts settled on 4 February 2026, and the Commission estimates the agreement will lower patients' costs for drugs such as insulin by up to $7 billion over ten years; Caremark settled on 14 July 2026; Optum Rx reached a proposed settlement in June 2026 whose terms had not been published. All three denied the allegations. [cite:ad8_ftc_insulin] [cite:ad8_ftc_caremark] [cite:ad8_bp_optum] Congress also acted: the Consolidated Appropriations Act signed on 3 February 2026 separates PBM pay in Medicare Part D from drug list prices, replacing it with flat fees, generally from 1 January 2028, and requires PBMs serving larger employer plans to pass through all rebates and disclose their pay, from about 2028. [cite:ad8_ajmc_caa26] [cite:ad8_bipc_caa26]

Prices, not visits

The largest driver of the American bill is the price of each service. RAND's employer-led study of hospital prices found that private insurers and employers paid hospitals on average 254 per cent of what Medicare would have paid for the same services in 2022, up from 224 per cent in 2020. Prices exceeded 300 per cent of Medicare in seven states, including California, Florida and New York, and stayed below 200 per cent in five, including Massachusetts and Michigan. The American Hospital Association called the study "skewed and incomplete", noting that it covers under 2 per cent of hospital spending and arguing that Medicare rates are not a fair benchmark. [cite:ad8_rand_hosp] For drugs, RAND found American prices averaged 2.78 times those in 33 other high-income countries in 2022, and brand-name drugs 4.22 times before rebates, while unbranded generics were cheaper in the United States than in most comparison countries. [cite:us16_rand_drugs] Under the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, Medicare's first ten negotiated drug prices took effect on 1 January 2026; the 2025 budget law widened an exemption for drugs treating rare diseases, at a cost the CBO put at $8.8 billion. [cite:us16_kff_negotiation] Part Fifteen follows the lobbying behind such rules. [scene:ad15_lobbying_by_industry|Who writes the rules]

Where it works as promised

Most claims are paid, and most prior authorization requests are approved: in Medicare Advantage more than nine in ten requests went through in 2024, and in the marketplace about four in five in-network claims were paid on first submission. [cite:ad8_kff_ma_pa24] [cite:ad8_kff_denials24] Generic drugs, which make up most prescriptions, are cheaper in the United States than in most rich countries. [cite:us16_rand_drugs] And the system has shown that it can correct itself through ordinary law: a Biden-era statute let Medicare negotiate drug prices, a regulatory study and lawsuit under both administrations produced settlements with all three large PBMs, and a funding law signed in 2026 rewrote how PBMs are paid. [cite:us16_kff_negotiation] [cite:ad8_ftc_insulin] [cite:ad8_ajmc_caa26] The documented problem is not that the machine never pays. It is that its prices are high, its denials are hard to read and seldom contested, and its middlemen were, on the regulators' findings, paid in ways tied to higher prices. When a denial or a deductible leaves a bill unpaid, the next stage is debt. [scene:ad8_medical_debt|Medical debt]
