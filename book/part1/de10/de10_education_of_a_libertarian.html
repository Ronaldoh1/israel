The Education of a Libertarian

Peter Thiel has published relatively little under his own name, and what he has published tends to be quoted one sentence at a time. Read whole and in order, four texts carry most of his argument: a long essay on Leo Strauss, Carl Schmitt and René Girard, first delivered at a conference he paid for in 2004; the 2009 essay for the Cato Institute that contains the most quoted sentence in this book; a 2014 book on start-ups that made the case for monopoly; and a run of speeches, interviews and signatures since 2016 in which he has said what he thinks of democracy now. His life, from Frankfurt to Stanford to Buenos Aires, is told in [scene:de9_formation|Part Nine]; this section takes the texts on their own terms, in their own words, and asks what kind of argument they make.

"The Straussian Moment," 2004

The earliest of the four is the least read. Thiel first delivered "The Straussian Moment" at a 2004 conference called "Politics and the Apocalypse," which he funded, and it was published in 2007 in a volume of the same name edited by the Stanford theologian Robert Hamerton-Kelly, where it runs from page 189 to page 218. [cite:de10_larb] [cite:de10_straussian_es] It is a dense essay in political philosophy, and its subject is what the attacks of 11 September 2001, with which it opens, revealed about the modern West. [cite:de10_straussian_es]

Its argument runs roughly as follows. The Enlightenment compromise associated with John Locke set aside the old questions about human nature and the good life, putting in their place, as Thiel put it, an unknown "X," and "the new science of economics and the practice of capitalism filled the vacuum." [cite:de10_straussian_es] [cite:de10_larb] The attacks showed that this compromise could not answer people who were willing to die for a fundamental truth. Thiel then set three thinkers against one another. From Carl Schmitt, the German jurist who joined the Nazi Party in 1933 and helped give its seizure of power a legal form, he took the idea that politics is the field on which people are forced to choose between friend and enemy. [cite:de10_brit_schmitt] From Leo Strauss he took the idea that philosophers write on two levels, for the many and for "trustworthy and intelligent readers only," and he quoted Strauss's remark that "the most just society cannot survive without 'intelligence,' i.e., espionage." From Girard he took the theory, explained in the next section, that human societies were founded on a hidden act of collective violence, and that once the mechanism is revealed it cannot be hidden again. [cite:de10_straussian_es] [cite:de10_larb]

Two passages matter for this book. In one, Thiel proposed that instead of a United Nations of endless parliamentary debate one should consider Echelon, the secret signals-intelligence network shared by the United States and its allies, as a truly decisive path toward a Pax Americana. In the other he wrote that the Straussian project sets out to preserve the "katechon," the restraining force of Saint Paul's letter to the Thessalonians, a term he would return to two decades later. [cite:de10_straussian_es] The essay ends not with a call to arms but with advice that the Christian statesman would be wise, in every case, to side with peace. [cite:de10_straussian_es] It is a meditation on the limits of liberal politics after 2001, with a frank endorsement of secret intelligence over open deliberation. It is not a program, and it proposes no change to American elections.

Cato Unbound, 2009

The 2009 essay, and the follow-up on the suffrage passage that Thiel posted on 1 May, are set out in [scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One], [scene:de3_other_lineages|Part Three] and [scene:de9_politics_and_argentina|Part Nine], and are not repeated here. Read whole, the essay contains several sentences that are quoted less often and say more about its shape. It locates the decline in time: "The 1920s were the last decade in American history during which one could be genuinely optimistic about politics." [cite:de10_cato] It places the remedy outside politics, in technology: the essay closes on the claim that "the fate of our world may depend on the effort of a single person who builds or propagates the machinery of freedom." [cite:de10_cato] The single person it has in mind is an inventor or founder, not a ruler; the "machinery" is cyberspace, seasteads and rockets.

The follow-up explains what he meant by politics. "Politics is about interfering with other people's lives without their consent," he wrote, and "I believe that politics is way too intense. That's why I'm a libertarian." [cite:de10_cato] He listed the rights he thought were actually in danger: people "imprisoned for using even very mild drugs, tortured by our own government, and forced to bail out reckless financial companies." [cite:de10_cato] And he described his own course as "focusing energy elsewhere, onto peaceful projects that some consider utopian." [cite:de10_cato] In 2009, then, the incompatibility of freedom and democracy was an argument for leaving politics, not for winning it. That is the reading Nick Land took from it three years later, when he opened his serial with the sentence as a sign that libertarians were choosing exit over voice. [cite:de1_land_gwern]

Zero to One, 2014

Five years later the argument moved from politics to markets. In September 2014 the Wall Street Journal ran an essay by Thiel adapted from his book Zero to One, written with Blake Masters, under the title "Competition Is for Losers." [cite:de10_lambert] The book argues that what Americans call competition destroys profit, that the ruthless "competitive ecosystem pushes people toward ruthlessness or death," and that what it called the "ideology of competition" distorts how people think. [cite:de10_netzpolitik] Its alternative is the "creative monopoly," a company so far ahead that it has no real rival, which Thiel distinguished from monopolies that are "illegal bullies or government favourites" and so do not deserve their rents. [cite:de10_netzpolitik] He added that "monopolists lie to protect themselves," pointing to Google, which he said presented itself as "just another tech company" to escape attention; in a debate with Google's Eric Schmidt he said a dominant position like Google's in search was "quite legal" so long as it was not abused. [cite:de10_netzpolitik]

The book's case is about business strategy, and it is in this book that Thiel's reading of Girard is easiest to see. Rivalry, in Girard's account, comes from imitation; in Thiel's business advice, a founder who copies rivals ends up fighting over the same prize. "If you're copying these guys, you have nothing new to offer," he said of the point. [cite:de10_worldcrunch] What the case for monopoly means when the company is a government contractor is taken up in [scene:de10_philosophy_vs_practice|the last section of this part].

Democracy, in his own words, 2016–2026

Since 2016 Thiel has not published a second essay on democracy, and his later statements have to be assembled from speeches, interviews and one signature. The signature is the most precise. In June 2022 he was among the signers, listed as "Peter Thiel / Founders Fund," of "National Conservatism: A Statement of Principles," issued by the Edmund Burke Foundation. [cite:de16_natcon_signers] The statement calls for "accepting and living in accordance with the Constitution of 1787" and for "a strong but limited state, subject to constitutional restraints and a division of powers," and it opposes transferring "the authority of elected governments" to supranational bodies. [cite:de16_tac_natcon] Whatever else it is, that is not a text about abolishing elections or retiring the Constitution, which is what Curtis Yarvin's program proposes ([scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]).

His speeches point in the same direction. Opening the first National Conservatism conference in Washington in July 2019, he called Google's conduct toward China "seemingly treasonous" and said "these questions need to be asked by the FBI and the CIA," which is a call for the existing state's agencies to act, not to be replaced. [cite:de16_bloomberg_natcon] In June 2025 Ross Douthat of the New York Times asked him about the populist turn he had backed since 2016; he said it was "still by far the best option we have." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] In November 2023 he had asked The Atlantic's Barton Gellman to publish his promise to give no money to any politician in the 2024 presidential race, in Gellman's paraphrase, and in the same series of interviews, as CNN later reported, he called Yarvin's ideas unworkable. [cite:de10_politicalwire] [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] How that promise compares with his giving is tested in [scene:de9_claims_vs_record|Part Nine].

Read in sequence, the texts describe a man who has doubted mass democracy in writing for twenty years and has never published a design to replace it. The 2004 essay prefers intelligence services to parliaments in foreign affairs; the 2009 essay prefers exit to voting; the 2014 book prefers monopoly to competition; the 2022 signature accepts the Constitution. The common thread is a distrust of crowds and of deliberation, and a confidence in the exceptional individual, the founder or the statesman, that runs through all four. That distrust is documented in his own words. A plan to end elections is not, and on the record of these texts he sits on a different rung of the ladder from Yarvin: an on-record critic of democracy, not an author of the sovereign-executive design.
