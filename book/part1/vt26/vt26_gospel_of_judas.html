The Gospel of Judas: The Text a Bishop Named, Lost for 1,800 Years

The Gospel of Judas is the one text in this part that the institution's own writers named and condemned in antiquity. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, mentioned it in about 180, and for eighteen centuries his hostile sentence was all that was known of it. A Coptic copy surfaced in Egypt in the 1970s, passed through dealers, a theft, a bank vault and a freezer, and was published by the National Geographic Society on 6 April 2006 as the story of a Judas who obeyed Yeshua rather than betrayed him. Within a year other specialists argued that the published translation had made Judas a hero by mistranslating a text that makes him a demon. This section gives the text's history, its outline and teaching, both readings, and the institution's responses in 180, the 370s and 2006. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] [cite:vt26_natgeo_journey]

What the bishops said first

Irenaeus described a group that honoured the figures the Hebrew Bible condemns: Cain, Esau, Korah and the men of Sodom. Of Judas they taught that he "alone, knowing the truth as no others did, accomplished the mystery of the betrayal". "They produce a fictitious history of this kind," he wrote, "which they style the Gospel of Judas." [cite:vt26_na_iren131] Later heresiologists gave this group the label "Cainites". Epiphanius of Salamis attacked the same group and its gospel in his Panarion, begun in 374 or 375. [cite:vt26_arys_epiphanius] [cite:vt26_wiki_panarion] Whether the text Irenaeus knew is the one recovered in the twentieth century cannot be proved; the recovered text does not mention Cain or Korah, but it matches his central claim, that Judas alone understood. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

Irenaeus's condemnation is the institution's documented act against this book. It was a bishop's polemic, not a council's decree, written in Gaul about a century and a half before Constantine, and it ordered nothing to be done to the text. No later council names it, and it is absent from the rejected books of the sixth-century Decretum Gelasianum. [cite:vt26_tert_decretum] On the survival of the Judas text, as on the Nag Hammadi books, the record shows condemnation by writing, not a documented order to destroy. The wider pattern is set out in [scene:vt24_the_heresy_hunters|the heresy hunters].

How the codex reached print

The modern history is a case study in what the antiquities trade does to a manuscript. In the 1970s an Egyptian farmer reportedly found a limestone box in a tomb near Maghagha in Middle Egypt, in the El Minya area. In 1978 he sold the codex to a Cairo dealer known as Hanna. In about 1980 it was stolen with other objects and taken to Geneva; Hanna recovered it in 1982. In May 1983 Stephen Emmel and two other scholars examined it in a Geneva hotel room; Hanna asked $3 million and no sale followed. In 1984 it was offered in New York, found no buyer and was placed in a safe-deposit box in Hicksville, New York, where it stayed for about sixteen years. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] [cite:vt26_natgeo_journey]

The Zurich dealer Frieda Nussberger-Tchacos bought it in April 2000, and the codex now bears her name. Yale declined to buy it over its provenance. She passed it to the American dealer Bruce Ferrini, who stored it in a freezer, which accelerated its decay; after a dispute over payment she recovered it and in February 2001 transferred it to the Maecenas Foundation for Ancient Art in Basel. Rodolphe Kasser announced its existence in July 2004. By then the papyrus had broken into nearly a thousand pieces, some blackened past reading; restorers recovered roughly 90 to 95 per cent of what had survived. A University of Arizona laboratory dated papyrus and leather samples to between 220 and 340 CE. The National Geographic Society unveiled the English translation in Washington on 6 April 2006, and a critical edition by Kasser, Marvin Meyer, Gregor Wurst and François Gaudard followed in 2007. National Geographic reported that the pages are to be returned to the Coptic Museum in Cairo; whether that has happened has not been publicly confirmed. [cite:vt26_natgeo_journey] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] [cite:vt26_wiki_tchacos] Further fragments held by Ferrini surfaced later; Meyer and Wurst presented a preliminary translation of photographs of them in November 2009. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

What the codex contains

Codex Tchacos holds four texts in Coptic: the Letter of Peter to Philip, a version of the First Apocalypse of James (both also known from Nag Hammadi), the Gospel of Judas, and a fragment of a book featuring Allogenes that differs from the Nag Hammadi Allogenes. About a dozen original pages are missing. [cite:vt26_wiki_tchacos] The Gospel of Judas is a Coptic translation of a Greek work that most scholars date before about 180, in line with Irenaeus's report. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] Quotations below follow the 2006 National Geographic translation by Kasser, Meyer and Wurst, which is copyrighted and quoted in short phrases only.

Outline

The text names itself at the start as "the secret account of the revelation that Jesus spoke in conversation with Judas Iscariot". It then runs in five movements. First, Jesus finds the disciples giving thanks over bread and laughs at them, saying they do this because "it is through this that your god [will be] praised"; they grow angry, and only Judas can stand before him, telling him: "You are from the immortal realm of Barbelo." Second, Jesus takes Judas aside and tells him the mysteries of the kingdom. Third, the disciples report a vision of the great Temple, with priests offering sacrifices at the altar; Jesus interprets it for them: "Those you have seen receiving the offerings at the altar—that is who you are," and "the cattle you have seen brought for sacrifice are the many people you lead astray". Fourth, Judas reports his own vision, and Jesus gives him a long revelation of the cosmos: a great invisible Spirit and a "luminous cloud", the self-generated Adamas, and the rulers of the lower world, Nebro, also called Yaldabaoth, and Saklas, who made humanity. Fifth, Jesus tells Judas that he "will become the thirteenth", will be cursed by the other generations and will come to rule over them, and that he will surpass the others: "you will sacrifice the man that clothes me"; Judas looks up, sees the luminous cloud and enters it; the text ends with Judas taking money and handing Jesus over. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

The cosmology belongs to the family of texts scholars call Sethian: Barbelo, Saklas and Yaldabaoth are the same names that appear in the Apocryphon of John, where the maker of this world is an ignorant lower power. That is set out in [scene:vt25_secret_cosmos|the secret cosmos of Nag Hammadi]. The text reinterprets the creator of Genesis inside a Christian argument; it is not a statement about Jews or Judaism, and should not be read as one.

What it teaches, and where it cuts against the apostles

Whatever Judas's role, the gospel's attack on the other eleven is not in dispute. Their thanksgiving meal, the rite that became the church's central sacrament, is mocked as worship of a lesser god. Their own vision turns them into the priests at the altar, and the animals they sacrifice into the people they lead astray. Elaine Pagels and Karen King, in Reading Judas (2007), read the text as "a kind of protest literature" aimed at church leaders of the author's day who, in a time of persecution, encouraged believers to die as martyrs; in Pagels's account the author was not against martyrdom as such but against the claim that God wants it, and questioned whether God requires a son's death at all. [cite:vt26_salon_pagels2007] On that reading, a Christian group in the second century used the figure most hated in the apostolic tradition to accuse that tradition's leaders of offering human sacrifice. That is the text's direct challenge to the institution: not a secret teaching outside the church, but a charge that the apostles' successors had misunderstood the god they served.

Hero or demon: two readings

National Geographic's 2006 presentation, with commentary by Bart Ehrman, read Judas as the one disciple who understood Yeshua and who, by handing him over, freed the divine being from "the man that clothes" it: obedience, not betrayal. Pagels argued that the Greek word behind "betray" means "hand over". [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] [cite:vt26_salon_pagels2007]

April DeConick of Rice University, in The Thirteenth Apostle (2007) and a New York Times op-ed of 1 December 2007, argued that the published translation made three decisive errors. Jesus calls Judas a daimon, which the team rendered "spirit"; in these texts, she argued, a daimon is always a demon. Judas is set apart "from" the holy generation, not "for" it. And in one passage the team had dropped a negative, so that Judas will not ascend to the holy generation. In her reading Judas is a demon who truly betrays Jesus, and the "sacrifice" line condemns him rather than praising him. [cite:vt26_getrel_deconick] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] National Geographic replied that "virtually all" the issues she raised were addressed in the edition's footnotes. Others joined the dispute: André Gagné argued that a key word should be translated "denial"; Géza Vermes called the text typical Greek-Christian speculation of no historical value for Judas. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

Both readings agree on what matters most for this book: the text's quarrel is with the twelve and with the sacrificial meaning the emerging church gave to Yeshua's death. They disagree on whether Judas is the exception to that error or its instrument. Neither reading claims the text tells us anything reliable about the historical Judas. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

What the institution said in 2006

In January 2006, before publication, the Times of London reported that Vatican scholars intended to give Judas a "makeover", quoting Monsignor Walter Brandmüller as saying the manuscript could help reconstruct how early Christians saw Christ's teaching. [cite:vt26_ct_times2006] No Vatican document followed. On Holy Thursday, 13 April 2006, a week after the National Geographic launch, Pope Benedict XVI preached at St John Lateran on the "obscure mystery of rejection" in Judas, who "evaluated Jesus in accordance with the criteria of power and success" and for whom money mattered more than communion with Jesus. He did not name the new text. [cite:vt26_va_benedict2006] On 18 October 2006 he devoted a general audience to Judas, attributing the betrayal to Satan's influence and Judas's own responsibility, while adding that "it is not up to us to judge his gesture". [cite:vt26_va_aud2006] Rowan Williams, Archbishop of Canterbury, used his Easter sermon that year to call it "a demonstrably late text". [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] The documented response of 2006 was restatement of the canonical Judas, not condemnation of the codex; the condemnation on record is still the one Irenaeus wrote around 180.
