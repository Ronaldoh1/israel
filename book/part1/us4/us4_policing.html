Policing: Who Polices, and Who Is Held to Account

The two countries organise policing in opposite ways. The United States has no national police force for ordinary crime. In June 2018 the Bureau of Justice Statistics counted 17,541 state and local agencies performing law-enforcement functions, employing about 1.2 million sworn officers and civilian staff between them: city police departments, county sheriffs, state highway patrols and a long tail of small-town forces, each with its own rules on the use of force, its own training and its own complaint system. [cite:us4_bjs_agencies] Israel has one national police force, the Israel Police, with the Border Police as its gendarmerie arm, answering to a single minister. Under the coalition agreement of 25 November 2022 that minister became Itamar Ben-Gvir, whose expanded National Security Ministry took control of the police and of the Border Police in the West Bank. [cite:hm_jta_coalition2022] One country spreads the power to police, and therefore the responsibility for abuses, across thousands of local governments; the other concentrates it in one office, so that a change of minister changes policing for the whole state at once.

Deaths at the hands of police: the American count

The United States government does not publish a complete annual count of people killed by police, so the standard series comes from outside government. Mapping Police Violence, a database run by the research group Campaign Zero, counted 1,383 people killed by police in 2024, the highest since its records began in 2013, and 1,314 in 2025, a preliminary figure that may rise. The 2025 fall of about 5 per cent was the first year-on-year decline since 2019. Measured against population, the rate hovered around 0.345 per 100,000 from 2013 to 2020 and rose after 2021. [cite:us4_mpv_2025] [cite:us4_cz_2025] The series is short, about thirteen years, so it cannot show what policing looked like in 1980 or 1990; that is a limit of the record, not a sign that nothing happened earlier.

The same report breaks the 2025 figure down. Black Americans were killed by police at 0.75 per 100,000 (300 of about 40 million) and white Americans at 0.29 (560 of about 193 million), a ratio of 2.6. About 22 per cent of those killed in 2025 were unarmed. [cite:us4_mpv_2025] These are measures of how the system's encounters end, not of anything about the people involved; the report itself finds that most killings follow a call for service, which is where the mechanism lies. [cite:us4_cz_2025] Prosecution is rare. Officers were charged in 15 of the 2025 incidents by the time of the report, about 1 per cent, a number that usually rises as cases proceed; between 2019 and 2023 the share of killings that led to charges rose from about 2 per cent to closer to 4 per cent. [cite:us4_mpv_2025]

Israel: no public series, and the cases that defined the record

Israel has no published annual series of people killed by police comparable to the American databases; that is a gap in this book's data, stated here rather than filled with an estimate. What the record does hold are a small number of cases that were fully investigated, and what they show is the accountability system at work. In October 2000, at the start of the second intifada, police killed 13 Arab protesters, described in the commission's coverage as Palestinians living in Israel, during a week of demonstrations centred on the Galilee. A state commission of inquiry led by a three-member Supreme Court panel, known as the Orr Commission, reported on 1 September 2003. Its 860-page report found that police commanders had made "errors of judgment", that police had used live rounds, rubber bullets and tear gas against stone-throwing demonstrators, that they had shown "prior hostility" towards them, and that some of their actions stemmed from prejudice against Arabs. It recommended dismissing several officers and barring the former internal security minister, Shlomo Ben Ami, from any future public security post, and it criticised Prime Minister Ehud Barak for giving the police a "green light"; its findings were not legally binding. [cite:us4_aje_orr] The criminal follow-up ended two years later. In September 2005 the Justice Ministry's Police Investigation Department (known by its Hebrew acronym Mahash) decided not to issue an indictment in any of the 13 killings. [cite:us4_adalah_accused]

A second case shows the same pattern in a courtroom. On 30 May 2020 a Border Police officer shot dead Eyad al-Hallaq, a 32-year-old autistic Palestinian man from East Jerusalem, near the Lions' Gate of the Old City, as he walked to a special-needs institution. The Justice Ministry said in October 2020 that al-Hallaq "posed no danger to police or civilians" and charged the officer with reckless manslaughter in June 2021. On 6 July 2023 the Jerusalem District Court acquitted him, calling the shooting a "tragic mistake" made in self-defence and good faith; the police investigations unit said after the verdict that he had defied instructions to stop shooting. [cite:us4_aje_hallaq] In the West Bank, where the Israel Police is responsible for Israeli civilians, the monitoring group Yesh Din followed police files on settler violence from 2005 to 2025: 93.6 per cent were closed without an indictment, and about 3 per cent led to a full or partial conviction. [cite:wb_yeshdin_2025] The West Bank volume sets out that record in detail. [scene:wb8_settlers|The West Bank: settlers and enforcement]

The exchange programmes

The two police systems are linked. Since the 1990s and early 2000s the Jewish Institute for National Security of America (JINSA), the Anti-Defamation League and Georgia State University's GILEE programme have taken more than 1,000 American law-enforcement officials, most of them senior, to Israel for seminars, and the New York Police Department has kept a liaison office at Israel Police headquarters in Kfar Saba since 2012. [cite:ow6_jpost_adl] [cite:ow6_toi_nypd2012] Critics, among them Amnesty International USA in 2016 and Jewish Voice for Peace's "Deadly Exchange" campaign from 2017, argued that the trips expose American police to methods used in an occupation. [cite:ow6_amnesty_2016] [cite:ow6_reason_cops] A review by Reason magazine found little evidence that the trips cause excessive force by American officers, and no evidence for the claim that American officers learn chokeholds in Israel; the organisers deny it, and GILEE's founder says Israeli rules on the use of force are "much more restrictive". [cite:ow6_reason_cops] The claim of a causal link is therefore unsupported on the present record, while the programmes themselves are documented. The One-Way Mirror sets out who pays for them and who takes part. [scene:ow6_exchanges|The One-Way Mirror: police exchanges]

The comparison so far is uneven in a way that matters. The American record has a yearly count and a measured racial gap but a weak prosecution record spread across thousands of jurisdictions. The Israeli record has formal commissions and a central investigations unit but no public count, and its best-documented cases, involving Arab citizens and Palestinians in Jerusalem and the West Bank, ended without convictions. Who the Israeli police serve and who they fail is taken further in the parts on public safety and on equality among citizens.
