Emergency Powers: Israel Since 1948, America Since 1976

Both states let the executive act with fewer constraints in an emergency, and in both the emergency has become a lasting condition rather than an interruption. The difference is one of scale and form. Israel has lived under a single declared state of emergency, renewed without a break, since its first week. The United States has accumulated dozens of separate emergencies, each declared by a president for a stated purpose, many of which have never been ended. [cite:us2_acri_emergency2012] [cite:us2_brennan_nea]

Israel: one emergency, renewed every year

On 19 May 1948 the Provisional State Council adopted the Law and Administration Ordinance, whose section 9 let the government declare a state of emergency and issue emergency regulations with the force of law. The declaration made that month has never lapsed. [cite:us2_wiki_ilemergency] [cite:us2_adalah_emergency2003] For decades it continued without parliamentary review. Basic Law: The Government of 1992 limited each declaration to one year but allowed unlimited renewals, and the power now sits in section 38 of the version re-enacted in 2001. Every government since has renewed it. [cite:us2_acri_emergency2012] [cite:us2_wiki_ilemergency] When Israel ratified the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights it told the United Nations, on 3 October 1991, that the emergency declared in 1948 "has remained in force ever since" and that it was derogating from the treaty's guarantee against arbitrary detention to the extent its measures required. [cite:us2_adalah_emergency2003]

The declaration matters because other laws hang on it. By Adalah's reading in 2003, regulations issued under section 9, the Prevention of Terrorism Ordinance of 1948 and the Prevention of Infiltration Law of 1954 are valid only while the emergency lasts, and the Control of Products and Services Law of 1957 gives ministers wide powers "during a state of emergency". [cite:us2_adalah_emergency2003] Governments have used the power for ordinary economic business: 204 emergency regulations on labour disputes and economic questions were issued between 1974 and 1982 alone. [cite:us2_wiki_ilemergency] A separate body of emergency law does not depend on the declaration at all. The British Mandate's Defence (Emergency) Regulations of 1945 were absorbed into Israeli law by the same 1948 ordinance, and Adalah notes that they "remain in force today" whether or not an emergency is declared. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] [cite:us2_adalah_emergency2003]

Those regulations were the legal frame of the military government imposed on most of Israel's Arab citizens from October 1948. Regulation 125 let a commander declare any area closed, and between 1948 and 1967 about a third of all convictions of Arab citizens were for breaches of the Defence Regulations, most of them for lacking a permit. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] When the military government ended on 1 December 1966 the regulations stayed. Enforcement passed to the police and in some cases the Shin Bet, and the powers kept included closed areas (Regulation 125), restriction orders (109), police supervision (110), administrative detention (111, replaced in 1979 by a statute) and deportation (112). [cite:rs_cw6_akevot_gl17005] [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_mg_e] Earlier volumes follow that history. [scene:rd6_military_government|The Road to 1948: military government] [scene:cw6_military_rule_end|The end of military rule] Part Four takes up administrative detention as it is used today. [scene:us4_detention|Part Four: detention without trial]

The only sustained legal challenge to the emergency itself failed slowly. The Association for Civil Rights in Israel petitioned the High Court in 1999. The court required the government to report on its progress in moving emergency rules into ordinary law, and on 8 May 2012 dismissed the petition, finding that it "has exhausted itself, although the work has not been finished"; some of the most contested emergency measures remained in force. [cite:us2_acri_emergency2012] [cite:us2_wiki_ilemergency]

The United States: many emergencies, rarely ended

The American framework dates from the National Emergencies Act of 1976. It requires a president to declare an emergency formally to use the special powers in other statutes, and lets Congress end one. In practice Congress has seldom done so, and the count has risen steadily. By the Brennan Center's count, 75 emergencies had been declared under the Act by May 2022, 68 of them chiefly to impose sanctions under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), and 41 were still in force. The oldest, declared by President Carter in 1979 during the Iran hostage crisis, was still active. [cite:us2_brennan_2022] By 19 October 2026 the number in effect had reached 51. [cite:us2_brennan_nea] Since January 2025 President Trump has declared a national energy emergency and used IEEPA emergencies over synthetic opioids, migration and trade deficits as the basis for tariffs. [cite:us2_cato_nea50] On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs, one of the few times a court has set limits on what a declared emergency can do. [cite:us2_wiki_learning] [scene:us2_courts|The courts and the overhaul]

The two records cut in different directions. Israel's single emergency is broader in principle, since it underpins whole statutes and sits beside colonial-era regulations that need no declaration at all, and its use against Arab citizens before 1966 shows whom such powers fell on hardest. The American emergencies are narrower one by one but have multiplied, and since 2025 they have been used as the basis for trade policy. Neither system has ended its emergencies through the legislature; in each the most consequential limit of recent years came from a court, and in Israel's case the court declined to impose one. This book did not find a current, itemised official list of which Israeli laws still depend on the declaration; Adalah's 2003 account is the latest such list found, and it should be read as of that date.
