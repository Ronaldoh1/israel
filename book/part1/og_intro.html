This book follows the gods and goddesses that were worshipped before the Church came, or beside it, or underneath it: the mysteries of Eleusis and Isis, the figure of Wisdom, the mother on the hill of Tepeyac, the sun gods of the Inca, the Mexica and the Maya, and the healers and ceremonies of the Andes and the Great Plains. The popular story says the Church destroyed them all, or secretly renamed them all. The record shows something more varied and more interesting.

Some were ended by law and force. The emperor Theodosius banned sacrifice and closed the temples in 391 and 392; a Gothic army wrecked the hall of initiation at Eleusis in 396; Justinian's general shut the last temple of Isis on the Nile around 535. [cite:vt21_wiki_theodpagan] [cite:vt33_wiki_telesterion] [cite:vt33_procopius_philae] Some were stripped for gold: the sun temple at Cusco paid a ransom in 1533. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] Some were burned as idolatry: the Maya books at Maní in 1562, the Andean ancestors in the campaigns of 1609 to 1670. [cite:vt34_arch_mani] [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] And some were banned not by any church but by a government: the United States outlawed the Sun Dance in 1883. [cite:vt35_tah_teller]

Others were absorbed. A pope wrote in 601 that temples should be kept and given new altars, so that people would "come to places familiar and dear to them". [cite:vt36_fordham_mellitus] A Franciscan friar complained in 1576 that pilgrims at Tepeyac were still calling the Virgin by the name of the old mother. [cite:vt36_sahagun1576] This book keeps the two stories apart: what was destroyed, by whom, and what was carried forward, and by whom. Where a Christian figure is said to be an older god in disguise, the book tests the claim against the documents and says which parts hold. The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads what the old gods taught about descent, light and the mother.
