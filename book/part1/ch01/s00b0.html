A Word Named by the Haters, Not the Hated

The word's paper trail runs from 1781 to 2015, and it tells a story in four steps. A linguist named a family of languages. Race science turned the label into a "race." A Jewish scholar first wrote "antisemitic" to attack that race theory. Then a German agitator who was not Jewish made "antisemitism" the name of his own movement against Jews. The familiar version says the word is precise, naming hatred of a people called Semites. The documents show a misnomer. Its meaning, though, is not in doubt.

The first step was linguistic. In 1781 the Göttingen historian August Ludwig von Schlözer coined "Semitic" (semitisch) for a family of related languages, borrowing the name of Shem, son of Noah, from Genesis 10. Today that family includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Amharic, Tigrinya and Maltese. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] The term classified languages. It said nothing about blood, race or religion, and it never belonged to one people.

The second step turned grammar into hierarchy. In 1855 the French scholar Ernest Renan published a comparative history of the Semitic languages that treated their speakers, Hebrews and Arabs alike, as a "Semitic race" inferior to the "Aryans." This was the race science of the era: a label made for languages, converted into a ranking of peoples. Historians now regard "Semitic race" as a discredited category, which is one reason the word still confuses. [cite:wiki_renan] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym]

The adjective came next, and a Jewish scholar coined it. In 1860 the bibliographer Moritz Steinschneider wrote of "antisemitic prejudices" (antisemitische Vorurteile) to rebut Renan's claim that Semitic peoples were inferior. In that first use, the target was the whole invented "Semitic race." [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] Within twenty years the word would be seized and narrowed to one people.

The man most associated with that narrowing was Wilhelm Marr (1819–1904), a German journalist and a Lutheran, not a Jew as is sometimes claimed. In 1879 he published The Victory of Judaism over Germandom and founded the League of Antisemites, the first German organization devoted to the alleged Jewish threat. The league called for forcing Jews out of Germany. Marr's Antisemitic Pamphlets of 1881 spread the word further. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] The term's purpose was to make old Jew-hatred sound like modern science. If Jews were a foreign "Semitic race," then no conversion and no number of centuries in Germany could make them German. The word was coined by the people doing the hating, about the people being hated. Marr's biographer Moshe Zimmermann reports that near the end of his life Marr renounced antisemitism in a text he never published. [cite:jvl_marr]

Jews had their own name for the same hatred. In 1882, after pogroms in the Russian Empire, the Odessa physician Leon Pinsker published Auto-Emancipation. He called the hatred "Judeophobia," an irrational fear of Jews that he described as an inherited disease. The name left no doubt about the target. Pinsker concluded that Jews needed a homeland of their own, which makes the pamphlet one of the founding texts of Zionism. [cite:jvl_pinsker] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] Marr and Pinsker wrote within three years of each other: one named the hatred to make it respectable, the other named it to answer it.

More than a century later, the institutions most invested in the word acknowledged the flaw in its root. In April 2015 the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance recommended writing "antisemitism" without a hyphen. The hyphen, it reasoned, "allows for the possibility of something called Semitism," lending weight to a discredited racial classification. Style guides followed, and The New York Times dropped the hyphen in 2021. [cite:ihra_spelling_2015] [cite:wjc_nyt_spelling]

Two things are therefore true together. The root is a misnomer: "Semitic" describes languages, Palestinians who speak Arabic are as Semitic as anyone, and many Jews have never spoken a Semitic language at home. And the meaning is settled by use: since the 1880s, "antisemitism" has meant hostility to Jews, and nothing broader. That cuts both ways. The retort "Arabs are Semites too, so I can't be antisemitic" fails, because words mean what they are used to mean, not what their parts suggest. Whether the word should stretch to cover political criticism of a state is a separate, still-contested question, set out in [scene:s00b|the preceding section]. The test lies in the target and the content of what is said, not in the label. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] [cite:ihra_spelling_2015]
