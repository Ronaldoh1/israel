Abdullah Between the Jewish Agency, Britain and the Arab League, 1947–1948

Of the Arab rulers who sent armies into Palestine in May 1948, one had been talking to the Jewish Agency for months beforehand. King Abdullah of Transjordan commanded the Arab Legion, a force trained and commanded by British officers, and he had territorial aims of his own. [cite:wiki_jordan_annexation] A familiar account holds that Jordan went to war in 1948 solely to destroy the new Jewish state alongside its allies, with no separate interest of its own. The record of his meetings, and of what his army actually did, tells a more complicated story. Whether it amounts to "collusion" is one of the longest-running arguments in the history of the war.

Abdullah's separate position was visible before the partition vote. When the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine toured the region in the summer of 1947, Transjordan declined to send a representative to testify outside the country. Instead the committee's chairman and seven of its members visited the king in Amman on 25 July 1947. [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop]

In early November 1947, at Naharayim on the Jordan, Abdullah met Golda Meyerson, later Golda Meir, of the Jewish Agency. According to the Center for Israel Education's account, he told her that he intended to annex the Arab areas of Palestine, and she replied that the Zionist leaders would not oppose this if there were no clashes between Jewish and Transjordanian forces. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_golda] The historian Avi Shlaim calls the understanding reached that November a "tacit agreement." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab] The sources record no written text. What existed, on this account, was a shared expectation: the king would take the Arab part of Palestine, and the Jewish state would not fight him for it.

The British side of the triangle came into view in February 1948. That month Britain's Foreign Secretary, an office Ernest Bevin had held since July 1945 [cite:rs_rd5_britannica_bevin], met the prime minister of Transjordan and John Glubb, the British officer who commanded the Arab Legion. After that meeting, according to Graham Jevon's study of Glubb and the Legion (Cambridge University Press, 2017), Glubb "actively sought to thwart the efforts of the Arab irregular forces" in Palestine and sought a military accord with the Zionists. Jevon writes that his findings corroborate Shlaim's disputed claim that Britain gave Abdullah a "green light" to occupy the areas allotted to the Arab state. He also concludes that Glubb was not the architect of this Greater Transjordan scheme but the man who carried it out. [cite:rs_rd5_jevon_2017] The abstract of Jevon's chapter, which is what this book has drawn on, does not give the date of the meeting or record what was said.

Shlaim states the British policy plainly. Britain, he writes, "was resigned to the emergence of a Jewish state but supported her client, King Abdullah," and in doing so "helped to abort the birth of a Palestinian state." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_ab]

The Arab League pulled Abdullah the other way. The League's anti-Hashemite members suspected him of collaborating with the Zionists. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab] He, for his part, opposed every move toward Palestinian independence and refused to deal with Hajj Amin al-Husayni, the Mufti, who led the Arab Higher Committee. [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] The quarrel over who would speak for the Palestinians is told in [scene:rd5_arab_leadership|the section on Arab leadership]. On 30 April 1948 the League's Political Committee decided that "all the Arab states must prepare their armies for the invasion of Palestine on 15 May." Under pressure from Transjordan and Iraq, Abdullah was named commander-in-chief. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab] The man suspected of a private arrangement with the Jewish Agency was now the nominal head of the Arab war against it.

On 10 May 1948, four days before the end of the Mandate, Meyerson crossed to Amman with Ezra Danin. The two travelled disguised as an Arab couple. According to the Center for Israel Education, Abdullah told her that he was now "one of five" and asked: "why don't you wait a few years? Drop your demands for free immigration." [cite:rs_rd5_cie_golda] Meyerson's own report at the time, as summarized in Shlaim's notes, records that the king did not deny their earlier understanding, said he had no choice but to join the invasion, and that they did not part as enemies. On 13 May Yaacov Shimoni of the Jewish Agency weighed it up: the king "has not entirely betrayed the agreement, nor is he entirely loyal to it." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab]

On 15 May, as the armies moved, Syria's president, Shukri al-Quwwatli, urged a halt to the advance. Abdullah refused. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab]

What the Arab Legion then did can be set against those meetings. Even before the Mandate ended, the Legion attacked the Gush Etzion bloc of Jewish settlements; its defenders surrendered and some were massacred, according to Shlaim's notes. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_cw] Once the war between states began, the State Department's Office of the Historian records, Transjordanian forces intervened only in areas designated for the Arab state or for the Jerusalem corpus separatum, the separate Jerusalem zone of the partition plan. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] Within those limits the Legion fought hard. In the first round, according to Shlaim's notes, the Jewish Quarter of Jerusalem surrendered on 28 May, and three frontal Israeli attacks on Latrun, on 25 and 30 May and 9 June, were repelled by the Arab Legion. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] After the second truce began on 18 July, the Legion continued shelling West Jerusalem through October and on 12 August demolished the Latrun pumping station, which the United Nations held, according to an Israeli army account. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce2] The Legion's commander was also the source of many of the most-quoted figures on the balance of forces, which historians treat with caution because he commanded one of the sides. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance]

By the end of the war the Legion held East Jerusalem's Old City and the West Bank. It was Jordan's most consequential military action of the war, and the territory remained under its administration for the next nineteen years. [cite:wiki_jordan_annexation] Under the armistice agreements of 1949, Jordan kept the West Bank. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] The Arab League's All-Palestine Government, proclaimed in Gaza in September 1948, was opposed by Abdullah, who said it would not operate in areas held by the Arab Legion. On 1 December 1948 a congress of about 3,000 delegates at Jericho proclaimed the union of Palestine and Transjordan and acknowledged him as king; the figures come from Shlaim alone. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni]

The formal annexation came later and is told in full in [scene:ijr_src_s05|Jordan's war, armistice and annexation]. In brief: on 24 April 1950 Jordan's parliament annexed the West Bank, declaring support for "complete unity between the two sides of the Jordan and their union into one State." [cite:wiki_jordan_annexation] Only Britain, Iraq and Pakistan recognized it, and Britain's recognition excluded Jerusalem. The strongest regional opposition came from Jordan's fellow Arab League states, whose Political Committee ruled in Cairo that the annexation violated the League's earlier resolution and considered expelling Jordan. [cite:sixdaywar_jerusalem] During the nineteen years of Jordanian rule that followed, Jewish access to the Western Wall and other religious sites in the Old City was denied, contrary to the 1949 armistice terms, and dozens of historic synagogues were damaged or destroyed. One reading notes that the 1950 resolution's "two banks" language mirrors the Revisionist Zionist claim, associated with Jabotinsky, that both banks of the Jordan belong together, used here in the opposite direction; it is a structural parallel, and the record does not show that either side was answering the other.

Was there collusion across the Jordan? The case that there was, in substance, is Shlaim's, and on the British side Jevon's study says its findings corroborate it. On this view there was a tacit understanding between Abdullah and the Jewish Agency to divide the territory the United Nations had allotted to an Arab state, Britain gave the king a green light to take his share, and Glubb restrained the Arab irregulars and sought an accord with the Zionists. The Legion's conduct fits: it stayed out of the territory allotted to the Jewish state, and the Palestinian state that the partition plan foresaw was never born. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_ab] [cite:rs_rd5_jevon_2017] [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b]

The case that the arrangement was partial at best rests on the same meetings. In Amman on 10 May Abdullah called himself "one of five," asked the Jews to defer statehood and drop their demand for free immigration, and joined the invasion. The Jewish Agency's own Shimoni judged him neither loyal to the understanding nor in full breach of it. And the Legion fought Israeli forces hard where their aims met, in Jerusalem and at Latrun. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_golda] [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_ab] [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war]

The two cases are not as far apart as the word "collusion" suggests. Both accept that Abdullah and the Jewish Agency talked, that he wanted the Arab part of Palestine, and that he went to war as part of the Arab coalition. They differ on how firm the understanding was and how much it shaped the fighting. Several things remain unsettled by the record gathered here. The Naharayim and Amman exchanges come largely through the Center for Israel Education's account and Meyerson's own report, not through a Jordanian record. The February 1948 meeting is known from the abstract of Jevon's study, without its date or the words spoken. And the Legion's restraint toward the Jewish state's territory can be read as the keeping of an understanding or as the pursuit of the king's own aims, which happened to coincide with it.
