Reform by Scandal: From the 2010 Law to Central Control of the Funds

The Holy See's financial rules today were not drawn up on a clean sheet. Nearly every layer of them was added after a particular case exposed a gap: a seizure by Italian prosecutors in 2010, the arrest of a curial accountant in 2013, an embezzlement found inside the IOR's own property sales, and the London building examined in [scene:vt10_london_trial|the previous section]. This section follows those cases and the reforms that answered them, in order, and closes with the outside evaluators' verdict on the result. The external checks themselves, the euro agreement with the European Union and the Council of Europe's MONEYVAL committee, are described in full in [scene:vt5_moneyval_euro|Part Five] and are summarised here only where a reform responded to them.

The first law and the 2010 seizure

Vatican City's Monetary Agreement with the European Union, signed on 17 December 2009, obliged it to adopt the Union's anti-money-laundering rules. [cite:vt5_va_monetary] The Holy See's first anti-money-laundering law and a supervisory body to apply it, the Financial Information Authority (AIF), followed on 30 December 2010, by motu proprio of Benedict XVI. [cite:vt5_va_aif_profile] In between came the case that showed why. On 21 September 2010 Italian prosecutors seized about 23 million euros of the IOR's money held at a small Italian bank, Credito Artigiano, after two transfers were reported as suspicious, and placed the IOR's president, Ettore Gotti Tedeschi, and another senior official under investigation. The Holy See said it was "perplexed and astonished," expressed full confidence in Gotti Tedeschi, and said it was already working to bring its finances into line with international anti-money-laundering rules. [cite:vt10_bbc_seizure2010] The money was released in 2011. Gotti Tedeschi was ousted as president on 24 May 2012; the laundering case against him was dropped in 2013, and he was acquitted again in 2014. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior] The Holy See joined MONEYVAL in 2011, and the committee's first report, in July 2012, found 9 of 16 key standards met and "strongly recommended" that the IOR be given a prudential supervisor. [cite:vt5_ncreg_aml2017] [cite:vt5_mv_2012es]

2013–2015: the IOR clears its books

In June 2013 Italian police arrested Monsignor Nunzio Scarano, for 22 years a senior accountant at the Administration of the Patrimony of the Apostolic See (APSA), on charges of plotting with a secret-service agent and a broker to bring 20 million euros into Italy from Switzerland; the IOR froze more than 2 million euros in his ten accounts there. In January 2016 a Rome court acquitted him of corruption and attempted smuggling and convicted him of making a false accusation; he said his money came from donations. [cite:vt10_rns_scarano2016] The same year the IOR hired the consultancy Promontory to review every client relationship, and from October 2013 it asked lay customers who were neither clergy nor Holy See employees to close their accounts. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior] By the end of 2014 it had closed 2,600 dormant accounts and 554 that did not fit its client profile, while 1,460 more ended normally, leaving 15,181 clients. [cite:vt10_ansa_ior2015] By October 2015 about 4,800 accounts had been closed as "no longer in compliance with the Vatican regulatory framework," on grounds of the holder's nationality, residence or use of the money. [cite:vt10_ansa_aif2016] The IOR's president, Jean-Baptiste de Franssu, said the institute had shown "great commitment in dealing with offenses that in past have affected the Institute." [cite:vt10_ansa_ior2015]

The clean-up also reached the IOR's past. Promontory's review of 2014 led to the prosecution of Angelo Caloia, IOR president from 1989 to 2009, and the lawyer Gabriele Liuzzo. On 21 January 2021 the Vatican tribunal found that 29 properties of the IOR and its subsidiary SGIR had been sold below value between 2002 and 2007 and that about 19 million euros had been taken; it sentenced Caloia and Liuzzo to eight years and eleven months, Liuzzo's son Lamberto to five years and two months, and ordered about 38 million euros confiscated and more than 20 million euros paid in damages. [cite:vt10_vn_caloia2021] The defendants maintained their innocence and had sought to call former secretaries of state and cardinals to show that the sales were approved. [cite:vt10_aj_caloia2021] The Holy See described the case as the first application of its December 2018 anti-laundering legislation. [cite:vt10_vn_caloia2021] An earlier case had ended less clearly: in February 2017 two former senior IOR managers, Cipriani and Tulliò, were convicted of reporting omissions and acquitted of laundering. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior]

2014–2022: new offices and one bank for the Holy See

Francis created the Secretariat for the Economy on 24 February 2014, with Cardinal George Pell as its first prefect. It supervises the administration of the curial offices and Peter's Pence and prepares the Holy See's budget and consolidated accounts, under a Council for the Economy. [cite:vt6_gc_spe] [cite:vt6_va_spe] The AIF received prudential supervision of the IOR in 2013 and was renamed the Supervisory and Financial Information Authority (ASIF) in December 2020. [cite:vt5_va_aif_profile] [cite:vt5_wiki_asif] The IOR received new statutes from Francis on 8 August 2019. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior]

The London case then changed who holds the money. By a motu proprio of 26 December 2020, in force from 1 January 2021, Francis took the management of the Secretariat of State's investments, property and funds, including the administration of Peter's Pence, away from the Secretariat and gave it to APSA under the oversight of the Secretariat for the Economy. [cite:vt6_vn_apsa2020] [scene:vt9_apsa_governorate|Part Nine: APSA] The 2022 constitution of the Curia, Praedicate Evangelium, provided that the Holy See's financial transactions run through the IOR, and a rescript of 23 August 2022 interpreted that as making the IOR "the exclusive" manager and custodian of the Holy See's movable assets: every Holy See body, the Secretariat of State included, had to move its money out of other banks into the IOR within 30 days of 1 September 2022. [cite:vt10_ncreg_rescript2022] The Secretariat had held accounts at Swiss banks, including Credit Suisse, through which the London investment was first made. [cite:vt10_ncreg_rescript2022]

The evaluators' verdict

MONEYVAL's second evaluation of the Holy See, adopted in April 2021, rated its system's effectiveness "substantial" in five areas and "moderate" in six, with none "low." It also found that insider risk at middle and senior levels was underrated, that the Promoter of Justice's office lacked staff for complex financial cases, and that far less money had been confiscated than had been frozen. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] The Holy See said it "welcomes the Moneyval Report" and would keep working towards full compliance. [cite:vt5_pv_moneyval2021] A follow-up report in May 2024 raised the rating on correspondent banking, the IOR's links to foreign banks, from non-compliant to compliant, and two others from partially to largely compliant. [cite:vt5_fatf_fur2024] [cite:vt5_pv_moneyval2024] No later MONEYVAL report on the Holy See was found as of October 2026.

What the record shows

The pattern across forty years is plain in the documents: reform followed exposure. The Ambrosiano losses produced a settlement but no supervisor; an Italian seizure produced the first law; an arrest and a consultancy review produced the account closures; a London investment produced the transfer of the Secretariat of State's money to APSA and the rule that the IOR alone holds the Holy See's funds. [cite:vt10_avv_anfossi] [cite:vt10_bbc_seizure2010] [cite:vt10_ansa_aif2016] [cite:vt6_vn_apsa2020] [cite:vt10_ncreg_rescript2022] The record does not show the opposite claims either: the IOR is not outside anti-money-laundering law, and its money is not yet fully transparent, by the evaluators' own findings. [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] Whether the system that the London trial was meant to vindicate also respects the rights of the accused is the question that the Court of Appeal's ruling of 17 March 2026 has left open. [cite:vt10_vn_mistrial2026]
