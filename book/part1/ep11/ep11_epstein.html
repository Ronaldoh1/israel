Epstein: The Official Finding and What Surrounds It

Jeffrey Epstein was found dead in his cell at the Metropolitan Correctional Center in Manhattan on the morning of 10 August 2019. The New York City medical examiner ruled the manner of death suicide. The Justice Department's Inspector General, in 2023, found negligence and misconduct by the jail's staff and no evidence contradicting the FBI's conclusion that no crime caused his death. [cite:ep9_nhpr_ig2023]

What the jail failed to do

The failures are documented in the government's own reports. He had apparently tried to take his own life weeks earlier and was then taken off suicide watch. He was left without a cellmate even though staff had been told he needed one. Officers missed more than 75 required checks overnight and then falsified the records; the two on duty were charged, and the prosecution was deferred and later dropped. Cameras malfunctioned. [cite:ep9_nhpr_ig2023] The New York Times, reporting in June 2026, added what his cellmates had said: one told investigators he had warned guards twice; the last, moved out the day before, warned guards that Epstein "was not good to be left alone". No one replaced him, and Epstein was found dead within a day. The Times concluded that the evidence points to suicide, and that a murder in that setting would have required a near-impossible conspiracy. [cite:ep11_nyt_nbcps2026]

What happened to the evidence

In June 2024 an FBI agent obtained authorisation to destroy the master recording of the jail's video archive as "no longer pertinent", and a prosecutor agreed. When the department needed the footage in 2025, it re-recorded it from a backup system and lost 62 seconds just before midnight; the Attorney General's explanation for the gap was a theory the FBI's own specialist had not been able to test. When Congress released the complete footage in September 2025, that minute showed nothing unusual. [cite:ep11_cbs_minute2026] The video does not change the finding. The destruction of the original is itself a documented failure: a central piece of evidence in the most scrutinised death in American custody was discarded five years later.

The dissents

Doubt has been expressed by qualified people, and the book records it. In October 2019 Dr Michael Baden, a former New York City chief medical examiner hired by Epstein's brother, said the injuries were more consistent with homicide than suicide; the chief medical examiner, Barbara Sampson, stood by her office's ruling. [cite:ep11_npr_baden2019] Professor Michael Freeman, editor of the Journal of Forensic and Legal Medicine, has reviewed the medical examiner's file, which Epstein's brother gave him. He says that no photographs were taken of the body where it was found and that the ligature was never preserved, and he plans to present his findings in London on 16 November 2026; he has not yet said what he concludes. [cite:ep11_political_freeman2026] The book will add his findings once they are published, and will treat them as it treats every dissent: as a challenge to the finding, not a replacement for it.

Two other items circulate widely. One of the guards on duty, Tova Noel, searched online for the latest news on Epstein about forty minutes before he was found and had made a $5,000 cash deposit ten days earlier; she told House Oversight in May 2026 that her finances had nothing to do with Epstein and that she believes he killed himself. [cite:ep11_wt_noel2026] And an inmate told the FBI he heard officers talk of a cover-up that morning. His account was never substantiated; three inmates who could see the cell door said no one went in or out after Epstein was locked in. [cite:ep11_herald_inmate]

The cameras that were being replaced

The jail knew its cameras were failing a year before Epstein arrived. On 6 September 2018 the warden asked for $800,000 to replace the whole system. Two contracts followed: $698,108.99 on 21 September 2018 to one company for equipment and labour, and $34,089.28 three days later to another for networking equipment and wiring. The inspector general's report names neither company. [cite:ep11_oig_cameras] A jail email from April 2018 shows a technician discussing "a potential camera project" with a company called Signet, and records released in 2026 have been read as identifying Signet as the main contractor, a reading this book has not been able to confirm against the contract itself. [cite:ep11_signet_email2018]

The work was not finished by August 2019. From March 2019 the Bureau of Prisons sent technicians from other prisons to help, but they were sometimes pulled away to cover guard shortages and some lacked the skills. A recorder failed on 29 July, leaving the cameras connected to it unable to record, and no one noticed until 8 August. [cite:ep11_oig_cameras] Nothing in the record suggests a contractor caused the failure. What it shows is a year-long replacement, under-staffed, still incomplete when the most important prisoner in the building died.

What the death closed

However he died, the consequence is documented. There was no trial, no cross-examination, and no chance that a man facing life in prison would trade what he knew for leniency. Part Nine set out how much has had to be rebuilt in pieces since. That loss, not a hidden killing, is what the documents establish.
