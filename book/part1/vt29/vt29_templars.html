The Templars: Bankers, Heretics or Victims of a King?

The Knights Templar sit awkwardly in a book about inner traditions. They were not mystics, they had no secret gospel, and they were created and protected by the papacy for almost two centuries. They belong here for a different reason: their destruction between 1307 and 1314 is the clearest medieval case of heresy procedure being turned against a body of Catholic religious, and it has gathered more popular legend than almost any other episode of the period. This section sets the record beside those legends. [cite:vt29_barber_cup2] [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon]

From pilgrim guards to a papal order

The order began in 1119, when two French knights, Hugh of Payns and Godfrey of Saint-Omer, undertook to guard pilgrims on the road from Jaffa to Jerusalem. King Baldwin II and Patriarch Warmund gave them quarters on the Temple platform, in the building the Latins called the "Temple of Solomon", the al-Aqsa mosque; from it they took their name. Church approval probably came at a council at Nablus in January 1120. In January 1129 a council at Troyes, encouraged by Bernard of Clairvaux, gave them a Latin Rule of 71 clauses, and Bernard wrote a treatise in praise of this "new knighthood" of monks who fought. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

On 29 March 1139 Pope Innocent II issued Omne datum optimum. The bull called the Templars defenders of the Catholic Church, placed their possessions under papal protection and granted them privileges that freed them from much local church authority. This is the document behind the common statement that the Templars "answered only to the pope". It is accurate as a description of their legal standing, and it explains a later fact: the only authority that could lawfully dissolve them was the pope. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

Gifts of land across Europe, and the need to move money from estates in the West to castles in the East, made the order a financial institution as well as a military one. Pilgrims deposited valuables with one house and drew on them at another by letters of credit; kings and nobles borrowed from the order and stored treasure with it. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar] In France the Paris Temple became, in the words of a 2016 Madrid doctoral study of its accounts, "banker to kings", and the French royal treasury's accounts were kept there through much of the thirteenth century, the study covering 1221 to 1295. [cite:vt29_ucm_temple] Popular accounts call this "Europe's first international banking network". The safer description is one of the earliest cross-border deposit and transfer systems in Latin Europe; Italian banking houses developed alongside it, not out of it.

Friday 13 October 1307

In the early hours of Friday 13 October 1307, officials of King Philip IV of France arrested the Templars throughout his kingdom and seized their property, acting formally on behalf of the papal inquisitors in France. The charges were that at secret receptions new brothers were made to deny Christ and spit on the crucifix, that they exchanged indecent kisses, that sodomy was permitted and that they worshipped an idol. In October and November almost all the prisoners, including the Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, confessed. Malcolm Barber, the leading historian of the trial, writes that on many of the prisoners "torture was freely used". [cite:vt29_barber_cup2]

Pope Clement V had not authorised the arrests and at first treated them as an affront to his own authority over an exempt order. On 22 November 1307 he changed course and issued Pastoralis praeeminentiae, ordering every Christian ruler to arrest the Templars in his lands. [cite:vt29_barber_cup2] Why Philip acted is a question of motive, and the documents do not give a single answer. He was short of money for his war with England and owed the order large sums, and the arrests freed him from those debts and gave him control of its treasuries. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]

No reliable count of those arrested across France survives. The best-documented number is a different one: by early May 1310 almost six hundred Templars in Paris had come forward to defend the order before the papal commission. The king's answer was swift. On 12 May 1310 fifty-four of them were burned outside Paris as relapsed heretics, men who had confessed, then withdrawn their confessions. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

Chinon, August 1308: what the parchment says

Between 17 and 20 August 1308, three cardinals sent by Clement V, Bérenger Frédol, Étienne de Suisy and Landolfo Brancacci, questioned Molay and four other leaders held in the castle of Chinon. The record of that inquiry is the Chinon parchment. The Vatican Apostolic Archive holds the original (Archivum Arcis, Armarium D 217) and an authentic copy (D 218). The Italian palaeographer Barbara Frale found it in September 2001, misfiled, and published it in the Journal of Medieval History in 2004; in 2007 the archive issued a facsimile edition, Processus contra Templarios, in 799 numbered copies. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon] The Archive itself is described in [scene:vt14_apostolic_archive|Part Fourteen].

The parchment records that the leaders confessed to some of the charges, chiefly the denial of Christ and spitting at reception, which several described as done "in words only, not in spirit", denied others, asked for pardon, and were absolved from excommunication and restored to the sacraments. It does not declare the order innocent. Frale reads it as evidence that Clement thought the order was not heretical and could be reformed, and that he tried and failed to keep it out of Philip's hands. Other historians, such as Anne Gilmour-Bryson, warn that testimony given after torture cannot be read at face value in either direction. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon]

Popular versions go further than this, saying that Clement found the Templars innocent and that the parchment proves a frame-up. An absolution is granted to people who have confessed and repented; it is the opposite of an acquittal. Some accounts also say the document was hidden for 700 years and then released in a costly edition to keep it from the public. The parchment was misfiled rather than locked away, a related Chinon document addressed to Philip IV, also recording absolution of confessing Templars, had been in print since Étienne Baluze published it in 1693, and Frale's article reached print three years before the facsimile. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon]

Suppression by provision, 1312

At the Council of Vienne, on 22 March 1312, Clement issued Vox in excelso, approved in session on 3 April. Its wording matters. The pope suppressed the order "not by definitive sentence, but by apostolic provision or ordinance": the council had not convicted it of heresy, and the bull says that a definitive sentence would not have been lawful on the evidence. The reason given was scandal: the order had lost its good name, recruits would stop and its property would be lost, and the scandal was "impossible to allay as long as the order continues to exist". Anyone who joined it or wore its habit afterwards was excommunicated automatically. [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne]

On 2 May 1312 Ad providam gave the order's houses, lands and revenues to the Knights Hospitaller, except in Castile, Aragon, Portugal and Majorca, where the pope reserved the property to himself. [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne] In practice much Templar property in France stayed under the crown's control for years, and in Portugal King Dinis refounded the Templar houses as the Order of Christ. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]

Molay and Geoffroi de Charney were held for almost two more years. They were sentenced on 18 March 1314, publicly withdrew their confessions, and were burned that evening on the Île des Javiaux in the Seine. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1] [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]

Friday the 13th and the afterlife of the order

The widely repeated claim that the arrests of Friday 13 October 1307 are the origin of the Friday-the-13th superstition has no documentary basis. Friday and the number thirteen were each thought unlucky before the twentieth century, but the combined date as a fixed superstition is hard to find before then; a 1907 novel titled Friday, the Thirteenth is one early landmark. The Templar link was spread by the 1980s bestseller Holy Blood, Holy Grail and popularised by Dan Brown's The Da Vinci Code in 2003. [cite:vt29_natgeo_fri13]

The claim that the Templars survived in secret and passed their knowledge to the Freemasons is a separate and contested question, treated with the other fraternities in [scene:vt30_rosicrucians_masons|Part Thirty].

What the record does support is narrower and still serious. A religious order under papal protection was arrested by a king, its members were tortured into confessions, men who defended it were burned, and the pope, who had privately absolved its leaders, dissolved it without a verdict because the scandal could not be undone. Read on its own terms, the Templar case shows the papacy losing a contest with the French crown rather than hunting an inner tradition. [cite:vt29_barber_cup2] [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne]
