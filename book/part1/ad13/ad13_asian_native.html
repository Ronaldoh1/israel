Asian and Native Americans: The Highest Average and the Longest Dispossession

The two groups in this section sit at opposite ends of most of the measures in this book. Asian Americans as a whole have the highest median income, wealth and life expectancy of any major group, a fact often cited as proof that the Dream is open to anyone. American Indians and Alaska Natives have among the lowest mobility, the highest poverty and the shortest lives, and their relationship with the federal government runs through land, trust and treaty rather than through the labour market alone. In both cases the single number hides the mechanism. For Asian Americans the average conceals the widest spread of income of any group; for Native Americans the outcomes trace to documented federal land policy and to the rules that still govern land held in trust.

The average behind the "model minority"

The phrase "model minority" entered American writing in the 1960s. One of the articles that introduced the idea, William Petersen's "Success Story, Japanese-American Style" in the New York Times Magazine of 9 January 1966, credited Japanese Americans' rise after wartime internment to their cultural values and set them against Black Americans; scholars have since argued that the concept was used to cast doubt on social programmes for other groups. [cite:ad13_densho_petersen] The averages it rests on are real. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey put the median net worth of Asian families at $677,600, about twice the white median of $332,100. [cite:us8_scf2025] Asian American life expectancy at birth was 85.8 years in 2024, the highest of any group and nearly seven years above the national 79.0, and the Asian infant mortality rate of 3.44 per 1,000 births in 2023 was the lowest. [cite:ad13_nchs_lt2024] [cite:us16_cdc_imr2023] Homeownership among Asian, Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander households was 58.6 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, below the non-Hispanic white rate of 74.5 per cent. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226]

Mobility data show where the high average comes from. The Chetty, Hendren, Jones and Porter study found that the children of Asian immigrants have much higher upward mobility than any other group, while Asian children of US-born parents have mobility similar to white children. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] The advantage belongs mainly to the first step from immigrant parent to American child, the same pattern found for immigrants generally in [scene:ad13_immigrants_latinos|the previous section].

The widest spread of any group

The Pew Research Center measured income inequality within each group as the ratio between the income of a person at the 90th percentile (richer than nine in ten of the group) and one at the 10th percentile. In 2016 the ratio was 10.7 for Asian Americans, against 9.8 for Black, 7.8 for white and 7.8 for Hispanic Americans, and it had risen 77 per cent since 1970, from about 6.0. At the top, Asian Americans earned 13 per cent more than white Americans at the same percentile; at the bottom they earned 17 per cent less, where in 1970 they had earned 8 per cent more. [cite:ad13_pew_ineq]

Pew traced the spread mainly to immigration. Immigrants accounted for 81 per cent of the growth of the Asian adult population from 1970 to 2016, after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened entry from Asia, and they arrived with very different levels of education and income. Bachelor's degree attainment ranged from 9 per cent among Bhutanese Americans to 72 per cent among Indian Americans, and median household income from $36,000 for Burmese to $100,000 for Indian Americans; Pew also named discrimination, past and present, as a factor. [cite:ad13_pew_ineq] A 2024 Pew analysis of 2022 Census data found about one Asian American in ten in poverty: 19 per cent of Burmese, about 17 per cent of Hmong and about 16 per cent of Mongolian Americans, against about 6 per cent of Indian Americans. [cite:ad13_npr_pew_pov] For comparison, the official poverty rate for all Americans in 2025 was 10.2 per cent. [cite:us8_census_2025]

Native Americans: the land record

The federal government recognised 574 tribes and 325 reservations in 2025, and 7.7 million people identified as American Indian or Alaska Native, alone or with another race, in 2024. [cite:ad13_census_aian25] Their economic record cannot be separated from land. The General Allotment Act of 1887, known as the Dawes Act, divided reservation land held in common into individual parcels: 160 acres for a head of family and up to 80 acres for other individuals, with title held in trust by the federal government for 25 years. Land left over after allotment was declared "surplus" and could be sold to non-Indians. Indian-held land fell from 138 million acres to 48 million acres by 1934, when allotment stopped, and almost half of what remained was desert or semi-desert. [cite:ad13_gp_dawes] The arithmetic: 138 − 48 = 90 million acres, about 65 per cent of the 1887 total.

Land still held in trust was divided again with each generation of heirs. By 2012 more than 2.9 million fractional interests in trust land were owned by about 220,000 people. The federal government also managed the income from that land, and a class action brought on behalf of nearly 500,000 account holders, known as the Cobell case, alleged that it had failed to account for the money. Congress approved a $3.4 billion settlement in the Claims Resolution Act, signed on 8 December 2010, and it became final on 24 November 2012: $1.5 billion for the account and mismanagement claims and $1.9 billion to buy fractional interests from willing sellers and return the land to tribal ownership. [cite:ad13_doi_cobell]

Why a reservation home is hard to finance

The asset that built the middle-class Dream, the mortgaged home described in [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six], works differently on trust land. Because trust land cannot be sold or pledged as collateral, a lender cannot take the land if the borrower defaults; the borrower usually holds a lease rather than ownership, and the Bureau of Indian Affairs must review the loan. In 2015 there were 22.3 home loans per 1,000 residents across the United States and 1.9 per 1,000 on reservations, and 46 per cent of Native applicants on reservations were denied, against 29 per cent off them. A federal guarantee programme, HUD's Section 184 created in 1992, made more than 90 per cent of its loans off reservations and fewer than 200 a year on them between 2005 and 2016. [cite:ad13_hac_trust] Many reservation families buy manufactured homes instead, financed as personal-property loans that cost about 1.5 percentage points more than mortgages; researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis found denial rates of about 75 per cent for such loans to American Indian applicants in mostly reservation tracts in 2015 and 2016, and judged that trust status may explain part of the pattern without reaching a firm conclusion. [cite:ad13_mplsfed_mh]

Outcomes

The poverty rate for American Indians and Alaska Natives was 19.3 per cent in 2024, the highest of the groups the Census Bureau reports, against 7.5 per cent for Asian Americans. [cite:ad13_usafacts_pov] On reservation land it was 29.2 per cent in the mid-2010s, against 15.5 per cent nationally. [cite:ad13_hac_trust] Life expectancy at birth was 71.1 years in 2024 for non-Hispanic American Indians and Alaska Natives, eight years below the national 79.0 and nearly fifteen below Asian Americans; the infant mortality rate was 9.20 per 1,000 births in 2023, about twice the white rate of 4.48. [cite:ad13_nchs_lt2024] [cite:us16_cdc_imr2023] The Chetty study found that American Indian children, like Black children, have substantially lower rates of upward mobility than other groups, and that without a change in those rates neither group's position in the national income distribution is likely to change. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum]

Where it works as promised

For Asian immigrants and their children, the record matches the promise at least as well as for any group in this book: the second generation climbs further than any other, and the group's life expectancy is the highest in the country. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:ad13_nchs_lt2024] For Native Americans, the federal government has acknowledged part of the land record in law: allotment ended in 1934, and the Cobell settlement paid account holders and funded the return of fractional interests to tribes. [cite:ad13_gp_dawes] [cite:ad13_doi_cobell] Off reservations, Section 184 lending reaches tribal members in more than 20 states. [cite:ad13_hac_trust] What the record does not show is any of these measures closing the gaps in poverty, mobility or life expectancy, which for American Indians and Alaska Natives remain the widest in this part.
