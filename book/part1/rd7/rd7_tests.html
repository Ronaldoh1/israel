The Tests

The map comes with a set of prepared questions, each of which removes part of the documented network and reads the result. There are now 60 of them, together with nine that take a part of the record often denied. They are listed below from the lowest chance to the highest, as in the map's Tests panel, and anyone can run them there, change them, or remove any other combination of nodes. The figures are the chance of a founding on time, in 1948, and the chance of a founding ever, against about 93 percent for the full record.

Twenty-two observations follow from the table, and each is a statement about the record rather than about what anyone intended. Rows marked as often denied take a part of the record that accounts of the founding commonly deny or play down; the map's panel gives, for each, the claim usually made beside the record. Rows marked as an assumption go further than the documents: they remove people or bodies the record does not show would have fallen, and are there to show how much a figure depends on that step.

The movement and its armed groups were the two things nothing else replaced. Remove the organized movement and no one is left to pursue the state; remove the armed groups and the arms channel is empty, with nothing outside it able to fill it. Every other removal leaves something to rebuild with.

The Rothschild figure depends on what is assumed to fall with the family. On the documented record, removing the family and its houses, 46 nodes in all, leaves about 30 percent on time; it was about 34 before the land the family's PICA held, about half a million dunams in 1934, was counted. Following everything that is then cut off from the founding bodies, PICA, the colonies of 1882 to 1896, Mikveh Israel, the Carmel wine company and two banks the family dealt with among them, leaves the same figure, because the map already counts what no longer connects. The lower figures come only from assumptions. If the Balfour Declaration could not have been issued without its addressee, the figure falls to about 16 percent; the record shows the Declaration was addressed to Walter Rothschild and that he worked on its draft, but the map holds the family's link to the British government only as a name on the same page, not as a stated tie. If everyone with a documented tie to the family is taken out too, it falls to about 9 percent, but that list includes Herzl, Weizmann, Balfour and the Jewish Agency, so the test comes closer to removing the movement than to removing a family. Thirty percent is the figure the record supports.

The network before Herzl mattered through its settlement money, not its ideas. Remove the whole network that existed before 1897, from Kalischer and Hess to the Lovers of Zion, the Odessa Committee and the Alliance Israélite Universelle, and the chance falls to about 70 percent, with about 16 years lost. Almost all of that comes from the Lovers of Zion and the donors and settlements around them. The thinkers and the persecutions that set the movement going score nothing, because no document states a tie from them to a later act in a form the map can weigh. Removed with the Rothschilds, the same network brings the chance to about 20 percent. Herzl, removed on his own, costs about two points. He founded the political movement; he did not originate the idea or build the first network, and the institutions he started outlived him. The network itself is told in [scene:rd0_network|the first network, 1836–1896].

Legitimacy mattered, and the survivors' case most of all. Take away the Holocaust as the map holds it, which is only through the case its survivors made in the displaced-persons camps, the Harrison Report and the inquiries that followed, and the chance falls from about 93 to about 88 percent. Take away American Christian and public support, the committees, Blackstone's memorials and the endorsement by Congress in 1922, and it falls to about 89. Both are floors. The map can measure only the legitimacy these ties lent to decisions; it cannot measure how the Holocaust changed everything else, from immigration and money to the will of the movement itself. What it does show is that the American case, already the heaviest single influence on the map, rested partly on that legitimacy: with the new channel, removing the United States lowers the chance to about 24 percent, from about 28 before legitimacy was counted.

The public campaign was loud, but on the record it was one voice among several. Truman wrote that he had never faced as much pressure and propaganda aimed at the White House as over Palestine; in October 1947 he told a senator he had received about 35,000 pieces of mail on it, and a late-1947 poll found 65 percent of Americans in favour of partition. [cite:md_jta56] [cite:md_mew] Remove the mail campaign and the opinion polls as ties to the president and the chance falls only to about 92.5 percent, because the survivors' case, the UN committee and Congress carry the same channel. Counting the campaign also spreads the channel's weight, which is why the survivors' case and the Christian committees each cost about a point less than before. The movement's own press, Herzl's Die Welt, the Jewish Telegraphic Agency of 1917 and the Palestine Post of 1932, carried its case to editors and readers, but no document ties a decision to it, so it carries no weight. [cite:md_wiki_jta] [cite:md_jvl_agron] The same is true of the 1943 pageant We Will Never Die, seen by at least 100,000 people and by more than 200 members of Congress in Washington, which pressed for rescue, not statehood. [cite:md_ushmm_wwnd] The other side campaigned too: in 1946 the Arab Office put the Arab case to the Anglo-American Committee in more than a thousand pages, and Britain's own wartime motive for the Balfour Declaration is argued by some historians to have been propaganda aimed at Jewish opinion in America and Russia. [cite:md_palquest_ao] [cite:md_ej_balfour] Both are recorded and will be weighed with the other counterforces.

Religious institutions shaped who joined, more than what was decided. Christian missions came first: the London Society for Promoting Christianity Amongst the Jews, founded in 1809, counted the physical restoration of the Jews among its aims, and helped create the Anglo-Prussian bishopric in Jerusalem in 1841, whose first bishop was one of its workers. [cite:rl_cmj] [cite:rl_bish] On the Jewish side, Mizrachi kept religious Jews inside the Zionist Organization from 1902, the Mandate recognized a Chief Rabbinate under Rav Kook in 1921, and in 1937 American Reform rabbis reversed their 1885 position that Jews were "no longer a nation" and endorsed aid to a Jewish homeland. [cite:rl_kook] [cite:rl_reform] Opposition was organized too. Agudat Israel and the old Orthodox community of Jerusalem rejected a secular state; their representative abroad, Jacob de Haan, lobbied the British against the Zionist leadership and was shot dead in Jerusalem on 30 June 1924 by a Haganah member, on a decision the Haganah's first commander later testified he and its Jerusalem commander had made. [cite:rl_dehaan] In June 1947, four days after the UN committee began its tour, Ben-Gurion wrote to Agudat Israel promising a Jewish Sabbath, kosher state kitchens, religious marriage law and autonomy for religious schools, to present a united position. [cite:rl_sq] Remove religious Jewish support and the chance stays at about 93 percent; remove every religious body, both sides, and it falls to about 90, most of that the Christian support already counted as legitimacy. The opposition is recorded but not yet weighed; that is the next task.

The opposition was real, and it was beaten before the vote as much as at it. The Palestinian national movement had been broken a decade earlier. The revolt of 1936 to 1939 cost about 5,000 Arab lives; Britain outlawed the Arab Higher Committee, deported three of its members to the Seychelles and drove Hajj Amin al-Husseini into exile, and historians count that defeat as an important reason for the Palestinian defeat in 1948. [cite:cf_osu] [cite:cf_ahc] The revolt also won the 1939 White Paper, the sharpest British reversal of the Mandate years. When the UN committee came in 1947, the reconstituted Arab Higher Committee boycotted it, holding that the Palestinian Arabs' rights were "self-evident and could not continue to be subject to investigation"; the Arab states put their case to it in Lebanon instead. [cite:cf_unscop] The earliest Arab agreement on the record, Faisal's with Weizmann in 1919, had been conditional on Arab independence, and the condition was never met. [cite:cf_fw] In Washington, the Secretary of State, the State Department, the Defense Secretary and the Joint Chiefs all opposed partition, and in March 1948 the United States briefly proposed a trusteeship instead. [cite:cf_jvl_trustee] Correcting the map so that opponents no longer count as part of the movement's network changed no result. Counting the opposition inside the American government twice lowers the chance to about 88 percent; three times, to about 84, with the founding delayed. It could reach the American decisions, and through them the timing, but not the movement's own forces or the votes of the other powers.

The money and the votes were international, and so was the lobbying. In its first nine years the Keren Hayesod, the movement's main fund, raised about $20 million worldwide. A little more than half came from the United States; the rest came from South Africa, Germany, Romania, Britain, Poland, Canada and elsewhere, in that order. [cite:lb_kh30] At the United Nations the decisive margin was not only American. Two members of the UN committee, Guatemala's Jorge García Granados and Uruguay's Enrique Rodríguez Fabregat, organized the Latin American delegations into a bloc for partition, at Abba Eban's request; thirteen of the thirty-three votes for came from Latin America. [cite:lb_fabregat] [cite:lb_jias] If eleven of those states had abstained instead, an upper bound, the vote fails and the chance falls to about 78 percent, with the founding delayed. In Washington the documented money is still the money already on the map: Abraham Feinberg's pledge for Truman's 1948 train tour, which a second historian also records, adding that Truman believed it had made his election possible. [cite:lb_jps] Federal campaign reports of the 1940s are not itemized in any public database, so the search for other donors to the 1944 and 1948 campaigns found nothing beyond what is already listed; the access that reached Truman in March 1948 came through a telephone call from two fundraisers, Dewey Stone and Frank Goldman, to Eddie Jacobson. [cite:lb_mjl]

Britain trained the army that would later fight it, and the map undercounts that. During the Arab revolt the Mandate government raised a Jewish police force from June 1936, which grew to 22,000 men; "those trained became the nucleus of the Haganah." [cite:mt_jsp] In 1938 a British officer, Orde Wingate, formed Special Night Squads made up mainly of Haganah fighters, and many of the men he trained went on to lead the Palmach and the Israeli army. [cite:mt_jvl_wingate] The Jewish Brigade followed in 1944. Poland's army trained Irgun members in 1938 and 1939, and one Polish account puts the rifles it sent at about 20,000, part of them never delivered because the war intervened. [cite:mt_wi] From the early 1940s the Haganah compiled files on Arab villages, with maps and aerial photographs, "in preparation for a future conflict." [cite:mt_vf] In December 1947 two ships bought in the United States carried about 15,000 immigrants out of Bulgaria; Washington had threatened to withdraw its support for partition if they sailed, and Britain interned them in Cyprus. [cite:mt_pans] Removing Britain's training of Jewish forces leaves the chance at about 93 percent, but that says more about the map than about the history: its arms channel counts weapons and supply ties, and the trained manpower these units produced is not measured at all.

Britain had its own reasons to hold Palestine, and some of them ran through Haifa. From October 1934 oil from Kirkuk in Iraq reached the Mediterranean at Haifa through the Iraq Petroleum Company's pipeline, which the British government considered strategically important; a refinery followed in 1939, and in the Second World War the line fuelled British and American forces in the Mediterranean. [cite:ec_pipe] The Colonial Office granted the Dead Sea potash concession in principle to a Jewish and a British engineer in 1927, over peers who wanted a British company. [cite:ec_ds] The war itself turned Jewish unemployment of 10 percent into a boom as industry supplied the British army, and about 30,000 Jews of Palestine enlisted. [cite:ec_ww2] None of this is weighted on the map, because none of it is a documented act on a founding decision, and the capital it brought is already counted as money. It does answer a different question, why Britain stayed for thirty years, and it weakens one popular story: that the Balfour Declaration was Britain's reward for Weizmann's wartime acetone. That account comes from Lloyd George's memoirs of 1933, and historians list it as one explanation among several, none of them settled. [cite:ec_acet] [cite:ec_acet2]

The experts kept warning, and the warnings were overruled. The inquiries the powers sent mostly found against the project, and their findings were set aside. In 1916 the Sykes–Picot agreement had already planned an international Palestine while Britain was promising Sharif Hussein Arab independence; the Bolsheviks published the secret agreement in 1917. [cite:ib_sp] President Wilson's King–Crane Commission found in 1919 that 72 percent of about 1,875 petitions opposed the Zionist programme and advised "a serious modification" of it; nothing came of it. [cite:ib_kc] The Hope Simpson inquiry of 1930 found almost 30 percent of Palestinians landless and blamed Jewish land purchases; its recommendations went into the Passfield White Paper, which the MacDonald letter reversed within months. [cite:ib_hs] The Peel Commission of 1937 first proposed partition, with a population exchange of up to 225,000 Arabs; the Zionist Congress accepted partition as a basis for negotiation, the Arab Higher Committee rejected it, and the Woodhead Commission buried it in 1938. [cite:ib_peel] The same year Britain and the United States kept Palestine off the agenda at Évian, where thirty-two countries met on refugees and almost none offered them a home. [cite:ib_ev] Ten years later the partition idea came back through the UN committee and was carried to the vote by a committee chaired by Australia's H. V. Evatt. [cite:ib_evatt] These reports and conferences carry no weight on the map: the decisions they fed already carry their own. What they show is a pattern: the inquiries recorded the opposition, and the decisions went the other way.

The largest single turn was one the movement did not make. When the Ottoman Empire entered the First World War in November 1914, Britain made its lands a war aim: on 9 November Asquith said the Ottoman government had "rung the death knell of Ottoman dominion," and Palestine came before the Cabinet the same day. Within weeks Lloyd George told Herbert Samuel he was "very keen to see a Jewish state established in Palestine," and Samuel's memorandum followed in January 1915. [cite:cg_fop] Suppose instead, as an assumption, that the Ottomans had stayed neutral: no British war aim, so no Sykes–Picot, no Balfour Declaration, no Mandate and none of its instruments. The chance falls to about 44 percent, with about twenty-six years lost, lower than for removing the Balfour Declaration alone. Other turns came from outside too. The Kishinev pogrom of 1903 sent tens of thousands of Russian Jews west or to Palestine and led Herzl to the Uganda scheme. [cite:cg_kish] The Ottoman authorities drove some 7,000 Jews to Egypt early in the war and expelled about 8,000 from Jaffa in 1917. [cite:cg_jamal] The Farhud in Baghdad in 1941 turned young Iraqi Jews toward Zionism. [cite:cg_farhud] And in February 1947 an exhausted Britain, declaring the Mandate "unworkable in practice," handed the question to the United Nations. [cite:cg_hansard] The movement could prepare for openings like these; it could not create them.

The state the founders declared already existed in most respects. By 1948 the Yishuv had its own elected assembly and National Council, set up in 1920 to run its schools, religious services and welfare [cite:yi_jcm]; a language revived from scripture to speech, beginning with one Jerusalem household in 1881 and made an official language of Palestine by Article 22 of the Mandate [cite:yi_by] [cite:yi_mand]; universities, a medical service, and eighty-two kibbutzim by 1940 that took on "central functions in settlement, immigration, defense and agricultural development" [cite:yi_kib]; and a pioneer movement, HeHalutz, that by 1939 had trained and sent some 60,000 young people from twenty-five countries. [cite:yi_hh] Remove all of these together and the chance falls only to about 88 percent, with about three years of delay, because the Jewish Agency, the Histadrut and the Haganah stay. That is a limit worth stating plainly: the map measures what decided the founding, not the society the new state took over, and these institutions built most of that society.

Central banks were almost absent; the one that paid in was Nazi Germany's. Removing every central bank with a documented tie leaves the chance at about 93 percent. The only central-bank money on the record is the Reichsbank's share, about a third, of the Transfer Agreement of 1933, and removing the whole agreement lowers the chance to about 91 percent. [scene:rd7_central|Following the money to the central banks] sets out what the search found.

Two sources of money mattered more than any single leader. Edmond de Rothschild's colonies and the American appeals were the two largest sums, and they came at opposite ends of the period: his from 1882, when almost no one else was funding settlement, theirs in 1946 to 1948. Removing either makes the founding unlikely; removing both makes it impossible on this record.

Britain built the framework, and the framework was the Mandate. Without the Balfour Declaration, or without the British state that issued it and held the Mandate, immigration and settlement from 1920 to 1948 go at the Ottoman pace, and about 26 years of that work are lost. The stricter reading, under which the Declaration could not have been issued without its named addressee, Walter Rothschild, produces the same result because it removes the same document; the record shows that it was addressed to him and that he worked on its draft, not that it would have been withheld otherwise.

Britain also supplied the people. Since the people channel was added, removing the British government costs more than before, about 35 percent on time instead of about 45, because the Mandate's certificates let in most of the immigrants who made up the population of 1948, and its land law was the route for most of the land the Jewish National Fund bought. Removing those certificates alone, with everything else Britain did kept, lowers the chance to about 78 percent; removing the largest wave, the Fifth Aliyah of 1933 to 1939, to about 87. The earliest waves and the clandestine voyages, small in number, move it by less than a point. The limits at the end of this appendix explain why these figures are probably too gentle.

Two policies did more than any single person: the doors the Mandate opened. Article 6 of the Mandate obliged Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and close settlement on the land, and two instruments carried it out: the immigration certificates and the Land Transfer Ordinance of 1920, the legal route for about 914,000 of the 936,000 dunams the Jewish National Fund held by May 1948. [cite:pl_pij] Removing the land law alone lowers the chance to about 86 percent; removing it together with the certificates, an assumption marked as such, lowers it to about 73, far more than removing Truman or Herzl. The reversals mattered too. When the Passfield White Paper of 1930 limited immigration and land purchase, the prime minister's letter to Weizmann of February 1931 was treated as withdrawing it; even if its limits had held through the whole of the 1930s, the chance falls no lower than about 90 percent. [cite:pl_macdonald] The 1939 White Paper and the land regulations of 1940 pushed the other way, and four of the seven voting members of the League's mandates commission found the White Paper inconsistent with the Mandate, but the war prevented any ruling, and the Jewish National Fund still acquired 390,000 dunams between 1940 and 1946. [cite:pl_pmc1939] [cite:pl_stein]

The United States counted far more than the Soviet Union, and the difference was its bloc. In 1947 the two powers voted the same way. Remove the American government and its bloc follows it only at its measured independence; the vote becomes uncertain, American recognition is gone, and the founding is delayed. Remove Stalin, or the Soviet bloc whole, and the vote still clears two-thirds in most outcomes. The map now weighs the Czechoslovak arms by what arrived before 15 May 1948, about 14,700 rifles and 1,655 machine guns for a force of about 30,000, close to half the rifles in hand at the founding; on their own they cost about ten points, and with the other arms from abroad, much of it bought in breach of embargoes, they bring the chance down to about two in three. Their full weight fell in the war after the founding, which the next section explains. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel]

The men who decided mattered less than the decisions. Remove Truman and Henry Wallace decides in his place, a man on record as a supporter; the founding still happens on time. That is the same as asking what would have happened had the 1944 convention not been adjourned in the middle of the Wallace demonstration. Even if Wallace's support is set aside as a campaign position, the chance stays at about 85 percent, the same as for an unknown Democrat. Keep Roosevelt alive and bound by his April 1945 pledge to Ibn Saud, and the American vote and recognition become uncertain, and the founding is delayed. The decisions were contested, and the record shows who pushed and who resisted. But in most of the tests someone else would have taken them, and the map can say who.

Who sat in the White House mattered through his own position, not through his party. Roosevelt's heart failure was diagnosed in March 1944, and in July his surgeon Frank Lahey wrote that he would not survive another term; the public was not told, and he ran and won. [cite:rd7_history_fdr_health] [cite:rd7_wiki_lahey] Had the diagnosis been public and the Republican Thomas Dewey won instead, little changes: Dewey had declared for a Jewish commonwealth in October 1944, after meeting Abba Hillel Silver, and in October 1946 asked for several hundred thousand immigrants, more than Truman did; the 1944 Republican platform had condemned Roosevelt for not pressing Britain. [cite:rd7_jta_dewey_1944] [cite:rd7_jta_dewey_1946] [cite:rd7_jvl_gop_1944] A Democrat with no recorded position lowers it to about 85 percent. Only a president bound by Roosevelt's own pledge to Ibn Saud lowers the chance materially, and even then to about 82 percent. American recognition on 14 May, removed on its own, costs only two points on the founding; its weight came afterwards, in arms, loans and membership of the United Nations.

The Holy Land did its work in the choice of the land, more than through organized Christian support. Remove the Christian Zionists the map holds, from Darby and Scofield to Blackstone's memorials and the American Christian Palestine committees, and the chance falls only to about 90 percent. But every other homeland the movement weighed, without either the Christian or the Jewish attachment to the land, scores between 0 and 2 percent on the same formula. The religious motives of Balfour and Lloyd George are recorded but not counted as a separate push, so the first figure is a floor.
