Who Sets the Wage: Monopsony, Lock-in and Wage Theft

The taught version of the labour market is the one in the economics textbook: many employers compete for workers, a worker who is underpaid quits for a better offer, and so wages settle at roughly what each worker's labour is worth. That model is not wrong; it describes much of the economy much of the time. This section examines four documented ways in which real labour markets depart from it. Some employers face little competition for workers. Some workers sign contracts that stop them taking a rival's offer. Some occupations require a licence before anyone may enter them. And some employers simply do not pay what the law requires. Each departure has its own evidence and its own critics, and they are kept separate here.

Monopsony: when the employer sets the wage

A monopoly is a market with one seller; a monopsony is a market with one buyer. In a labour market the buyer is the employer, and economists now use the word for any situation in which an employer can pay less than a worker's output is worth without losing all its staff, because workers cannot easily find or move to another job. The gap between what a worker produces and what the worker is paid is called the markdown. Alan Manning's 2021 review of the field concluded that the evidence that employers hold "considerable monopsony power" had been mounting. [cite:ad5_manning2021] A survey published in August 2026 by Sydnee Caldwell, Arindrajit Dube and Suresh Naidu traces that power to three sources: the time and effort it takes to search for a job, workers' differing attachments to particular employers, and plain concentration, a few employers dominating a local market. They caution that the size of the markdown cannot be read straight off any one measure. [cite:ad5_nber_cdn2026]

How large the effect is remains disputed, and the estimates depend on the data and method. José Azar, Ioana Marinescu and Marshall Steinbaum measured concentration in more than 8,000 local occupational markets using job postings on CareerBuilder.com, found that the average market was highly concentrated by the standard used for company mergers, and estimated that moving from a market at the 25th percentile of concentration to one at the 75th was associated with posted wages 17 per cent lower. [cite:ad5_nber_azar] Efraim Benmelech, Nittai Bergman and Hyunseob Kim, using Census plant data from 1977 to 2009, found that local employer concentration had risen, that it was associated with lower wages, more so where unions were weak, and that wages tracked productivity more closely where markets were less concentrated. [cite:ad5_bbk2018] Kevin Rinz, a Census Bureau economist using tax records, found the opposite trend: local concentration generally fell between 1976 and 2015, to about three-quarters of its 1976 level, even as national concentration rose. His estimate of the wage effect was also smaller in practice: moving from the median to the 75th percentile of concentration would cut earnings by nearly 10 per cent, but a typical change within a market moved earnings by about 1 per cent. [cite:ad5_rinz2022] The range, then, runs from about one per cent for a typical change within a market to seventeen per cent for the widest comparison across markets, and the direction of the trend over time is itself contested.

Noncompetes: a contract that closes the exit

A noncompete clause is a term in an employment contract that bars the worker, after leaving, from working for a competitor or starting a competing business for a set time and area. In a survey of 11,505 workers, Evan Starr, J.J. Prescott and Norman Bishara found that about 18 per cent were bound by one and 38 per cent had signed one at some point. The clauses were most common in skilled, well-paid jobs but appeared often in low-wage jobs and even in states where courts will not enforce them; only 10 per cent of workers negotiated them, and about a third received the clause only after accepting the offer. Wages were lower where noncompetes were easier to enforce. [cite:ad5_spb2021]

On 23 April 2024 the Federal Trade Commission voted to ban most noncompetes. It estimated that about 30 million workers, roughly one in five, were bound by them and that the ban would raise earnings by $400 to $488 billion over ten years, about $524 a year for the average worker; existing clauses for senior executives earning over $151,164 in policy-making roles were left in place. [cite:ad5_ftc_factsheet] The rule never took effect. On 20 August 2024 Judge Ada Brown of the Northern District of Texas, in Ryan LLC v. FTC, held that the Commission had exceeded its authority and that the rule was arbitrary and capricious, and set it aside nationwide. [cite:ad5_faegre_ryan] On 5 September 2025 the Commission, by three votes to one, dropped its appeals in two circuits and accepted the vacatur, turning instead to examining noncompetes case by case. [cite:ad5_ftc_accede] [cite:ad5_tsl_ftc] Status as of October 2026: there is no federal rule; enforceability is set by each state's law.

Licensing: a gate built for safety

An occupational licence is a government permission to work in a trade, granted after required training, examinations or fees. A 2015 report by the Treasury, the Council of Economic Advisers and the Labor Department found that fewer than 5 per cent of workers needed a state licence in the early 1950s and about a quarter did by the 2010s, and that two-thirds of the rise came from more occupations being licensed rather than from workers moving into already licensed ones. Licensed workers earned 10 to 15 per cent more after accounting for education and experience; workers in the most licensed occupations moved between states about 14 per cent less often; and most studies the report reviewed found higher prices for consumers. [cite:ad5_wh_licensing] Morris Kleiner and Alan Krueger, from a national survey, put the share fully licensed at 29 per cent and the wage gain at about 14 per cent. [cite:ad5_nber_kk] In 2025, 21.6 per cent of employed Americans held a licence and 24.0 per cent held a licence or certification. [cite:ad5_bls_cps49] Licensing is designed friction: it exists to protect the public from incompetent practitioners, and its defenders point to that purpose. The documented costs, fewer moves and higher prices, are the price of the protection, and how much protection each licence buys is the question the 2015 report put to state legislatures.

Job lock: insurance as a tether

For the many Americans insured through an employer, leaving a job can mean losing coverage. Economists call the result job lock. Brigitte Madrian's 1994 study estimated that it reduced the voluntary job-leaving rate of insured workers by about a quarter, from 16 to 12 per cent a year. [cite:ad5_nber_madrian] A West Health and Gallup survey fielded in late 2025 found that 24 per cent of workers covered through their employer, about 23 million people, were staying in a job they wanted to leave because they feared losing health insurance, eight points more than in 2021. The share was 44 per cent among those with medical debt they could not repay within a year, against 21 per cent among those without. [cite:ad5_gallup_joblock] The insurance system behind this is the subject of [scene:ad8_insurance_machine|Part Eight].

Wage theft: the departure that is simply illegal

Wage theft is the term used for an employer's failure to pay wages the law requires: pay below the minimum, unpaid overtime, work off the clock, tips taken, illegal deductions. Unlike the mechanisms above, it is not a matter of market structure or legal design. It is a breach of law, and the dispute is over its size. The 2009 study Broken Laws, Unprotected Workers surveyed 4,387 low-wage workers in Chicago, Los Angeles and New York in 2008, using methods designed to reach undocumented and cash-paid workers, and found that 26 per cent had been paid less than the minimum wage in the previous week, 76 per cent of those who worked over forty hours had not been paid overtime, and 68 per cent had suffered at least one pay violation. [cite:ad5_prospect_broken] The Economic Policy Institute, a labour-aligned research group, estimated in 2017 from the government's household survey that minimum-wage violations alone cost 2.4 million workers in the ten most populous states about $8 billion a year, an average of about $3,300 for a year-round worker, and extrapolated to more than $15 billion nationally. EPI itself notes that the figures rest on self-reported pay and hours, cover only minimum-wage violations, and treat the national total as an extrapolation rather than a measurement. [cite:ad5_epi_theft2017]

A study commissioned by the Labor Department from Eastern Research Group, using the same household survey and a second one for California and New York in 2011, found lower rates: 3.5 to 6.8 per cent of covered jobs, and 11 to 12 per cent of low-wage jobs, paid below the minimum, with losses of $20 to $29 million a week across the two states. Its estimates varied with the survey used. [cite:ad5_dol_erg2014] The difference between a quarter of workers in three cities' low-wage industries and about a tenth of low-wage jobs in two states is mostly a difference in who was surveyed and how, and both studies measure something real. Enforcement recovers a small part of any of these totals. EPI counted more than $1.5 billion recovered in 2021 to 2023 by the Labor Department ($659.8 million for 510,534 workers), state agencies and attorneys general, and the largest class-action settlements; it reported that by 2022 each federal investigator was responsible for almost three times as many workers as in 1973. [cite:ad5_epi_theft2024] Workers without legal status, about 8.3 million in the labour force in 2022, are the easiest to underpay, because a complaint can bring the threat of deportation. [cite:us13_epi_unauth]

Where the market works as taught

The textbook picture holds in more places than these mechanisms might suggest. On Rinz's evidence most local labour markets became less concentrated over four decades, and his estimate of what a typical change in concentration does to pay is about one per cent. [cite:ad5_rinz2022] Workers do quit and move: even under job lock as Madrian measured it, insured workers left their jobs at 12 per cent a year. [cite:ad5_nber_madrian] Most workers are not bound by noncompetes, and most low-wage jobs in the Labor Department's own study were paid at least the minimum. [cite:ad5_spb2021] [cite:ad5_dol_erg2014] Licences exist to protect the public, the purpose their defenders cite, and the 2015 report did not dispute that purpose. [cite:ad5_wh_licensing] The record does not show a labour market without competition. It shows specific, measured places where the worker's ability to walk away, which the taught version relies on, is weaker than the textbook assumes: a concentrated local market, a contract clause, a licence, a health plan, or a complaint that would cost more than the wage. How those weaknesses grew alongside the decline of unions is the subject of [scene:ad4_labor_power|Part Four], and how the rules governing them are written is examined in [scene:ad15_pricing_power|Part Fifteen].
