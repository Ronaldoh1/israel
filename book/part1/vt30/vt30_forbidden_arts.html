The Forbidden Arts: Alchemy, the Stars, the Cards and the Index

Sophia's Forbidden Arts catalogue gathers alchemy, astrology, scrying, tarot, theurgy and their neighbours under one heading and gives one reason for their fate: each "offers information or experience obtained without institutional mediation", and the condemnations, it says, "rarely argue the practices don't work; they argue they are illegitimate." That is a claim about documents, and it can be checked against them. This section takes the main arts in turn, sets out what practitioners actually did, and names each document in which popes, councils and the Holy Office dealt with them, ending with the Index of Forbidden Books, which from 1559 to 1966 was the main instrument for controlling what Catholics could read about all of them. Where the catalogue's reason fits the record, the section says so; where the record shows a different reason, it says that too.

Alchemy: a fraud law, not a heresy

Alchemy was the art of transforming matter, above all of turning base metals into gold or silver, carried on in workshops with furnaces, stills and a symbolic language of marriages, deaths and rebirths of substances. Read by its practitioners, it was both laboratory work and, for some, a discipline of the operator's own transformation; Sophia's entry gives both readings and labels the second as the inner one. The best-known papal act against it is a decretal of John XXII issued around 1317, named from its opening words Spondent quas non exhibent, "they promise what they do not deliver". It targeted alchemists who sold false gold and silver, and those who struck coin from it, at a time when counterfeit money was a real problem at the papal court in Avignon. The penalty was a fine equal to the weight of real gold or silver claimed, other punishment for those who could not pay, and permanent loss of benefices for clerics. It did not declare alchemy heretical; its grounds were fraud and harm to the poor. [cite:vt30_wiki_spondent] Sophia's entry says alchemy was "suppressed as fraud and heresy"; the documented papal act concerns fraud only. The art continued in the open among physicians and natural philosophers: Isaac Newton's alchemical manuscripts run to about a million words and are being edited by Indiana University's Chymistry of Isaac Newton project. [cite:vt30_shi_newton]

Astrology and divination: Trent, Sixtus V and Urban VIII

Astrology is the reading of human affairs from the positions of the planets. Catholic theology in the line of Thomas Aquinas did not reject it outright: Aquinas had allowed conjectural predictions, and the line the Church drew ran against claims to know free human acts and fortunes with certainty. [cite:vt30_ox_coeli] The Council of Trent's rules for the Index, published in 1564, fixed that line in law. Rule IX "absolutely repudiated" all books of geomancy, hydromancy, aeromancy, pyromancy, oneiromancy, chiromancy (palm reading), necromancy, sortilege, augury, sorcery and the magic arts, and told bishops to keep out of circulation books of astrology that claimed to determine destiny or affirm future human acts as certain, while expressly allowing astronomical observations written for navigation, agriculture and medicine. [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9]

Sixtus V's bull Coeli et terrae of 1586 went further than Thomas Aquinas had, banning even "uncertain predictions about future human actions"; Robert Bellarmine later wrote on how it should be applied. [cite:vt30_ox_coeli] The most severe act was political. In 1629 the Roman astrologer Orazio Morandi circulated a prediction that Urban VIII would die after a solar eclipse in June 1630; Morandi was jailed and died in prison in 1630. In 1631 Urban decreed death for anyone who predicted "the life or death of the sitting Roman Pontiff" or of his relatives to the third degree. [cite:vt30_torch_popes] The ground in that case was the destabilising force of prophecy in a papal election, not the illegitimacy of unmediated knowledge.

The modern position is set out in the Catechism of the Catholic Church. Paragraph 2116 says "All forms of divination are to be rejected", naming horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, the interpretation of omens and lots, clairvoyance and recourse to mediums, because they "conceal a desire for power over time, history, and, in the last analysis, other human beings" and "contradict the honor, respect, and loving fear that we owe to God alone." Paragraph 2117 calls magic and sorcery "gravely contrary to the virtue of religion", even when used to restore health, calls the wearing of charms reprehensible and warns against spiritism. [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] Here Sophia's reading fits the text closely: the Catechism does not argue that divination fails, it argues that seeking it is a wrong owed to God.

Tarot: a card game that became an oracle

The tarot's history is mostly documented, and the documented version is less ancient than the popular one. Decks with an added suit of picture trumps, trionfi, appear in Milan and Ferrara around 1440 to 1450 as cards for a trick-taking game played by the nobility. A Dominican sermon of about 1450 to 1470 condemned dice, playing cards and "triumphs" together, as gambling. [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy] No divinatory use is documented for more than three centuries. In 1781 the French scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin, in volume eight of Le Monde primitif, claimed that the trumps preserved the wisdom of ancient Egypt, an etymology Egyptologists found no support for; an essay in the same volume by the Comte de Mellet first linked the cards to fortune-telling. Jean-Baptiste Alliette, who wrote as Etteilla, published a divination method in 1783, a treatise in 1785 and founded a society of tarot cartomancers in 1788. Éliphas Lévi tied the trumps to the Hebrew alphabet and the Kabbalah in 1861, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in 1888, built them into its system, and the Rider–Waite–Smith deck of 1909, designed by Arthur Edward Waite and painted by Pamela Colman Smith, gave the modern cards their images. [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy] No papal document specifically condemning tarot was found for this section. The Church's objection today falls under the Catechism's general rejection of divination, which does not name the cards. [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] [scene:ts13_mysteries|The Theology Shield: the mysteries and their modern heirs]

The Index of Forbidden Books, 1559–1966

The instrument that reached all these arts at once was the Index Librorum Prohibitorum. Paul IV issued the first Roman Index in 1557 and 1559; the Council of Trent's Index of 1564, with its ten rules, became the basis of every later edition; a Congregation of the Index was set up in 1571 and, when it was abolished in 1917, its work passed to the Holy Office. [cite:vt14_nwe_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index] The 1564 rules extended the ban beyond named titles to whole classes of "heretical and superstitious writings", with excommunication for possessing them, and Rule IX's ban on books of divination and magic was one such class. [cite:vt14_brit_index] [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] The 1559 Index already banned astrological "divinations" of future events. [cite:vt30_torch_popes] Giordano Bruno's works were listed, along with Galileo, Descartes, Voltaire, Hume and Sartre, among many others; the last regular edition, of 1948, held about 4,000 titles. [cite:vt14_nwe_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index] [scene:vt29_bruno_galileo|Part Twenty-Nine: Bruno and Galileo]

The Index ended without a repudiation. A notification of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith dated 14 June 1966, signed by Cardinal Alfredo Ottaviani, stopped its publication and removed its force as church law, while stating that it kept its "moral value" as a guide to the faithful. [cite:vt14_nwe_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index] What it meant for the forbidden arts is plain from the rules: for four centuries a Catholic who owned a book of geomancy or judicial astrology without permission was, in canon law, liable to excommunication. [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] [cite:vt14_brit_index] [scene:vt14_canon|Part Fourteen: the canon and the Index]

Interpretation, not finding: unmediated knowledge and the institution

Read through the lens of Sophia's "Why These Were Forbidden" entry, which this book attributes to the Sophia platform, the documents above share a structure: each art promises knowledge of the future, of hidden powers or of the self that is reached without a priest, a sacrament or a licence, and the Church answers not that the knowledge is false but that seeking it is owed to God and his ministers alone. In this reading the Catechism's phrase about honour owed "to God alone" is the clearest statement of the principle, and the Index was its enforcement arm. The reading is strongest for divination and judicial astrology, where the texts say almost exactly what it predicts. [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9]

The counter-reading is also on the record. The one papal act against alchemy is a law against counterfeiting; Urban VIII's decree protected a reigning pope from destabilising prophecy; the sermon against tarot was a sermon against gambling; and Thomist theology had itself allowed conjectural astrological prediction until Sixtus V narrowed it. [cite:vt30_wiki_spondent] [cite:vt30_torch_popes] [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy] [cite:vt30_ox_coeli] A reader who holds the mediation thesis can fairly cite the divination texts in its support; a reader who doubts it can fairly cite the alchemy and astrology record against its claim to explain every case.
