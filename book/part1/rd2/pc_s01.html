Paris, 1919: A Conditional Signature and a Shelved Report

In 1919 the powers that had won the war met in Paris to settle the peace. Two records from that year bear directly on Palestine. One was edited before it reached the peace conference. The other reached the conference and was set aside.

The first is the agreement signed in London on 3 January 1919 by the Arab leader Emir Faisal and the Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann. In it, Faisal accepted Jewish immigration into Palestine and the Balfour Declaration. That much is often cited. Less often cited is what Faisal wrote beside his signature. In Arabic, he added a condition, recorded in the text of the agreement held by the United Nations. He would concur only if the Arabs obtained their independence as he had demanded in his memorandum to the British Foreign Office. If there were any departure from that, he would not be bound. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text]

The familiar version stops at the signature. The documents do not. The Zionist Organization submitted the agreement to the peace conference without the caveat. [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] [cite:israeled_faisal_weizmann] The condition soon failed in any case. When the mandates were divided along the lines of Sykes–Picot, the secret Anglo-French partition plan, the Arab independence Faisal had made his precondition never materialized. An Arab congress in Damascus then repudiated his authority to have signed the agreement at all. For decades the agreement would be cited as Arab acceptance of the national home. That consent had been conditional from the start, and the condition had been removed from the copy the conference saw.

The second record came from Washington. President Wilson sent an American commission, known as the King–Crane Commission, to find out what the people of the former Ottoman territories actually wanted. The commission toured from June to July 1919. It received about 1,875 petitions, 72 percent of them hostile to the Zionist plan. Its report found that the non-Jewish population, nearly nine-tenths of the whole, was "emphatically against the entire Zionist program," and it recommended the program's "serious modification." [cite:britannica_king_crane] [cite:jewishmag_king_crane]

The report reached the conference in August 1919 and was shelved. Wilson may never have seen it. It was first published in December 1922, in Editor & Publisher, by which time Britain and France had already established their mandates. [cite:wiki_king_crane] [cite:palquest_king_crane] Why it stayed unpublished for more than three years, the record does not settle. Some members of the commission believed the State Department and Zionists were responsible for suppressing it. The sources record that as their belief, not as an established finding about who decided to bury the report. [cite:palquest_king_crane]

The sequence, however, is not in dispute. In the summer of 1919 a commission sent by an American president measured the opinion of the population. Its findings were in the conference's hands from August. The decisions about who would rule Palestine, and on what terms, were then made without the report being made public. It surfaced only after those decisions were in place. [cite:wiki_king_crane]

The two documents point in the same direction. The Arab consent so often invoked was qualified in Faisal's own hand, and the qualification was dropped. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text] [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] The wishes of the inhabitants were surveyed and written down, and the survey was put aside until it could no longer affect the outcome. Did the governments at Paris know what the people of Palestine wanted? That question need not be answered by inference. There is a documented answer, dated 1919, and it was available to them.
