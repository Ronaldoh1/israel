The Map: Israel, the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem

Much of the confusion surrounding this history begins with geography. Four names recur throughout this book, and they are often used as though they were interchangeable: Israel, the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem. They are not the same place under different names. Each is a distinct territory with its own history since 1948 and its own legal regime today, and most of the arguments that follow are, at bottom, arguments about where these lines run and who governs on either side of them.

Israel, in the narrow sense used here, is the state within the 1949 armistice lines, known as the "Green Line." The West Bank is the territory east of that line. Jordan held it from 1948 to 1967, and Israel has occupied it since 1967. In 1995 the Oslo II accord divided the West Bank into three zones, explicitly as a temporary arrangement pending a final agreement.[cite:wiki_oslo_areas] Area A, about 18 percent of the territory, was placed under Palestinian Authority civil and security control; it contains the major cities, among them Ramallah, Bethlehem, Nablus, Jenin, Tulkarem, Qalqilya, Jericho and most of Hebron. Area B, about 22 percent, came under Palestinian civil control with joint security control. Area C, about 61 percent, remained under full Israeli control.[cite:anera_areas_abc] Areas A and B together hold some 2.8 million Palestinians.

The divisions were meant to be transitional. The plan was for these areas to be transferred gradually to Palestinian control. That transfer never took place, and since 2002 the Israeli army has entered Area A routinely. An arrangement designed as a stage on the way to something else became, in practice, the lasting shape of the territory.

Area C matters most for understanding the land question. Every Israeli settlement outside East Jerusalem lies within it. About 60 percent of Area C consists of firing zones, military land, state land or nature reserves, and Palestinian building there is tightly restricted, with permits rarely granted.[cite:wiki_area_c] [cite:anera_areas_abc] When later chapters discuss settlement growth or the prospects for a Palestinian state, the land at stake is, for the most part, in Area C.

Gaza is a strip on the Mediterranean coast. Egypt held it from 1948 to 1967, and Israel occupied it from 1967. In 2005 Israel withdrew its settlers from Gaza but kept control of its borders, airspace and coast.[cite:jerusalemstory_area_a] The withdrawal of settlers and the withdrawal of control are therefore two different things, and the distinction runs through every later debate about Gaza's status.

East Jerusalem was occupied in 1967 and then annexed by Israel, a step that has not been recognized internationally. Roughly 360,000 Palestinians live there.[cite:minorityrights_palestine] Across East Jerusalem and the West Bank together, about 737,000 Israeli settlers now live.

The familiar shorthand treats "Israel," "the West Bank" and "Gaza" as loose synonyms for a single place. The documents describe four places under four different rules. A resident of Ramallah, a resident of a settlement in Area C, a resident of Gaza and a Palestinian resident of East Jerusalem live under different authorities and different laws. When this book refers to one of these territories, it means that specific territory under that specific rule. Keeping the map in mind is the first condition for reading everything that follows.
