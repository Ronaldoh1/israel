Was There Anywhere Else?

A question follows every account of the road to 1948: with the whole world to choose from, why Palestine, a small land already inhabited? The record answers the first half of the question before the second. By the time the movement was looking, there was almost nowhere left to choose from. In 1870 Europeans controlled about a tenth of Africa, all of it near the coasts; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained outside European rule. [cite:a_wp_scramble] The Americas, Asia and Oceania were divided among states and empires. The one large land claimed by no one was Antarctica. Every alternative, like Palestine itself, therefore needed a ruler willing to give land, and in Der Judenstaat Herzl had already named what that ruler would have to give: not permission to settle, which could be withdrawn, but "a sovereign right" to keep immigration going. [cite:a_wp_judenstaat]

Eight other lands were proposed, offered or studied between 1891 and 1948, and each is documented well enough to be measured. This appendix takes them in the order they were raised. Each is held to three tests the record itself sets: was there a ruler willing to give the land; was the land free of a people who already lived on it and would resist; and were Jews willing to go. Each is then measured on the same six things the state needed, as a share of what the Palestine record had, and its chance of a state by 1948 is computed on the same formula as every test on the network map, where the full record already reads at about 93 percent. The shares are judgments read from the record, not counts from the map, and each is shown with its reason so that a reader can disagree with any of them.
