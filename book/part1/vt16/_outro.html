Part Sixteen has set out the record from 1904 to 1945, and on the private-record test it runs one way. Herzl's own diary records Pius X's "Non possumus" and a refusal tied to Jerusalem and the shrines. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] The one exhibit for private warmth, Sokolow's audience of May 1917, rests on Sokolow's report, while Minerbi's archival account gives the pope's conditions as the protection of the holy places, to be settled "between the Church and the Great Powers." [cite:vt16_ej_minerbi] [cite:vt16_wiki_sokolow] From December 1917 the written record is opposition: Benedict XV's allocution of 10 March 1919 against a "position of privilege and preponderance" in Palestine, his allocution of 1921, and Gasparri's notes to the League in 1922, which failed when the Mandate was approved on 24 July 1922. [cite:vt16_va_1919] [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] [cite:vt16_un_mandate] The private papers of 1943, published by the Holy See itself, are harder than its public line: Maglione, Tardini and Cicognani's letter of 22 June 1943 all opposed a Jewish home in Palestine, while the same letter recorded the Holy See's help for interned Jewish children. [cite:vt16_jta1976] [cite:vt16_cicognani1943] The 1944 note said to favour a homeland remains unverified. [cite:vt16_ptwf_zenit] The record of the war ends with Moshe Shertok leaving his audience of April 1945 without the support he sought; [scene:vt17_jerusalem_1947_49|Part Seventeen] takes up what the Holy See did when the United Nations divided Palestine in 1947 and Israel declared its independence.
