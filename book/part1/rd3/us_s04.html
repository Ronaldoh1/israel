Christian Committees, Built by One Office

In the 1940s a striking number of prominent non-Jewish Americans, among them senators, governors, cabinet members and clergy, publicly backed a Jewish commonwealth in Palestine. It is tempting to read that support as spontaneous. The archives tell a different story. The two committees that gave the support its voice were both created by the same Zionist executive, and a historian of the period calls their operations "hardly autonomous."

The executive was Emanuel Neumann, a member of the Zionist Executive, the movement's governing body. In 1932 he founded the American Palestine Committee to organize prominent, mainly non-Jewish Americans in support of a Jewish homeland [cite:aja_neumann_bio]. In January 1941 he joined the Emergency Committee for Zionist Affairs as head of public relations and political action. From that post he revived the American Palestine Committee, with Senator Robert Wagner as its chairman [cite:aja_neumann_bio].

In December 1942 Neumann organized a parallel body for clergy, the Christian Council on Palestine. Its founding minutes describe it as an "autonomous committee working in cooperation with the American Palestine Committee" [cite:aja_ccp_minutes_1942]. Both committees, and Neumann's role in founding them, are documented in the American Jewish Archives.

The committees grew large. By the end of the war the American Palestine Committee claimed some 6,500 members, including senators, congressmen, cabinet members and governors, and the Christian Council about 3,000 clergy [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee]. In 1946 the two merged into the American Christian Palestine Committee, which disbanded after 1948 [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby].

On the question of independence, the sources part ways. The founding minutes call the Christian Council "autonomous." The historian Peter Grose wrote that the committees' operations were "hardly autonomous" [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby]. Reporting based on Grose's work states that Zionist funds, reaching $150,000 by 1946, sustained them [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby]. That funding figure comes from secondary reporting, not primary documents, and should be read with that in mind. The founding of the committees by a Zionist official, by contrast, rests on the archival record.

None of this means the support was false. The senators, governors and clergy who joined were real people lending their real names. What the record shows is how that support was assembled. It was organized from inside the Zionist political apparatus, by an official whose job was public relations and political action, and presented through bodies that described themselves as autonomous.

The pattern is worth naming because it recurs. Committees that appear independent, but are organized and partly funded from within a political movement's own office, are the same structure later documented for the predecessors of AIPAC. The Christian committees of the 1930s and 1940s are an early example. Alongside the enlarged Jewish Agency of 1929, described in [scene:us_s03|bringing in the non-Zionist money], they show the movement widening its base in the United States beyond committed Zionists.
