One Heartbeat Away

The phrase that gives this section its title is old American shorthand for the vice presidency: an office with little power of its own whose holder is one death, resignation or removal from the presidency. For this book the phrase carries a specific weight. The man who stands there has named Curtis Yarvin approvingly in public, once urged a president to fire the civil service and defy the Supreme Court, and had his first campaign financed more heavily by Peter Thiel than by any other individual. This section sets out what the office actually allows, what Vance has done with it from January 2025 to October 2026, what he has said about the courts since taking it, and how succession works in law.

What the office allows

The Constitution gives the Vice President two functions. The first is in Article I: "The Vice President of the United States shall be President of the Senate, but shall have no Vote, unless they be equally divided." [cite:de12_cornell_art1] The second is succession. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment provides that if the President is removed from office, dies or resigns, "the Vice President shall become President." [cite:de12_crs_25th] Its third section lets a President hand power temporarily to the Vice President, who becomes Acting President; its fourth lets the Vice President, together with a majority of the heads of the executive departments, declare the President unable to serve. If the President disputes that declaration, Congress decides, and the Vice President continues to act only if two-thirds of both Houses agree within twenty-one days. [cite:de12_crs_25th] Everything else a Vice President does, from chairing councils to travelling abroad to raising money, is delegated by the President and can be withdrawn by him.

Three plain consequences follow. Vance is first in the line of succession, ahead of the Speaker of the House, the President pro tempore of the Senate and then the Cabinet in a fixed statutory order beginning with the Secretary of State. [cite:de12_cornell_3usc19] He cannot remove a President on his own: the incapacity procedure requires a majority of the Cabinet and, if contested, a two-thirds vote of Congress. [cite:de12_crs_25th] And no donor, adviser or private party has any role in succession at any point. The worst-case reading of this book attaches to the first consequence; the second and third set its limits.

The deciding votes

Because the Republicans hold a majority in the Senate, Vance's tie-breaking vote has mattered only when enough Republicans have joined the Democrats to produce a tie. By the count kept from the Senate's roll calls, he had cast eight such votes as of October 2026, the most recent on 14 January 2026. [cite:de12_wiki_ties] The first, on 24 January 2025, confirmed Pete Hegseth as Secretary of Defense, 51 to 50. On 30 April 2025 he voted to table a resolution that would have ended the national emergency on which the President's global tariffs rested. On 1 July 2025 he cast three votes on the reconciliation bill known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, two on amendments and the last on final passage. On 15 July 2025 he cast two procedural votes that moved the Rescissions Act of 2025, a bill cancelling previously approved spending, toward passage. [cite:de12_wiki_ties]

The eighth came on 14 January 2026, on a resolution under the War Powers Act to bar further military action against Venezuela without congressional approval. Three Republicans, Rand Paul, Lisa Murkowski and Susan Collins, voted with the Democrats; two others who had signalled support changed their votes after receiving assurances from the White House and from Secretary of State Marco Rubio; and at 50 to 50 Vance announced "the point of order is sustained," ending the measure. [cite:de12_dw_venezuela] [cite:de12_wiki_ties] For comparison, Kamala Harris cast thirty-three tie-breaking votes as Vice President, the most in history. [cite:de12_wiki_ties]

The list is a matter of record, and one pattern in it is plain without interpretation: two of the eight votes defeated attempts by the Senate to limit the President's emergency and war powers. That is what a tie-breaking Vice President of the President's party is expected to do. It is also, in this book's terms, a documented instance of the office being used to keep a check on the executive from operating.

What he has said about the courts

In September 2021, as a candidate, Vance said a President who fired the civil service and was stopped by the courts should "stand before the country" like Andrew Jackson and say, "The chief justice has made his ruling. Now let him enforce it." [cite:de5_nextgov_vance] As Vice President he returned to the subject within three weeks of taking office. On 9 February 2025, the day after a federal judge in New York, Paul Engelmayer, temporarily barred the Department of Government Efficiency's staff from the Treasury's payment systems, he posted: "If a judge tried to tell a general how to conduct a military operation, that would be illegal. If a judge tried to command the attorney general in how to use her discretion as a prosecutor, that's also illegal. Judges aren't allowed to control the executive's legitimate power." [cite:de12_ap_judges] The post did not say the administration would disobey the order, and it did not say it would comply; the qualifier "legitimate" left the question of who decides what is legitimate unstated. [cite:de12_ap_judges]

The two statements are consistent with each other. Neither is an act: no document shows Vance, as Vice President, directing any official to disregard a court order, and the office gives him no power to do so. What they establish is his stated view of where executive power ends, in his own words, at two dates four years apart. The legal fights over that boundary in 2025 and 2026 are the subject of [scene:de19_the_courts|Part Nineteen].

The rest of the record in office

Three other episodes from the first months show how he has used the platform. On 7 February 2025, after a staff member of the Department of Government Efficiency, Marko Elez, resigned over racist posts the Wall Street Journal linked to him, Vance argued that he should be rehired: "I don't think stupid social media activity should ruin a kid's life." Trump answered, "I'm with the vice president." [cite:de12_ap_elez] On 11 February, at the AI Action Summit in Paris, he said he was "not here this morning to talk about AI safety," but "AI opportunity," and that "excessive regulation of the AI sector could kill a transformative industry." [cite:de12_wex_paris] On 14 February, at the Munich Security Conference, he told European leaders that "what I worry about is the threat from within" and that "in Britain and across Europe, free speech, I fear, is in retreat." [cite:de12_cnbc_munich] On 18 March the Republican National Committee named him its finance chairman, the first sitting Vice President to hold the post. [cite:de12_rnc_finance]

As of October 2026 he was campaigning for Republican candidates in the November midterm elections, dividing the map with the President, and had said he would decide about running for President in 2028 only after the midterms; Rubio had said publicly that he would support him if he ran. [cite:de12_cnn_2028] In August 2026 the Democratic Senate nominee in Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed, called him "Peter Thiel's boy"; Vance replied that "the guy's crazy" and did not address Thiel. [cite:de12_wear_elsayed] No meeting or transaction between Vance and Thiel since January 2025 has been reported.

The position, then, is this. A man who in 2021 named Yarvin and endorsed replacing the civil service and ignoring the Supreme Court is first in line to the presidency, holds that view of judicial power in substantially the same words in 2025, and has used the one vote the Constitution gives him to defeat two attempts to check the executive. Nothing in the record shows him acting outside the office's limits, directing anyone to defy a court, or taking instructions from Thiel or anyone else; and the path from his office to the presidency runs, in law, only through the President's death, resignation or removal, or through a procedure that needs the Cabinet and, if contested, two-thirds of Congress.
