NSO Group: Licensed by Israel, Blacklisted by Washington, Tested by the FBI

NSO Group, maker of the Pegasus phone-hacking software, is the Israeli surveillance company whose relationship with the American state is the most contradictory. The United States blacklisted it in 2021 for selling tools used against American officials. In the same years the FBI bought its software, tested it and came close to using it. The company's Israeli licence, its American legal defeats and its 2025 change of ownership all bear on the same question: who decides where such a tool goes, and who answers for it. [cite:ow10_doc_entity] [cite:ow10_globe_fbi]

The Israeli licence. NSO cannot sell abroad without the Israeli state. Israel's Ministry of Defense decides to which countries Israeli companies may sell offensive cyber tools; it says it approves exports only to government bodies, for lawful use in preventing and investigating crime and fighting terrorism, under commitments from the buyer. NSO has said repeatedly that it sold only to countries the ministry approved. In November 2021, as scrutiny grew, the ministry cut the list of countries eligible to buy such tools from 102 to 37, dropping, among others, Morocco, Mexico, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates; it said the state was "constantly reassessing its policy." [cite:ow10_ctech_37] The licensing gives Israel's government a decisive role in where Pegasus went. It does not, on the public record, give it a role in how each customer used it, and NSO has argued throughout that it is not responsible for how its customers use the tool. [cite:ow10_axios_jury]

Pegasus and American officials. In December 2021 Reuters reported that the iPhones of at least nine State Department employees, based in Uganda or working on matters concerning it, had been hacked with NSO's tools; Reuters could not say who launched the attacks. NSO said it had no indication its tools were used but had cut off the relevant customers' access. The Israeli embassy in Washington said that if the claims were accurate, "it is a severe violation" of the terms on which the spyware is licensed for export, which permit use only against terrorism and serious crime. [cite:ow10_reu_state] A month earlier, on 3 November 2021, the Commerce Department had added NSO and another Israeli firm, Candiru, to its Entity List, which bars American companies from selling them technology without a licence, citing evidence that they "developed and supplied spyware to foreign governments" that used it to "maliciously target government officials, journalists, businesspeople, activists, academics, and embassy workers." [cite:ow10_doc_entity] In March 2023 President Biden's Executive Order 14093 barred federal agencies from operational use of commercial spyware that posed counterintelligence risks to the United States or risks of misuse by foreign governments, allowing waivers of up to a year. [cite:ow10_eo14093]

The FBI's own Pegasus. The New York Times reported in January 2022 that the FBI had secretly bought Pegasus, which it dated to 2019, and that NSO had shown the Bureau Phantom, a version able to hack American phone numbers; after about two years of deliberation the FBI decided in the summer of 2021 against using it. [cite:ow10_ai_nytmag] Documents the Times later obtained through a freedom-of-information lawsuit placed the first purchase in 2018, put the Bureau's payments to NSO at about $5 million, and showed that FBI officials pressed from late 2020 to mid-2021 to use the tool in criminal cases before the Bureau decided on 22 July 2021 to "cease all efforts regarding the potential use of the NSO product." The FBI says there was "no operational use of the NSO product to support any FBI investigation," and that the Pegasus licence was held for testing and evaluation. [cite:ow10_globe_fbi] A separate episode came to light in 2023: an American contractor, Riva Networks, had used NSO's location-tracking tool, Landmark, on Mexican phone numbers on the FBI's behalf after the 2021 ban. The FBI says it was misled into thinking Riva was using its own tool; Director Christopher Wray terminated the contract, and the White House said it had not known of the arrangement. [cite:ow10_9to5_riva] The reporting used here does not show that the FBI's Pegasus licence was bought through Riva; the two purchases should not be merged.

In American courts. WhatsApp, owned by Meta, sued NSO in 2019 over the hacking of about 1,400 of its users' devices. A federal judge in California found NSO liable in December 2024, and on 6 May 2025 a jury awarded Meta $444,719 in compensatory damages and $167.25 million in punitive damages. [cite:ow10_axios_jury] [cite:ow10_sw_contempt] NSO argued that it was not responsible for how its government customers used Pegasus, a tool it said was "deployed responsibly by authorized government agencies." [cite:ow10_axios_jury] In October 2025 Judge Phyllis Hamilton cut the punitive award to $4 million and issued a permanent injunction barring NSO from targeting WhatsApp or its users; NSO's spokesman called the reduction a step in the right direction. [cite:ow10_cs_inj] NSO appealed to the Ninth Circuit, and in June 2026 WhatsApp said it had detected a new NSO-linked phishing campaign against its users and was seeking a contempt order; neither the appeal nor the contempt motion had been decided in the reporting found for this part. [cite:ow10_sw_contempt] Apple, which had sued NSO in 2021, asked to drop its case in September 2024, arguing that it might never obtain NSO's most important files and that disclosure could expose its own threat intelligence. Its filing cited a Guardian report that Israeli officials had taken files from NSO's offices and asked an Israeli court to keep the step secret; Israeli officials denied interfering in the American litigation. [cite:ow10_stripes_apple]

New owners, 2025. In late 2025 NSO said its three founders no longer held any stake; it was reported to be controlled by investors led by the Hollywood producer Robert Simonds, and in November it named David Friedman, President Trump's first-term ambassador to Israel, as executive chairman. Friedman said he expected the administration to be "receptive to considering any opportunity that might keep Americans safer," and called the WhatsApp ruling a "meaningful setback." [cite:ow10_rec_friedman] [cite:ow10_ts_owner] The record checked for this part does not show NSO's removal from the Entity List as of October 2026.

The asymmetry here is specific. By NSO's own account, Israel's Defense Ministry approved every country it sold to, and when the tool was used against American diplomats, the Israeli government's answer was that the customer had broken the licence. [cite:ow10_reu_state] [cite:ow10_ctech_37] In the public record the hacking surfaced through Apple's alerts to the victims and Reuters' reporting; the record does not show Israel informing Washington. The United States sanctioned the company, not the licensing state. What the United States received in return is also on the record: an evaluation copy of the most capable phone-hacking tool on the market, which the FBI chose not to use. [cite:ow10_globe_fbi]
