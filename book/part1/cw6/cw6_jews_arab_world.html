The Jews of Egypt and Morocco, 1956–1964

Volume II told the departure of Jews from the Arab states up to the Suez war in [scene:rd6_exodus|the exodus of 1948–56]; this section picks up the story in the weeks after Suez and follows it to 1964, in the two countries for which the record has been researched, Egypt and Morocco. The two cases are very different. In Egypt a community already much reduced was largely emptied in about two years, under measures taken by the Egyptian government after the Suez attack of 1956. In Morocco a far larger community left over three years under a negotiated arrangement between the Moroccan king and Israel's prime minister, with money paid per person. Both belong to the history of this stage, because both changed where the Jews of these countries lived by the eve of June 1967.

Egypt before Suez. Israel's Foreign Ministry estimated that there were 75,000 to 80,000 Jews in Egypt in 1948. About 40,000 of them remained after Israel's independence. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] These figures come from a summary by the Stroum Center for Jewish Studies at the University of Washington, which draws on the historian Joel Beinin's study The Dispersion of Egyptian Jewry. On these figures, the community that faced the Suez war was about half the size it had been in 1948.

Arrests and expulsions, 1956. Beinin's account, as the Stroum Center summarizes it, records what followed the attack on Egypt. About 1,000 Jews were arrested in Cairo in 1956, roughly half of them Egyptian citizens. About 13,000 British and French citizens, many of them Jews, were expelled after the Suez attack. Another 500 Jews who held neither British nor French nationality were also expelled. And about 450 Jewish-owned businesses were confiscated. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] The distinction between the categories is the mechanism. The expulsion of British and French nationals was an act against the citizens of two states at war with Egypt, and it took many Jews with it because many Jews in Egypt held those nationalities. The arrest of Jews who were Egyptian citizens, the expulsion of 500 Jews who held neither nationality, and the confiscation of Jewish-owned businesses were, on Beinin's account, measures that reached people because they were Jews. Read that way they were antisemitic in the plain sense of the word, and they were the acts of the Egyptian government of the day.

The order of 15 November 1956. A factsheet issued by the American Jewish Committee in March 1957, cited by the Stroum Center, describes the procedure. Jews were told on 15 November 1956 to report to the Interior Ministry and to leave within days. Those who left signed a "voluntary" renunciation of their claims, their property and their citizenship. By 10 February 1957, 8,500 had left by ship for Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] The quotation marks around "voluntary" are in the source; the form recorded consent, and the circumstances in which it was signed are the reason the word is in quotation marks.

The Stroum Center also cites figures from JIMENA: about 25,000 departures in 1956, and about 15,000 Jews left in 1957. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] The second figure is given in the summary in that form, and it can be read either as departures in 1957 or as the number still in Egypt that year; the source does not say which.

Lyn Julius, writing in 2017 in Fathom, an advocacy publication, gives a similar total over a longer period: 23,000 to 25,000 departures between November 1956 and the end of 1958. She records at least 500 Jewish firms sequestered between November 1956 and March 1957, and exit visas stamped "ONE WAY-NO RETURN," and she writes that the community fell to a few thousand during the 1960s. [cite:rs_cw6_julius_fathom] Her figures run close to the JIMENA figures on departures and slightly above Beinin's on businesses (at least 500 sequestered against about 450 confiscated), though the two counts may not measure the same thing, since sequestration and confiscation are different legal acts.

Expelled or emigrated? Whether Egypt's Jews were expelled after Suez is disputed, and the dispute is about more than words. On one side, Beinin documents what he calls a "systematic expulsion": the arrests, the expulsions of British, French and stateless Jews, and the forced renunciations. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] Julius goes further in her framing, presenting the exodus as antisemitic persecution. [cite:rs_cw6_julius_fathom] The strongest form of this case rests on the order of 15 November and the renunciation forms: people told to leave within days, who signed away property and citizenship to do so, did not choose to emigrate in any ordinary sense.

On the other side, the writer Eyal Sagui Bizawe, in a 2017 article in Haaretz quoted by the Stroum Center, argues that "most of Egypt's Jews were not expelled," and objects to the way the Israeli school curriculum frames their departure. Many already held European nationality, on this account, and chose Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_uw_egypt1956] The strongest form of this case rests on the numbers and the destinations: many of those who left already held European nationality and went to Europe, and a single word, "expelled," flattens a set of different situations into one story. The two positions agree on the measures; they differ on whether those measures describe what happened to most of the community or to part of it. The sources fetched here do not give a breakdown, by nationality, of everyone who left, and without it the question cannot be settled by counting.

Morocco: Operation Yachin. The second case was an organized emigration. According to the Center for Israel Education, an educational organization whose timeline is the source for this account, Operation Yachin began on 28 November 1961 under an understanding between King Hassan II and Ben-Gurion. By 1964 more than 97,000 Jews had left Morocco, travelling via France or Italy; some settled in France, Canada or the United States rather than Israel. [cite:rs_cw6_cie_yachin]

The arrangement had a price. The same account says Israel agreed to pay $500,000, plus $100 for each emigrant for the first 50,000 and $250 for each one after that, and that HIAS contributed $50 million to the operation's costs. [cite:rs_cw6_cie_yachin] The terms describe the mechanism: emigration organized under an understanding between two heads of government, paid for by Israel per head, at a rate that rose once the first 50,000 had gone, with HIAS contributing $50 million. The account does not say, in the text fetched here, to whom in Morocco the payments were made, and it is a single educational source; these figures are reported as its figures, not as a documented ledger.

What the record does not settle. This section rests on three sources: a university summary of Beinin's scholarship and of the documents and figures it cites, an essay in an advocacy publication, and an educational timeline. Beyond the questions already noted, they leave several things open. They do not say how many of Egypt's Jews went to Israel and how many to Europe or elsewhere. They do not give a Moroccan or Israeli government record of the Yachin payments, or say who received them. Nor do they say what led the Moroccan government to agree to an arrangement of this kind, or what the conditions of Morocco's Jews were before it. And the story of the Jews of Iraq, Syria, Libya and Yemen between 1956 and 1967 has not been researched for this book; its absence here is a gap in the research, not a sign that nothing happened in those countries.
