How to Test a Claim

A reader meeting this history for the first time has good reason to suspect that any account of it is someone's opinion dressed up as history. The suspicion is fair, and it deserves an answer before the history begins rather than after. The answer is not a promise of neutrality, which any author can make. It is a test, stated in advance, that a reader can apply to any claim in the chapters that follow, including this book's own.

Why should the record differ at all from what readers already learned? Not, for the most part, because they were told lies. Most people meet this history through textbooks, speeches and news headlines, and these are summaries. Summaries leave things out, and what they leave out shapes what remains. This book puts the underlying documents beside the summaries so that the two can be compared, section by section, in the boxes that open each one: what readers are commonly told, and what the record shows.

The comparison matters even more for the claims that circulate outside textbooks, in the comment threads and viral posts where many readers now first meet this subject. Most of those claims are not pure invention. They take a true fact and attach to it a conclusion the fact does not support. Egypt built a wall. Rome renamed a province. Arafat was born in Cairo, which the record shows to be largely accurate, though some sources dispute it.[cite:w_arafat] Each detail can be checked, and each checks out, more or less. The trouble lies in what comes next. The birthplace of one leader, true or not, says nothing about the history of a people. The Roman renaming is real, but the name it imposed was centuries older than Rome's use of it, a point taken up in [scene:ah_s01|the section on the land's ancient names]. The pattern works because a verifiable detail creates trust, and the reader accepts the conclusion along with it. Anyone, on any side, can be fooled this way.

The remedy is to separate the fact from the conclusion and test each on its own. The test reduces to three questions.

The first question is whether the fact is true. The way to answer it is to find the primary source or a credible record, and often the answer is yes. The second is whether the fact proves the conclusion attached to it, and often it does not: a true fact can carry a false claim. The third is what has been left out, because the missing context usually decides the matter. A wall may exist and still have been built for reasons other than the one the post supplies. A province may have been renamed and still have carried a name older than the empire that renamed it. In each case the first answer is yes, the second is no, and the third explains why.

Claims tested this way receive one of four verdicts: true, partly true, false, or opinion. The last matters as much as the others. A statement of value, or of what someone ought to think, is not a fact that a record can confirm or refute, and calling it false would be as much an error as calling it true. Insults receive no verdict at all. Name-calling is not a claim; it is skipped, and the claims around it are checked. And the checking runs in every direction. The myths examined in this book are not one side's myths only. A claim that flatters Israel and a claim that flatters the Palestinians are held to the same test, and a false claim aimed at either people is treated the same way.

That last rule binds this book as well. The three questions apply to every claim, favorable or unfavorable to any side, and to the book's own conclusions as much as to anyone else's. What the test cannot do should be said plainly too. It cannot make a contested question uncontested, or supply a record that was never kept. It can only show where each claim stands and where its evidence lies.
