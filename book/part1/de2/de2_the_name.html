The Name: From "Formalism" to "the Dark Enlightenment"

The current this book follows has had at least four names, and the order in which they appeared explains a good deal of the confusion around it. It began, in 2007, as a blog with no label at all. Its author tried out "formalism" and "neocameralism," accepted "reactionary" and then "neoreactionary," and in 2012 a second writer gave the whole body of work the name by which it is now best known. Tracing the names one by one also settles two attributions that are widely repeated the wrong way round.

2007: a blog and a manifesto

In 2007 Curtis Yarvin, a software engineer, began a blog called Unqualified Reservations under the pen name Mencius Moldbug. Its inaugural post was titled "A formalist manifesto." [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] The essay, dated 23 April 2007 and later issued as a short 14-page booklet, is now held by Princeton University Library. [cite:de2_princeton_fm] [cite:de2_le] Its word was "formalism," and in that first year Yarvin used it as the name for his position: the proposal that the real distribution of power in a society should be written down and made official, the way property is. The next section reads the manifesto's argument. Here what matters is that the first name for this body of thought was a method, not a mood.

Within months a second term appeared. Yarvin argued that a state could be run as a company owned by shareholders, and called the idea "neocameralism," after cameralism, the administrative science of eighteenth-century German states and particularly of Prussia under Frederick the Great. [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] As the sociologists Smith and Burrows put it in a 2021 study of the texts, his position was "originally called 'neocameralism'" before it "soon became known as 'neoreactionary' philosophy." [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin]

2008: "reactionary," then "neoreactionary"

In April 2008 Yarvin began a long serial addressed to progressive readers, "An Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives," whose first chapter opened by asking whether the reader was one, and noting that the reader probably had friends who were. [cite:de2_upcarta] In it he addressed the question of labels directly. If he had to choose one word and stick with it, he wrote, he would pick "restorationist"; if he had to concede one pejorative that fair writers could fairly apply, he would accept "reactionary." [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] The word is chosen with care. A reactionary, in the historical sense, is someone who wants to undo a revolution and restore what came before it; for Yarvin the revolution in question was, broadly, the rise of popular sovereignty since the eighteenth century. The same year, he first used "neoreactionary" to describe his project. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] The prefix marks a difference from the older reactionaries of the nineteenth century: this was a reaction argued in the language of software, corporate governance and economics.

2012: Land names it

Four years later Nick Land, a British philosopher with a long prior career writing about technology and capitalism, began a serial essay that took Yarvin's work as its main subject. The first instalment, published on 2 March 2012, was titled "Neo-reactionaries head for the exit." Its opening paragraph introduced the phrase that became the second name. Land argued that "enlightenment," as a historical event, is self-confirming: once a society believes certain truths are self-evident, there can be no turning back, so "a retrograde, or reactionary, 'dark enlightenment' amounts almost to intrinsic contradiction." [cite:de1_land_gwern] A few paragraphs later he used the phrase for the thing he was describing: a current of thought, "recognizably Hobbesian, a coherent dark enlightenment," which treated democracy as an engine of decline and asked, "From Thomas Hobbes to Hans-Hermann Hoppe and beyond," how a sovereign could be stopped from "devouring society." [cite:de1_land_gwern] [cite:de2_land_ebook]

The name was a deliberate paradox. The Enlightenment, in the standard account, is the eighteenth-century movement of reason, rights and representative government; a "dark" enlightenment keeps the claim to clear-eyed reason and turns it against the political conclusions the original drew. Land titled the whole serial "The Dark Enlightenment," posted it in ten instalments between 2 March and 20 July 2012, and the parts were compiled into a single e-book on 2 July 2014. [cite:de2_land_ebook] By the time Klint Finley wrote about the subject for TechCrunch in November 2013, the two names were interchangeable: "neoreaction," he wrote, was "also called 'dark enlightenment.'" [cite:de1_finley_tc]

Who coined what

Two attributions circulate the wrong way round. A widely repeated line credits Yarvin with coining "the Dark Enlightenment." He did not; Land did, in 2012, as the serial itself shows. [cite:de1_land_gwern] The reverse error is just as common: commentary, including some published as recently as 2025, credits Land with "the Cathedral." That term is Yarvin's, and Land says so in plain words. After describing a ruling network of officials, journalists and academics, Land wrote: "Moldbug calls it the Cathedral." He also credits Yarvin with neocameralism, which he introduces as "an approach he calls 'neo-cameralism,'" and with "Universalism," Yarvin's name for progressive belief understood as a secular religion. [cite:de1_land_gwern] [cite:de2_land_ebook]

The division of labour matters for how the rest of this book reads both men. Land's serial is, for long stretches, a reading of Yarvin, with a great deal of quotation. It calls him the "supreme Sith Lord of the neoreactionaries," a joke borrowed from Star Wars that is also a statement of rank, and quotes him asking whether one can "imagine a 21st-century post-demotist society," one "that saw itself as recovering from democracy, much as Eastern Europe sees itself as recovering from Communism." [cite:de2_land_ebook] But Land also added things Yarvin had not written: a philosophical frame built on his own earlier work on technology and capital, a lineage running back through Hobbes and the libertarian economist Hans-Hermann Hoppe, and extended treatment of race that [scene:de4_the_hard_passages|Part Four] quotes and examines. Yarvin's vocabulary and Land's frame travel together under one name, but they are two authors' work, and the book keeps them apart.

A label chosen by its own authors is useful evidence in one direction only. It shows what Yarvin and Land called their work. It does not show who else holds the ideas, a question Part One answered on the record, and it does not make the label a membership card for anyone who later uses a word from the vocabulary.
