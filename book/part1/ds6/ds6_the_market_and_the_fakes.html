The Market and the Fakes

The scrolls were sold from the beginning. In the 1950s and 1960s many fragments were sold legally, and the Bethlehem dealer Khalil Iskander Shahin, known as Kando, who had bought the first four scrolls, sold many more. Laws passed in the 1970s restricted sales of looted scrolls. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries] Then, around 2002, fragments that looked like lost pieces of the scrolls began to appear on the market, with no documented history of ownership. Many of them reportedly traced back to the Kando family, who were rumoured to have kept pieces in a Swiss vault. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries]

The biggest buyer was the Green family. Steve Green, president of the craft-store chain Hobby Lobby and founder of the Museum of the Bible in Washington, began buying biblical artefacts in 2009, and between 2009 and 2014 he bought sixteen scroll fragments in four batches: four from Craig Lampe, one from Michael Sharpe, four from Andrew Stimer and seven from William Kando. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries]

On 13 March 2020 the museum published the findings of an investigation it had paid for. A team of five conservators and scientists led by the art-fraud investigator Colette Loll concluded, in a report of more than two hundred pages, that all sixteen were modern forgeries. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries] The forgers had painted the text on old leather, not the tanned parchment of the real scrolls; the ink pooled in cracks and on torn edges, showing that it was applied after the damage; the pieces had been soaked in an amber coating, probably animal-skin glue, to imitate a feature of the real scrolls, and dusted with clay minerals consistent with the sediments at Qumran. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries] The museum's chief executive called it a "victim of fraud". William Kando denied that any fragment he sold was inauthentic. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries]

The problem was larger than one museum. At least seventy post-2002 fragments are suspected, and others are held by institutions such as Azusa Pacific University and Southwestern Baptist Theological Seminary. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries]

The money trail completes a pattern. The scrolls were found by shepherds who sold them for what dealers would pay; they became trophies of a state, assets of a market and, finally, the raw material of a forgery business aimed at the most eager buyers. At every step the value of the scrolls came from their authority, the claim to be the oldest words of scripture, and at every step someone found a way to own or sell that authority. The forgers simply sold the authority without the scroll.
