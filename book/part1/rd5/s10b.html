The Military Balance of 1948

Two stories about the military balance of 1948 have outlived the war. In the first, Israel was a tiny nation, vastly outnumbered, that defeated overwhelming Arab armies through will and endurance, a story often told as a near-miracle. In the second, Israel won not through real capability but because international sympathy, born of the Holocaust, tipped the scales. Each is repeated as if it described the whole war. The documents describe something neither story accounts for: a balance that changed, in stages, between the invasion of May 1948 and January 1949.

A report written during the war. The most valuable single document is a US government assessment dated 1 July 1948, written while the outcome was still unknown. It put Israeli forces at roughly 90,000 in total, with about 35,000 of them used in active operations. It also found that the Arab armies were, at that moment, stronger than Israel in artillery and tanks, while carrying a wide variety of small arms. [cite:frus1948_military_situation] Its value lies in its timing. Nobody writing it could shape the numbers around how the war ended, because nobody yet knew. Its limit lies in the same place: it is a snapshot of one moment, not an account of the war's course.

The sources for this section disagree on what kind of report it was. The account on which this section draws calls it, in one place, a contemporaneous CIA assessment and, in another, a US State Department report. [cite:frus1948_military_situation] This book has not resolved which office produced it, and refers to it here simply as the July 1948 US assessment.

Two phases, not one picture. Historians describe the war as two distinct military phases. From the invasion of 15 May until the first truce in June 1948, accounts describe the Israeli forces as initially inferior in both numbers and armament. After the truce, new enlistments and arms deliveries, among them the Czechoslovak shipments traced in [scene:cz_s01|the section on the Czech arms], allowed Israel to seize the initiative. One historical anthology states that for most of the war, excluding that first period, "the Jews were superior in personnel, equipment, logistics and organization." [cite:jvl_israel_studies_1948] The early disadvantage was real; so was the reversal that followed it.

On one point within that first phase, the sources do not agree. Avi Shlaim gives the forces on 15 May 1948 as 35,000 Israeli troops against 20,000–25,000 Arab troops. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_war] His seminar notes put it more precisely: the Israeli army had more men but weaker firepower, and the forces the Arab states actually sent were "well below the level demanded by the Military Committee of the Arab League." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] On Shlaim's figures, then, Israel was not outnumbered even in May; it was outgunned. The anthology's "inferior in both numbers and armament" and Shlaim's "more men but weaker firepower" cannot both be right about numbers, though they agree about weapons. The reader should also keep the dates apart. Shlaim's 35,000 describes 15 May. The US assessment's 90,000 describes 1 July, and its own figure of about 35,000 refers to the troops in active operations at that later date, not to the army's size at the invasion. The coincidence of the two 35,000s is not a confirmation of either.

Shlaim calls the June truce "a turning-point," and he argues that "illicit arms-imports decisively tipped the military balance in favour of Israel." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_war] That judgment places the turn where the anthology places it: in the weeks of the first truce, when arms reached the Israeli army and the Arab states did not match them. The course of that fighting, from the first round to the armistices, is told in [scene:rd5_invasion|the section on the Arab states' war].

Whose numbers. Much of the popular "tiny, outnumbered Israel" arithmetic traces back to estimates by John Glubb, commander of Jordan's Arab Legion during the war. Historians who have worked on the order of battle note that, as the commander of one of the fighting armies, Glubb had a direct and plausible incentive to exaggerate Israeli numbers and play down Arab ones. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] This is ordinary source criticism, and it cuts only so far. It does not show that Glubb falsified anything; it shows that a widely repeated figure comes from an interested party, a fact rarely mentioned when his numbers are cited as neutral.

The ending the story leaves out. By the later stages of the war, accounts state that Israel's army outnumbered the combined Arab armies by roughly two to one. The Arab states reinforced their forces over the course of the war, but they never matched the pace of Israeli growth in men and arms. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] A story about beating impossible odds usually implies that the odds stayed impossible until the end. On this record they did not: the advantage had passed decisively to Israel well before the fighting stopped. That does not make the outcome inevitable or easy in hindsight. The difficulty of the first weeks, when the Arab armies held real advantages in heavy weapons, stands in the record beside the reversal.

Why neither story survives. The underdog story holds, at most, for the opening weeks, and even there it leans on one commander's disputed figures and on a count of men that Shlaim's figures contradict; what the record does support is an Arab advantage in firepower, in artillery and tanks, which the US assessment still found on 1 July. The sympathy story fares no better. To hold, it must pass over a dated military reversal, the arms that arrived during and after the first truce, and a final numerical advantage of about two to one that, in the account this section draws on, owed nothing to international goodwill and a great deal to logistics, organization and weapons that arrived and were used. [cite:jvl_israel_studies_1948] [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance]

What remains open. The record gathered here does not settle the size of the Israeli force on 15 May: one account says it was inferior in numbers, Shlaim says it was larger. It does not say which office of the US government wrote the July 1948 assessment. It gives the final two-to-one ratio as an approximation without a precise date or count of either side. And it offers no independent figures for the Arab armies' strength after May against which Glubb's estimates could be checked. What it does establish is the shape of the war's balance: a real early disadvantage in firepower, a turn at the first truce, and a decisive Israeli superiority by the end.
