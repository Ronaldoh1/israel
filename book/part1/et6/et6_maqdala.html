Maqdala, 1868: Fifteen Elephants and Two Hundred Mules

The largest removal of Ethiopian manuscripts in history was not carried out by a church. It was carried out by the British Army, with a curator from the British Museum attached, and the books were sold at auction to pay the soldiers.

In 1868 Britain sent an expedition against the Ethiopian emperor Tewodros II and his mountain fortress of Maqdala. No fewer than 13,000 troops were deployed to the fortress and its treasury. [cite:et_ox_maqdala] When the fortress fell, Tewodros chose to kill himself with a pistol that had been a gift from Queen Victoria. [cite:et_ox_maqdala] The fortress held a treasury and a library of manuscripts, and the soldiers took them.

The removal was planned before the army sailed. On 3 October 1867 Charles Thomas Newton, Keeper of Greek and Roman Antiquities at the British Museum, wrote to Sir Roderick Murchison, President of the Royal Geographical Society, asking that a museum archaeologist accompany the expedition. [cite:et_cup_maqdala_blog] The man sent was Richard Holmes, a curator with a budget of one thousand pounds for excavations and acquisitions. [cite:et_cup_maqdala] [cite:et_cup_maqdala_blog] The historians Lucia Patrizio Gunning and Debbie Challis call it "planned plunder", though, they argue, it did not go to plan, and they note that the inclusion of a museum man made the expedition unique in the Museum's history. [cite:et_cup_maqdala_blog]

The scale is recorded. The soldiers left the fortress with fifteen elephants and two hundred mules carrying the loot. [cite:et_cup_maqdala] What was not kept by officers was sold at a two-day auction on the Delanta plain to raise funds; the sale made five thousand pounds, and each non-commissioned officer and soldier entitled to prize money received just over four pounds. [cite:et_cup_maqdala] [cite:et_ox_maqdala] Holmes bought for the Museum, among other things, thirteen objects at the auction, and the government later passed on regalia, liturgical objects and tabots that the commander had acquired. [cite:et_bm_maqdala] Over three hundred manuscripts were acquired on the spot by the British Museum, and many are now in the British Library. [cite:et_ox_maqdala] In 1877 the cataloguer William Wright counted 350 manuscripts in the Museum, and estimated that at least five hundred had been taken. [cite:et_cup_maqdala]

Embarrassment came quickly. In 1871 the prime minister, William Gladstone, told the House of Commons that he regretted "that those articles were ever brought from Abyssinia", calling them "hallowed by association" for the Ethiopians. [cite:et_cup_maqdala] [cite:et_cup_maqdala_blog] The objects stayed. The Bodleian Library in Oxford holds Ethiopian manuscripts, eleven of them definitively linked to Maqdala. [cite:et_ox_maqdala] The British Museum holds eleven inscribed tabots, the consecrated tablets that represent the Ark in every Ethiopian church; it keeps them off public display out of respect for their sacred nature, and its stated long-term aim is to lend them to an Ethiopian Orthodox church in Britain. [cite:et_bm_maqdala] Ethiopia's ambassador has renewed calls for museums to return the Maqdala heritage. [cite:et_tan_2021]

Some objects have returned through private purchase rather than institutional decision. In September 2021 the writer Tahir Shah, through his Scheherazade Foundation, bought a group of Maqdala objects at auction, including a Bible, a processional cross and a priestly crown, and handed them to the Ethiopian ambassador in London, who called it the largest single restitution of Maqdala-era artefacts in Ethiopian history and renewed calls for museums to return the rest. [cite:et_tan_2021] The objects were bought back, from the market that had been created when they were sold for four pounds a soldier.

The tabots in London complete a circle that began at Aksum. In Ethiopia, the holiest object in each church is hidden from all but its priests. In the British Museum, eleven of them are kept from the public in a space set aside for them. [cite:et_bm_maqdala] [cite:et_wh_solomonic] The concealment is the same; the keeper has changed. A national museum in the capital of the empire that took them now decides who may see the objects that the Ethiopian church decided no layperson should see, and when, if ever, they go home.
