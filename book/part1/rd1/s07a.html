What Stood by 1917: Five Dates Before the Holocaust

A familiar account holds that the demand for a Jewish state grew out of the Holocaust, that "never again" was its direct cause. That is a claim about sequence, and the calendar can test it. Five documented dates bear on it, and all five fall between 1896 and 1922. Each comes at least a decade before Hitler took power in Germany in 1933, and at least sixteen years before the Holocaust itself. Three of them lie beyond this stage and are told in full in Stage Two; they are named here because the argument needs all five. The second half of this section then sets out what this stage actually built: the structure that stood in Palestine, Europe and America in 1917, and what stood against it.

The first date is 1896. That year Theodor Herzl published Der Judenstaat, The Jewish State, which openly weighed Palestine against Argentina as candidate territories for a political answer to European antisemitism.[cite:herzl_judenstaat] This was a political program, dated and in print, thirty-seven years before Hitler took power. It was not reconstructed afterwards. On the arithmetic alone, the later catastrophe cannot have caused it. That does not mean everything in Zionist history flowed in a straight line from one pamphlet. The chapter that ends here has shown how much was contested: the Basel Program of 1897 spoke of a "home" (Heimstätte), not a state (Staat), and the movement argued for decades over culture, religion, territory and method.[cite:rs_rd1_basel_palquest]

The second date is a pair, 1903 and 1905. The Sixth Zionist Congress voted in 1903 to investigate British East Africa; the Seventh Congress formally rejected it in 1905.[cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress] This matters because it shows an active choice. Choosing a place because nothing else is on offer is different from choosing it after seriously weighing a real alternative and turning it down. Palestine's place was confirmed by a contested decision in two stages, and the decision split the movement: the Territorialists who left founded the Jewish Territorial Organization.[cite:wiki_jewish_territorial_organization] It was far from the movement's last internal disagreement.

The third date is 1917. In November that year Britain committed in writing to "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people."[cite:balfour_letter] The commitment came sixteen years before Hitler's rise and twenty-four years before the systematic phase of the Holocaust began. A letter issued that early cannot have been a response to what came later. Nor did it by itself guarantee a state. Decades of uncertain, gradual and contested development lay between the letter and 1948. Its drafting, its addressee and the opposition to it inside the British cabinet are told in [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration].

The fourth date is 1920. At San Remo the Allied powers formally assigned Britain the Mandate for Palestine and wrote the Balfour language into an international agreement.[cite:san_remo_wiki] The commitment moved from one government's letter to a multilateral agreement, a real change in its legal character and not a repetition of the same promise. It does not follow that every Allied power shared the same enthusiasm or motive; the national interests behind the agreement varied.

The fifth date is 1922. The League of Nations approved the Mandate for Palestine in July 1922, and it came into force in September 1923. Its Article 6 committed Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and "close settlement by Jews on the land."[cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] This was a binding international legal framework, eleven years before Hitler took power, complete before the word "Holocaust" described anything. It was not applied consistently. Within the same decade Britain's Churchill White Paper narrowed the same commitment, as Stage Two shows.

By the time Hitler took power, then, the movement had a political program thirty-seven years old, a territorial question it had settled twice in its own favor, a great-power commitment, the international ratification of that commitment and a binding legal mandate to build toward it. The Holocaust changed what happened next, and this book does not minimize it. It intensified the urgency and reshaped the world's willingness to act. The clearest evidence of that acceleration is the Biltmore Program of May 1942, the movement's first explicit, public demand for a state, in the form of a "Jewish Commonwealth."[cite:biltmore_wiki] [cite:biltmore_medoff] But Biltmore was the culmination of forty-six years of institution-building and political commitment, not their origin. "Never again" describes why the world finally acted. It does not describe when the planning began.

The strongest form of the opposite view deserves its place. The program of 1896 and the commitments of 1917–22 did not make a state inevitable, and for most of this period the movement was one current among many in Jewish life, opposed by Reform rabbis, the Bund, Orthodox opponents and, in 1917, the official bodies of British Jewry. The Basel Program avoided the word "state." On this reading, it took the catastrophe of the 1930s and 1940s to turn a contested project with institutions into a demand the world would grant. Both things are true, and they are claims of different kinds: one is about when the structure existed, the other about why it succeeded when it did. The documents settle the first. The second is a matter of historical judgment.

What, then, existed by the end of 1917? At the top stood the Zionist Organization founded at Basel. Any individual or group that accepted the Basel Program and paid the annual dues called the shekel could join. Its 1899 constitution created a seven-member Smaller Actions Committee, chaired by the Congress president, and a 37-member Larger Actions Committee of national leaders; the Congress met every year until 1901 and every two years after.[cite:palquest_wzo] [cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] Herzl, its first president, died in 1904; David Wolffsohn succeeded him in 1905, and the botanist Otto Warburg succeeded Wolffsohn in 1911.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] National federations recruited members and raised money: the Federation of American Zionists from 1898 and the English Zionist Federation from 1899.[cite:w_zf_gb] When war came the organization's head offices were in Germany and it declared itself neutral, while in London Chaim Weizmann, a Manchester chemist and president of the English Zionist Federation, and Nahum Sokolow became its de facto leaders.[cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] [cite:weizmann_papers_v9]

Beneath the organization sat its money and land. The Jewish Colonial Trust, incorporated in London in 1899 with an authorized capital of £2,000,000 in £1 shares, had sold only about 250,000 shares, raising about £250,000, but it had proved that the project could be financed by many small shareholders.[cite:jct_wiki] Its subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Company, founded in 1902, opened its first branch in Jaffa in 1903 and by 1914 had branches in Jerusalem, Beirut, Hebron, Haifa, Safed, Tiberias and Gaza; its loan to the Ahuzat Bayit association financed the first neighborhood of what became Tel Aviv.[cite:apc_wiki] The Jewish National Fund, founded in 1901, held the land it bought as permanent national property, leased and never sold. It began with 200 dunams east of Hadera in 1903, and its blue collection box appeared around 1904.[cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] [cite:jnf_wiki] [cite:wiki_blue_box_jnf] In Jaffa, the Palestine Office opened by Arthur Ruppin in 1908 coordinated purchase and settlement, and the Palestine Land Development Company had acquired and prepared roughly 50,000 dunams by the outbreak of war, through named agents including Yehoshua Hankin, Ruppin himself and Jacob Thon.[cite:wiki_palestine_office] [cite:encyclopedia_otto_warburg] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]

Around that structure, and only partly under its control, a settler society had grown. The Second Aliyah, from 1904 to the outbreak of war, brought about 35,000 immigrants by the National Library of Israel's count or 40,000 by the Jewish Virtual Library's, and at least half left within a few years by the first count, nearly half by the second; the Jewish community numbered about 80,000 at the end of the period.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_second] It had a Hebrew high school in Jaffa from 1905, the lots of Tel Aviv raffled among 60 families at Passover 1909, and Degania, counted as the first kibbutz, founded on 28 October 1910 on land leased from the Jewish National Fund (another source dates it to 1909).[cite:rs_rd1_nli_second] [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_degania] It had armed guards: Bar-Giora from 1907, and Hashomer, which grew out of it in 1909 and lasted until 1920.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_defense] In the United States, Henrietta Szold had founded Hadassah in 1912, and in 1914 Louis Brandeis took up Zionist leadership and became the movement's channel to President Wilson.[cite:hadassah_wiki] [cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] A year after this stage closes, those groups merged into the Zionist Organization of America with 147,551 members.[cite:zoa_wiki] Separately, a Christian constituency had a text: the Scofield Reference Bible, published by Oxford University Press in 1909.[cite:wiki_scofield_bible] And in the war, NILI's intelligence and Jewish soldiers in British uniform had given the movement standing in London.[cite:wiki_nili] [cite:algemeiner_jewish_legion]

Two of the dashboards that accompany this library summarize the same build-out. The year ledger for this period shows that almost every year from 1896 to 1916 added a piece, a bank, a fund, an office, a guard, a newspaper or a lobby, and it keeps a column for who objected. The funding overview compresses the same story into steps: a bank and a land fund in 1899–1902, then an American organization in 1912–18 that it rounds to 147,000 members, against the 147,551 the source gives for 1918. One small difference in the ledger: it dates the Jewish Colonization Association's takeover of the Rothschild colonies to 1900, while the 1906 Jewish Encyclopedia says "since 1899".[cite:rs_rd1_je_ica]

Against this structure stood real forces, and the record of 1917 is incomplete without them. The Ottoman state had refused Herzl a charter: his diary records Abdul Hamid II's message that he could not sell even a foot of the land, because it belonged not to him but to his people. From the 1880s the government restricted Jewish immigration and land purchase, though unevenly, and the restrictions were undermined by corruption and foreign consular protection.[cite:w_herzl] [cite:w_abdulhamid] Arab opposition had a public voice. Negib Azouri predicted in 1905 that the Arab and Jewish national movements were "destined to fight each other continually until one of them wins."[cite:rs_rd1_azouri] Najib Nassar's al-Karmil in Haifa campaigned against Zionism from 1908, and from 1911 Filastin in Jaffa sent every issue to every village in its district.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_nassar] [cite:wiki_falastin] In 1911 the Jerusalem deputies Ruhi al-Khalidi and Sa'id al-Husayni raised Zionism in the Ottoman parliament.[cite:rs_rd1_ruhi_wiki] The Arab Congress in Paris in June 1913 showed other priorities: it adopted ten resolutions on decentralization and the Arabic language, and none on Zionism.[cite:rs_rd1_arabcongress_wiki]

Jewish dissent was organized as well. The Bund had rejected Zionism since 1897.[cite:wiki_bund] Reform rabbis in America had declared Judaism "not political nor national, but spiritual," and Agudat Israel, founded in 1912, gathered Torah-observant Jews largely against religious Zionism.[cite:rs_rd1_reform_wiki] [cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] In Germany the Antizionistisches Komitee reportedly had more than 1,000 members before the war.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] In May 1917 the Board of Deputies and the Anglo-Jewish Association, the official bodies of British Jewry, published an anti-Zionist statement in The Times; that is why the Declaration was addressed to Lord Rothschild and not to them.[cite:jpost_why_rothschild] And the war itself had cut into everything: the expulsions of 1914–15, the famine in the wider region, the hanging of Arab nationalists and the deportation from Jaffa in April 1917.[cite:rs_rd1_jaffa_wiki] One claim often added to this picture, that the founding of the Federal Reserve in 1913 was tied to Zionist finance, finds no support in the record, as [scene:s04ga|the Federal Reserve section] shows.

The architecture at the end of 1917 was therefore real but small, and it was contested from every side. It had an elected Congress and executive, federations in America and Britain, a bank with a Jaffa branch, a land fund with land, a professional land office, a labor movement with its own guards, an American constituency and now a British promise. It did not have a majority of Palestine's people, the support of many organized Jewish bodies in the West, the official representatives of British Jewry among them, or any power of its own to govern. Ottoman rule ended in December 1917, when Britain occupied the country.[cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] What the record cannot settle is how much of what followed was set by this structure and how much by British policy, Arab resistance and the catastrophes of the next thirty years. The documents show when the machinery existed. They do not show that its outcome was fixed.
