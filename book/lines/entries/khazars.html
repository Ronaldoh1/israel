The genetic studies that tested this claim found no evidence for it. The Khazars were a Turkic people whose rulers adopted Judaism around the 8th century, and a 2012 paper by geneticist Eran Elhaik argued they were a major source of Ashkenazi ancestry; critics faulted its use of modern Armenians and Georgians as stand-ins for Khazars [cite:adl_khazar] [cite:aap_khazar_factcheck]. A 2013 study in Human Biology, analyzing 1,774 samples from 106 populations including the Khazar region, found no particular similarity with Caucasus populations and traced Ashkenazi ancestry primarily to the Middle East and Europe [cite:behar_2013_khazar] [cite:nli_behar_khazar]. A 2010 Nature study of 14 Jewish communities found most cluster closer to each other and to Middle Eastern populations than to their host populations [cite:behar_2010_nature].

The kernel of truth is real European ancestry, substantial on the maternal line; one researcher puts the Middle Eastern share at 15–50%. The Khazar theory has been used since the 19th century to cast Jews as impostors [cite:jewishpress_khazar]. And genetics settles no one's political rights, Jewish or Palestinian.
