The Platforms: Moderation as Contested Ground

The [scene:s00a|classroom and the textbook] are older arenas. A newer one is entirely contemporary: what happens to Palestinian and pro-Palestinian content on Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and X, and what those platforms remove, suppress or leave up. Two simple stories dominate that argument. One holds that moderation is neutral, a matter of each company applying its own rules. The other holds that it is a secret, coordinated campaign to silence one side, and which side depends on who is telling it. The record for 2021 to 2026 is made up of formal human rights investigations, reviews the platforms commissioned themselves, a government office's own request figures, leaked internal data, the platforms' written policies and their denials, and watchdog findings that cut the other way. Read in full, it supports neither story.

The heaviest finding rests on two inquiries, begun by different parties two years apart. The first was Meta's own. In 2021 the company commissioned an independent investigation from Business for Social Responsibility (BSR) into its handling of content during the escalation of May 2021. The review, published in September 2022, concluded that Meta's moderation "appear[s] to have had an adverse human rights impact on the rights of Palestinian users." [cite:hrw_meta_broken_promises] The same review did not identify intentional bias. [cite:ibt_echoicide] The second inquiry came from outside. In December 2023 Human Rights Watch published a 51-page report, Meta's Broken Promises: Systemic Censorship of Palestine Content on Instagram and Facebook, which documented 1,050 cases of content being removed or suppressed, from more than 60 countries. [cite:hrw_meta_broken_promises] After its publication, Senator Elizabeth Warren wrote to Meta's chief executive, Mark Zuckerberg, demanding accountability. [cite:hrw_meta_broken_promises] An outside critic's conclusion is one kind of evidence. A conclusion reached by reviewers the company itself hired and paid is a stronger kind, and here the two point the same way on harm, while differing in emphasis on intent.

The number most often repeated from the Human Rights Watch report is "1,049 to 1." The cases were gathered from October and November 2023. Of the 1,050 the organization reviewed, 1,049 involved peaceful pro-Palestinian content and one involved content supporting Israel. [cite:hrw_meta_promises] [cite:bhrrc_hrw_meta] The report named the causes it found: flawed Meta policies, opaque enforcement, heavy reliance on automated systems, and deference to government requests, among them those of Israel's Cyber Unit. [cite:hrw_meta_promises] What the number can and cannot show matters as much as the number. Human Rights Watch described its cases as a collected dataset, not a representative global sample and not a comparison of censorship across platforms. The cases were reported by users who chose to send them in. [cite:ibt_echoicide] A set built that way can establish that a pattern of suppression occurred and what it looked like. It cannot, by its construction, measure the ratio of all pro-Palestinian to all pro-Israel content removed on Meta's services. Meta, for its part, disputes that it deliberately or systemically suppresses any voice. [cite:ibt_echoicide]

A methodological critique of the report also exists, and it belongs beside the report rather than after it. Calcalist, an Israeli technology publication, examined the Human Rights Watch study and identified potential biases in its sampling and in its choice of platforms, which could have affected its findings. [cite:calcalist_hrw_methodology_critique] The critique and the organization's own statement of its limits overlap: both turn on how the cases were collected.

The most precise reading of these sources together is narrower than either popular story. The record documents lopsided harm to pro-Palestinian speech on Meta's platforms. It does not document a deliberate plan. What it does document, in the BSR review and in the causes Human Rights Watch listed, is a design: policies, automated enforcement at scale, and a channel through which a government's requests reach the moderators. That finding is harder to dismiss than an accusation of intent, because it does not depend on proving what anyone wanted.

That channel has a name and its own published figures. Israel's Cyber Unit sits inside the State Attorney's Office. It sends what it calls "voluntary" requests to platforms asking them to remove content it considers incitement or support for terrorism. The requests carry no public legal process. [cite:eff_cyber_unit] The platform decides whether to act, and it almost always has. Since the office began reporting, the compliance rate has never fallen below 77 percent. In 2018 the Unit sent more than 14,000 requests to Google and Facebook, with compliance of about 90 to 92 percent. [cite:hrw_meta_promises] [cite:cjr_cyber_unit] In 2021 it sent 5,990 requests with 82 percent compliance, about 90 percent of them to Facebook and Instagram during the May escalation, the same weeks the BSR review examined. [cite:cjr_cyber_unit]

After 7 October 2023 the volume rose sharply. Between 7 October and 14 November 2023, a little under six weeks, the Unit sent 9,500 takedown requests, 60 percent of them to Meta. According to an Israeli official, the compliance rate was 94 percent. [cite:hrw_meta_promises] Leaked internal data reported by Drop Site News in 2025 indicates that Meta removed more than 90,000 posts at Israel's request, often within seconds and without human review. [cite:dropsite_meta_leak] Drop Site also reported that Meta expanded its automated removals after 7 October, affecting an estimated 38.8 million additional posts. These are reported figures from leaked material and are attributed here as such. [cite:dropsite_meta_leak]

The gap in this part of the record is one of accountability. Meta's Oversight Board recommended that the company formalize and publish how it handles government requests. Human Rights Watch asked the Cyber Unit which policies the removed posts had violated, and received no response. [cite:hrw_meta_promises] Israel describes the Unit's work as countering incitement and terrorism, which is a legitimate aim, and no source here disputes that some of what it flags is exactly that. The dispute is over scope, over transparency, and over who ends up silenced when a state office routinely asks platforms to remove speech and nearly always gets its way, with little public accounting of what was removed or why.

Human Rights Watch concentrated on Meta and noted that it received fewer reports about TikTok. [cite:sada_social_digital_rights_index_2024] A more granular, platform-by-platform count comes from Sada Social, a tracking organization, whose Digital Rights Index 2024 documented more than 25,000 violations against Palestinian digital content across the major platforms. It broke them down as Instagram 31 percent, TikTok 27 percent, Facebook 24 percent and X 12 percent. [cite:sada_social_digital_rights_index_2024] The breakdown is real and should be read precisely. A larger share for one platform can reflect more suppression on that platform. It can equally reflect a larger user base, easier complaint mechanisms, or the reach of Sada Social's own monitoring. The difference in reporting volume between Meta and TikTok is documented. What it says about each platform's actual moderation rate is not settled by these figures.

TikTok's ownership changed in the period. After the Oracle-led acquisition of TikTok's US operations in 2025–26, a Palestinian journalist and influencer known as Owda was banned from the platform. The new ownership has not publicly addressed the case. [cite:meo_us_tiktok_ownership_bans] Larry Ellison, central to the Oracle side of that deal, has an extensively documented history of major personal donations to Israeli military-support causes, traced elsewhere in this library. Those two facts, the ownership history and the ban, stand side by side in the record. The causal link between them has been discussed by observers. It has not been documented, and one named, dated case after a change of owner is not, on its own, proof of a new policy.

The rules themselves are another layer, and here the companies' own texts are the best evidence. Meta's Transparency Center states that the term "Zionist" "often refers to supporters of a political movement, which is not itself a protected characteristic under our policies, but in some cases may be used as a proxy to refer to Jewish or Israeli people, which are protected characteristics." [cite:meta_transparency_zionist_update] The distinction between a political movement and a people, which [scene:s00c|an earlier section of this volume] draws at length, is therefore written into the platform's own policy. Under its longstanding approach, Meta treated "Zionist" as a proxy for a protected group in two narrow circumstances: comparisons to rats, a documented antisemitic trope, and clear contextual substitution for "Jew" or "Israeli." Following a Policy Forum in March 2024, it expanded that proxy treatment further. [cite:meta_transparency_zionist_update] The policy text sets out the framework. It does not record the individual enforcement decisions made under it.

Meta's own press materials say it consulted roughly 145 stakeholders on the 2024 expansion, among them more than 10 pro-Palestinian, Muslim and Arab organizations, and Jewish Voice for Peace, an anti-Zionist Jewish organization. Jewish Voice for Peace was, in other words, inside the consultation, and it remained among the policy's critics. An academic co-signer of the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism publicly criticized the resulting policy as ambiguous. Existing frameworks, including that declaration, already give clearer guidance on separating anti-Zionism from antisemitism, the critic argued, and under the new wording a call for equality within a single state could be misread as an antisemitic "denial of existence." [cite:novara_meta_zionist_ban] How much weight any one participant's input carried in the final text is not on the public record.

The case for the policy also rests on documented evidence. The American Jewish Committee, which advocated for it, cited its own 2023 survey, which found that 80 percent of American Jews say caring about Israel is an important part of being Jewish. On that basis it argued that "Zionist" is a meaningful proxy for Jewish identity in moderation decisions. [cite:ajc_meta_policy_welcome] The figure is real, and it carries weight: for a large majority of American Jews surveyed, caring about Israel is, by their own account, part of Jewish identity. By the same survey's arithmetic, roughly one in five American Jews do not say this. The survey figures do not describe what that fifth believe, only that a rule built on the proxy does not straightforwardly fit them.

TikTok moved separately to treat "Zionist" as hate speech in circumstances comparable to Meta's. [cite:chrisbennett_tiktok_zionist_label] As of July 2025, its Public Policy Manager for Hate Speech, who joined in 2025, was Erica Mindel, an American-Israeli former soldier in the Israel Defense Forces, described in press coverage including the Jerusalem Post and Jewish Insider as a "proud Zionist." [cite:wiki_erica_mindel] That is a fact about who holds the role that shapes the platform's rules on this exact question. It is stated here as such. No source here documents any specific biased decision by her, and none is implied.

Meta's rules then moved again. In January 2025, after a reported six-week internal review led by Zuckerberg and a small group of confidants, the company rolled back broader hate-speech enforcement and permitted content it had previously banned. The Anti-Defamation League's chief executive, Jonathan Greenblatt, publicly criticized the reversal. Meta did not immediately clarify whether the "Zionist" proxy rule survived it. [cite:jta_meta_reversal] Separately, a formal letter from Senator Warren's office set out that Meta had applied uneven moderation standards to Palestinian-related content compared with other comparable material. [cite:warren_senate_meta_letter]

The record also runs in the other direction, and it is just as documented. In November 2023 the Anti-Defamation League published a study finding specific loopholes in TikTok's own moderation that were being used to spread antisemitic content. It named two: slideshows made in TikTok's Photo Mode, and hashtag manipulation, both of which slipped past automated detection. [cite:adl_tiktok_antisemitism_loopholes] In January 2025 CyberWell, a watchdog organization, praised TikTok for improved removal of Holocaust-denial content relative to other platforms. And TikTok has formally denied the bias accusations made against it: Reuters reported the company calling claims from Malaysia that it suppressed pro-Palestinian content "unfounded." These findings do not cancel the earlier ones. Moderation failures that harm Palestinian users and moderation failures that let antisemitic content through are not mutually exclusive, and on this record both occurred, on the same platforms, in the same years.

Users, for their part, adapted. Palestinian and pro-Palestinian users developed documented ways around automated filters, the most visible being the watermelon. Its red, green, black and white match the colors of the Palestinian flag, and users turned to it because keyword- and image-based filters that flagged the flag did not flag the fruit. It is now recognized worldwide as shorthand for solidarity with Palestine, and it became that because of its origin as a way around moderation. [cite:wagingnonviolence_watermelon_algorithms] The workaround is evidence that the moderation of flag-related content was experienced widely enough to prompt one. It does not, by itself, measure how widespread or systematic the underlying pattern was. That measurement is what the other sources in this section attempt, each with its own limits.

Held together, the record looks like this. A formal human rights investigation and a review Meta commissioned and paid for both found an adverse pattern affecting Palestinian users, and the review found no intentional bias. A government office's own figures show it asking platforms to remove speech at scale, through a channel without public legal process, with compliance above 90 percent in its busiest period. A platform's written policy distinguishes a political movement from a protected people and then defines when it treats one as the other, a rule opposed by an anti-Zionist Jewish organization it consulted and defended with survey evidence by a Jewish organization that advocated it. A separate watchdog found antisemitic content evading moderation on TikTok, and another credited TikTok with improvement. TikTok denies systemic bias, and a named methodological critique of the most prominent report exists. The "neutral platforms" story does not survive the Cyber Unit's own numbers or Meta's own review. The "coordinated conspiracy" story does not survive the absence of documented intent, the antisemitic content that got through, or the limits the investigators stated themselves.

What the record does not settle should be stated plainly. No source here measures the overall rate at which pro-Palestinian and pro-Israel content was removed across any platform, and the best-known dataset was not built to. No source documents intent behind Meta's pattern, and Meta's own reviewers found none. Whether the "Zionist" proxy rule survived Meta's January 2025 overhaul was left unclear. Whether the ban that followed TikTok's change of ownership reflects a new policy is unknown. And the Cyber Unit did not tell Human Rights Watch which rules the posts it asked to remove had broken. How the same years were covered by the major newspapers, where the record shows a different kind of asymmetry, is examined in [scene:gx_s07|the study of how press coverage differed].
