Verdict II: Mediated Access and the Inner Traditions

Book Two set out to test a thesis that Ron put in one sentence: institutional religion built a mediated, taxed and obligatory system, of priest, tithe, Sunday duty and commandments, on top of teachings that offered direct and unmediated knowledge of God, and then condemned, burned or absorbed the groups who kept those teachings. Book One's verdict, in [scene:vt19_verdict|Part Nineteen], graded its answer in three registers: what is settled by documents, what is probable on reasonable inference, and what is not established. This verdict uses the same three registers. Because the thesis is a bundle of several claims, it is first split into its parts, and each part is graded on its own; a single verdict on the whole would hide where the record is strong and where it is silent.

The thesis, taken apart

Six separate claims are folded into the sentence. First, that the early teaching of Yeshua and his first followers included an unmediated "kingdom within". Second, that the Gnostic teachers of the second and third centuries, whom the Sophia platform calls the Evangelical Gnostics, kept that teaching and lived without tithe, commandments or obligatory Sunday service. Third, that the institutional church built a system of mediation and obligation. Fourth, that it condemned those teachers and their successors. Fifth, that it burned them or their books. Sixth, that it did so because unmediated access to God threatened an institution built on mediation. The figure grades each.

What is documented

The record settles more of the thesis than its critics usually allow. The texts of the inner traditions do teach what Ron says they teach. The Gospel of Thomas places the kingdom "inside of you, and ... outside of you"; the Gospel of Mary says the Son of Humanity exists within and tells the disciples not to "lay down any rules" beyond the Saviour's, "nor make a law like the lawgiver"; the Gospel of Philip and the Treatise on the Resurrection teach that resurrection is to be realised now. [cite:vt24_gthom] [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt24_gphil] [cite:vt24_treatres] No tithe, no fixed holy-day obligation and no church tax is documented for any Gnostic school, and the Apocryphon of John curses anyone who would trade its teaching for a gift or food. [cite:vt24_apjohn] [cite:vt24_ce_tithes]

It is equally documented that the institution which won built a system of mediation, obligation and enforcement. Ignatius of Antioch wrote early in the second century that it was not lawful "without the bishop" to baptise or hold the common meal; Irenaeus made the line of bishops the test of truth; Constantine's law of 321 made Sunday a civic day of rest; the councils of Tours and Mâcon made the tithe a church law in 567 and 585, the second with excommunication for refusal; Charlemagne's capitulary of Herstal made it obligatory throughout the Frankish kingdom in 779; and the Church's law today binds Catholics to Sunday Mass, the Catechism stating that those who "deliberately fail in this obligation commit a grave sin". [cite:vt20_na_ignatius_smyrn] [cite:vt24_ah3_03] [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] [cite:vt24_ce_tithes] [cite:vt32_wiki_herstal] [cite:vt32_va_ccc2181] And, as the ledger in [scene:vt32_suppression_ledger|the previous section] shows, that institution condemned every group this book has followed. It had the state on its side against them from the fourth century, when emperors ordered heretical books burned (Arius's in 325, Eunomian and Montanist ones in 398, Nestorius's in 435), and from 1184 organised the search, trial and handing over of heretics to the secular power for punishment, which from 1231 meant the stake for the obstinate. [cite:vt22_socrates1] [cite:vt21_fc_laws] [cite:vt28_wiki_adabolendam] [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] The Cathars of Montségur who would not recant, Marguerite Porete and Giordano Bruno died by fire as heretics judged by church authorities; the Templar leaders Molay and Charney were burned on the evening they were sentenced in 1314, after withdrawing the confessions they had made. [cite:vt28_wiki_montsegur] [cite:vt29_wiki_porete] [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_barber_cup1] [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]

What the record corrects

The same documents correct the thesis at four points, and a fair verdict has to carry the corrections with the findings. The first is timing. Mediation did not arrive with Constantine or Nicaea: the single bishop with presbyters and deacons is already demanded in Ignatius's letters, and Christians met on Sunday in Justin's Rome about 155, more than a century and a half before any Sunday law. [cite:vt20_na_ignatius_smyrn] [cite:vt20_justin_67] Nor was the early church taxed: no church law required a tithe until the sixth century, and it lived on offerings, as the Gnostic schools did, with imperial endowments added from Constantine. [cite:vt20_cathen_tithes] [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol] The contrast between a free inner tradition and a taxing institution is real, but it is a contrast with the church of the sixth century onward, not with the church of the second.

The second is the inner traditions themselves. They were not one movement and not all were free of rule. The Valentinians worshipped inside the catholic congregations and complained of being shut out; Ptolemy kept the Ten Commandments as the "pure law"; Sethian texts centre on a baptismal rite of five seals; Pistis Sophia lays down a long list of renunciations; and the Cathar perfecti of the twelfth century lived under bishops, vows and strict fasts. [cite:vt24_ah3_15] [cite:vt24_ect_flora] [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism] [cite:vt26_mead_ch102] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Valentinian teachers also divided humanity into spiritual, animal and material people, an inner hierarchy of their own. [cite:vt24_ah1_06]

The third is the burning of the ancient Gnostics. For the second- to fourth-century schools, the documented means were refutation, exclusion and, from Constantine, a ban on assembly with confiscation of meeting houses and a search for books. [cite:vt24_vc365] [cite:vt24_vc366] No ancient order to burn a Gnostic gospel by name and no execution of a Gnostic teacher has been found. The Nag Hammadi books were buried, not documented as burned. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] Even the text most often called suppressed, the Gospel of Mary, appears in no ancient condemnation. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] The violence of the thesis belongs, on the record, to the medieval church and to the groups it pursued then.

The fourth is the actor. Before the twelfth century the bishop of Rome was seldom the actor in the ledger and most heresy hunters were not Roman; book-burning laws were imperial; the ban on the Illuminati was a Bavarian elector's; and the witch executions were mainly the work of secular courts, though in south-west Germany about four in five took place in Catholic territories, while the Roman and Spanish inquisitions were the more cautious tribunals. [cite:vt24_wiki_panarion] [cite:vt21_fc_laws] [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati] [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] [cite:vt30_wiki_basque] The papacy's own record, from 1184 to 1966, is heavy enough without carrying acts it did not perform.

What is probable

Two parts of the thesis rest on reasonable inference rather than on a document that states them. The first is that the doctrine of apostolic succession served, among other things, to deny teachers of private revelation any authority. That is what Irenaeus's and Tertullian's arguments did in practice: Tertullian denied heretics the right even to argue from scripture, using a plea from Roman law. [cite:vt24_ah3_03] [cite:vt24_praescr] Elaine Pagels made the stronger version a scholarly thesis in 1979: that the doctrine of bodily resurrection "serves an essential political function" and that gnosis supplied a theological justification "for refusing to obey the bishops". [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] It is a named historian's argument, widely read and criticised by others: Henry Chadwick found it lacking in rigour, and Hyam Maccoby faulted it for idealising the Gnostics and passing over their elitism. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] The second probable part is that institutional competition, not only doctrine, decided the outcome: the side with bishops, a closed list of books and, from 312, an emperor's patronage outlasted rivals that had none of these. The sequence of events supports that reading; no document states it as a plan.

What is not supported

Three things are not supported by the record used in this book. That the motive for condemning the inner traditions was the threat of unmediated access, rather than the reasons the heresy hunters gave (doctrine, the unity of the church, the creator God of Genesis, the reality of Christ's body), is not shown by any document; it is an inference about intent that the sources neither confirm nor rule out. [cite:vt24_ah1_pref] [cite:vt25_iren_129] That the canon was chosen to remove these texts at a single moment is contradicted by the slow and uneven way the lists formed between Irenaeus and Trent. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt14_trent_s4] And that the ancient Gnostic texts were burned by church order is, as shown, unsupported. A document showing a church authority ordering Gnostic books destroyed in the fourth or fifth century would change that finding; none has been produced.

Beside these findings stands the Church's own stated position. Its councils and teachers held that they were guarding revealed truth; its modern leaders have said that "men of the church" sometimes "used methods not in keeping with the Gospel", and John Paul II in 2004 asked for "exact knowledge of the facts" about the Inquisition before judgment. [cite:vt28_america_2000] [cite:vt28_va_jp2_2004] This book has tried to supply that knowledge in both directions.

Interpretation, not finding: the lenses of Book Two

Earlier work on the Sophia platform, the Reignite pillar's Suppressed Lineages and Forbidden Arts and the Evangelical Gnostics module, reads this history through several lenses, and Book Two promised to present them only as labelled readings. The first is Ron's four-stage pattern: appropriation, in which an institution takes over a teaching and its symbols; suppression, in which it condemns those who keep the original; occultation, in which the teaching survives hidden, buried in a jar, coded in ritual or carried by small orders; and recovery, in which it returns by accident and late, as at Nag Hammadi in 1945. Read through this lens, the ledger is not a list of separate events but one cycle repeated from the second century to the twentieth. The second is the argument that what threatens an institution most is the claim that it is unnecessary: a teaching that God is reached within makes priest, sacrament and tithe optional, and an institution built on them must answer it. The third reads the Gnostic myths as maps of inner transformation: Sophia's fall and return as the soul's descent and ascent, the alchemists' "solve et coagula" (dissolve and bind) as the same process in the language of the laboratory, and, in some modern esoteric teaching, a "sacred secretion" as its bodily form. These readings are held by Ron and by the Sophia modules, and in different forms by Jung and by modern Gnostic and esoteric writers. [cite:vt24_wiki_septem]

They are readings because no document can confirm or refute them. The four-stage pattern fits some cases closely (the Cathars were suppressed and their rituals survived in inquisitors' registers) and others poorly (the Valentinians were refuted rather than hunted, and Enoch was dropped in the West by doubt, not by order). The "unnecessary institution" argument meets its strongest counter-evidence in the church's own mystics: John of the Cross taught the soul's way to union with God through the "dark night" from inside the church; he was jailed in Toledo in 1577, yet canonised in 1726 and made a Doctor of the Church in 1926, which suggests that the line the institution drew was submission to its authority rather than inner experience as such. [cite:ts13_brit_john] The alchemical link is attested from the alchemists' side and later than the texts: Zosimos of Panopolis, about 300, author of the oldest known books of alchemy, drew on Sethian and Hermetic writings and described the work as a baptism bringing gnosis, but the Nag Hammadi texts themselves are not alchemical treatises. [cite:vt24_wiki_zosimos] [cite:vt24_fraser2007] Orthodox scholars read the same history as communal consensus forming over centuries, and the heresy hunters as pastors defending what they believed true. The lenses can help a reader see a pattern; in this book none of them carries a claim.

The verdict, then, is mixed in a precise way. The thesis is supported where it describes what the inner traditions taught and what the institution built and did from the sixth century, and above all from the twelfth. It is corrected on dates, on the variety of the inner traditions, on the burning of the ancient Gnostics and on the actors. It is not established on motive. What survived despite all of it is the subject of [scene:vt32_what_survived|the final section].
