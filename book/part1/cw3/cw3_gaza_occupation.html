Gaza Under Israeli Rule, November 1956–March 1957

For four months in the winter of 1956–57, Israel governed the Gaza Strip. It was the first time the Strip had come under Israeli rule, and the episode had three features: a military government over a population most of whom were refugees from 1948, an argument inside Israel about whether the territory should be held, and an outside power that decided the matter. The killings at Khan Yunis and Rafah in the first days of the occupation are told in [scene:cw3_gaza_killings|Khan Yunis and Rafah]. This section follows what came after them: how Israel ran the Strip, what it planned for it, and why it left on 8 March 1957.

The people. According to the historian Benny Morris, Israel's military government ruled "some 300,000 Palestinian Arabs," about three-quarters of them refugees of 1948. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] They were not Egyptian citizens. Egypt, which had administered the Strip since 1948, had never given its inhabitants Egyptian citizenship, as Morris notes; that administration is told in Volume II. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The point bears on Ben-Gurion's later argument, told below, that the Strip "was never Egyptian."

Curfew and screening. The UNRWA Director's special report, covering 1 November to mid-December 1956, gives the closest contemporary picture of daily life under the new authority. A curfew was imposed at the outset, and from 5 November it was lifted for six daytime hours. By late November there was "generally free movement of persons throughout the Strip outside curfew hours." Screening of the population continued "apparently in an orderly fashion," with detainees taken to interrogation centres. The refugees, the Director wrote, lived "under considerable tension." [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] The report's word "apparently" can be read as a marker of distance: the agency appears to have been describing what it could observe, not what it had verified inside the interrogation centres.

The agency's own position was precarious. The Director warned that "unless immediate measures were taken to put an end to such happenings" it could not continue its work, and staff for whom there was no work were put on leave without pay after 1 January 1957 (paragraphs 17 and 28). [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] The organization that fed and taught the refugee majority was, in other words, telling the General Assembly that the conditions of the first weeks threatened its ability to operate at all.

How the Strip was governed. Morris describes an administration that rebuilt local institutions under Israeli supervision rather than replacing them. Municipal councils were reinstated; Khan Yunis's was restored on 3 December 1956. Schools reopened, with 30 of 200 textbooks removed as Egyptian propaganda. By late December four police stations staffed by local men were operating under the supervision of the Israel Police. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The arrangement put Gazans in the front offices of town hall, classroom and police station, while authority over each ran to Israeli officers above them.

Two further measures in Morris's account show the military government reshaping the Strip as well as administering it. Bedouin living in the coastal dunes were ordered to move south-west of Rafah; the source for the order is a report of 16 December 1956 by the United Nations envoy, Colonel K. R. Nelson. And monuments to Colonel Mustafa Hafiz, who had run the fedayeen, were destroyed. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] One reading of the second act follows from Israel's case for the war. Hafiz's raids from the Strip had been at the centre of Israel's case for the war, as told in [scene:cw3_road_to_war|the road to war]; on that reading, removing his monuments was a statement about whose memory the Strip would now keep.

The plan to keep it. Ben-Gurion, Morris writes, hoped to keep the Strip. On 19 December 1956 he told the Knesset that Israel would never let Egypt resume control of the Strip. On 23 January 1957 he argued, "This Strip was never Egyptian." [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The argument appears to rest on the legal status described above: Egypt had administered Gaza but never made its people citizens, so its return to Egypt was not, in his framing, a restoration of anything Egypt had held by right.

The decisions of the government and the Knesset in January went in the same direction. The Gush Katif Heritage Center, citing Hagai Huberman (2019), records that a Nahal outpost was set up at Rafiah on 3 January 1957; that the government resolved on 13 January 1957 that "Israel will hold the Gaza Strip," opposing a United Nations force for its security; and that the Knesset approved the decision not to withdraw from Gaza on 23 January 1957. [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] Morris describes a government "Blueprint for Gaza," dated 31 January 1957, which called Gaza and the nearby Jewish settlements "a topographical and economic unit." [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza]

A second account of January. Not every source describes January 1957 in the same terms. According to Eli Ashkenazi, writing for Walla News in 2023, Israel agreed in January 1957 to pull its army out of Gaza while leaving its police and administrative forces in place, and then withdrew in stages. [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] That account and the Katif Center's do not have to contradict each other, since a decision to "hold" the Strip could be carried out by civil administration rather than by troops; but they place different emphasis on the same weeks, one on keeping the territory and the other on pulling the army back. The sources used here do not show how the two were reconciled inside the cabinet. On 15 February the American embassy reported that the prospect Ben-Gurion most adamantly opposed was the return of Egyptian authority to Gaza "under any guise"; about 22 February he said Israel was prepared to withdraw its military forces from Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d100] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d132] That exchange belongs to the story of the withdrawal fight told in the next section.

The dispute: annexation or security? Whether Israel meant to keep Gaza is answered differently depending on which documents are given weight. The case for retention rests on a run of records: Ben-Gurion's December and January statements in the Knesset, the cabinet resolution of 13 January, the Knesset vote of 23 January, the Blueprint, and the Nahal outpost all point the same way. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] To these the Katif Center adds that Ben-Gurion had proposed retaking the Strip as early as March 1955 and that the cabinet listed control of it among the war's aims in October 1956, both told in [scene:cw3_road_to_war|the road to war].

The case for security is Israel's stated one, and it is not a thin case. Israel argued that only it could stop the fedayeen raids from the Strip, and it set as its condition for leaving a United Nations, not an Egyptian, administration. [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] On this reading, the January resolutions were bargaining positions meant to prevent Egypt's return rather than declarations of annexation, and the outcome Israel finally accepted, a United Nations force in Gaza, was the security arrangement it had set as its condition. In his address to the Knesset of 5 March 1957, Ben-Gurion counted United Nations forces in Gaza among the campaign's gains. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57]

The two readings are not mutually exclusive. A government can want both security and territory, and the documents show both aims stated by the same men in the same months. What the sources used here do not contain is a single document in which the cabinet chose between them; the decision was in the end made for it in Washington and New York.

The end. Israel left Gaza completely on 8 March 1957. [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] Morris gives the same date and the reason: the withdrawal came "under international (mainly US) pressure," after four months of military rule. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The diplomacy that produced it, from the General Assembly's resolutions to Eisenhower's address of 20 February and the American statement on the Straits of Tiran, is told in the section that follows.

What the record leaves open. The sources drawn on here give the institutions of the occupation but few numbers for its operation: how many people were detained in the screening, how many Bedouin were moved, and how many Israeli personnel administered the Strip are not given. The text of the Blueprint for Gaza is known only through Morris's summary, and the January decisions are known through the Katif Center's account rather than from the cabinet's own minutes. Whether the government's January position was a plan to annex or a lever to prevent Egypt's return is disputed, and the documents support parts of both answers. What the people of Gaza themselves thought of the councils and police stations set up under Israeli supervision is not recorded in these sources beyond the UNRWA Director's description of a refugee population living "under considerable tension."
