Falwell, Begin and the Moral Majority

By the late 1970s the end-times system traced in the earlier parts had schools, a press, radio and television behind it. [scene:ts7_media|Part Seven: the media] What it did not yet have was a national political organisation. That arrived in June 1979, when the Lynchburg, Virginia, pastor Jerry Falwell and the conservative strategist Paul Weyrich founded the Moral Majority. Weyrich coined the name. Three other New Right organisers, Richard Viguerie, Terry Dolan and Howard Phillips, had urged Falwell to lead such a body, and it was built on the mailing list of his broadcast, the Old Time Gospel Hour. At its height it claimed more than four million members and over two million donors. Falwell disbanded it in 1989 after money troubles, telling a meeting in Las Vegas that "Our goal has been achieved." [cite:ts8_wiki_mm]

What the movement was founded on

Israel was not the issue that brought the religious right into being, and the record of its founding should be stated before the record of its Israel policy. Historians disagree about the founding issue, and neither side names Israel. The historian Randall Balmer argued in 2014 that the movement began in the defence of private Christian schools against the Internal Revenue Service, which after the 1971 case Green v. Connally moved to withdraw tax exemptions from racially segregated schools, Bob Jones University among them. He quoted Weyrich's recollection, from a 1990 conference, of years spent trying to rally evangelicals on other issues: "I was trying to get these people interested in those issues and I utterly failed." [cite:ts8_balmer] Jonathan Whitehead, writing for The Gospel Coalition in 2022, disputed Balmer's emphasis. He placed the trigger in the IRS's proposed rules of 1978, which would have presumed church schools with few minority pupils to be discriminatory and drew about 150,000 letters of protest, and he treated opposition to abortion as one of several streams that came together under President Carter. [cite:ts8_tgc_whitehead] Either way, Israel came into the Moral Majority's programme as one item among several. It did not found it.

Begin's outreach

The Israeli side of the relationship began with Menachem Begin, whose Likud party won the election of 17 May 1977. [cite:gi5_djn_begin77] In 1979 Falwell joined a group of pastors, invited by the National Religious Broadcasters, on a tour of Israel, Jordan and Egypt, and met Begin, Anwar Sadat and King Hussein. Liberty University's own history counts about 15 visits by Falwell to Israel in his lifetime, the largest a tour of 1,500 people in January 1999. [cite:ts8_lib_falwell] In November 1980 Begin presented the Jabotinsky Centennial Medal, named for the founder of his own political movement, to 100 Americans at a dinner in New York. The Jabotinsky Foundation of New York gave the medal, and Falwell was among the recipients; Senator Frank Church declined his in protest at its going also to Falwell. [cite:ts8_wiki_jabmedal] Liberty's account describes the award as an Israeli government honour; the encyclopaedic record names the foundation as the giver and Begin as the presenter, and that is the form used here. [cite:ts8_lib_falwell] [cite:ts8_wiki_jabmedal]

Begin's choice of partner drew criticism from Jewish leaders at the time. In September 1981 Rabbi Marc Tanenbaum wrote in a syndicated column that on his American visit Begin had "met only with Rev. Falwell" and had "in effect, ignored the presence in America of 50 million Roman Catholics." He called the "repeated singling out of Rev. Falwell as the only American Christian leader with whom to counsel" a mistake. Tanenbaum did not reject fundamentalist support, which he said Israel "badly needs"; his complaint was that a wider Christian friendship needed to be "cultivated and not alienated." [cite:ts8_djn_tanenbaum]

Two further stories about the Begin years recur in later accounts: that Israel gave Falwell a Learjet, and that Begin telephoned Falwell to explain the bombing of Iraq's Osirak reactor in June 1981. Neither was found in a contemporary report or a document checked for this volume, and neither is relied on here.

Across the Israeli party line

The relationship did not end with Likud. In March 1985 Prime Minister Shimon Peres of Labour received Falwell at his office, briefly and, by the Jewish Telegraphic Agency's account, reluctantly: his aides barred photographers. Falwell told Peres that Israel was the region's most important democracy and a bulwark of American influence against the Soviet Union, and said: "We are for Israel and not for the (Labor) Alignment or the Likud." In the same visit he telephoned the retired Begin, met the Likud leaders Yitzhak Shamir and Ariel Sharon, and heard Moshe Arens address the 850 delegates of his "Friendship Journey to Israel–85". The agency recorded both sides of American Jewish opinion: many mainstream organisations objected strongly to the Moral Majority's positions on social questions, while others favoured the friendship because of its consistent support for Israel. [cite:ts8_jta_peres1985]

What this period established

The documented pattern of these years is a relationship between named people. An Israeli prime minister chose an American pastor with a national organisation as his main Christian interlocutor and honoured him publicly; the pastor declared his support for the state rather than for either of its main parties; and Jewish leaders in the United States argued openly about whether the partnership was wise. [cite:ts8_wiki_jabmedal] [cite:ts8_djn_tanenbaum] [cite:ts8_jta_peres1985] What the record does not show is that support for Israel was the reason the religious right organised, or that Falwell's organisation made Israel policy. Its founding issues lay elsewhere, and it dissolved in 1989.
