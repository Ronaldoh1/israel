The Right of Return: What Resolution 194 Says, and How Each Side Reads It

On 11 December 1948 the UN General Assembly adopted a resolution whose eleventh paragraph is still argued over today. The "right of return" it is said to contain is read in sharply different ways. Two simple versions are common. One holds that international law guarantees every refugee's descendant a return. The other holds that the resolution behind the claim means nothing. The text itself is short, and both sides cite it. The argument is over what it requires.

Resolution 194 (III) was adopted on that December day. Its paragraph 11 resolved that

the refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date

and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return. [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:wiki_res_194] The General Assembly has reaffirmed the resolution every year since 1949. [cite:wiki_res_194]

How the resolution was first received is less tidy than either side's present position suggests. Arab states and Palestinian representatives at first rejected it, because accepting it implied accepting Israel. Palestinians now cite it as the foundation of the right of return.

Israel stated its reading early. Ben-Gurion told the UN conciliators that the clause "live at peace with their neighbours" made return conditional on peace with the Arab states. [cite:unispal_194_survey] Critics of the Palestinian reading add three points. General Assembly resolutions are recommendations, not binding law. The text says refugees "should be permitted" to return, not that they must be. And the return of several million descendants would end Israel's Jewish majority. [cite:unwatch_194]

Supporters of the Palestinian reading answer that the right rests not on Resolution 194 alone but on broader international law. They also point to Israel's own admission to the United Nations in 1949, which came with an assurance from Abba Eban that Israel would honor resolutions 181 and 194. [cite:wiki_res_194]

Both readings are documented, and this book does not settle between them. What can be said is where each puts its weight. The Israeli reading rests on the conditional clause and on the legal standing of a General Assembly resolution. The Palestinian reading rests on the yearly reaffirmation, on wider legal principle, and on Israel's own assurance at admission. Each side quotes the same sentence and stresses a different part of it.

In practice the result has been unbroken deadlock. The talks at [scene:lau_s01|Lausanne] in 1949 broke down on this question, with Israel offering return only within a final peace and the Arab states demanding return first. More than fifty years later, at Camp David in 2000, it was still unresolved. Every negotiation in between has run into the same paragraph. The most lasting disputes of 1948 were not only about land and armies. They were also about how a few lines of text, adopted that December, would be read.
