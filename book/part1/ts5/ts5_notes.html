What the Notes Say about Israel and the Nations

Much is said about the Scofield notes and little is quoted from them. Popular accounts summarise them as teaching a seven-dispensation scheme, a pre-tribulation rapture and the political return of the Jews to Palestine as a prerequisite of the Second Coming. The first two hold. The third needs testing against the text. This section quotes the 1917 notes, now in the public domain, on five passages that bear on Israel, the land and the nations, and then sets out what they say and what they do not. The quotations are short; the full notes are longer and carry many cross-references.

The covenant with Abraham

Scofield's scheme divides history into seven "dispensations", periods in which God tests humanity under different terms. At Genesis 12:1 the note opens "The Fourth Dispensation: Promise", calls the covenant with Abraham "wholly gracious and unconditional", and says the dispensation "was exclusively Israelitish". [cite:ts1_sac_gen12] The summary of that covenant at Genesis 15:18 is the most quoted note in the book. On the clause "curse him that curseth thee" it says: "It has invariably fared ill with the people who have persecuted the Jew -- well with those who have protected him." [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] On the land it says that "The gift of the land is modified by prophecies of three dispossessions and restorations", two of them already past, and that "Israel is now in the third dispersion, from which she will be restored" at "the return of the Lord as King". [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] Part One examines this note and the verse behind it in detail ([scene:ts1_genesis|Part One]). Two points matter here. The note speaks of "the Jew", a people in dispersion, because in 1909 and 1917 there was no Jewish state. And it places the restoration of the land at Christ's return, not before it.

The "Palestinian Covenant"

The note at Deuteronomy 30:3 gives the clearest statement of the sequence. Scofield calls the passage "The Palestinian Covenant", which "gives the conditions under which Israel entered the land of promise", and lists seven parts in order: "Dispersion for disobedience"; "The future repentance of Israel while in the dispersion"; "The return of the Lord"; "Restoration to the land"; "National conversion"; "The judgment of Israel's oppressors"; and "National prosperity". [cite:ts5_sac_deut30] It adds that "the nation has never as yet taken the land under the unconditional Abrahamic Covenant, nor has it ever possessed the whole land". [cite:ts5_sac_deut30] In this list the Lord's return comes third and the restoration to the land fourth. On the 1917 text, then, a return of Jews to the land is something that follows the Second Coming. It is not a political precondition for it, and the notes examined here give no instruction to Christians to bring it about.

Israel and the Church

The notes on Romans 11 state the distinction on which the whole system turns. "That Israel has not been forever set aside is the theme of this chapter," the note at 11:1 begins; "That the Christian now inherits the distinctive Jewish promises is not taught in Scripture"; Israel "is yet to have its greatest exaltation as the earthly people of God." [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] The note on the remnant looks to the end: "During the great tribulation, a remnant out of all Israel will turn to Jesus as Messiah." At 11:26 Scofield writes that "Israel, regathered from all nations, restored to her own land and converted, is yet to have her greatest earthly exaltation and glory." [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] This is the core of the Scofield position. It rejects the older Christian teaching that the Church replaced Israel in God's promises, and it keeps a future for the Jewish people as a nation. It also expects that future to include a tribulation and a conversion to Christian faith. The 1967 revisers kept this Israel/Church distinction unchanged. [cite:ts5_ct_1967]

The nations: Gog, Magog and Russia

The notes also assigned roles to modern nations. At Ezekiel 38:2 the note says: "That the primary reference is to the northern (European) powers, headed up by Russia, all agree." It reads the names Meshech and Tubal as "Moscow and Tobolsk", calls "Russia and the northern powers" "the latest persecutors of dispersed Israel", and expects their destruction "at the climax of the last mad attempt to exterminate the remnant of Israel in Jerusalem", in the future "day of Jehovah" and the battle of Armageddon. [cite:ts5_sac_ezek38] The note joined a reading of prophecy to the politics of its own day, naming a contemporary power as the persecutor of Jews and as the enemy of the prophets. That habit of identifying a present-day state with a named enemy in Ezekiel or Daniel outlived Scofield; Part Seven follows the prophecy writers who carried it into the Cold War ([scene:ts7_media|Part Seven]).

From the note to the movement

The notes did not call for political action, yet they came to be credited with founding a political movement. The link runs through what happened after 1948. Once a Jewish state existed in the land, the notes' language about a regathered Israel and about nations that "fared ill" or well could be read as describing that state and the governments that dealt with it. Later teachers made the step explicitly. John Hagee, founder of Christians United for Israel, is quoted in the reference literature putting the Genesis principle in these terms: "The man or nation that lifts a voice or hand against Israel invites the wrath of God." [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] Scofield wrote of "the Jew" and of a restoration at Christ's return; Hagee speaks of "Israel" in the present. The difference is the history of the twentieth century, and Parts Eight and Ten follow it ([scene:ts8_cufi|Part Eight], [scene:ts10_mechanism|Part Ten]).

Two cautions follow from the text. First, the notes did not create the belief in a future for Israel. Restorationist writers had argued it since the seventeenth century, and Darby and the Niagara Creed of 1878 already held the Israel/Church distinction and a restored Israel ([scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two], [scene:ts3_niagara|Part Three]). What Scofield added was reach: the scheme printed beside the verse in a Bible that sold about two million copies by 1945. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] Second, the notes contain both of the features that later admirers and critics each emphasise. They deny that Christians inherit "the distinctive Jewish promises", and they keep a national future for the Jewish people; they also place that future after a "great tribulation" in which a "remnant" turns to Jesus as Messiah. [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] Which feature defines the system is argued between its teachers and its critics, and Part Ten sets out both sides with their named sources ([scene:ts10_failures|Part Ten]). This section quotes the text and leaves the theology to its readers.

Claims about further doctrines, such as a rebuilt Third Temple as a necessary precondition of Christ's return, are sometimes attributed to "the Scofield Bible" in general. They are not in the notes examined for this part, and where they appear in this volume they are attributed to the named teachers who made them.
