Judaism is an ancient religion and set of ethnic-cultural lineages; Zionism is a political movement founded in Basel in August 1897 by Theodor Herzl, a secular Viennese journalist. Herzl himself wrote in his diary, "At Basel I founded the Jewish State," and he considered Argentina and Uganda before Palestine was settled on [cite:herzl_diaries]. Jewish opposition to political Zionism is documented from the start: American Reform Judaism's 1885 Pittsburgh Platform declared Jews "no longer a nation, but a religious community" [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885], and the Jewish Labor Bund, founded in Vilna in 1897, rejected emigration to Palestine [cite:wiki_bund].

Today Satmar, the largest Hasidic dynasty, still teaches Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum's 1958 case against a Jewish state [cite:wiki_satmar], and Mizrahi scholars such as Ella Shohat and Sami Shalom Chetrit have built their own critique of the state [cite:chetrit_mizrahi_struggle]. Jewish views also form a spectrum: Martin Buber and Judah Magnes were Zionists who rejected partition. Zionism has always been a contested position within Jewish life, so opposing it is not, by itself, opposing Jews.
