The Room: Who Summoned Nicaea, Who Came, Who Ran It

The Council of Nicaea is often described as the moment when the Church took shape. It happened in a specific place: the lakeside town of Nicaea in north-western Asia Minor, near the emperor's residence at Nicomedia. It ran from about May to the end of July 325. Socrates Scholasticus, writing about a century later, dates the opening to 20 May; some modern historians think business began in June. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] This section reconstructs the room from the people who described it: why it was called, who paid for the journey, who came and who did not, and where the emperor sat. The next section sets out what the room decided. [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided]

A quarrel in Alexandria becomes an imperial problem

The dispute began in Egypt around 318. Arius, a priest in Alexandria born about 250 in Libya, taught that the Son of God had been created by the Father and was not eternal. His bishop, Alexander of Alexandria, condemned the teaching, and a synod at Alexandria denounced Arius around 321. Arius did not go quietly. About 323 he set out his views in the Thalia ("Banquet"), a work in verse, and his ideas spread through popular songs. [cite:vt22_brit_arius] [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] Bishops outside Egypt took sides, and by the time Constantine became sole ruler of the empire in 324 the eastern churches were divided.

Constantine's first move was a letter to both men, preserved by Eusebius of Caesarea in his Life of Constantine. In it the emperor called the cause "of a truly insignificant character, and quite unworthy of such fierce contention", described the matter as "this truly idle question", and asked the two to resume "your mutual feelings of friendship". [cite:vt22_vc2] The letter shows Constantine's priority at the start: peace among the bishops, not a ruling on the Son's nature. The letter failed, and the emperor then did something no emperor had done before. He called bishops from across the empire to one assembly under his own eye.

Summoned and carried by the state

Eusebius, who attended, says Constantine sent letters summoning the bishops and that "he allowed some the use of the public means of conveyance", while others were given horses. [cite:vt22_vc3] The imperial post was a state service normally reserved for officials. Using it for bishops was a material sign that the meeting was the emperor's project. Eusebius also describes a banquet for the twentieth year of Constantine's reign, held near the end of the council, at which "not one of the bishops was wanting". [cite:vt22_vc3] Bishops who had lived through the persecution begun under Diocletian in 303 now travelled at the emperor's expense and dined at his table. Part Twenty-One sets out how that change came about. [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Church and empire under Constantine]

How many came, and from where

No full register of the council survives, and the figures differ by source. Eusebius gives "exceeding two hundred and fifty" in the Life of Constantine; Socrates, quoting the same passage, gives the number as one that "exceeded three hundred", and himself speaks of 318. [cite:vt22_vc3] [cite:vt22_socrates1] The figure of 318, which became the traditional number in later writers, matches the 318 servants of Abraham in Genesis 14 and is not a count. Contemporary reports range from about 250 to 300, and the surviving lists of signatures, copied in several languages, hold between 200 and 220 names. If the priests and deacons who travelled with each bishop are included, modern estimates of everyone present run from about 1,200 to 1,900. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] A figure of "1,800 invited bishops", which appears in some earlier Observe The System material, has no ancient source: no invitation list survives, and the 1,800 figure seems to be a modern estimate of the number of sees in the empire, not of letters sent.

The room was overwhelmingly eastern and Greek-speaking. Only five western bishops are known to have attended: Hosius of Corduba in Spain, Caecilianus of Carthage, Nicasius of Die in Gaul, Marcus of Calabria and Domnus of Pannonia. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] Eusebius notes the reach at the edges: "A Persian bishop too was present", and "even a Scythian" from the Black Sea region. [cite:vt22_vc3] The bishop of Rome, Sylvester, did not come. Eusebius says the bishop of the imperial city was absent because of his age, "but his presbyters were present, and supplied his place"; later lists name these two priests as Victor (or Vitus) and Vincentius. [cite:vt22_vc3] [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] Rome's see was represented and respected, but it did not run the meeting. Alexandria, Antioch and the bishops of Asia Minor carried the debate.

Two absences are often misread. There was no Ethiopian bishop at Nicaea because there was not yet an Ethiopian bishop. The first, Frumentius, was consecrated for the kingdom of Aksum by Athanasius of Alexandria, traditionally in 328 and on other reckonings between 340 and 346; the Ethiopian church that grew from his mission accepts Nicaea. [cite:vt22_wiki_frumentius] Part Twenty-Seven tells that church's story. [scene:vt27_church_and_canon|The Ethiopian church and its canon] Nor is there a record that the council kept out bishops of rival Christian groups. The one documented case runs the other way. Sozomen reports that Constantine called Acesius, bishop of the Novatians, a rigorist church that had broken with the majority over readmitting Christians who had lapsed under persecution. Acesius accepted the creed and the Easter ruling but would not rejoin, and the emperor told him: "take a ladder and ascend alone to heaven". [cite:vt22_sozomen122] There is equally no record that Valentinians, Sethians or other teachers of gnosis were invited or debated. Their quarrels with the bishops had been fought in books during the second and third centuries; Nicaea was about something else.

Where the emperor sat

Eusebius describes the opening scene as theatre. The bishops waited in the palace hall in rows; Constantine entered "clothed in raiment which glittered as it were with rays of light", and he sat only when the bishops signalled, on "a low chair of wrought gold". [cite:vt22_vc3] Popular retellings turn this into a golden throne. The source says a low chair, and the detail matters to Eusebius because it shows the emperor deferring to the bishops in form. In substance he was the most powerful person present. He spoke in Greek, and Eusebius says he heard each side and moved the most heated toward agreement by "occasionally assisting the argument of each party in turn". [cite:vt22_vc3]

Constantine was not a bishop and did not vote; he was baptised only on his deathbed in 337. Hosius of Corduba, a western bishop close to the court, most likely presided over the sessions as the emperor's representative. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] [cite:vt22_wiki_eusnic] That arrangement, a bishop presiding and an emperor present, summoning, paying and later enforcing, is the core of what Nicaea changed. Before 325 bishops had met in regional synods on their own initiative. At Nicaea the question of how Christians should describe their God was settled in a hall of the imperial palace, by men brought there by the state, in a session the emperor opened.

The room was therefore neither a free assembly of all Christians nor a stage-managed vote. It was a gathering of several hundred mostly eastern bishops, summoned and supplied by an emperor who wanted unity, in which one priest's teaching was on trial. What they produced, and how the emperor's preferences shaped it, is the subject of the next section. [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided]
