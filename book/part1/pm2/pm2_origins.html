From a One-Man Office to the Largest Foreign-Policy Lobby

AIPAC began as one man's office. Its founder, Isaiah L. Kenen, had directed lobbying for the wartime American Zionist emergency committee, served as the Jewish Agency's information director at the United Nations and sat on Israel's first delegation there before he moved to Washington in 1951. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit] Earlier volumes of this library tell that beginning in detail: [scene:cw1_lobby|the lobby in its Cold War frame] follows Kenen's first campaign, for Israel's first American aid grant, and [scene:s07g|the line from AZEC to AIPAC] traces the organizations from which his committee grew. This section does not retell them. It follows the organization from that office to the institution that, by the 2020s, outspent every other committee OpenSecrets classed as pro-Israel, [cite:pm_israelite_2024] and it marks the episodes that shaped how it works and how it is argued about.

A separate committee, and the foreign-agent question. The structure Kenen chose carried a legal argument inside it. His lobbying had secured $65 million and then $73 million in American aid for Israel in 1951 and 1952. In 1953 he formed the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs, a body separate from the American Zionist Council, partly because of concerns about registration under the Foreign Agents Registration Act; it began work in 1954 and took the name American Israel Public Affairs Committee in 1959. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The concern was not hypothetical. The Council's purchases of Kenen's newsletter had been paid for in part by the Jewish Agency, a body already registered as a foreign agent, [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] and in November 1962 the Justice Department ordered the Council to register; the Senate Foreign Relations Committee examined the payments the following year. Those events are told in [scene:s11a|the order of 21 November 1962] and [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the Senate]. The separation Kenen designed is the root of the argument that has followed AIPAC ever since: it says it is an American organization, funded by Americans, that receives no money from Israel or any foreign group, and its critics, from Senator J. William Fulbright in the 1970s to the former CIA officer Victor Marchetti in the 1980s, have argued it should register anyway. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac]

From lobby to network. For its first two decades AIPAC was a small Washington office whose best-known product was Kenen's newsletter, the Near East Report. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The change came under Kenen's successors. Morris Amitay followed him in 1974, and Thomas Dine, who became executive director in 1980, built a national grassroots organization of members and donors around the Washington staff. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The model was the one AIPAC still uses: a lobby that gave no money to candidates itself, surrounded by members who did, and who were organized by state and congressional district. Its first great defeat came in the same period. In 1981 Congress narrowly allowed President Reagan's sale of AWACS surveillance aircraft to Saudi Arabia over the lobby's opposition, a fight told in [scene:aw_s01|AWACS, 1981]. Its reputation for electoral power dates from the same years. In 1982 activists connected with AIPAC backed Richard Durbin against Representative Paul Findley of Illinois, and in 1984 they backed Paul Simon against Senator Charles Percy; both incumbents lost. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] How much the pro-Israel money decided those races, against all the other causes of a close election, is disputed, and Part Five returns to them as the first precedents for the unseating campaigns of the 2020s.

Two scandals. Two episodes from the following decades are still cited whenever AIPAC's influence is discussed, and both are documented. In 1992 AIPAC's president, David Steiner, was recorded without his knowledge in a telephone call with a New York property developer, Haim Katz, who gave the tape to the Washington Times. On the tape Steiner claimed that AIPAC had "a dozen people in the campaign, in the headquarters, in Little Rock" working for Bill Clinton, and spoke of positions such as secretary of state with the words "we're just negotiating." After the transcript was published Steiner said his statements were "simply and totally untrue," apologized, and resigned in November 1992. [cite:pm_wiki_steiner] The episode proved that an AIPAC president had boasted of influence over appointments; it did not prove that he had any, and he denied it.

The second episode involved the law. In 2005 Lawrence Franklin, a Defense Department analyst, pleaded guilty to passing classified information, some of it to two senior AIPAC staff members, Steven Rosen and Keith Weissman. AIPAC dismissed both men in April 2005, and they were indicted under the Espionage Act for receiving and passing on national defense information. In 2009 the government dropped all charges, saying that the judge's rulings had made a conviction unlikely and that it did not want to reveal classified information at trial. [cite:pm_wiki_franklin] [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] Franklin was convicted; the AIPAC staff members were not, and AIPAC itself was never charged.

The argument that never closed. Through all of it, the question Kenen's structure was built to answer stayed open. To AIPAC and its supporters, it is what it says it is: an American membership organization, funded by private donations, that advocates for a policy, the support of an ally, that most American voters and both parties have long endorsed. To its critics, its closeness to the priorities of successive Israeli governments makes it something the law ought to treat differently. The 1962 order, the 1963 hearings, the arguments of Fulbright and Marchetti and the questions raised again in the 2020s are all versions of the same dispute. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] This volume does not settle it. It describes what AIPAC and its affiliates do with money, which is a matter of public record, and leaves the legal characterization to the reader.
