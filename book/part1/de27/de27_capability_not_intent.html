Capability Is Not Intent: What the Software Does and What Its Makers Say

Every argument about Palantir turns on one distinction, and this book holds to it throughout: what a tool can do is documented by how it is built and used; what anyone intends to do with it is documented only by their own words or acts. This section sets out the first as plainly as possible, then the second, then the strongest case on each side of the question that matters for this book, which is whether Palantir is a neutral piece of plumbing or something more.

Integration, not collection

Palantir's software does not go out into the world and gather information. It has no cameras, no phone taps, no web crawlers of its own in the ordinary sense of those words. It works on records the customer already holds or chooses to load: case files, border-crossing logs, police reports, purchased commercial data, sensor feeds. Its job is integration, which in plain terms means three steps. First, it connects to many separate databases, often through an API, an application programming interface, the standard doorway by which one program asks another for data. Second, it performs what engineers call entity resolution: deciding that the "J. Smith" in one file, the "John Smith" at the same address in another and the owner of a particular phone in a third are one person, and linking those records together. Third, it lets analysts search, map and chart the linked result, and set alerts when something new appears. [cite:de27_pal_notdata]

The company's own account of this role is precise. "We do not collect, store, or sell personal data," it says; its customers "define what can and cannot be done with their data," and "We do not and cannot reuse or transfer our clients' data for our own purposes." [cite:de27_pal_notdata] It builds in what it calls privacy and civil-liberties engineering: permissions fine-grained enough that an analyst sees only what his role allows, and a default configuration that records every user's actions so that they can later be traced, an audit log. The same company adds a caution that bears on everything that follows: "technical controls alone cannot avert negative societal outcomes." [cite:de27_pal_pcl] To European regulators its spokesman put the point in one line after Germany's constitutional court ruled in 2023: the company "merely provides the software for processing data, not the data itself." [cite:de27_reuters_bverfg]

What the men who run it say it is for

The intent behind a tool is not the intent of its customers, but the people who run Palantir have said a great deal about what they built it to do, and their words are the right evidence for their own purposes. Karp's are unusually direct. In an interview with Axios broadcast on HBO in May 2020 he said the company's product was used "on occasion" to target and kill people, describing the hunt for a terrorist: "you're probably doing the operation that actually takes out the person with another product we built." Asked in the same interview whether he regretted the company's work for ICE, he said, "No, no, no," adding, "I've had some of my favorite employees leave ... over ICE." [cite:de27_inverse_karp2020] On a call with investors in early February 2025 he said Palantir was there to disrupt the institutions it worked with and, when necessary, "to scare enemies and on occasion kill them." [cite:de27_giz_karp2025]

His book with Nicholas Zamiska, Palantir's head of corporate affairs, The Technological Republic, published on 18 February 2025, argues the general case: it criticises what its publisher called "Silicon Valley's failure to come to the defense of the nation that made its rise possible." [cite:de27_prh_techrep] On 18 April 2026 the company posted a 22-point summary of the book on X, including the lines "Silicon Valley owes a moral debt to the country that made its rise possible" and "National service should be a universal duty," and argued that the question about AI weapons was who would build them, not whether. [cite:de27_reason_manifesto]

Read on their own terms, these statements describe a company that sees itself as an arm of national defence and law enforcement, and is proud of lethal work against foreign enemies and of its immigration contracts. They are not statements of an intent to watch domestic political opponents, and no statement by Karp, Thiel or Cohen found here says so. The words are hard, and they should be quoted as hard; they should not be converted into something they do not say. Thiel's own account of the company's founding purpose, given in 2016, was that the government "was collecting a lot of data ... more than they could analyze," and that helping it make sense of that data could "end indiscriminate surveillance." [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] How that founding claim fares against Thiel's later politics is examined in [scene:de10_philosophy_vs_practice|Part Ten].

The strongest case that it is only a tool

The case that Palantir is a sophisticated database program and nothing more rests on four documented points. The data belongs to the customer, which decides what goes in and who may see it, and the company says it cannot reuse it. [cite:de27_pal_notdata] The software's audit logs make misuse more traceable than it is in the scattered systems it replaces, where an officer's search of a spreadsheet leaves no central record. [cite:de27_pal_pcl] The company's government relationships have limits that it did not set: the NSA work ended in 2015 without a contract, and in 2025 Palantir said publicly that it "is not a vendor on any master database project to unify databases across federal agencies" and "has no contracts with DOGE." [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015] [cite:de19_palantir_record] And what any customer does with the software is bounded by that customer's law, the third-party doctrine and the purchased-data gap in the United States, a constitutional threshold in Germany, not by Palantir's code. On this view, the right target for anyone worried about surveillance is the law of data acquisition, which is where [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Twenty-Eight] turns.

The strongest case that it is more

The case that it is more than a tool rests on documented points too. The first is that integration is itself the power. Records that are harmless apart can be revealing together, which is why stripping names from data protects less than it seems, as [scene:de24_the_myth_of_anonymous_data|Part Twenty-Four] explains; a platform whose purpose is to join records is a platform for producing exactly that effect. The second is what joined data has done in practice. A study of the Los Angeles Police Department's use of Palantir, published by the sociologist Sarah Brayne in 2017 after interviews with 75 officers and staff, found that its system held people who had never had direct contact with the police, linked to a "person of interest" by something as slight as a car accident with a gang member, and that a relationship category allowed partners to be entered too; licence-plate readers fed the system, and a points system for "risk" sent officers to stop the same people again, which added more points. [cite:de27_cnn_brayne] [cite:de27_asa_brayne] Palantir and the department declined to comment. [cite:de27_cnn_brayne]

The third is that the company's people are not at arm's length. Palantir's model, as Fortune described it, sends its own engineers to work inside a customer, reviewing its data sources and adding new kinds of data as needs change. [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] The line between "the customer decides" and "the vendor's staff build what the customer asks for" is thinner in such a model than in an ordinary software licence. The fourth is lock-in. ICE has justified awards on the ground that "Palantir is the only source that can provide the required capabilities," and an August 2026 work statement for a data broker's bridge contract required connections to "the Palantir platform." [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:de28_bu_lexis] Once a platform becomes the hub through which other data flows, the vendor's design choices shape what the agency can see, even if the vendor never touches the data. The fifth is output: ICE's ELITE tool, reported in January 2026, gives each person's current address a "confidence score." [cite:ow9_fortune_elite] A score is not a fact, and the people at the other end of a wrong one are the ones who bear the cost.

Where the two cases meet

Both cases are true in part, and they are not opposites. Palantir is documented as a tool that does not collect, and documented as a tool whose purpose is to make collected data more powerful by joining it. Its makers' stated intent is national defence and law enforcement; its customers' uses are set by their law and their policy; and the capability to turn scattered records into a list of people and their likely addresses exists, at ICE, today. Whether that capability is turned against anyone beyond the people the law already targets is not a question about Palantir's software. It is a question about the law of acquisition, the agencies' policies and the courts, which are the subjects of the next part. The money that keeps the platform growing, and the wars it has been used in, come first.
