Every part of this volume so far has asked what the record shows. This part asks a prior question: who decides which parts of that record a child is taught. The answer differs from country to country, and the differences are themselves a finding. In Israel one ministry decides. In Germany sixteen states must agree. For the Palestinians, foreign donors have paid for the curriculum and have stopped paying when they objected to it. In the United States no one in Washington may decide at all, and the gap is filled by state boards, publishers and whoever is organized enough to take part.

The part begins with a documented example of how open that gap is: a British press baron, described by named biographers as tied to Israeli intelligence and by his own government's inspectors as unfit to run a public company, who in 1989 owned half of one of America's two largest school publishers. [cite:pen_edweek_mmh1989] [cite:pen_wiki_maxwell] It follows ownership to the investment funds that own the publishers today, maps who reviews the books in the American states and what one organized network asked Virginia to change, [cite:pen_jcrc_va_letter2018] and compares the ministry, federation and donor models abroad, including the one study that examined Israeli and Palestinian books by the same method. [cite:pen_forward_wexler2013] It ends with the history that made publishers cautious and the state laws, passed since 2023, that now bring the same argument into the classroom. [cite:pen_time_rugg1939] [cite:pen_elestoque_ab715]

Throughout, what a document shows is kept apart from what it is said to show. Ownership is not editing, a request is not a change, and a law's examples are not a ruling. Where the record stops, the text says so.
