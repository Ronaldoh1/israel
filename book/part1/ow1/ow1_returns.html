What Washington Says It Gets: The Cooperative Programmes

This volume's title describes a one-way mirror: a relationship in which, critics argue, the United States gives far more than it can see or receive. That claim has to be tested against what the United States itself says it gains. The government's case is set out in the same documents as the aid: missile-defence systems developed jointly and partly built in America, access to Israeli technology, research foundations funded by both governments, and an aid package spent almost entirely on American products. This section sets out those programmes and their legal basis, then compares what each side receives on the documented record.

Missile defence. The largest cooperative programme began with the 1986 agreement on Israeli participation in the Strategic Defense Initiative, under which the Arrow anti-ballistic missile was developed. [cite:ow1_cie_sdi] Arrow 2 was first deployed in 2000 and Arrow 3 in 2017; development of David's Sling, against medium-range rockets, began in 2006 and it was fielded in 2017; Iron Dome, against short-range rockets, was first fielded in 2011. [cite:ow1_csis_imd] Iron Dome was built by Israel's Rafael, and American funding for it began in FY2011: $205 million in FY2011, $70 million in FY2012, $194 million in FY2013 and $460.3 million in FY2014 including an emergency supplemental, $929.3 million in its first four years. [cite:ow1_crs_irondome] In March 2014 the two governments signed a co-production agreement allowing Iron Dome components to be manufactured in the United States and giving the US Missile Defense Agency full access to Iron Dome technology, which had been proprietary; Raytheon became Rafael's American partner and received a $149 million contract for interceptor parts on 30 September 2014. [cite:ow1_crs_irondome] The 2016 memorandum fixed missile-defence support at $500 million a year and required that at least half of annual procurement and co-production funds be spent in the United States. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] [cite:ow1_crs_aid2025]

Tunnels, drones and new technology. Congress has since added smaller programmes, each authorized in a defence bill. Section 1279 of the FY2016 National Defense Authorization Act created a US–Israel anti-tunnel cooperation programme, and Section 1278 of the FY2020 act a counter-drone programme. [cite:ow1_s554] For FY2025 they were funded at $47.5 million and $55 million, alongside $20 million for emerging technology, and the FY2025 defence act added joint subterranean exercises and a partnership with the Pentagon's Defense Innovation Unit. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] A bill introduced in February 2025 by Senator Dan Sullivan of Alaska and others would raise the caps and authorize $50 million a year for joint work on artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, robotics and quantum technology; its stated condition is a cost-sharing agreement before any money is spent. [cite:ow1_s554] Its status should be checked against the FY2026 defence act, which this section has not verified.

The research foundations. Older and less visible are the binational foundations. The Binational Industrial Research and Development (BIRD) Foundation was established by the two governments in 1977, with an endowment of $60 million raised to $110 million in 1984, contributed equally; it has approved more than 900 joint projects between American and Israeli companies. [cite:ow1_wiki_bird] Homeland security research runs through the same channel. Under a 2009 agreement on science and technology cooperation in homeland security, the Department of Homeland Security's Science and Technology Directorate and Israel's Ministry of Public Security set up a joint pilot in 2016, run by BIRD and renamed BIRD HLS in 2019; by 2021 the two ministries had put in $9.65 million and selected ten projects, which private partners matched with at least $20 million. [cite:ow1_dhs_birdhls] The 2014 Strategic Partnership Act authorized further joint research on border, maritime and aviation security. [cite:ow_jvl_2014act] Part Six follows this channel inside DHS.

The stated return. Read together, the official documents give four reasons the United States says the arrangement serves American interests. Strategically, they describe Israel as a partner that shares American goals in the region and whose qualitative edge, maintained with American weapons, is the best guarantee of stability; the White House called American capabilities "the best way" for Israel to keep that edge. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] [cite:ow1_crs_aid2025] Technologically, co-production gave the Missile Defense Agency access to a combat-tested Israeli system. [cite:ow1_crs_irondome] Industrially, the end of offshore procurement means up to $1.2 billion more a year spent on American-made equipment. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] And logistically, the WRSA-I stockpile is American property on Israeli soil. [cite:ow1_npr_wrsa2014] Intelligence sharing, which the government also cites, is examined in Parts Three and Four.

Where the asymmetry lies. On this record the "one-way" description is too simple for the programmes themselves. The research foundations are funded equally; the missile-defence money buys systems the United States can now build and study; and most of the aid is recycled into American factories. The asymmetry the documents do show is of a different kind. It is in money: Israel received about 40 per cent of the worldwide Foreign Military Financing budget at the previous memorandum's closing level, according to former ambassador Daniel Shapiro. [cite:ow1_crs_aid2025] It is in terms: early disbursement, interest and cash-flow financing are privileges other recipients lack. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] And it is in law: the United States is bound by its own statute to protect Israel's edge, and the documents examined here contain no reciprocal commitment by Israel. Whether the United States can see what its money and weapons do once they arrive is the question the rest of this volume follows, agency by agency.
