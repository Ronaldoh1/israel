# The Epstein Architecture
## The Network, the Files and Power Protects Power
### Working draft · introduction and Parts One–Four · 31 October 2026

## Introduction
In June 2008 a Florida court accepted Jeffrey Epstein's guilty plea to procuring a minor for prostitution. The federal prosecutors who had investigated him had already signed an agreement that ended their case, granted immunity to "any potential co-conspirators" they did not name, and kept the girls he had abused from learning about it until it was done. A federal judge later ruled that keeping the victims in the dark broke the law. [cite:marra] [cite:cbs_marra] [cite:politico_acosta] For the next eleven years the most powerful people in the world kept him close. Banks kept his accounts. Universities kept his money. When he was arrested again in July 2019 and found dead in his cell a month later, the one proceeding that might have put the people around him under oath ended with him.

The question this book asks is not whether he was guilty. He pleaded guilty in 2008, and in December 2021 a federal jury convicted Ghislaine Maxwell on five counts, including sex trafficking, for recruiting and grooming girls for him; Judge Alison Nathan sentenced her to twenty years. [cite:ep1_abc_maxwell_sentence2022] The question is what kept the system around him running: who funded it, who vouched for it, who looked away, which laws and offices protected it, and why, in 2026, the government that holds the files has still released only part of what it found. When the Justice Department closed its review on 30 January 2026 it had published almost 3.5 million pages out of the 6 million it said it had reviewed, after missing the deadline Congress had set. [cite:ep1_rollcall_release2026]

### What this book will not do

Many readers will come to this book looking for "the list". There is no list in it, and that is deliberate. A name in Epstein's papers can mean almost anything: an email he sent, an entry in an address book, a dinner on a calendar, a seat on a flight, a payment, or a tip phoned in to the FBI by a stranger. Printing names without saying which of these each one is would be the fastest way to bury the people who did the most harm among the people who merely knew him, and to hand everyone named a reason to dismiss the whole record. Instead, every named person in this book is placed at one of four levels of evidence, and the level is stated each time.

**Decided by a court.** A conviction, a guilty plea, or a judge's finding of fact. **Sworn.** An allegation made under oath, in a deposition, an affidavit or testimony, by a named accuser; it has not been tested at trial unless the book says so. **Documented contact.** Records show the person was in contact with Epstein: an email, a meeting, a flight, a payment. That is a fact about contact, not a finding of wrongdoing, and the book says what kind of contact each record shows. **Not carried.** A claim that circulates widely but rests on no record the book can stand behind; it is named only where readers will already have met it, and the book explains why it goes no further.

A claim that someone took part in a crime appears only where a court record, sworn testimony or a document from the case files supports it. Where a person has said one thing in public and the files show another, the two are set side by side with their dates. That is a contradiction on the record; it is not a finding about why they said it.

### The rules the book keeps

The same standard applies to every party, every country and every administration. The Republican Speaker of the House and the Labour peer, the Democratic president and the Republican one, the Justice Department under one attorney general and under the next: each is judged act by act, from documents, in the same words.

Several parts of this story touch on intelligence services, on Israel and on the Maxwell family. Each of those passages is held to a simple test. A named official, a named firm or a dated act of a state may appear, because documents can show them. A drift from those named actors to an ethnic or religious group is never made, because no document can show it, and because that drift is one of the oldest falsehoods in history. A father's relationships are not evidence about his daughter, and a man's business partners are not evidence about the country they come from.

The survivors are the subject of this book, not its backdrop. Abuse is described no more graphically than the courts and the survivors themselves have described it. Survivors are named only where they have chosen to go public. Every death in this story is given its official finding first, before any dispute about it.

### How to read the sources

The documents released by the Justice Department under the Epstein Files Transparency Act carry a numbering system: each page has an EFTA number. Wherever this book relies on a released page, the note gives that number or the court filing, so a reader can check the document itself rather than someone's account of it. The book prefers, in order: court records and sworn testimony; government documents and filings; contemporaneous reporting by named journalists; later reporting. Aggregator sites, anonymous wikis and social-media threads are not used as sources, however widely they circulate.

### The shape of the book

The book moves from the man to the system. Part One follows Epstein from Brooklyn to the Wexner power of attorney and the first time he walked away from a fraud that sent his partner to prison. Part Two lays out how the abuse operated, as prosecutors proved it. Part Three follows the money, dollar by documented dollar. Part Four shows who stayed close after 2008, and Part Five gives the people named in this book their own biographies, rebuilt from the record rather than the headlines. The middle of the book turns to the image he built, the agencies that circled him, the law and the Justice Department that protected him, and the fight over the files. The last parts ask whether the network still operates, what the record shows about the deaths around the case, how often the same pattern has appeared before, and what, stated plainly, the architecture was.

## Part One · Before the Money
Part One covers the thirty-eight years before Jeffrey Epstein had a fortune anyone could see: from his birth in Brooklyn in 1953 to the summer of 1991, when the founder of The Limited signed a power of attorney that let him hire and fire, buy and sell, and borrow in the billionaire's name. It asks one question, and it is the question every later part depends on: is there any documented source for a self-made fortune?

The record for these years is thin, and most of what has been written about them was gathered after 2005 by people working backward from what he became. This part therefore keeps three kinds of material apart: what documents and court records show; what named people recalled, often decades later; and what Epstein said about himself, which changed from one telling to the next. Where the record is empty, the text says so. The pattern that emerges is not a secret patron or a hidden fortune. It is something plainer and, in its way, more revealing: at every stage after high school, a person vouched for him where a qualification should have been, and at two of those stages, a firm that had reason to look closer kept him anyway.

### Sea Gate, Lafayette and Two Universities, 1953–1974

Jeffrey Edward Epstein was born in Brooklyn on 20 January 1953, the elder of two sons. [cite:legalclarity] [cite:hoffenberg] His parents, Seymour and Paula Epstein (born Stolofsky), were both children of European immigrants, and much of their extended families died in the Holocaust. Seymour worked first for his own father's house-wrecking business and then for the New York City Parks Department; Paula also worked outside the home. [cite:ep1_forward_childhood] Public records place the family in Sea Gate, a small gated community at the western tip of Coney Island, although Epstein himself later said he grew up in Coney Island. [cite:ep1_forward_childhood] It was a household of municipal wages. There was no trust, no family firm and no inherited network.

That plain fact matters more than any colour about his childhood, because it is the baseline against which everything that follows has to be measured. By the time he died, Epstein held a Manhattan mansion, a house in Palm Beach, a ranch in New Mexico, an apartment in Paris, two islands in the Caribbean, aircraft, cars and boats; the will he signed two days before his death valued the estate at more than $577 million, and his executors later revised the figure to $634.8 million. [cite:cbs_epstein_will] [cite:foxbiz_estate_634m] None of it was inherited. Whatever produced it began here, with nothing, and that cuts two ways. It makes the rise genuinely remarkable. It also removes the most ordinary explanation for how a person comes to hold that much, which is why the question of where the money came from cannot be waved away as a detail.

### What is known, and how it is known

Almost nothing about Epstein's early years was written down at the time. The fullest account of his boyhood, in the 2019 reporting that followed his second arrest, draws on a book by James Patterson, John Connolly and Tim Malloy, on a childhood friend, on a classmate who said she and Epstein had been teenage sweethearts, on an unnamed graduate of his high school, and on public records for the family's address. [cite:ep1_forward_childhood] Those are recollections gathered decades later, by people who by then knew how the story ended, and this book treats the colour they supply as reported rather than established. It builds no argument on it.

What those accounts agree on is modest. Both brothers were gifted students with an interest in mathematics. Jeffrey learned the piano young. His friends called him "Eppy". [cite:ep1_forward_childhood] For the years before high school the documentary record is, in effect, empty: his school records are not public and no contemporaneous account survives. A chronology that jumped from his birth straight to the first interesting event would give a false impression that his life is continuously documented. It is not. Roughly a third of the years in this part rest on little more than enrolment dates, and that proportion is itself information about how much of this life is actually known. [cite:hoffenberg] [cite:legalclarity]

### Lafayette High School

He attended Lafayette High School in Gravesend, Brooklyn, and advanced two grades ahead of his age group, graduating in 1969 at sixteen. [cite:ep1_forward_childhood] [cite:legalclarity] The acceleration is consistently reported, and it is the first documented instance of a shape that recurs through his life: moving past a stage faster than the ordinary route allows. In this first instance it was earned. The ability was real and it was mathematical, and it is what took him next into two demanding institutions.

### Cooper Union and the Courant Institute

In 1969, at sixteen, he enrolled at the Cooper Union in Manhattan, which was then tuition-free and highly selective; admission alone shows real academic standing. He left in 1971 without a degree. He then attended New York University's Courant Institute of Mathematical Sciences, a serious centre of graduate-level mathematics, and left around 1974, again without a degree. [cite:legalclarity] [cite:hoffenberg] No public record explains either departure. Accounts written later are retrospective and inconsistent, and this book adopts none of the theories in circulation.

By 1974, then, Epstein was a twenty-one-year-old with substantial mathematical training and no qualification of any kind. That is the condition that shaped everything after it. He had real capability and no document attesting to it, in a set of professions (teaching, banking, finance) that ordinarily admit people by their documents. Every position he held from then on had to be obtained through a person rather than through a process. That is not a moral observation; it is a structural one. It explains why, in the record that follows, the relationships matter more than the qualifications, and why the people who vouched for him are part of the story.

### The brother

His younger brother, Mark, is the only member of his immediate family who appears in the later record, and he appears in his own right. Mark became a property developer in New York. In the early 1990s Les Wexner, Epstein's principal client, sold the apartment building at 301 East 66th Street to Mark, according to the *Wall Street Journal*, citing *Crain's*; Mark told *Crain's* that a tip from Jeffrey prompted the purchase. The buyer was Ossa Properties, a former affiliate of Jeffrey's firm, J. Epstein & Company, of which Mark had once been listed as president. [cite:ep1_trd_mark2019] The building later mattered: the *Miami Herald* reported that Epstein housed some of the young women he abused in apartments there, charging them $1,000 a month through the modelling agent Jean-Luc Brunel. [cite:ep1_trd_mark2019] Mark has described his relationship with Jeffrey, beyond being brothers, as "landlord/tenant", and after 2019 he publicly disputed the official finding on his brother's death. [cite:ep1_trd_mark2019] [cite:legalclarity]

The book holds one rule firmly here. A brother's business and statements are his own; they establish nothing about Jeffrey's conduct, and Jeffrey's conduct establishes nothing about his. The 66th Street building returns in Part Two as a property in the operation. Mark Epstein enters this story as its owner, and as a man who says he dealt with his brother as a landlord, and the book says no more than the record does.

### The Dalton School, 1974–1976

In the autumn of 1974 the Dalton School, a private school on Manhattan's Upper East Side, put a twenty-one-year-old without a college degree in front of its classes to teach physics and mathematics. [cite:ep1_nyt_dalton2019] [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] Dalton's yearbooks show Epstein on the faculty from the 1974–75 academic year; a later magazine profile dated his arrival to 1973, but the yearbooks do not bear that out. [cite:ep1_nyt_dalton2019] The headmaster who hired him was Donald Barr. [cite:legalclarity]

The appointment is the first time in Epstein's life that a position came to him without the qualification it normally required. The shape is the one that recurs through the rest of this part: an institution that ordinarily admitted people by their papers admitted him by someone's judgment of him.

### What students remembered

When the *New York Times* spoke to former students in 2019, eight of them said his conduct toward teenage girls had left a lasting impression. Most recalled his persistent attention to girls in the halls and classrooms. One recalled him at a high-school party where students were drinking, the only teacher present. Scott Spizer, who graduated in 1976, said: "I can remember thinking at the time, 'This is wrong.'" Millicent Young, of the same class, said she had seen him flirting with girls and thought it inappropriate. Others remembered him differently: Leslie Kitziger, who graduated in 1978, described a caring, attentive teacher who was always professional with her. None of the women who spoke to the newspaper remembered him making unwanted physical contact with them. [cite:ep1_nyt_dalton2019]

These are recollections, gathered more than forty years later from people who by then knew what Epstein became. They are documented as recollections, not as findings of misconduct, and this book reports them that way. Their value is narrower and more specific: they show that some of what later defined him was visible to teenagers at the time, and that the adults responsible for the school either did not see it or did not record it. The interim headmaster who followed Barr, Peter Branch, said he did not recall anyone raising concerns with him about Epstein's conduct with students, and a lawyer for a later headmaster, Gardner Dunnan, said Dunnan had not been aware of any. [cite:ep1_nyt_dalton2019]

### Dismissal

Epstein's time at Dalton was short and ended in a dismissal. An administrator told the *Times* he had dismissed Epstein for poor performance. Branch, who later ran the high school, said faculty had raised concerns about his teaching and that he "eventually determined that he needed to go", describing him as "a young teacher who didn't come up to snuff". [cite:ep1_nyt_dalton2019] The dismissal came in the spring of 1976. [cite:legalclarity]

### The parents

What mattered most about Dalton for the rest of the story was not the teaching but the parents. The school's parent body in the mid-1970s was among the wealthiest in New York, and a young teacher who made himself interesting to them had access to a network no credential would have opened. [cite:legalclarity] One of those parents was Alan "Ace" Greenberg, a senior partner at the investment bank Bear Stearns; Epstein tutored Greenberg's son at the school. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] Another Dalton parent, impressed by the young teacher, telephoned Greenberg about him; Greenberg's widow later recalled the call. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] In 1976, as his teaching career ended, Greenberg hired him. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] [cite:britannica_epstein_bio]

That is the method, in its first clean instance: access gained through a sponsor rather than a qualification, and a sponsor found among the people he had been hired to serve.

### A coincidence, and why it stays one

Donald Barr's son, William Barr, was Attorney General of the United States when Epstein was arrested in July 2019 and when he died in federal custody that August. The coincidence circulates widely, often with the suggestion that the father's hiring decision and the son's office are connected. No document connects them. Nothing in the record links a 1974 hiring at a private school to anything in 2019, and this book reports the coincidence as exactly that and nothing more. [cite:legalclarity] The Justice Department's handling of the 2019 case is examined in Parts Eight and Nine on its own record.

### Bear Stearns, 1976–1981

Epstein arrived at Bear Stearns in 1976 with no training in finance, no degree and no securities examination behind him. Greenberg first hired him to trade stock options on the floor of the American Stock Exchange. In a letter he wrote for Epstein's fiftieth birthday in 2003, Greenberg recalled that Epstein said no to the floor job; it was, Greenberg wrote, the only disagreement the two men ever had. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] Epstein moved instead into work with the firm's wealthiest clients on their tax affairs. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] Subsequent reporting describes the speciality as finding inventive ways to reduce the tax exposure of people who already had a great deal of money. [cite:britannica_epstein_bio] It is the direct antecedent of the business he later built: not managing money for returns, but structuring it for the very rich.

He advanced quickly. Greenberg became the firm's chief executive in about 1978, and in 1980 Epstein was named a limited partner, a rung below full partner, roughly four years after joining. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] Some accounts call him the youngest limited partner in the firm's history. [cite:doj_efta_epstein_profile] Whatever else is true of him, this was a real performance in a demanding firm by someone who had arrived without the preparation his colleagues had. The book records the competence because the rest of the record has to be read honestly, and because it is part of why powerful people believed in him.

Several of the people from those years stayed in his life. The 2003 birthday book, assembled by Ghislaine Maxwell and released by a congressional committee in 2025, includes letters from Greenberg; from James Cayne, who became Bear Stearns' chief executive in 1993; from Elliot Wolk, a long-time Bear Stearns employee who ran an account for Robert Maxwell; from Ira Zicherman, who later became one of the original trustees of Epstein's foundation; and from Ted Serure, who wrote that he and Epstein had started at the same desk about twenty-five years earlier. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] A letter in a birthday book is evidence of a friendship and nothing more.

### The exit

Epstein resigned from Bear Stearns on 12 March 1981. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] The departure has been described in so many ways since that it is worth setting out exactly what the record contains, because for once the record contains his own words.

That spring the Securities and Exchange Commission was investigating insider trading around the Seagram Company's 1981 tender offer for St. Joe Minerals. On 1 April 1981, nineteen days after he resigned, Epstein gave testimony to the SEC. He denied knowing of any insider information about St. Joe involving Cayne. Vanity Fair, which reviewed the testimony in 2003, reported that the SEC "seemed to be" sceptical of his answers; the commission never charged anyone at Bear Stearns over St. Joe. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003]

In the same testimony Epstein offered his own explanation of his departure. Bear Stearns' management, he said, had found "offensive" a loan he had made to a close friend, a loan that may have broken the firm's rules under what he called "Reg D". In a deposition in 1989 he named the friend as Warren Eisenstein and put the loan at about $20,000. He said the firm's executive committee fined him $2,500 and that the episode cost him his chance at a full partnership. The firm nonetheless agreed to pay his bonus, which he expected to be about $100,000. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003]

Others told it differently. Cayne said Epstein "left Bear Stearns of his own volition". Epstein denied rumours of what was called a "technical infringement". Greenberg said he could not recall the matter. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] Greenberg's widow, decades later, remembered her husband receiving a call saying that Epstein would be "fired", without knowing why. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] Vanity Fair's own sources asked why anyone would leave a promising partnership over a "mere $2,500 fine". [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003]

What the record establishes is narrower than any of the stories. He resigned in March 1981. He was questioned under oath in an insider-trading inquiry weeks later and was not charged. By his own sworn account, the firm had disciplined him over a loan. Accounts differ on whether he was pushed or chose to go, and the people who could settle it are gone: Greenberg died in 2014, and Bear Stearns itself collapsed in 2008. [cite:britannica_epstein_bio] [cite:wiki_epstein_mirror_2026] This book has found no published SEC or Bear Stearns record of the internal discipline; his own testimony is the source for it.

There is a second thing the exit shows, and it matters more for this book than the reason. A firm that had disciplined him, by his own account, still paid his bonus, and its senior partners went on writing him affectionate letters for decades. It was the first time an institution that had grounds to look closer at Jeffrey Epstein chose to keep its good opinion of him. It would not be the last.

### The Missing Years, 1981–1987

The six years after Bear Stearns are the least documented of Epstein's adult life, and they are the years in which, by every account, his methods and his money first took shape. More has been written about this stretch than about any other part of his early life, and less of it rests on records. This section sorts what is documented from what is told, and holds each claim at the level its source supports.

### What he called himself

After leaving Bear Stearns, Epstein set up on his own as an adviser. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] In the 1980s he described himself to acquaintances as a "financial bounty hunter" who recovered lost or stolen money for governments and for very rich people. Two writers recount this independently: the journalist Vicky Ward, from friends who recalled him saying it, and a writer who met him in 1987 and remembered him dropping names, including that of the Saudi arms dealer Adnan Khashoggi. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] [cite:airmail_tea_with_epstein] [cite:lawandcrime_ward_bounty] Ward later reported, citing her own source, that Khashoggi was among the people he chased money for. [cite:lawandcrime_ward_bounty]

The description is documented as his description. Nothing in the public record shows which governments or clients he worked for, what money he recovered or what he was paid. It is a story he told about himself in the years when he had no institution behind him, and it should be read as one.

### A passport in a safe

One object from those years is documented beyond doubt, because federal agents found it. On the day of his arrest in July 2019, a search of his Manhattan house turned up a locked safe holding cash, diamonds and an Austrian passport bearing Epstein's photograph and a different name. In a letter to Judge Richard Berman opposing his release on bail, prosecutors said the passport carried entry stamps for France, Spain, the United Kingdom and Saudi Arabia in the 1980s. [cite:ep1_cnbc_passport2019] Reporting on the passport describes it as listing an address in Saudi Arabia. [cite:notion_leese_profile] [cite:jpost_ward_rollingstone]

Epstein's lawyers answered that the passport had expired thirty-two years earlier, that he had obtained it in the 1980s for his personal protection when travelling in the Middle East at a time of frequent hijackings, and that prosecutors had offered "nothing to suggest — and certainly no evidence — that Epstein ever used" it. Prosecutors replied that the lawyers had not explained how he came to hold a foreign passport in another name, and that he had not disclosed whether he was a citizen or permanent resident of another country. [cite:ep1_cnbc_passport2019] The question was never resolved in court; he died before trial.

### The introduction to Towers

The third documented thread leads to the next stage of his life. In 1987 Steven Hoffenberg, who ran a debt-collection and bill-consolidation company called Towers Financial, took Epstein on as a consultant. Who introduced them is itself disputed. Hoffenberg said it was Douglas Leese, a British defence contractor; Epstein said it was John Mitchell. Epstein told Vicky Ward in 2002 that he had never met Leese. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] [cite:wiki_steven_hoffenberg] [cite:jpost_ward_rollingstone]

### The arms-money account

Around that introduction a larger story has grown. Hoffenberg later claimed that Epstein had laundered money for Khashoggi and Leese, including money connected to arms shipments to Iran in the years of the Iran–Contra affair, and that Leese had introduced him to Robert Maxwell and to British intelligence circles. [cite:jpost_ward_rollingstone] [cite:wionews_bounty_hunter_doj] That account is the source of most of what is written about Epstein as an arms-world operator in the 1980s.

It rests on one witness, and the witness has to be weighed. Hoffenberg had knowledge: he worked with Epstein every day in the late 1980s. He also had motive: he was convicted of running one of the largest Ponzi schemes in American history, he called Epstein its architect, and he sued Epstein in 2016. [cite:jpost_ward_rollingstone] [cite:wiki_steven_hoffenberg] No court record or released document corroborates the arms-laundering account. This book does not carry it as fact. It records that Hoffenberg said it, that Epstein denied the Leese connection on which it depends, and that the released files, still being read, may yet confirm or refute it.

The plain explanation of these years needs no arms dealers at all. Epstein left Bear Stearns with an expertise in tax structuring for the very rich, a reputation for brilliance among the partners there, and a habit of describing himself in dramatic terms. He found work as a paid adviser, and in 1987 he found a paymaster. That explanation fits every documented fact in this section. The more dramatic one fits some of them and needs a single interested witness for the rest.

### Towers Financial: The First Impunity, 1987–1993

This is the most under-reported episode in Epstein's life, and in some ways the most important for this book. It has nothing to do with sex. It took place fifteen years before the agreement everyone knows about. And it shows, in its cleanest form, a pattern the rest of the book traces: two men inside one enterprise, one of them prosecuted and imprisoned, the other never charged, and no public record explaining the difference.

### The job

Steven Hoffenberg ran Towers Financial Corporation, a company that collected debts and consolidated bills. In 1987 he hired Epstein as a consultant at $25,000 a month and set him up in an office in the Villard Houses. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] [cite:ep1_towers_record] Hoffenberg later said he had hired Epstein specifically to raise capital, described him as his closest professional partner of the period, working alongside him every day, and told investigators that Epstein was involved in the transfer and sale of bonds and investments. [cite:wiki_steven_hoffenberg] [cite:hoffenberg] He also called him the smartest person he knew when it came to money. [cite:ep1_npr_hoffenberg2022]

Together they tried to use Towers to take over large companies. A 1987 press release identified Epstein as chairman of the board of an entity called Intercontinental Assets Group and as a financial adviser on a proposed acquisition of Pan Am that Towers was pursuing; the bid failed. [cite:hoffenberg] [cite:legalclarity] They also went after the company Pennwalt, and here the two men's accounts part. Hoffenberg said the money Epstein used came from him as a loan; Epstein said in 1989 that he had put in $300,000 of his own. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003]

### The loan

A loan from Hoffenberg to Epstein is one of the most repeated facts about these years, and one of the least settled. Later reporting describes a $2 million loan in 1988 that Epstein never had to repay. [cite:hoffenberg] [cite:wiki_steven_hoffenberg] Towers' own financial statements, as reported by Vanity Fair in 2003, show a loan to Epstein of $400,000; Epstein said he could not remember the details and disputed the accuracy of the reports. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] The two figures may describe different loans or different moments, or one of them may be wrong. What is documented is that money moved from Hoffenberg's company to Epstein outside his consulting fee. How much, and whether it was ever repaid, the record does not settle.

While still consulting for Hoffenberg, in 1988, Epstein founded his own firm, J. Epstein & Company, reportedly with a minimum client threshold in the hundreds of millions. It was private, had no duty to disclose anything, and produced no audited record of performance. [cite:legalclarity] He also joined the board of the New York Academy of Art in 1987, an early example of a cultural board seat acquired through social standing, the currency of access in New York. [cite:legalclarity] He left Towers around 1989. [cite:hoffenberg]

### The collapse

In February 1993 the Securities and Exchange Commission charged that Towers had reported a profit of $13 million over the four years ending in June 1991, when it had in fact lost $137 million. [cite:ep1_towers_record] The company had been paying old investors with new investors' money. In April 1995 Hoffenberg pleaded guilty to five criminal counts; he admitted defrauding thousands of investors of about $460 million. In March 1997 Judge Robert W. Sweet sentenced him to twenty years in prison. He served eighteen and was released in 2013. [cite:ep1_towers_record] [cite:ep1_npr_hoffenberg2022] It was one of the largest Ponzi schemes in American history up to that time, and it is settled fact, with a docket behind it.

### The man who was not charged

Epstein was never charged in connection with Towers. [cite:ep1_towers_record] [cite:ep1_npr_hoffenberg2022] Hoffenberg spent the rest of his life saying that Epstein had been deeply involved and, by some of his accounts, the architect of the scheme. In 2019 he called Epstein his "uncharged co-conspirator". ### Why this matters more than it seems

Every explanation later offered for the 2008 agreement that let Epstein avoid federal prosecution concerns the difficulty of that case: reluctant victims, problems of evidence, the sensitivity of the subject. None of those explanations applies here. Towers was a securities fraud with a paper trail, losses of hundreds of millions of dollars, and a convicted principal willing to name him. The difficulty of prosecution was low, and he was still not charged. That is why this episode belongs at the start of the book. It separates the pattern from the subject matter.

The book is careful about what that does and does not show. It does not show that Epstein was guilty of fraud; no court has said so, and the only witness against him was a man with a motive. It does not show that anyone protected him; no record explains the decision either way, and the absence of an explanation is not evidence of a reason. [cite:hoffenberg] [cite:legalclarity] What it shows is an outcome: the first documented time that a system with grounds to examine Jeffrey Epstein closely did not, and he walked on to the next, larger thing.

### Wexner and the Power of Attorney, 1986–2007

If there is a single document from which Jeffrey Epstein's fortune and his access both flow, it is a three-page power of attorney signed in July 1991 by Leslie Wexner, the founder of The Limited, the retail group that owned Victoria's Secret. It allowed Epstein to hire people, sign cheques, buy and sell property and borrow money on Wexner's behalf, and it stayed in force for about sixteen years. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Some later accounts date the arrangement to 1987; the better sources put the full instrument in 1991, and this book uses that date. [cite:legalclarity]

It is worth pausing on how unusual it was. Billionaires do not commonly hand legal authority over their affairs to a former schoolteacher with no degree, no audited track record and a recent paymaster whose company would be exposed two years later as one of the largest frauds of its time. Wexner did. Why he did is a question this book cannot answer from the record, because the only people who could answer it are Wexner and Epstein, and their accounts are incomplete in different ways.

### How they met

According to the *New York Times*, an insurance executive, Robert Meister, introduced the two men in the mid-to-late 1980s, and Epstein soon spent more and more of his time with Wexner. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Epstein's own accounts changed. He once said he had met Wexner in Palm Beach in 1986 and won his confidence by advising him against the stock market before the crash of 1987; he later said he began working for Wexner in 1989 and that it was "certainly not the same time" as his dealings with Hoffenberg. [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] The dates matter because they overlap: if the relationship began in 1986 or 1987, Epstein was advising one of the richest men in America while on Towers' payroll.

By the late 1980s Epstein was managing the construction and design of Wexner's 316-foot yacht, *Limitless*. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] A source close to Wexner told Vanity Fair in 2003 that he had given Epstein "the power of fiduciary over all of his private trusts and foundations". [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003] Epstein became a director of the Wexner Foundation and president of Wexner's property company. [cite:legalclarity] Between 1991 and 2006 he oversaw the sale of roughly $3.1 billion of stock in Wexner's company. [cite:legalclarity] What he was paid for any of it has never been established in public. The *Times* found no record of whether any agreement set his compensation at all. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019]

### The houses, the plane and the island

The clearest record of what passed from Wexner to Epstein is the property record, and it is worth reading in order.

In 1989 Wexner bought a townhouse at 9 East 71st Street, one of the largest private houses in Manhattan, for $13.2 million. Epstein moved in by 1996. In 1998 Wexner sold the building to a company called Nine East 71st Street Corporation, in which Epstein had some ownership interest, for a sum that was not disclosed. [cite:ep1_bi_townhouse2019] A person familiar with Wexner's finances told the *Times* that Epstein paid $20 million; no public record confirms it. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] In 2011 the deed moved again, from Nine East 71st Street Corporation to Maple Inc., a company in the US Virgin Islands also controlled by Epstein, for a recorded price of $0, with Epstein signing on both sides of the transfer. A court document later estimated the house's value at $77 million. [cite:ep1_bi_townhouse2019]

The pattern repeats elsewhere. In 1992 Epstein acquired a twenty-three-room mansion in New Albany, Ohio, for $3.5 million, a property Wexner legally controlled; in 1998 a Wexner-linked entity bought it back from him for $8 million. In about 2000 an Epstein company obtained a Boeing 727 that had belonged to The Limited, for $10 million according to a person familiar with Wexner's finances, though again no public record of the transaction exists. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Also in 1998, through a separate company, Epstein bought Little St. James, an island in the US Virgin Islands, for $7.95 million. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] By the *Times*' count, the assets Epstein obtained through the Wexner relationship were worth roughly $100 million together. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Separately, in 1990, he bought a house at 358 El Brillo Way in Palm Beach for $2.5 million; it became the centre of the Palm Beach police investigation that began in 2005. [cite:ep1_pb_mansion] [cite:marra]

### What the relationship bought

The money mattered less than what it opened. The Wexner relationship gave Epstein a billionaire's credibility to present as his own, and he used it to bring other wealthy people into business with him. [cite:legalclarity] It also gave him a name that opened doors with young women. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] The later court record establishes that Epstein used the Victoria's Secret connection as a pretext in recruiting. [cite:legalclarity] In the summer of 1996 the artist Maria Farmer was at Wexner's estate in Ohio; she alleges that Epstein sexually assaulted her there and that Wexner's security staff kept her on the property for twelve hours. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Her report to the FBI that year is the subject of Part Two.

The foundations gave too. Two of Wexner's charitable foundations contributed about $21 million in stock and cash to Epstein's own charity, COUQ. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019]

### The break, and Wexner's account

The relationship ended after Epstein's first arrest. Florida authorities charged him in 2006; by early autumn 2007, Epstein had agreed to step back from managing Wexner's personal finances. [cite:ep1_cnbc_wexner2019] [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] In 2019 Wexner wrote to members of his foundation that Epstein had "misappropriated vast sums of money from me and my family", that the discovery had been "a tremendous shock", and that Epstein had returned part of it shortly before going to jail in 2008: a $46 million contribution in Apple stock and other assets to the family's YLK Charitable Fund. Wexner's lawyers gave federal prosecutors documents they said showed the misappropriation. [cite:ep1_cnbc_wexner2019] He told his employees that he was "NEVER aware of the illegal activity charged in the indictment". [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] In 2003, before any of this, he had called Epstein "always a most loyal friend". [cite:ep1_vf_ward2003]

That is Wexner's account, on the record, and this book reports it as such. No court has adjudicated what he knew. What is documented is the instrument, its sixteen-year duration, the $3.1 billion, the property transfers, the warning about recruiting in his company's name, and the rupture. His full biography, and the sworn record on him, are in Part Five.

### A note on sources

The Wexner relationship is where the worst writing about this subject collects. Some widely shared pages tag every person in it by religion and present a philanthropic group Wexner joined in 1991 as an ethnic conspiracy. They are not used here. [cite:legalclarity] The version of this story that needs an ethnic frame is not a stronger version. It is a version that discards the documents.

### The Father and the Daughter, 1923–1992

In the same year Wexner signed the power of attorney, the person who would become Epstein's closest partner arrived in his life. Ghislaine Maxwell told the Justice Department in July 2025 that she struck up a friendship with Epstein in 1991, that their sexual relationship began in 1992, and that from 1992 he paid her $25,000 a year to work for him. [cite:ep1_cbs_maxwell2025] She said it in a two-day interview with Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, under a grant of limited immunity that did not shield her from prosecution for lying; Blanche told her it was not a cooperation deal. [cite:ep1_cbs_maxwell2025] It is her account, given by a person serving twenty years for her crimes with him, and it is recorded here as hers. Her arrival belongs at the end of Part One because it closes the period before the money and opens the period of the operation.

### Robert Maxwell

Her father's life and death are among the most cited and most misused facts in this story, and they need to be set down accurately.

Robert Maxwell was born Ján Hoch in Czechoslovakia in 1923. He escaped the Nazis, who murdered his family, fought for Britain in the Second World War, and built a global publishing empire, Maxwell Communications. [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell] On 5 November 1991 he disappeared from his yacht, the *Lady Ghislaine*, and was found dead in the Atlantic. The official finding was a heart attack and drowning; the circumstances remain disputed. [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell] After his death his companies were found to have looted the pension funds of the Mirror Group's employees. [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell]

On 10 November 1991 he was buried on the Mount of Olives in Jerusalem with the ceremony of a state funeral. Israel's president, Chaim Herzog, and prime minister, Yitzhak Shamir, attended. Reuters footage records his body lying in state at Jerusalem's convention centre, and accounts report at least six serving and former heads of Israeli intelligence among the mourners. [cite:jta_maxwell_funeral_1991] [cite:reuters_maxwell_funeral] [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell] A line is widely attributed to Shamir: "He has done more for Israel than can today be told." The contemporaneous report by the Jewish Telegraphic Agency says it was Herzog who delivered the eulogy. [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell] [cite:jta_maxwell_funeral_1991] Maxwell had pledged large investments in Israel and organised airlifts of Soviet Jews. [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell] Several books allege that he worked with Israeli intelligence; those claims rely heavily on a single former Israeli intelligence officer, Ari Ben-Menashe, and on secondary accounts. [cite:wiki_ari_ben_menashe] [cite:wiki_robert_maxwell]

A Bear Stearns employee who wrote in Epstein's 2003 birthday book, Elliot Wolk, had run an account for Robert Maxwell. [cite:ep1_bi_birthday2025] It is a thin thread, and the book records it at its actual weight: a broker who knew both men.

### What the funeral shows, and what it does not

The funeral is a documented and extraordinary fact about Robert Maxwell's standing in Israel. It is not evidence about what his daughter did, or about what Epstein did, decades later. The step from "the father was honoured by Israel's leaders" to "the daughter and her partner ran an Israeli intelligence operation" is an inference with no document under it. It moves a relationship from a father to his daughter, and from her to a man she met in the year her father died. That is the step this book declines to take, here and in Part Seven, where the intelligence question is examined in full on its own record.

There is a second step the book also declines. Claims about Maxwell's intelligence ties, true or false, are claims about one man and, at most, about named agencies and officials of a state. They are not claims about a people. Where such passages in this story drift from a named official to an ethnic or religious group, they leave the record behind entirely, and they repeat a falsehood far older than the Epstein case. The documents in this book are about named people and dated acts. So is the book.

### What this part established
By the end of 1992 the architecture that the rest of this book examines was in place, and none of it had been built from a fortune. Epstein had the use of a Manhattan townhouse his client had bought and he would later take over, a house in Palm Beach, a private firm with no duty to disclose anything, a power of attorney over a billionaire's affairs, and a partner, Ghislaine Maxwell, whom he paid to work for him. What he did not have, at any point in these thirty-eight years, was a documented source of wealth of his own: no inheritance, no audited record of investment returns, no business that made a product anyone could buy.

The question Part One set out to answer was whether any documented source exists for a self-made fortune. The answer from the record is no. What the record shows instead is a sequence: talent, then sponsorship, then money that arrived through other people's trust. A Dalton parent vouched for him to Bear Stearns. Bear Stearns disciplined him, by his own sworn account, and paid his bonus. Towers paid him and lent to him, collapsed as a fraud, and sent its owner to prison while he was never charged. Wexner gave him authority over a fortune, and the houses, the plane and the foundations' money followed. After Dalton, at each stage an institution had reason to look harder: a firm that disciplined him, prosecutors who charged his partner, a client warned about recruiting in his company's name. Each time, he kept what he had. That is the first appearance of the pattern this book is about, and it appears before the abuse that made him notorious. Part Two turns to the operation that the money and the access made possible.

## Part Two · How It Operated
Part Two describes what Epstein did with the money and access Part One traced, as courts and prosecutors established it. It begins with the record that no one disputes, a federal indictment, a judge's finding and a jury's verdict, and works outward from there: how girls were brought to him and how they were paid to bring others, who scheduled them, where it happened, who tried to report it and what became of their reports, and what the famous documents, the flight logs and the address book, actually prove.

The question Part Two asks is the one the 2008 agreement and the 2019 indictment both answer: could he have done this alone? He could not, and the court record says so. What it does not do is name everyone who helped, and the distance between those two facts is the subject of most of this book.

Two rules govern this part with special force. The abuse is described no more graphically than the courts and the survivors themselves have described it. And survivors are named only where they have chosen to speak publicly under their own names.

### What the Courts Established

On 8 July 2019 federal prosecutors in Manhattan unsealed a two-count indictment charging Jeffrey Epstein with sex trafficking of minors and conspiracy to commit it. It alleged that from at least 2002 to at least 2005 he recruited girls, some as young as fourteen, to his houses in Manhattan and Palm Beach to give "massages" that became increasingly sexual, typically including one or more sex acts; that he paid them hundreds of dollars in cash each time; that some were abused many times, in some cases almost daily; and that he knew many of them were under eighteen, in some cases because they had told him so. [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] [cite:thewrap_epstein_indictment] "As alleged," the US attorney, Geoffrey Berman, said, "Jeffrey Epstein abused underage girls for years." [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] An indictment is an accusation, and Epstein died before it could be tried. But it sets out the pattern prosecutors were prepared to prove, and the later trial of his partner proved much of it.

### A judge's finding

Months earlier, in February 2019, a federal judge in Florida had already made a finding of fact. In a case brought by two of Epstein's victims under the Crime Victims' Rights Act, Judge Kenneth Marra found that Epstein had worked in concert with others and had directed others to obtain girls for him. [cite:marra] [cite:nbc_marra] It is a short finding, and it matters out of proportion to its length: a federal court, on the evidence before it, concluded that the operation was not a one-man act. The same ruling found that prosecutors had broken the law by keeping the 2008 agreement from the victims, a story told in Part Eight.

### A jury's verdict

On 29 December 2021, after five full days of deliberation, a jury in New York convicted Ghislaine Maxwell on five of the six counts against her, including sex trafficking of a minor. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] [cite:ep1_abc_maxwell_sentence2022] Four women testified that she had recruited and groomed them for Epstein when they were teenagers, in the 1990s and early 2000s, at his houses in Florida, New York and New Mexico; one had been as young as fourteen. Three testified under first names or pseudonyms: Jane, now a television actress; Kate, a former model from Britain; and Carolyn. The fourth, the psychologist Annie Farmer, testified under her own name. Each described the same sequence: charm and gifts to win their trust, then "massages" that became sexual. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021]

The prosecution called twenty-four witnesses, among them a housekeeper and pilots of Epstein's jets. The evidence included a folding massage table, a "black book" listing girls' contact details under the heading "massages", and bank records showing that Epstein had transferred $30.7 million to Maxwell. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] Maxwell did not testify. Her lawyers argued that she was the target of a vindictive prosecution, called a memory expert, Elizabeth Loftus, to testify that suggestion can contaminate recollection, and told the jury that "she is not Jeffrey Epstein". [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] The jury rejected that defence. In June 2022 Judge Alison Nathan sentenced her to twenty years, calling her conduct "heinous and predatory". [cite:ep1_abc_maxwell_sentence2022] She is the only person other than Epstein ever convicted in connection with the operation.

### How many

A book about the powerful can lose sight of how many people this was done to. No court has produced a complete count, and the indictment's own word was "dozens". [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] The closest thing to a census is the Epstein Victims' Compensation Program, set up with money from his estate after his death and run by an independent administrator, Jordana Feldman. It received about 225 claims, more than twice what had been expected. It paid about $121 million to about 150 people; some 75 claims were rejected, and 92 per cent of those offered compensation accepted it. [cite:ep2_nbc_vcp2021] Accepting meant giving up the right to sue the estate, though not the right to speak publicly or to cooperate with investigators. [cite:ep2_nbc_vcp2021]

That figure is a floor, not a total. It counts only people who came forward, met the program's terms and were believed by it. It leaves out those who never applied, those who settled earlier, and those the Palm Beach police identified in 2005 who never sought money at all. Where they came from, how old they were and how many were brought from outside the United States are questions the public record answers only in fragments; the Justice Department's files, still being released, are the likeliest place for a fuller answer.

### What these records do not say

The three court records together establish a structured operation: a recruiter, paid recruitment, staff who arranged the visits and several properties. They are the strongest evidence in this book. They do not, by themselves, establish that any of the famous people in Epstein's address book took part in it. That question is taken up name by name in Parts Four and Five, at the level of evidence each case allows. Keeping the two apart is not a courtesy to the powerful. It is what keeps the survivors' sworn evidence standing when weaker claims made around it fall.

### The Pipeline: Recruiters, Schedulers and an Agency

The indictment describes a structure that fed itself. Girls who came to Epstein's houses were paid not only for what was done to them but for bringing others; both the girl who recruited and the girl she brought were paid hundreds of dollars. [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] It is the mechanism that turned one victim into several, and it is why the Palm Beach police, once they began to interview girls in 2005, kept finding more. Their chief, Michael Reiter, said later that "the stories were all the same". [cite:ep2_nbc_reiter2019]

Above the girls who were paid to recruit sat adults who were paid to organise. The indictment says Epstein worked with employees and associates who contacted victims and scheduled their visits at both his Manhattan and his Palm Beach houses, without naming them. [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] Above them, by the jury's verdict, sat Ghislaine Maxwell, who recruited and groomed girls herself. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021]

### Four names in a federal agreement

The 2008 non-prosecution agreement that ended the federal case in Florida did something unusual: it promised that the United States would not bring criminal charges against "any potential co-conspirators of Epstein", and it named four women in that language: Sarah Kellen, Adriana Ross, Lesley Groff and Nadia Marcinkova. [cite:ep2_cbs_coconspirators2019] [cite:marra] Their names are therefore on the public record in this role through a federal document, and that is the only reason they appear here.

What each of them did is not settled. None has been charged with anything. In a 2019 lawsuit against Epstein's estate, a woman identified as Jane Doe, who says Epstein abused her from 2002 to 2005 beginning when she was fourteen, alleges that Kellen and Groff, whom she describes as his assistants, scheduled her visits, asked her to bring other girls and paid her, including for bringing others. Groff's lawyer denied it, saying she worked as part of a professional staff and "at no time" engaged in misconduct. Kellen did not comment. Ross and Marcinkova were not named in that suit. [cite:ep2_cbs_coconspirators2019] A civil complaint is an allegation, and a lawyer's denial is a denial; the book carries both and decides neither.

There is a further caution, and the book states it plainly because the alternative is to repeat the operation's own logic. Whether any of the young women who later worked around Epstein first came to him as girls is a question the record has to answer case by case. Where it does, it changes what their later role means, and an organisation chart that lists them only as functions would read as a list of accomplices. Part Five gives each person named here their own entry, with what the record shows about how they came to him.

### The modelling route

A second route ran through the fashion business. Jean-Luc Brunel, a French modelling agent and a frequent companion of Epstein, who often travelled to France and kept apartments in Paris, was detained at Charles de Gaulle airport in 2020 in a French investigation opened after Epstein's American charges. In December 2021 he was charged with the rape of minors; he was held in an inquiry into rape and the trafficking of minors for sexual exploitation. On 19 February 2022 he was found dead in his cell in Paris. His lawyers said he had always maintained his innocence. [cite:ep2_aj_brunel2022] He was never tried, and the French charges died with him.

The *Miami Herald* reported that Epstein housed some of the young women he abused in apartments at 301 East 66th Street in Manhattan, the building his brother's company owned, and that Brunel's agency charged them $1,000 a month for them. [cite:ep1_trd_mark2019] How women came to the United States through modelling, on what visas and with whose sponsorship, is one of the least-documented parts of the operation. It needs immigration and court records the public does not yet have, and this book treats it as an open question rather than filling it in.

### The Properties

The operation ran through houses, and every one of them was bought or obtained before anyone in authority took a serious look. The will Epstein signed two days before his death in August 2019 lists them with the values his lawyers put on them: Little St. James, his island in the US Virgin Islands, at more than $63 million, and the neighbouring Great St. James at more than $22 million; the Manhattan house at $55.9 million; a ranch in New Mexico at $17.2 million; the Palm Beach house at $12.4 million; and an apartment in Paris at $8.7 million. [cite:cbs_epstein_will] Each appears in the court record or in survivors' accounts. Taken in the order he acquired them, they also trace the arc of the money.

### Palm Beach, 1990

The house at 358 El Brillo Way cost $2.5 million in 1990. [cite:ep1_pb_mansion] Of all his properties it is the one most directly tied to a criminal case. It is where the girls interviewed by the Palm Beach police from 2005 said they had been brought, and it is one of the two residences named in the 2019 federal indictment. [cite:ep2_nbc_reiter2019] [cite:marra] [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] It had been in place for fifteen years before any investigation began. After his death his estate sold it for $18.5 million, and the new owner demolished it in 2021. [cite:ep1_pb_mansion]

### Manhattan, 1989 and after

The house on East 71st Street, bought by Les Wexner in 1989 and passed to Epstein-controlled companies in 1998 and 2011, is set out in Part One. [cite:ep1_bi_townhouse2019] It is the other residence named in the indictment, and it is where, on the day of his arrest in July 2019, agents found a locked safe of cash, diamonds and a foreign passport. [cite:ep2_sdny_pr2019] [cite:ep1_cnbc_passport2019] Across the city, the apartments at 301 East 66th Street, in a building his brother's company owned, were used, according to the *Miami Herald*, to house some of the young women he abused. [cite:ep1_trd_mark2019]

### The islands, from 1998

Epstein bought Little St. James in 1998, through a company of his own, for $7.95 million. ### The Warnings: 1996, 1997, 2003 and 2005

The most important fact in this part is not something Epstein did. It is that the authorities were told, in writing, by a named complainant, nine and a half years before the FBI opened a case, and the file sat.

### Maria Farmer, 1996

Maria Farmer, an artist who worked for Epstein as an art scout, reported him to the New York Police Department on 29 August 1996 and was told to contact the FBI. On 3 September 1996 the Bureau logged her complaint and classified it as child pornography. [cite:guardian_farmer] [cite:garcia] The complaint, in its own words now public, says she was a professional artist who had photographed her sisters, aged twelve and sixteen, for her own work; that Epstein had stolen the photographs and was believed to have sold them; that he had asked her to photograph young girls at swimming pools; and that he had threatened to burn her house down if she told anyone. [cite:guardian_farmer] [cite:factually_farmer]

The FBI opened no investigation of Epstein until 23 May 2006, nine years and eight months later. Farmer has said she received no meaningful follow-up in all that time. [cite:garcia] The document proving she had reported was not released until December 2025, when the Justice Department published it under the Epstein Files Transparency Act; she identified herself to the *New York Times* and said she had waited thirty years and that now they could not call her a liar. In July 2025 she had sued the Justice Department, alleging that the FBI chose to do nothing with her complaint. [cite:guardian_farmer] On 23 December 2025 Representative Robert Garcia, the senior Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, asked the Justice Department's acting inspector general to investigate the Bureau's failure. [cite:garcia]

The book keeps the 1996 document separate from Farmer's later and fuller account. The complaint as released concerns stolen photographs, a request to photograph girls and a threat. Her account of her own assault, and her allegations of a wider scheme, come from her later lawsuit and public statements. She has also alleged recordings and a blackmail operation tied to a foreign intelligence service; no court has examined or corroborated that, and this book does not carry it. [cite:factually_farmer] What the 1996 document proves needs nothing added to it: the FBI was warned, by name, in writing, in 1996.

That summer Maria Farmer was also at Les Wexner's estate in Ohio. She alleges that Epstein sexually assaulted her there and that Wexner's security staff kept her on the property for twelve hours. [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] Her sister Annie, then sixteen, says she was abused by Epstein and Maxwell at the New Mexico ranch in 1996; twenty-five years later she was the one accuser at Maxwell's trial to testify under her own name. [cite:ep2_mediaite_ward] [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021]

### What this did to the people who reported

The structure here is worth stating without sentiment. When a complaint produces no action, the absence of action becomes evidence that the complaint was unfounded. ### What the Paper Proves: Logs, the Book and the "List"

Three kinds of document dominate public discussion of Epstein, and each is routinely asked to prove more than it can. Getting them right is not a technicality. It decides whether the strongest evidence in this case survives contact with a hostile reader.

### The flight logs

The logs are manifests kept by Epstein's pilots, recording who boarded which aircraft on which date. They are genuine documents, entered in litigation, and pilots testified about them at Maxwell's trial. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] They establish one thing: that a person was on a plane. They do not record why, what happened at the destination, how long anyone stayed or what anyone knew. Many people appear once. Some appear only as initials. Treating a line in a manifest as an accusation is the most common error in writing about this case, and it has let serious findings be dismissed by association with careless ones.

### The address book

A bound contact book of some two thousand entries has circulated in redacted and unredacted versions. It is the address book of a man who spent thirty years cultivating proximity to wealth, and its size is the natural product of that method. Being in a rich man's address book is not a fact about you. A different book was evidence at Maxwell's trial: a "black book" listing girls' contact details under the heading "massages". [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] The two are often confused. The first is a rolodex; the second was a working tool of the operation.

### The "client list"

No document answering the description of a client list has been produced by any court, agency or litigant. What exists are flight logs, address books, survivors' testimony naming specific people in specific proceedings, and millions of pages of investigative material, much of it still unreleased or redacted. [cite:ep1_rollcall_release2026] The absence of a single master list is not evidence that nothing else exists; it is evidence that the record has to be read document by document. A book that promised a list it could not produce would have staked its credibility on something outside its control. This one names people where a court record, sworn testimony or a charging document does, and says which.

### Why the discipline pays

A reader hostile to this material does not engage its strongest finding. They find the weakest name on the weakest document and use it to discard everything. If this book listed manifest entries as accusations, it would hand them that lever, and the 1996 complaint, Judge Marra's ruling, the immunity clause and the Maxwell verdict would go into the bin alongside it. Restraint here is not squeamishness. It is what makes the severe material survivable, and it is the survivors' evidence that it protects most.

### What this part established
Part Two asked whether Epstein could have operated alone. The court record answers no. A federal indictment describes staff who scheduled girls and girls paid to recruit other girls; a federal judge found that he directed others; a jury convicted the woman who recruited and groomed them. About 150 people were compensated from his estate, a floor and not a total. The houses where it happened had been bought years earlier with money whose source Part One could not find.

The record also shows when it could have been stopped. The FBI was told in writing in September 1996. A model went to the police in 1997. The two women who had already reported were cut from the first national magazine profile in 2003. The investigation that finally began, in 2005, came from a parent, and the federal agreement that ended it, examined in Part Eight, immunised four named women and every "potential co-conspirator" no one named. What remains to be traced is what paid for all of it, and who stayed close once everyone knew. Part Three follows the money.

## Part Three · Follow the Money
Part Three follows every documented dollar: where Epstein's money came from, which institutions held it and moved it, what they saw while they did, and who eventually paid for what it bought. The question is simple to state and hard to answer: is there a documented source for a fortune of more than half a billion dollars? Part One found none for the years before 1991. This part looks at the rest of his life, and at the banks and the small territory that, for very different reasons, ended up holding the evidence.

Money is the layer of this story where the record is strongest, because banks keep records and regulators can compel them. It is also where the pattern of this book is clearest. The systems built to notice suspicious money noticed. What they did next is the subject of this part.

### The Fortune, Source by Source

Two days before he died in August 2019, Epstein signed a will that placed his estate in a vehicle called "The 1953 Trust" and valued it at more than $577 million. The items are specific: Little St. James at more than $63 million and Great St. James at more than $22 million; the Manhattan house at $55.9 million; the New Mexico ranch at $17.2 million; Palm Beach at $12.4 million; Paris at $8.7 million; $56.5 million in cash; more than $112 million in equities; $14 million in fixed income; and $18.5 million in aircraft, cars and boats. [cite:cbs_epstein_will] His executors later revised the total to $634.8 million. [cite:foxbiz_estate_634m]

The outputs, in other words, are documented to the dollar. The question is what produced them.

That list contains two confirmed clients and half a billion dollars. No audited list of other clients has ever been produced. ### Why it matters to the architecture

Three features of Black's payments recur throughout this book. They came after the 2008 conviction, when Epstein was a registered sex offender and anyone could read why. They were very large and largely undocumented, which is the condition under which money can buy almost anything. And the bank that moved them said nothing until after Epstein was arrested again. Each feature is a choice made by someone with the power to make it otherwise, and none of those choices was illegal on its face. That combination, discretion that is lawful at every step and unaccountable in sum, is what this book means by architecture.

### The Banks: Detected, Then Kept

It is tempting to imagine that Epstein's money moved in the shadows. It did not. It moved through some of the largest and most heavily regulated banks in the world, each of which had systems designed to flag exactly the kind of activity his accounts produced. The systems worked. Staff raised warnings. What failed was what happened after the warnings, and the record of that failure is unusually complete, because regulators and litigants forced it into the open.

### JPMorgan Chase, 1998–2013

Epstein became a client of JPMorgan in 1998, the year he bought his island, and the bank kept him until 2013, through his 2006 arrest, his 2008 guilty plea and his registration as a sex offender. [cite:npr_jpm] [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023] Litigation later showed that employees had urged the bank to stop working with him and that some bank officials stayed in contact with him long after he was dropped. [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023] Weeks after his death in 2019, the bank filed reports with regulators flagging more than $1 billion in suspicious transactions involving him. [cite:ep3_sa_jpm_sars] In June 2023 it agreed to pay about $290 million to settle a class action brought by his victims, and in September 2023 it paid the US Virgin Islands $75 million. It admitted no liability in either case, and said it "deeply regrets any association with this man". [cite:npr_jpm] [cite:aj_jpm] [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023]

The bank also tried to place the liability on one of its own. It sued its former executive Jes Staley, who had managed the relationship, seeking to make him cover its losses and forfeit eight years of pay; the two settled on confidential terms. [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023] Staley had gone on to run Barclays. In 2023 Britain's Financial Conduct Authority banned him from senior roles, finding that a 2019 letter Barclays sent the regulator, which said he did not have a "close relationship" with Epstein, was misleading and that he had acted "recklessly and without integrity" in letting it be sent. In June 2025 a tribunal upheld the ban, cut his fine from £1.8 million to about £1.1 million and said he showed "no remorse". The regulator's lawyers said he and Epstein had stayed in contact, through Staley's daughter, until at least February 2017. [cite:ep3_staley_tribunal2025] Staley has said he regrets the friendship and denies knowing of the trafficking. [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023]

### Deutsche Bank, 2013–2018

In 2013, the year JPMorgan let Epstein go, Deutsche Bank took him on. His conviction was public record. In July 2020 New York's Department of Financial Services fined the bank $150 million, finding that it had failed to monitor his accounts despite knowing his history. The regulator found hundreds of transactions totalling millions of dollars that should have drawn scrutiny: payments to Russian models, to women's school fees, hotels and rent, directly to many women with Eastern European surnames, and to people publicly alleged to be his co-conspirators; more than $7 million in settlements and more than $6 million to law firms that appeared to cover legal costs for him and his alleged co-conspirators; and more than $800,000 in suspicious cash withdrawals over about four years. ### Detection, then override

The finding underneath the numbers is precise. These banks were not outwitted. Their anti-money-laundering systems generated the flags; their employees raised the warnings; the regulators later found the payments to young women in plain sight in the account records. The systems then produced no consequence, because the client was valuable and the people who could override the flags did. A system defeated by clever concealment needs better technology. A system defeated by its own override authority needs something else entirely, and only the first is an engineering problem. [cite:aj_jpm] [cite:ep3_cnbc_dfs_db2020]

The settlements then closed the question they had opened. A settlement without admission produces money for victims and no finding of fault, no precedent and no public account of what the institution concluded internally. Nearly half a billion dollars changed hands, and whether anything wrong was done remains, formally, unanswered.

### The Territory That Acted

Epstein's money and his conduct met in one place. In 1996 his firm moved to St. Thomas, in the US Virgin Islands, for the tax advantages the territory offered; in 1998 he bought Little St. James, in the same territory. [cite:legalclarity] [cite:ep1_nyt_wexner2019] His company Southern Trust later received a ten-year tax break from the territory's Economic Development Commission. [cite:viconsortium_southern_trust] The jurisdiction that gave his finances their offshore base was the same jurisdiction where much of the abuse alleged against him took place, and that is why, when someone finally sued the banks as a government rather than as a victim, it was the Virgin Islands.

### What the territory recovered

In December 2022 Epstein's estate agreed to pay the territory more than $105 million, plus half the proceeds from selling both his islands; the executors denied wrongdoing. [cite:ep3_fox_usvi_estate2022] In 2023 Leon Black paid the territory $62.5 million over how Epstein had used Black's money to fund his operations there. [cite:yahoo_black_170m] In September 2023 JPMorgan paid it $75 million: $30 million for charities, $25 million to strengthen law enforcement against human trafficking and $20 million in legal fees. The territory had sought at least $190 million, including a $150 million civil penalty. [cite:ep3_aj_usvi_jpm2023] The territorial attorney general alleged that Southern Trust was a shell that helped hide his operations. [cite:viconsortium_southern_trust]

### Who actually moved

The structural finding here is about scale, and it runs in the opposite direction from what one would expect. The federal Justice Department signed an agreement in 2008 immunising unnamed co-conspirators. The FBI sat on a written complaint for nearly a decade. Three banks settled without admitting anything. The government that brought a sovereign action against the largest bank in the country was a territory of roughly a hundred thousand people whose residents cannot vote for president. [cite:aj_jpm] [cite:npr_jpm] In this case, enforcement did not grow with power. It shrank with it, and the jurisdiction with the least political weight was the one with the least to lose by acting.

That should not be romanticised. The same territory had given Epstein's company its tax break, and the money it recovered went to its own government, its charities and its lawyers as well as to anti-trafficking work. But it is the clearest single example in this book of an accountability tool that worked: a government suing, under its own law, with its own power to compel documents, and producing a record no one else had produced. Part Thirteen asks what would happen to tools like this one if the people they constrain were allowed to remove them.

### What this part established
Part Three asked whether there is a documented source for Epstein's fortune. There is a documented list of sources, and it is short: a client who gave him a power of attorney and says he stole from him; a client who paid him $170 million, $100 million of it without a contract; an offshore company with a territorial tax break; and the returns on what all that bought. There is no documented business of the kind that would explain half a billion dollars, and there are still no records of the clients who would have had to exist if he managed money as he claimed.

What the money record does show, in more detail than any other part of this story, is the pattern this book is about. Every institution that held his money had the information and the systems to act. JPMorgan's employees warned; Deutsche Bank's regulator later found the payments to young women in its own records; a bank reported Black's $170 million only after the arrest. Each kept the client because the client was valuable, and each settled without admitting anything once victims and a small territory forced the matter. Part Four turns from the money to the people: who stayed close to him after 2008, when everyone knew.

## Part Four · Who Stayed
After June 2008 there was no longer any doubt about who Jeffrey Epstein was. He had pleaded guilty to a sex crime involving a minor and registered as a sex offender, and anyone could read why. Part Four is about the people who stayed close to him anyway, and about how to read their names.

It begins with the method, because without it this part would be a list, and lists are how this case has been misread for years. Every person here is placed at one of the four levels of evidence set out in the introduction, and the kind of contact the record shows is stated each time. The part then moves cluster by cluster: the president and his predecessor's family, a British prince, the men around Peter Thiel's fund and Palantir, a cabinet secretary and the executives who resigned, and finally the countries where the release of the files produced arrests. The full biographies of the people named here are in Part Five. The question Part Four asks is narrower: after everyone knew, who stayed, and what happened to them?

### Reading a Name

Put every name that circulates in connection with Epstein into a single list and it looks like one kind of thing. It is not. Read across the record and the list contains, in order of strength: one criminal conviction; one foreign criminal charge that ended in a death in custody; a handful of civil settlements without admission; a small number of sworn allegations; at least one sworn allegation that the accuser herself later withdrew; and several hundred names for which the record establishes contact and nothing else. [cite:marra] [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] [cite:ep2_aj_brunel2022] These are not the same object, and treating them as one is how the strong cases get discredited by the weak ones.

### The four levels

**Decided by a court.** The only criminal verdict against anyone other than Epstein is Ghislaine Maxwell's conviction on five counts in 2021. [cite:ep2_voa_maxwell2021] A civil settlement is a weaker kind of legal event: a case resolved by agreement, usually without admission, which is a documented fact and not a finding of liability. That this level is so small after two decades is itself one of the findings of this book.

**Sworn.** A named accuser has alleged specific conduct by a specific person, in a proceeding where lying carries criminal exposure. This is stronger than any claim in the press and weaker than a verdict. Most sworn allegations in this case were never tested at trial, because no trial took place, and both facts belong in any honest account of them.

**Documented contact.** Flight manifests, emails, photographs, calendar entries, payments and quotations establish that two people knew each other, and sometimes how well. They establish nothing about conduct. Most names sit here and nowhere else. That is still a real finding: it describes the shape of the network that surrounded a convicted sex offender, and who chose to stay in it.

**Not carried.** A claim that a named person took part in a crime, with no court record, sworn testimony or case-file document behind it, is not written up as "disputed". It is left out, because "disputed" implies two credibly documented sides. This is the strictest rule in the book, and it applies however widely a name circulates.

### Why the caution is earned

One episode shows why the rule matters. In litigation, Virginia Giuffre named the lawyer Alan Dershowitz. He denied it and countersued. On 8 November 2022 the two settled, both withdrawing their claims; their joint statement said no money changed hands, and Giuffre said: "I now recognize I may have made a mistake in identifying Mr. Dershowitz." [cite:ep4_cbs_dershowitz2022] A sworn allegation from the most credible accuser in the case was withdrawn by the accuser herself, in a subject where dozens of names circulate on far less. This book reports allegations as allegations, never as findings, and it carries nothing against anyone on the strength of the withdrawn one.

The rule protects the survivors as much as the people named. Every careless accusation that collapses gives a hostile reader a reason to discard the sworn testimony beside it, and the sworn testimony is what convicted Maxwell.

### The people who are not named

One group is missing from almost every list, and it is the most consequential. The 2008 federal agreement promised not to prosecute "any potential co-conspirators" of Epstein; it named four women, and left the rest of the category open. [cite:ep2_cbs_coconspirators2019] A federal judge later found that Epstein worked in concert with others and directed others to obtain girls. [cite:marra] Those others exist as a judicial finding and as a category in a signed federal document. Almost none of them exist as names. In August 2019, five days after Epstein's death, FBI agents in New York circulated an internal list of eight alleged co-conspirators; the reporting on it has made three of those names public: Maxwell, the modelling agent Jean-Luc Brunel and Les Wexner. [cite:ep4_npr_wexner_fbi2026] The largest gap in this network map is the group the government agreed in writing not to charge.

### Donald Trump

The record on Donald Trump and Jeffrey Epstein has two parts that are almost always run together. One is a social relationship in the 1990s and early 2000s. The other is what Trump has done with the files since he returned to office. They need different evidence, and this section keeps them apart.

### The relationship

The two men moved in the same Palm Beach and Manhattan circles for about fifteen years, and the association is documented in photographs, footage and Trump's own words. In a 2002 profile in *New York* magazine he called Epstein "a terrific guy" who was "a lot of fun to be with", and said Epstein liked beautiful women as much as he did, "many of them on the younger side". [cite:ep4_nymag2002] The quotation is genuine. It establishes a friendship and a characterisation. It is documented contact, not conduct.

The files add specific dates. In an email of 7 January 2020, released by the Justice Department in December 2025, a federal prosecutor in New York wrote that flight records showed Trump had flown on Epstein's jet eight times in the 1990s, at least four times with Ghislaine Maxwell aboard, and once with only Epstein, Trump and a twenty-year-old woman whose name was redacted. The email alleges no crime. [cite:ep4_sbs_trump_flights] The Justice Department said some of the documents released contained "untrue and sensationalist claims" against Trump that had been sent to the FBI before the 2020 election; it released tip-line calls mentioning him without saying who made them or whether anyone found them credible. [cite:ep4_sbs_trump_flights]

In November 2025 House Oversight Democrats released emails from among 23,000 documents provided by Epstein's estate. In one, dated 2 April 2011, Epstein told Maxwell that "the dog that hasn't barked is Trump" and that Trump had "spent hours" at his house with a person whose name was redacted, whom the Democrats described as a victim. In another, in 2019, Epstein told the writer Michael Wolff that Trump "knew about the girls as he asked Ghislaine to stop." [cite:ep4_csm_emails2025] These are Epstein's words, written by a man with his own reasons, and they are evidence of what Epstein wrote, not of what Trump knew. The White House called the release a selective leak meant to "create a fake narrative", said the redacted person in the 2011 email was Virginia Giuffre, and said Trump had "kicked Jeffrey Epstein out of his club decades ago" for mistreating female employees. Trump denies any knowledge of Epstein's crimes. [cite:ep4_csm_emails2025]

Giuffre herself was recruited at Mar-a-Lago, Trump's club, in 2000. In a 2016 deposition, unsealed in 2024, she said: "I don't think Donald Trump participated in anything." She said she had never seen him take part, though she had heard he had been at Epstein's house. [cite:ep2_bi_giuffre_trump2024] No sworn allegation against Trump appears in the court record this book has examined, and none is carried here. The relationship broke down in the early-to-mid 2000s, before Epstein's 2006 arrest, though accounts of why it ended differ. [cite:legalclarity]

### The office

The second part of the record concerns not the 1990s but 2025 and 2026, and it is about power rather than friendship. On 19 November 2025 Trump signed the Epstein Files Transparency Act, which had passed the House 427 to 1 and the Senate by unanimous consent. ### Bill Clinton and Hillary Clinton

The Clintons are a test of the rule that one person's record does not pass to another by marriage. They are treated here as two people, with two records.

### Bill Clinton

When Epstein was arrested in July 2019, Bill Clinton's spokesman issued an account of the relationship. It said Clinton took four trips on Epstein's plane in 2002 and 2003, one to Europe, one to Asia and two to Africa, some connected with his foundation's work, with staff, foundation supporters and his Secret Service detail on every leg. It said Clinton met Epstein once at his Harlem office in 2002 and made one brief visit to Epstein's New York apartment with a staff member and his security detail present; that he had never been to Little St. James, the New Mexico ranch or the Florida house; and that he had not spoken to Epstein "in well over a decade". [cite:ep4_ap_clinton_statement2019]

The documents do not settle the count. Flight logs published by Gawker in 2015 suggested more than a dozen flights; Fox News reported in 2016 that they showed at least twenty-six. The *New York Times* noted that it was unclear how many individual flights made up each of the four trips, so the figures may describe the same travel counted differently. [cite:ep4_politifact_clinton2020] Photographs released in December 2025 show him with Epstein, Maxwell and others; many are undated and partly redacted, with no context for where or when they were taken. [cite:ep4_rnz_clinton]

One account does conflict with his. In court papers later unsealed, Virginia Giuffre said she once saw Clinton on Epstein's island with two young girls from New York. She did not accuse him of any wrongdoing. His statement says he never visited the island. [cite:ep4_politifact_clinton2020] The book records both, side by side, and resolves neither. A woman who was on one of the flights, Chauntae Davies, who accuses Epstein of raping her, has described Clinton as "a complete gentleman". [cite:ep4_politifact_clinton2020]

No accuser has alleged misconduct by Bill Clinton in any proceeding this book has examined, and no charging document names him. [cite:ep4_rnz_clinton] Clinton was deposed by the House Oversight Committee on 27 February 2026, and was reported as saying, "I did nothing wrong." [cite:ep4_ap_hillary2026] The documented travel, whatever its count, is a real finding about who surrounded Epstein in those years, and the book carries it as documented contact.

The timing of the December 2025 release raised a separate question. The first tranche of photographs featured Clinton prominently, and White House accounts posted them with captions such as "Oh My". [cite:ep4_rnz_clinton] That question belongs to Part Nine.

### Hillary Clinton

Hillary Clinton was deposed by the House Oversight Committee, behind closed doors, in Chappaqua on 26 February 2026. ### Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor

The case against the man formerly known as Prince Andrew, Duke of York, is the clearest example in this book of the difference between legal consequence and every other kind. In his case legal consequence for the allegation never came; the consequences that did come arrived by other routes, and late.

### The allegation

Virginia Giuffre alleged, in sworn litigation, that she was trafficked to Andrew and had sexual encounters with him on three occasions, the first when she was seventeen. She sued him in federal court in New York in 2021. He has consistently denied the allegations, said he had no recollection of meeting her, and questioned the authenticity of the 2001 photograph that shows him with his arm around her at Ghislaine Maxwell's house in London, with Maxwell behind them. [cite:ep2_ap_giuffre2025] It was the same structure as the bank settlements in Part Three: money moved, no finding was entered, and the question stayed formally open.

### Reading the difference fairly

The asymmetry is striking, and it has a partial explanation that the book must state before drawing any conclusion. The foreign cases are almost all about state secrets and corruption: officials accused of passing government information to Epstein or of taking money from him. Those are offences in which the documents themselves can be the evidence, and in which the released emails go straight to the elements of the crime. The American names, for the most part, appear in the files as social and business contacts, and contact is not a crime.

That explains part of the gap. It does not explain all of it. It does not explain why the FBI's own 2019 list of alleged co-conspirators produced no public account of what was done with it. It does not explain why a federal agreement immunised every "potential co-conspirator" without naming most of them. And it does not explain why no American agency has said publicly that it looked at the January 2026 release as evidence at all. Those are the questions of Parts Eight and Nine.

### What this part established
Part Four asked who stayed close to Epstein after 2008, and what happened to them. The answer from the record is that a great many powerful people stayed, at every level of contact from a single email to a $170 million relationship, and that what happened to them depended less on what they did than on where they lived and what kind of offence the documents could prove. A British prince and a British peer were arrested over state secrets. Three Norwegians were charged with corruption. In the United States, people resigned.

The part also showed why the names have to be read one at a time. The list that circulates contains a conviction, a death in custody, settlements without admission, sworn allegations, a withdrawn allegation and hundreds of contacts, and only the method of this book keeps them from collapsing into one another. The most consequential names are still missing: the co-conspirators a federal agreement immunised without naming, and the people on an FBI list of eight whose names this book has not found in any published record. Part Five gives every person named so far a full biography, rebuilt from the record, so the reader can see who they were before Epstein, how they came to him, and what the documents actually show.
