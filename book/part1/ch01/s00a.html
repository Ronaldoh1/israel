How the Story Is Taught

Most Americans meet this history long before they meet its documents. It reaches them in a sequence: a textbook, then a teacher, then a museum, a film and the evening news. Each pass reinforces the one before it. The version that results is usually remembered as the story itself, the true account of an ancient people returning home. What it actually is, on inspection, is one framing of a documented modern political project, and the framing and the project are not the same thing. This section is an inventory rather than an accusation. It sets out where the story is taught, who pays for which parts of the teaching, what the teaching leaves out, and how the same test applies to the textbooks on the other side.

The first stage is the classroom. Four publishers, Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, Pearson, McGraw-Hill and Holt McDougal, reach the great majority of American world-history students in grades 6 through 12. How their books cover Israel and Palestine has been studied in the peer-reviewed literature: Sean Yom examined the framing of American world-history textbooks in 2013, and Nurit Peled-Elhanan published a mirror study of Israeli school textbooks in 2012. [cite:peled_elhanan_textbooks] The pattern these studies describe is not falsehood. It is emphasis. Some events are named: the Holocaust, the 1948 declaration, the 1967 war. Others are compressed: the Nakba, the 1953 CIA coup in Iran that reshaped the region, the Sabra and Shatila massacre of 1982. Some words are used, such as "conflict," "war" and "unrest." Others tend to be avoided. One is "occupation," a status that, in the research behind this book, international law has applied for 59 years. Another is "apartheid," the subject of published reports since 2021 by B'Tselem, [cite:btselem_apartheid_2021] Human Rights Watch [cite:hrw_israel_apartheid] and Amnesty International. [cite:amnesty_israel_apartheid] The effect, on this account, is that a student can reach adulthood with a vocabulary and a set of reflexes but without the record itself.

"Emphasis, not lies" can sound like a small distinction. A concrete case shows how large it can be. A textbook can say, accurately and in one clean sentence, that Jewish settlers purchased land in Palestine. A fuller account would add what this library's own research on the land-purchase mechanism had to assemble from scratch, because the survey textbooks did not supply it. The Ottoman government passed a Palestine-specific law in 1881–82 barring land purchase by foreign Jews. In 1892 it required imperial approval even for purchases by Ottoman Jewish citizens. Buyers worked around these rules through Ottoman-citizen intermediaries and through the consular protections known as the "capitulations," and in the 1890s the United States State Department found itself in a dispute over Russian Jewish emigrants who had naturalized as Americans in order to claim consular protection for land purchases in Palestine. Entire villages of tenant farmers were evicted as a result of such sales. None of this is obscure. It appears in Wikipedia's article on Jewish land purchase in Palestine and in the United States government's own published diplomatic archive. It does not appear in the four publishers' coverage. Both versions "cover the topic." Only the second describes the laws, the loopholes and the institutions through which the process actually ran, and a reader who knows only the first has no way to ask the next question, because nothing signals that there is one.

That example has limits, and they should be stated. It shows one significant mechanism missing from mainstream coverage. It does not show that every omission is this consequential, and it does not show that the omission was deliberate rather than the ordinary product of space limits in a survey course. What it does show is that emphasis alone, without one false sentence, can remove a documented mechanism from what a reader is able to know.

Compression works on people as well as events. Jews are not one lineage. The documented traditions include the Ashkenazi of Central and Eastern Europe, the Sephardi of Iberia and the diaspora after 1492, the Mizrahi of the Middle East and North Africa (including Iraq, Yemen, Morocco, Iran, Syria and Egypt), the Beta Israel of Ethiopia, the Bene Israel of India, the Jews of Kaifeng in China, the Bukharan Jews of Central Asia, and Georgian, Yemenite and Karaite communities. No one disputes that any of these are Jewish. The point is that they exist, and that American K-12 material typically folds the whole tradition into a single narrative centered on Ashkenazi experience, with Mizrahi and Sephardi Jews appearing briefly or not at all. Ella Shohat's 1988 essay documented the same asymmetry inside Israel. [cite:shohat_sephardim_israel] The compression has a consequence. Political Zionism, organized in 1897, emerged in specifically European Jewish settings, and an education that leaves out the non-European traditions makes it easier to treat "Zionism" and "all Jews" as one thing, a conflation the record does not support. The lineages themselves are treated at length elsewhere in this volume.

Why the textbook chain carries no correction is a question of structure, and each link in it is a matter of law or record. Federal law, at 20 U.S.C. § 1232a, bars the Department of Education from exercising any "direction, supervision, or control" over school curriculum. [cite:usc_20_1232a] That is a legal prohibition, not an oversight gap. Approval of textbooks falls to the states, and even there the review process places responsibility for accuracy on the publishers, who certify their own books. Virginia's textbook review documentation says so directly. [cite:va_textbook_review] In 2015, after a widely publicized error, Texas considered adding independent university historians to check textbooks for factual accuracy, and the state board voted the proposal down. [cite:tx_board_rejects_factcheck] The third link is ownership. A major textbook publisher is a private company in a market with no ownership screening related to accuracy, so anyone may acquire one, for any reason and with any history.

Once an unchecked claim enters a textbook, it can also compound. Students and later writers meet it in good faith and cite it; others cite them; and each new citation looks like independent confirmation when it is the same uncorrected origin repeated. The cartoonist Randall Munroe coined a name for this sequence, "citogenesis." [cite:xkcd_978_citogenesis] A tracked case shows it at work: a false claim about the history of health economics began on Wikipedia with no real source behind it, was repeated across several apparently independent articles, and was finally cited as settled fact by a newspaper columnist who had no way of knowing that the whole trail led back to one unverified starting point. [cite:cornell_citogenesis_blog] No one in that chain acted in bad faith. Each assumed that someone earlier had done the checking.

The mechanism is general. Any well-funded owner of a major textbook or reference publisher inherits the same structural gap, on any subject, not because of a conspiracy but because no layer of the system performs an accuracy check. Each link is legal and, taken alone, unremarkable. Chained together, they describe a system in which the absence of a check is a matter of design rather than neglect.

The ownership link has a documented example. The story travels through the press, and the ownership of the press is a matter of record. Robert Maxwell was born Jan Ludvik Hoch in Czechoslovakia in 1923. He survived the Holocaust, which killed most of his family, served in the British Army and anglicized his name after the war. Between 1984 and 1991 he assembled Mirror Group Newspapers, publisher of the Daily Mirror, one of Britain's highest-circulation tabloids, which reached millions of readers a day; the academic publisher Pergamon Press; the language-education company Berlitz; and Macmillan Publishing, one of the largest textbook and reference publishers used in American schools, which he acquired in 1988 for $2.6 billion. His relationship with Israeli intelligence is described in Seymour Hersh's The Samson Option, [cite:hersh_samson] in the work of Tom Bower, whose biography Maxwell: The Outsider appeared in 1988, [cite:bower_maxwell] and in Gordon Thomas and Martin Dillon's Robert Maxwell, Israel's Superspy of 2002; the name Bergman also appears among the sources on the point. The biographies, working from different sources, describe a relationship with Mossad that reportedly included helping to sell software from Israeli technology firms to intelligence services around the world. The exact mechanism is contested in its specifics. The broader pattern, a well-connected publisher with intelligence relationships on several sides, including British ties and alleged Soviet-era contacts through Pergamon's dealings with Eastern Bloc academic publishing, appears in more than one of these books rather than resting on any single one.

Maxwell died on 5 November 1991, found floating near his yacht, the Lady Ghislaine, off the Canary Islands. The official Spanish autopsy and a later Israeli examination both concluded that he fell overboard, with drowning the probable cause. Whether that was an accident, a suicide amid mounting financial exposure or something else was never settled to the satisfaction of every investigator, and this book leaves the uncertainty as uncertainty. He received an Israeli state funeral on the Mount of Olives, with six intelligence chiefs in attendance. Days after his death it emerged that he had taken hundreds of millions of pounds from his companies' pension funds to cover debt. His daughter Ghislaine was convicted in 2021 of sex trafficking with Jeffrey Epstein, a later turn the record captures but does not explain. His full story is told in [scene:s16|Robert Maxwell and the press empire].

What the Maxwell case shows must be stated as carefully as what it does not. It shows that a man who owned major British media and a major American textbook publisher is described, in independently researched biographies, as having ties to Israeli intelligence. It does not show that any Daily Mirror story about Israel or Zionism was ordered by an intelligence service, and it does not show that Maxwell altered any Macmillan textbook. No primary source located for this book makes either claim, and this book does not make them. The point is structural. Ownership is a documented fact; editorial influence is a separate and much harder claim that would need its own evidence, case by case. What the case establishes is that the opportunity existed in a chain designed with no one checking the checker.

Beyond the drift of editorial emphasis there is a second, different thing: a formal, state-funded operation built to shape this same narrative. Its name is hasbara, Hebrew for "explanation." It was formalized as Zionist institutional policy before the Second World War, a step credited to Nahum Sokolow, and it has served continuously since as Israel's own name for its public diplomacy and propaganda. It is not a term invented by critics; it is the word the apparatus uses for itself. According to reporting on the 2026 budget, Israel quadrupled its public-diplomacy budget to $730 million, and the foreign minister described the matter as an "existential issue." [cite:nuclearnews_730m_budget] Israel's Institute for National Security Studies mapped, in a single research pass after October 2023, more than 120 civilian "war rooms," 40 organizations building hasbara technology and 100 databases, which is the apparatus documenting its own scale rather than an outside estimate. A documented influencer scheme has reportedly offered roughly $7,000 per pro-Israel social-media post. Many still-circulating talking points have been traced to a named source, Frank Luntz's 2009 "Global Language Dictionary," commissioned by The Israel Project. [cite:medium_luntz_kryptonite_hasbara] The contracts, filings and budgets of this operation are examined in [scene:s00a3|coordination on the record].

The difference between the two mechanisms matters. Textbook emphasis is closer to organic drift: many editors, trained alike, reaching for the same framing without coordination. Hasbara has a name, a budget and a stated goal. Neither explains all pro-Israel opinion. Many people hold pro-Israel views independent of any funded campaign, just as many hold critical views independent of any funded campaign. What is documented is the existence and the scale of the organized effort, not a claim that it accounts for every view.

For a subset of Jewish students, the story is reinforced twice more, through two separate programs at two different ages. The two should not be merged into one organization, which would overstate what is documented. The first is the day school. Jewish day schools set their own curricula, and disclosed philanthropic funding, from the Jim Joseph Foundation among others, specifically supports Israel-centered educational content, including elementary-level pilot programs. Comparable day-school systems exist in other countries. The schools are expensive. According to SAPIR journal's 2025 reporting, average annual tuition nationally is about $24,000, and runs from $25,000 to $50,000 a year in cities such as New York; according to reporting in JNS and the Cleveland Jewish News, a full K-12 day-school education for one child can cost about half a million dollars before college. In 2026 the Israeli government approved a NIS 100 million ($33.4 million) budget, matched by philanthropic donations through the Jewish Federations of North America, to raise day-school enrollment in the United States and Canada, a combined $66.7 million. A further NIS 200 million plan, about $67 million, was approved unanimously by the Israeli cabinet in 2026 with a focus on enrolling more American Jewish children in day schools, a direct government line for Diaspora education. The sources describe these as separate plans; whether they overlap is not established in the material behind this book. Behind both stands the broader base of the Jewish Federations of North America, whose combined endowments are described as holding "tens of billions" in assets and whose annual campaigns raise "hundreds of millions."

The second program is Birthright Israel, also known as Taglit. Founded in 1999, it offers a free ten-day trip to Israel to young adults aged 18 to 26. Benjamin Netanyahu made the first Israeli government funding guarantee in 1998, during his first term. More than 800,000 young adults have taken the trip. [cite:wiki_birthright_israel] Roughly 27 percent of the funding comes directly from the Israeli government; about 67 percent comes from individual donors, among them Sheldon Adelson, who gave more than $400 million before his death in 2021; the remainder comes from the Claims Conference and other sources, including Jewish Federations. More than $2 billion has been spent over the program's history. The Birthright Israel Foundation's own figures put current annual worldwide programming at $172 million to $175 million, up from a reported $92.9 million budget in 2023. Security has historically been its single most expensive line, at $72 million. An Ernst & Young study found that in its first 13 years the program brought more than $825 million into the Israeli economy through tourism, hotels and meals, separate from its own budget.

The program is international. Participants come from roughly 68 to 70 countries, and the foundation itself presents the trip as "part of a global movement," naming Argentina, Brazil, Mexico, France, the United Kingdom, South Africa and India among them. About 80 percent of participants come from the United States and Canada; the remaining fifth is spread across dozens of countries. [cite:birthright_global_movement] Birthright has also served as a model that heritage-trip programs in Hungary, Greece and Macedonia were built to copy.

Its effect has been measured by its own commissioned research. A 2025 Brandeis University study, funded by Birthright, found that the year's participants came back "much more connected to Israel, and much more likely to identify with the political right," and more likely to "provide a counterargument to claims that Israel is committing genocide." [cite:intercept_birthright_2025] This is the program's own research describing its own effect, not an outside accusation. It measured an average across a cohort, not a uniform outcome for every participant, and it describes only the subset of students who attend day school and later take the trip, not Jewish education as a whole.

The cumulative product of textbook, press, museum, film, news and, for some, school and trip is described here as a reflex rather than a conclusion. A person who has passed through the sequence reaches adulthood with associations already in place: Israel with democracy, Palestinians with terrorism, criticism with antisemitism, the conflict with antiquity. These are associations, not findings the record supports. The research behind this section points to Daniel Kahneman's work on fast, intuitive "System 1" thinking, in which emotional associations bypass analytical review. The practical test is simple. When the reflex fires against something the documents show, the sequence is doing its work, and noticing that is where examination begins. How the same associations were first put before the American public, through the themes of refuge, the Bible and democracy, is the subject of [scene:sell_s01|how Israel was sold to Americans in 1948].

A standard applied to one side's schoolbooks must be applied to the other's. The familiar claim runs in two forms: that Palestinian children are taught to hate, or that their textbooks are fine. A study commissioned by the European Union tested the question. The Georg Eckert Institute for International Textbook Research analyzed about 200 Palestinian Authority textbooks and teachers' guides released in 2020, and published its findings in 2021. It concluded that the books "adhere to UNESCO standards" on human-rights education while expressing "a narrative of resistance" and "antagonism towards Israel." It found some content with "escalatory potential," including in science, technology and mathematics subjects, and some antisemitic depictions. It found no direct calls for violence against Israelis. [cite:gei_faq] The Institute's director later told the European Parliament that books containing antisemitism or incitement do not comply with UNESCO standards. [cite:hansard_textbooks]

Each side reads the same study as proof of its case. IMPACT-se, a monitoring group whose reports the European Parliament has cited, says the study confirms systemic incitement. [cite:impactse_gei] That reading draws its strength from the study's documented findings of antagonism, escalatory content and antisemitic depictions, and from the director's statement that such material falls short of UNESCO standards. Others, including European analysts, say the study largely disproved exaggerated claims, and they note that it did not examine Israeli textbooks, which other researchers have also criticized. [cite:eumep_textbooks] That reading draws its strength from the study's finding of UNESCO-standard human-rights content and its finding of no direct calls for violence. Peled-Elhanan's work on Israeli schoolbooks, mentioned above, is one such criticism. Read as a whole, the study supports neither headline. Problem content exists and is documented; a curriculum of incitement to violence is not what the study found.

Several things remain unsettled. The research behind this section establishes what the four American publishers emphasize and omit, but not why: whether through deliberate choice or the ordinary compression of a survey course. It establishes Maxwell's ownership and the biographies' account of his intelligence ties, but not that he used either to shape a single story or textbook, and it does not resolve how he died. It measures Birthright's effect on one cohort through a study the program paid for, and has no independent study to set beside it. It reports the hasbara budget and the influencer payments through secondary reporting, not the budget documents themselves. And the European study covered one side's textbooks only. The method that follows from all of this is the one this book uses throughout: name the source, say who made it and who paid for it, separate what a document shows from what it is said to show, and apply the same test to both sides.
