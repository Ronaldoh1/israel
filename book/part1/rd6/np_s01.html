How the Flight Was Explained, 1948–1988

Once the war ended, the question of why so many Palestinian Arabs had left their homes became a political argument as much as a historical one, because the answer seemed to settle who was responsible for the refugees. For more than a decade the account that prevailed in the West was that they had left voluntarily, on the orders of Arab leaders, and that this had been established at the time. The record shows a slower and more complicated history: some of the war's events were reported within days, the explanation for the mass flight that took hold was not the one the documents supported, and the correction came in stages, the last and weightiest of them from Israel's own archives forty years later.

Parts of what happened were known almost at once. The killings at Deir Yassin in April 1948 were reported in Western newspapers within days, and they were documented by the delegate of the Red Cross who entered the village. [cite:w_morris_birth] News of the massacre spread through Arab Palestine. The Israeli historian Benny Morris, writing four decades later, records it as a major accelerant of flight. [cite:w_morris_birth] The Red Cross's place in that moment, before any international human-rights treaty existed, is part of [scene:ud_s01|the week of December 1948].

Objection in the West was public, and some of the most pointed came from prominent Jews. On 4 December 1948 a group that included Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and Sidney Hook published a letter in The New York Times warning against Menachem Begin's new Herut party. The letter described Deir Yassin and called the party fascist in its methods. [cite:w_arendt_letter] It is a documented Jewish objection, made at the time and printed in America's paper of record. It also shows that the facts of Deir Yassin were not hidden in 1948; what remained in contest was the larger question of why the flight as a whole had happened.

On that larger question an explanation took hold that ran the other way. Arab leaders, it held, had broadcast orders by radio telling civilians to leave. In the 1950s this became the standard explanation in Western and Israeli official accounts. Its political effect was clear: it placed responsibility for the refugee problem on the Arab states. The strongest form of the account rested on a real point that later research would confirm in part, that some evacuations were ordered by Arab commanders. Its weakness was that the general claim, a broadcast order to an entire population, was a claim about a record that existed and could be checked.

The broadcasts had been monitored. In 1959 the Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi argued from Arab sources that no such general order had been given. [cite:w_childers] In May 1961 the Irish writer Erskine Childers published "The Other Exodus" in The Spectator. He reported that he had examined the British and American monitoring records of Arab broadcasts in 1948, described in another summary as the BBC's and American records, and had found no evacuation orders; what he found were appeals to stay. [cite:w_childers] The article set off a long published dispute, with claims and counterclaims running for weeks in the magazine's letters pages. [cite:w_childers] The exchange did not settle the matter in public opinion. Childers's test was of one kind of evidence, the broadcasts; it could not by itself show what had driven people from particular towns and villages.

That evidence lay in Israeli military and state files. In 1988 Benny Morris published The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem, drawn from those newly opened archives. He concluded that the main causes of flight were military attack, expulsion and fear. In some places local Arab commanders had ordered the evacuation of women and children, but there was no general Arab order to leave. [cite:w_morris_birth] Later editions of the book found more expulsion orders. [cite:w_morris_birth]

The three tests did not cover the same ground, and the order in which they came matters. Khalidi and Childers answered the specific claim about broadcasts. Morris answered the larger question of causes, and he did so from the files of the Israeli state. The documents that corrected the early account were therefore not an adversary's claims but Israel's own.

Where this leaves each side's case can be stated fairly. The account that the refugees left on orders is no longer defended by mainstream scholarship in its general form. [cite:w_morris_birth] What survives of it is real but limited: Morris's own research found local evacuation orders by Arab commanders in some places, and he named fear, alongside attack and expulsion, among the main causes. The Palestinian account, that people were driven out by attack and expulsion, found strong support in the Israeli archives, and later editions of Morris's work added to the record of expulsion orders. [cite:w_morris_birth]

What the record does not settle is intent. Historians still disagree about intent and planning, an argument that turns on the military plan discussed in [scene:pd_s01|Plan Dalet]. The material here establishes the causes of flight that the archives show; it does not resolve the question of design. It also does not explain, beyond the sequence of dates, why the early account held for so long. What it does show is the length of the gap: the explanation that took hold in the 1950s was checked against the broadcasts only in 1959 and 1961, and against Israel's own archives only in 1988.
