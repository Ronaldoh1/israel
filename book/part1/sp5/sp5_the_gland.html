The Gland in the Middle of the Head

In letters of 1640 and again in The Passions of the Soul of 1649, René Descartes called the pineal gland "the principal seat of the soul". He chose it because it is the only part of the brain that is single rather than paired: we see one thing with two eyes and think one thought at a time, so the impressions must meet in one place. [cite:cn_sep_pineal] The anatomy soon turned against him. In 1664 Thomas Willis argued that animals with little imagination or memory have a large pineal too, and in 1669 Nicolaus Steno showed that the gland is not suspended in the ventricles as Descartes had assumed. [cite:cn_sep_pineal]

The gland's actual work was found three centuries later. In 1958 Aaron Lerner at Yale isolated its hormone and named it melatonin. Julius Axelrod and Richard Wurtman showed in the 1960s that the gland works as a biological clock, releasing melatonin in response to light and darkness. [cite:sp_nlm_pineal] The light-sensing role has a deeper history: the tuatara of New Zealand has a parietal "third eye" on top of its head, with a rudimentary lens, which is not used for sight and is thought to track day and night for the hormones. [cite:sp_brit_thirdeye] The modern image of the pineal as a third eye draws on that biology.

The spirit molecule

The most popular modern claim is that the pineal gland makes DMT, the psychedelic in ayahuasca, and releases it at birth, death and in visions. The researcher Rick Strassman put the idea forward and later acknowledged that it was a hypothesis. [cite:sp_sd_dmt] In 2019 a team led by Jimo Borjigin at the University of Michigan reported in Scientific Reports that rat brains contain DMT at levels similar to other monoamine neurotransmitters, made by neurons not only in the pineal gland but also in the neocortex and the hippocampus. Borjigin, who had studied the pineal for years, said she had never heard of the claim before a documentary made it. The study was in rats; human brains have not been tested in the same way. [cite:sp_sd_dmt]

A second claim is that fluoride "calcifies" the pineal and closes the third eye. A study of eleven elderly human cadavers, published in 2001, found fluoride in the calcified pineal at levels comparable to teeth, rising with the gland's calcium, which is expected since fluoride binds to calcium. A review of the evidence found "a dearth of clinical evidence" that fluoride harms the human pineal, and rated the claim unsupported while calling for more research. [cite:sp_sf_fluoride]
