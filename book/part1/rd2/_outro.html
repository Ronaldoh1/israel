By the end of 1922, and in some parts only during 1923, the promise of 1917 rested on several layers, each laid by different hands and each holding something the others did not.

The legal layer is the most settled. A letter of policy about land Britain did not then rule acquired force through a sequence: the League Covenant, in force from 10 January 1920; the allocation of the "A" mandates at San Remo on 25 April 1920; and the Mandate itself, approved conditionally by the League Council on 24 July 1922, with Balfour's words in its preamble. [cite:rs_a22_frus] [cite:w_balfour_decl] Its articles gave Britain "full powers of legislation and of administration," made the Mandatory responsible for securing the national home, required it to facilitate Jewish immigration, and recognized the Zionist Organization as the Jewish agency. [cite:rs_md_un_origins] [cite:rs_md_avalon] Two edges remain unsettled. The League recorded the Mandate as in force on 29 September 1923, a date John Quigley disputes. [cite:rs_md_lon1923] [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] And whether it kept Article 22's rule that the communities' wishes be "a principal consideration" in the selection of the Mandatory is argued both ways, by the Palestine Arab Delegation and the United Nations study on one side and the League's own 1945 study on the other, as [scene:blg_s01|the account of Article 22] shows. [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700] [cite:rs_a22_un_origins] [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] No court or League ruling decided it.

The administrative layer was built by Herbert Samuel. His Supreme Moslem Sharia Council Order of 20 December 1921 gave a new council control of the endowments, the Sharia judges and the appointment of Muftis, and its president from 9 January 1922 was Amin al-Husseini, whom Samuel had made Mufti in May 1921 after Storrs, at Samuel's request, secured a rival's withdrawal. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order] [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] Whether that was an overridden vote or a bid for calm is argued in [scene:rd2_mufti|the account of the appointment]. The political counterpart failed. The Order in Council of August 1922 offered a Legislative Council of ten officials and twelve elected members, eight Muslim, two Christian and two Jewish, which by Philip Mattar's reckoning gave Arabs 43 percent of the seats while they formed 88 percent of the population. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] [cite:rs_lc_mattar] After the boycott, an advisory council and an "Arab Agency" were also refused, and by the end of 1923 the High Commissioner governed with British officials alone. [cite:rs_lc_albright]

The Zionist layer joined legal recognition to institutions of its own. Keren Hayesod, founded on 24 December 1920 with a five-year target of £25,000,000 and open to non-Zionist donors, made fundraising permanent; an American tour in November 1921 raised nearly $5 million, after a fight that ended with Weizmann's centralized model prevailing over Brandeis's. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc168] The Haganah, founded in June 1920 as the Jewish Legion shrank from over 5,000 men to a few hundred, counted no more than a few hundred volunteers through the 1920s. [cite:rs_hg_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] Immigration continued under rules Britain tightened after the 1921 riots: 7,844 Jewish immigrants in 1922, in a country whose October census counted 83,794 Jews among 757,182 people. [cite:rs_ta_report1922] Against this stood the limits Britain set: the June 1922 White Paper's test of "economic absorptive capacity," its statement that Palestine would not become "as Jewish as England is English," and the September memorandum that switched the national-home articles off in Transjordan. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] [cite:rs_md_transjordan] The Zionist Executive accepted the White Paper, Weizmann judging a Jewish majority still possible; Jabotinsky built a movement on rejecting the separation. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_whitepaper] [cite:sunnafiles_jordan]

The Palestinian layer was a political movement without a formal channel. Seven congresses met between 1919 and 1928; the third, in December 1920, created the Arab Executive under Musa Kazim al-Husseini, and its delegation in London demanded on 21 February 1922 a "national independent Government," citing Article 22. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700] The Colonial Office answered that such a government "would preclude the fulfilment of the pledge." [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] On one reading, each side's strongest case turns on that sentence. For the Arab leadership, a council built on the Mandate made the majority a minority in its own legislature; for Britain, an elected Arab majority could undo a public pledge. The boycott resolved by the Fifth Congress at Nablus in August 1922 held: at most 213 of 809 secondary electors were chosen. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] It prevented the council; it won no recognition.

The regional and American layers framed the rest. At Cairo in March 1921 Britain gave Iraq to Faisal and Transjordan to Abdullah, while their father declined to ratify Versailles and refused to ratify the Anglo-Hejazi treaty. [cite:w_cairo1921] [cite:rs_hu_britannica] [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f111] Lawrence called the settlement a fulfillment "in letter and spirit"; the Hashemite account says the Allies "did not keep their promises"; the 1922 White Paper held that "the whole of Palestine west of the Jordan was thus excluded from Sir H. McMahon's pledge." [cite:rs_hu_jrb_richman_2020] [cite:rs_hu_arabrevolt] [cite:rs_hu_cmd1700] In Washington, Public Resolution 67-73, signed on 21 September 1922 over State Department opposition, endorsed the national home with its own safeguard for non-Jewish communities; the sources show no action it obliged. [cite:wiki_lodge_fish] [cite:jvl_lodge_fish]

What was settled, then, was the chain of authority and Britain's place at its head. What was contested was nearly everything that chain was for: whether the Mandate honoured the Covenant, whether McMahon had promised Palestine, whether Faisal's consent had ever held. What the record leaves open lies at the center of the Mandate itself. Article 2 bound Britain to the national home, to self-governing institutions and to the rights of all inhabitants, and named no rule for choosing among them. [cite:rs_md_avalon] By 1923 the second obligation had no institution behind it. Whose refusal that was, Britain's or the Arab leadership's, is the question [scene:s07a2|the High Commissioners] inherited.
