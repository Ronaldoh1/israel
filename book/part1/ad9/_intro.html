Part Nine follows the money that Americans borrow to reach the Dream and to bridge the months when pay falls short. It begins with what households owe, about $18.8 trillion in mid-2026, and with the credit card: how two Supreme Court decisions in 1978 and 1996 let card issuers set rates and fees under the law of the state they chose, what a minimum payment costs over time at today's rates, and how a three-digit credit score built by a private company came to decide who borrows and at what price, with its errors and the millions it cannot see. It then turns to the fees charged for holding, or not holding, a bank account: overdraft and late fees, the two federal rules written in 2024 to limit them and how both were removed in 2025, and the price of cashing a paycheck outside a bank. It ends with the lenders that have grown up in the gap between bills and paydays, from storefront payday loans to buy-now-pay-later plans, wage-advance apps and credit cards offered in the dentist's office, setting out how each charges, what regulators and courts have found, the industry's case, and the status of each as of October 2026.
