Was the Refusal a Front? Testing the Strongest Case

The record in the last section is, on its face, a record of opposition. There is an argument that it should not be taken at face value: that the Vatican's public refusal was a front, and that behind it the Holy See served, or at least did nothing to obstruct, a project it had its own reasons to want. This volume's author has put that view to the record, and it deserves to be stated in its strongest form before it is tested, because some of its pieces are real.

The case, at its strongest. It runs like this. The Holy See had every tool of a great moral power and used few of them. It did not campaign against the partition plan in 1947, though the plan carved a Jewish state out of the land of Christ's life; it was, by the most careful account, deliberately silent during the vote. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] Thirteen of the thirty-three votes for partition came from the Catholic republics of Latin America: Bolivia, Brazil, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Guatemala, Haiti, Nicaragua, Panama, Paraguay, Peru, Uruguay and Venezuela. [cite:dv3_unproject_181] Without them the resolution could not have reached the two-thirds majority it needed. When Catholic governments in Europe offered to push the Jerusalem question in 1948 and 1949, the Vatican declined to back them. [cite:dv3_dawn_irish_files] Its wartime pope had kept public silence about the destruction of Europe's Jews, a record the archives opened in 2020 have made harder, not easier, to defend. [cite:dv3_jewishnews_kertzer] Its finances ran for a century through banks tied to the same Rothschild name that sits on the Balfour Declaration. [cite:dv3_wiki_rothschild_loans] [cite:dv3_citywire_pauly] And in the end it recognized Israel, and has since kept the relationship through a war in which it lost a church in Gaza. [cite:dv3_wiki_fund_agreement] [cite:washingtonpost_holy_family_strike] On this reading, the refusal was what the Holy See said, and the silence was what it did.

What the claim predicts. A front is a public position that differs from a private one. If the claim is right, the documents should show at least some of the following: internal Vatican papers favouring the Zionist project while public statements opposed it; Vatican pressure on Catholic governments to vote for partition; money moving from the Holy See to the movement or the state; or teaching inside the Church that quietly supported a Jewish return. Each of these can be checked.

Private papers. The best test of a front is what officials wrote to each other when they did not expect to be read. The documents available point the same way as the public record. In 1922 the Secretary of State sent the League of Nations a formal objection to a Jewish "preponderant position" in Palestine. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] In May 1943, in internal correspondence with the apostolic delegate Cicognani, Cardinal Maglione warned that a Palestine "under Jewish predominance, would bring new and grave international problems." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The Irish diplomatic file of 1948–1949 records Vatican caution, not hidden enthusiasm. [cite:dv3_dawn_irish_files] Research on the Latin Patriarchate of Jerusalem in the founding years found some of its clergy describing Zionism in the language of Nazism, an attitude it traced through the war of 1947–1949, the 1950s and the Eichmann trial. [cite:dv3_rioli_2017] None of this is the private voice of an institution secretly sponsoring a Jewish state.

The 1947 votes. The Latin American votes are the strongest piece of the case, and they need care. The Holy See did not oppose partition, and it is fair to say its silence helped. But the record explains its silence by its own aim: the partition plan contained the international regime for Jerusalem that the Vatican had wanted since 1937, and opposing the plan would have meant opposing that. [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The detailed study of the Latin American votes, published in 1959, attributes them mainly to the activism of the region's own delegates, among them Venezuela's Pedro Zuloaga, Guatemala's Jorge García Granados, Uruguay's Enrique Rodríguez Fabregat and Brazil's Oswaldo Aranha, who presided over the Assembly; its summary does not credit the Vatican. [cite:dv3_jias_latam_1959] Six Latin American states abstained and Cuba voted against. [cite:dv3_unproject_181] A coordinated Catholic vote directed from the Vatican would not have split thirteen, six and one. And two years later, when Jerusalem was on the table by itself, many Latin American states voted for internationalization and against Israel's position. [cite:dv3_wiki_res303]

The wartime silence. The opening of the Pius XII archives on 2 March 2020 was expected to settle that pope's record. [cite:dv3_npr_archives_2020] It has sharpened the criticism. David Kertzer, among the few scholars present when they opened, concluded in The Pope at War (2022) that Pius XII's silence came mainly from "his priority for protecting the institutional church as he saw it"; during the round-up of Rome's Jews on 16 October 1943 the Vatican did not protest to the German ambassador but worked to free those detainees who were baptized or married to Catholics. [cite:dv3_brown_kertzer] [cite:dv3_jewishnews_kertzer] Historians still dispute how much rescue in Rome the pope directed himself. [cite:wiki_pius_xii_holocaust] But a pattern of protecting the Church's own institutions first is the opposite of a hidden alliance with the Zionist movement. It explains the silence in 1943 and the caution in 1947–1949 by the same motive, and that motive is documented.

The money and the teaching. The Rothschild loans were made to the popes, not by them, and the Vatican Bank's new president was hired from a firm that bears the Rothschild name nearly a century after Israel's founding; no document in this research shows money moving from the Vatican to the movement or the state. [cite:dv3_wiki_rothschild_loans] [cite:dv3_citywire_pauly] The teaching, as the earlier section showed, ran the other way at every point until 1965, and even after 1965 refused the state a religious meaning. [cite:dv3_jcr_notes_1985]

What the archives have not settled. One honest gap remains. A former Israeli ambassador to the Holy See noted in 2009 that, although the archives for the relevant pontificates are open, the Vatican's attitudes in these years are still known mainly from Jewish and Israeli sources. [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] The Holy See's own files on Palestine for 1939–1958 have not yet produced a published study answering the question this section asks. That is a reason to keep the question open in the narrow sense: a document could still surface. It is not evidence for the claim. Every document that has surfaced so far, public or private, points in one direction.

The finding. The claim that the refusal was a front is not supported by the record. What is supported is a narrower and still serious charge: that at the decisive moments of 1943 and 1947 the Holy See's first concern was the Church's own position, its institutions in Rome and its holy places in Palestine, and that it chose silence where speech would have cost it something. That is a charge about priorities, and it is documented. It does not make the Vatican a hidden parent of the Jewish state; the institution that wrote "we could never sanction it" in 1904 kept writing variations of that sentence, in private as well as in public, for most of the century that followed.
