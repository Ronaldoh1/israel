PayPal, Palantir, Facebook and the Funds

Between 1998 and 2012 Thiel made the fortune and built the institutions that the rest of this book follows. The fortune came from three bets: a payments company he co-founded, a social network in which he was the first outside investor, and a data-analysis company he incorporated and chaired. The institutions were a set of investment vehicles, each with its own partners and money, which are often run together in public discussion as if they were one man's wallet. This section keeps them apart. A position held by Founders Fund is the fund's, owned with its outside investors; a contract won by Palantir is Palantir's; neither is a personal holding of Thiel's unless a filing says so.

PayPal, 1998–2002

In 1998 Thiel put the first $100,000 into Fieldlink, a start-up founded with the programmer Max Levchin, which became Confinity and in 1999 launched a service for sending money by email called PayPal. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] In January 1999, while Confinity was being formed, he bought 1.7 million of its founders' shares at a tenth of a cent each, about $1,700 in all, through a Roth individual retirement account, a tax-free savings vehicle designed for middle-income earners; what that account became is told in [scene:de9_gawker_citizenship_and_tax|the next section]. [cite:de9_pp_roth]

In March 2000 Confinity merged with X.com, the online bank founded by Elon Musk, and the combined company kept the X.com name with Musk as chief executive. In September 2000, as Musk left on a fundraising trip that was also to be his honeymoon, executives loyal to Levchin and Thiel delivered letters of no confidence to the board, which replaced Musk with Thiel. [cite:de9_snopes_paypal] The company took the PayPal name, went public on 15 February 2002, and was bought by eBay for $1.5 billion in October 2002. Thiel, chief executive until the sale, held about 3.7 percent, worth some $55 million. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] [cite:de9_snopes_paypal] Musk's side of the episode is in [scene:de11_zip2_xcom_paypal|Part Eleven]. The early employees who left PayPal with money and with one another's phone numbers became the group that Fortune in 2007 called the "PayPal Mafia," mapped in [scene:de13_who_they_are|Part Thirteen]. [cite:de13_fortune_2007]

Clarium, 2002–2011

With $10 million of his PayPal proceeds Thiel started Clarium Capital, a "global macro" hedge fund that bets on large movements in currencies, interest rates and commodities. It returned 57.1 percent in 2005, lost 7.8 percent in 2006 and gained 40.3 percent in 2007, and by the first quarter of 2008 managed more than $7 billion. Then it turned sharply: by 2011 its assets had fallen to about $350 million, roughly two-thirds of it Thiel's own money. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] Clarium matters here mainly as a corrective to the image of an investor who never misjudges the world; his largest macro bets on the economy went badly wrong, and the money that endured came from start-ups.

Palantir, 2003

Palantir Technologies was incorporated in May 2003. Its initial funding of about $30 million came largely from Thiel and his fund, and he became, and remains, its chairman. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] In-Q-Tel, the venture-capital arm set up by the Central Intelligence Agency, made an early investment of about $2 million, dated to 2004 or 2005 by different accounts, and Palantir's software became deeply embedded at the agency. [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015] [cite:ow9_ap_s1] Fortune called In-Q-Tel the company's "first outside investor", a description that sets aside Thiel's own money, which came first; [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] Founders Fund, Thiel's own fund, is described as its first institutional investor. [cite:de9_wiki_ff] How the company was founded and who controls it is the subject of [scene:de27_origins_and_control|Part Twenty-Nine]; its federal contracts, including those with Immigration and Customs Enforcement, are in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty]. The relationship of type here is exact: Thiel is a founder, the chairman and a large shareholder; Palantir's contracts are the company's.

Facebook, 2004–2022

In August 2004 Thiel paid $500,000 for 10.2 percent of Facebook, then a start-up run by Harvard students, valuing it at $4.9 million. It was the company's first outside investment, and he later took a seat on its board. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] He sold 16.8 million shares for $638 million at Facebook's public offering in May 2012 and nearly all the rest in August 2012 for about $395.8 million, taking his proceeds above $1 billion. He stayed on the board of Facebook, later Meta Platforms, for seventeen years, and announced on 7 February 2022 that he would leave it to support candidates in that year's elections. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel]

The funds, 2005–2013

In 2005 he founded the venture firm Founders Fund with two PayPal colleagues, Ken Howery and Luke Nosek, starting with $50 million; by 2025 it managed about $17 billion. [cite:de9_wiki_ff] It was the first institutional investor in Elon Musk's SpaceX, from 2008, in a deal led by Nosek, and in 2025 put $1 billion into the defence company Anduril, its largest single investment. [cite:de9_wiki_ff] Those are positions of the fund, held for its investors; a widely repeated line that Thiel personally owns a tenth of SpaceX confuses the fund with the man.

Three more vehicles complete the set. Valar Ventures was co-founded in 2010; Jeffrey Epstein became a limited partner in it from 2015, a passive investor with no management role, and Thiel's spokesman has denied a claim by the former Israeli prime minister Ehud Barak that Epstein co-owned it. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] [cite:de9_byline_valar] The documents and what they do and do not show are examined in [scene:de14_epstein_valar|Part Fourteen]. Mithril Capital, launched in June 2012 with a $402 million fund, later employed J.D. Vance, the start of the working relationship told in [scene:de12_the_thiel_years|Part Twelve]. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] [cite:de9_fortune_net24] And the Thiel Foundation, his charity, ran the Thiel Fellowship, which from 2011 paid young founders $100,000 each over two years; one of the first twenty fellows, John Burnham, went on to co-found Tlon, Curtis Yarvin's company, in which Founders Fund also invested in 2013. [cite:de5_audit_tc2011] [cite:de5_wiki_urbit]

By the end of 2012 Thiel had sold most of his Facebook stock, wound Clarium down to a vehicle mostly for his own money, and held his wealth chiefly through Palantir, still private, and through the funds. The pattern matters for every later chapter of this book. When his name is attached to a company in the news, the first question is which vehicle holds it and with whom; the answer is usually a fund with outside partners, and the connection to him is real but one step removed. Where the connection is direct, as with Palantir's chair or the Facebook board, the record says so, and so does this book.
