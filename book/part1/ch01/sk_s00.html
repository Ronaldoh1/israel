How This Book Weighs Evidence

A reader meeting this history for the first time has good reason to suspect that any account of it is someone's opinion dressed up as history. The suspicion is fair, and it deserves an answer before the history begins rather than after. The answer is not a promise of neutrality, which any author can make. It is a method, stated in advance, so that every claim in the chapters that follow can be held against it: what counts as evidence here, whose records are used, how advocacy is marked, who produced the work, and what happens when it is wrong.

Every factual claim in this book rests on one of four levels of evidence, and the prose says which one is in play. A documented claim rests on a primary record: a law, a court ruling, an official filing, a census, a signed document. A reported claim rests on a named news outlet or a named investigator, and the book names them, so that the reader knows whose account is being relied on. A contested claim is one on which credible sources disagree. There, both positions are set out with their evidence, and the dispute is left open rather than quietly settled in one side's favor. Finally, some questions are stated as unresolved, because the record does not settle them. How many of Gaza's dead were combatants is one such question, and later volumes treat it as open rather than pretending to an answer the evidence does not give. A fifth kind of passage is not evidence at all: where the book offers a symbolic or interpretive reading, it labels it as interpretation, and never presents it as a finding.

The levels matter because the same sentence can be true at one level and false at another. "A court found" and "a newspaper reported" and "critics argue" are three different claims, even when they concern the same event. Confusion in public argument about Israel and Palestine can come from sliding between them: a report repeated often enough comes to sound like a finding, and a finding that one side dislikes comes to sound like an accusation. Keeping the levels visible is the first defense against both slides.

The second principle concerns whose records are used. Where both sides keep official counts, both are used, and each for its own people. Israeli deaths are drawn from Israel's National Insurance Institute and Defense Ministry. Palestinian deaths are drawn from the Gaza Health Ministry, United Nations agencies and peer-reviewed studies. Court records and archives come from Israeli, Palestinian, American and international institutions alike. This is not a claim that every official source is equally reliable on every point; it is a rule that no side's records are excluded in advance, and that where they diverge the divergence is shown.

The third principle concerns advocacy. Organizations that argue a position are useful sources. They often gather material no one else does. But they are not neutral, and the reader should know when one is speaking. Groups on every side, among them UN Watch, IMPACT-se, Track AIPAC and the Washington Report (WRMEA), are identified as advocacy groups whenever they are cited. The label is not a verdict on their accuracy. It is information the reader needs to weigh what they say.

Who made this book is also part of the evidence. It is the work of Observe The System, an independent research project. No government, party, lobby or donor funds it or reviews it. When a source proves wrong, or has been misread, the claim is corrected and the change is noted. Every claim carries a citation that opens the source itself, so a reader who disagrees with a reading of a document can go to the document and judge.

Why should the record differ at all from what readers already learned? Not, for the most part, because they were told lies. Most people meet this history through textbooks, speeches and news headlines, and these are summaries. Summaries leave things out, and what they leave out shapes what remains. This book puts the underlying documents beside the summaries so that the two can be compared, section by section, in the boxes that open each one: what readers are commonly told, and what the record shows.

The same method applies to the claims that circulate outside textbooks, in the comment threads and viral posts where many readers now first meet this subject. Most of those claims are not pure invention. They take a true fact and attach to it a conclusion the fact does not support. Egypt built a wall. Rome renamed a province. Arafat was born in Cairo, which the record shows to be largely accurate, though some sources dispute it.[cite:w_arafat] Each detail can be checked, and each checks out, more or less. The trouble lies in what comes next. The birthplace of one leader, true or not, says nothing about the history of a people. The Roman renaming is real, but the name it imposed was centuries older than Rome's use of it, a point taken up in [scene:ah_s01|the section on the land's ancient names]. The pattern works because a verifiable detail creates trust, and the reader accepts the conclusion along with it. Anyone, on any side, can be fooled this way.

The remedy is to separate the fact from the conclusion and test each on its own. The method used throughout this book reduces to three questions.

The first question is whether the fact is true. The way to answer it is to find the primary source or a credible record, and often the answer is yes. The second is whether the fact proves the conclusion attached to it, and often it does not: a true fact can carry a false claim. The third is what has been left out, because the missing context usually decides the matter. A wall may exist and still have been built for reasons other than the one the post supplies. A province may have been renamed and still have carried a name older than the empire that renamed it. In each case the first answer is yes, the second is no, and the third explains why.

Claims tested this way receive one of four verdicts: true, partly true, false, or opinion. The last matters as much as the others. A statement of value, or of what someone ought to think, is not a fact that a record can confirm or refute, and calling it false would be as much an error as calling it true. Insults receive no verdict at all. Name-calling is not a claim; it is skipped, and the claims around it are checked. And the checking runs in every direction. The myths examined in this book are not one side's myths only. A claim that flatters Israel and a claim that flatters the Palestinians are held to the same test, and a false claim aimed at either people is treated the same way.

That last rule binds this book as well. The four levels of evidence, the use of both sides' records, the labeling of advocacy and the three questions apply to every claim, favorable or unfavorable to any side, and to the book's own conclusions as much as to anyone else's. What the method cannot do should be said plainly too. It cannot make a contested question uncontested, or supply a record that was never kept, or guarantee that every reading of every source is right. It can only show the reader where each claim stands and where its evidence lies. Where the evidence is strong, the prose says so. Where it is thin, disputed or absent, the prose says that, and the citation is there for the reader to check.
