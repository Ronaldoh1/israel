Congress Endorses Balfour: The Lodge–Fish Resolution of 1922

Official American support for a Jewish homeland is usually dated to 1948 and President Truman's recognition of the new state. The first formal endorsement by the United States government came twenty-six years earlier. On 21 September 1922 President Warren Harding signed a joint resolution of Congress favouring a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. It had been requested by American Zionist leaders, opposed by the State Department, and passed by the House and, by unanimous consent, the Senate.

The initiative came from the American Zionist movement, then in the middle of the fundraising campaigns described in [scene:us_s01|the previous section]. American Zionist leaders approached two Republicans, Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts and Representative Hamilton Fish III of New York, and asked them to introduce a resolution endorsing the Balfour Declaration of 1917. The Jewish Virtual Library credits a significant lobbying effort by the American Zionist community, and in particular the work of Rabbi Simon Glazer. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] Nahum Sokolow had met Harding for nearly an hour on 13 January 1922, during the Keren Hayesod tour that raised nearly $5 million. [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] The record used here does not say whether that meeting touched on the resolution.

The resolution met objection from two quarters. The State Department opposed it, and so did the New York Times. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:wjc_lodge_fish] It passed regardless. The House approved it on 30 June 1922, and the Senate followed by unanimous consent on 13 July. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:wjc_lodge_fish] The Senate's passage came eleven days before the Mandate for Palestine, which wrote the Balfour Declaration into an instrument of the League of Nations, was done at London on 24 July 1922. [cite:rs_md_avalon] The Mandate itself is examined in [scene:s07|the section on the Mandate text].

Harding signed the measure on 21 September 1922 as Public Resolution 67-73. Its text favoured "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," repeating the phrase the Balfour Declaration had used in 1917. [cite:wiki_lodge_fish] [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:balfour_letter] It also carried a condition: that nothing should be done which might prejudice "the civil and religious rights of Christian and all other non-Jewish communities in Palestine." [cite:wiki_lodge_fish] [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] The condition belongs in any account of the resolution as fully as the endorsement does. Congress, like the British government before it, favoured a national home for one people and stated in the same text that the rights of the communities already living in the country were not to be prejudiced.

Inside American Jewish life the resolution mattered too. The World Jewish Congress notes that it helped legitimize Zionism within American Jewry by giving it a "stamp of approval" from the non-Jewish world. [cite:wjc_lodge_fish] That carries weight against the background of the previous year, when much of the established American Jewish community still kept its distance from the cause, and when Einstein's invitation to about fifty non-Zionist notables drew few replies. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252]

The episode shows how this resolution was made. The initiative did not come from the State Department, the arm of government responsible for foreign policy, which opposed it. It came from an organized domestic movement that approached members of Congress directly and won passage in both houses, after which the President signed. The resolution was lobbied for and passed over the objection of the foreign-policy bureaucracy. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:wjc_lodge_fish]

What it committed the United States to is a narrower question than its importance might suggest, and the record used here answers it only in part. The text says what Congress favoured and on what condition. The sources do not show that it obliged the government to take any particular action, and they do not describe any action taken under it. Nor do they give the grounds on which the State Department and the New York Times objected, or the House vote count. What is documented is the sequence, the words of the text with their condition, and the fact that the first official American endorsement of a Jewish national home came in 1922, not 1948.
