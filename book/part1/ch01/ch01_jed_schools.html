The Day School and the Hebrew School

The last part asked who decides what children are taught about this history in public schools. This part asks the same question of the institutions American Jews built to teach their own children. The answer matters for a reason this volume has stressed from the start: being Jewish and supporting a political movement are not the same thing. To see where the two are joined in a child's mind, and where they are kept apart, one has to look at the classroom where it happens.

Two systems, very different in size

American Jewish children are taught in two kinds of school. The first is the full-time day school, which teaches both general and Jewish subjects. The last national census of these schools, for the 2018–19 school year, counted a little over 900 of them, enrolling nearly 300,000 pupils, in at least 37 states. The sector is overwhelmingly Orthodox: about 87 percent of day-school pupils attend Orthodox schools, and two-thirds attend ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) schools. Outside New York and New Jersey, non-Orthodox schools account for about 35 percent of enrollment. [cite:jed_fwd_census2019]

The second is the supplementary school, often called Hebrew school or religious school, which meets for a few hours a week, usually at a synagogue. It is where most non-Orthodox American Jewish children have historically been taught, and it is shrinking fast. Enrollment fell from about 280,000 in 1987 and more than 230,000 in 2006 to about 141,000 in 2020, a drop of at least 45 percent in fourteen years, and more than 700 such schools closed. Over the same period Chabad's share of the remaining enrollment grew from 4 to 10 percent. [cite:jed_jw_hebrewschool2023]

Who decides

No single body sets the curriculum. There is no Jewish ministry of education in the United States. Each school answers to its own board, its denominational movement (Orthodox, Conservative, Reform or community), its own teachers and the outside organizations that supply materials and training. In structure, this is the same fragmented model this volume found in American public schools: whoever produces the materials and trains the teachers shapes what is taught. As the previous part showed, one source of funding is new and comes from abroad: in 2026 the Israeli government approved its own budgets to raise day-school enrollment in North America (see the section on how the story is taught).

Israel as the thing that holds a school together

The most careful study of how these schools teach Israel was carried out by the scholars Alex Pomson, Jack Wertheimer and Hagit Hacohen-Wolf for the AVI CHAI Foundation and published in 2014 as Hearts and Minds: Israel in North American Jewish Day Schools. One of its central findings was that "Israel is rarely contested in day schools": it works as a unifying force that holds school communities together. Pupils' attachment to Israel tracked their connection to Jews worldwide and how active their parents were in Jewish communal life. [cite:jed_prizmah_hearts]

That finding explains a great deal without any theory of manipulation. A school community made up of families with different levels of observance and different politics needs something they share. Israel became that shared centre. The cost is that whatever might divide the community, including a full account of 1948 or of the occupation, is the material a school has the strongest reason to leave out. Schools built Israel education to hold a community together, not as a political campaign. That makes the pattern easy to understand. It does not make what is left out matter less to the pupils who later meet it for the first time.

The full range, including refusal

"Jewish education" does not mean one view of Israel. At one end are religious-Zionist schools that teach the founding of the state as a step toward redemption. At the other are schools whose teaching is the opposite. The Satmar Hasidic community, rebuilt in Williamsburg, Brooklyn, and Kiryas Joel, New York, after the Holocaust by Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, holds that a Jewish state created by human effort before the messianic redemption is a sin. Teitelbaum set out the doctrine in his book Vayoel Moshe, reading literally the Talmud's "three oaths," under which the Jewish people swore not to go up to the land en masse or by force and not to rebel against the nations while in exile. His community built its own schools to pass that teaching on. [cite:jed_toi_satmar]

That such schools exist inside American Judaism, teaching on religious grounds that the state should not have been founded, is the plainest evidence that Jews are not one political bloc. The difference between Judaism and Zionism is not a distinction imposed from outside. Jewish communities teach it in their own classrooms.

Whose Jewish history

One more gap is documented from inside. American Jewish schools were built, in the words of the director of JIMENA, a group representing Jews from the Middle East and North Africa, "at a time when Jewish life was 'Ashkenormative,'" meaning that European Jewish culture and history were taught as the default. In 2024 the group released a toolkit to help day schools teach Sephardi and Mizrahi history. Pew's 2020 survey found that at least one American Jew in ten identifies as Sephardi, Mizrahi or of mixed heritage. [cite:jed_ejp_jimena2024]

The gap connects to distinctions made earlier in this volume. A pupil taught that Jewish history is European history may never learn that Jews from Baghdad, Fez and Sana'a lived for centuries as part of the Arab and Muslim world, or that "Semitic" names a family of languages that includes Arabic as well as Hebrew. Those are not facts about Israel's politics, but they shape how a young person hears every later argument about it.
