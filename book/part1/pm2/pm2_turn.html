The Turn of December 2021

For seventy years AIPAC kept out of campaign spending. Its members gave; it did not. In December 2021 it reversed that rule. It announced that, ahead of the 2022 elections, it would form a political action committee and a super PAC, [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] and in the four years that followed the two committees became the largest spenders among all the committees OpenSecrets classes as pro-Israel. [cite:pm_israelite_2024] This section follows the decision, the first cycle, the controversy that greeted it, and the scale it reached by 2026. The races themselves, and what the money did in them, are the subject of Part Five.

The first cycle. AIPAC PAC was created on 15 December 2021 and the United Democracy Project on 3 January 2022. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The super PAC moved fast. By the end of June 2022 it had raised almost $27.6 million, and by 2 August it had spent nearly $24.3 million in independent expenditures, all of it in Democratic primaries; FactCheck.org found it the largest outside spender in that year's Democratic primaries. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] Its largest donors were AIPAC itself, at $8.5 million, and a handful of very wealthy individuals: Jan Koum, a co-founder of WhatsApp, gave $2 million, and Jonathon Jacobson, Paul Singer, Bernard Marcus and Haim Saban gave $1 million each. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] Its spokesman, Patrick Dorton, explained the purpose in two sentences that remain the clearest statement of it: "What we're trying to do is build the broadest bipartisan pro-Israel coalition in Congress possible," and "There was rising concern in the pro-Israel community about candidates for Congress who held radical anti-Israel views." [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022]

The endorsement controversy. The PAC's first endorsements drew criticism from an unexpected side. Among the candidates AIPAC PAC endorsed in 2022 were Republican members of Congress who had voted against certifying the 2020 presidential election; by one account 37 of them were among 130 endorsements announced in March, and J Street, in August, put the number of such members AIPAC had supported at 109. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] [cite:pm_jstreet_sedition] The two figures describe different points in the year and should not be read as one count. Asked whether there was any pro-Israel candidate AIPAC would rule out, its chief executive, Howard Kohr, answered: "I'd have to think about that." J Street's policy director called on AIPAC to set "some moral red lines." [cite:pm_jstreet_sedition] The committees' stated test was support for the US–Israel relationship; the super PAC's website describes its work as helping to elect candidates who support that relationship. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] The episode showed the logic of the new structure: a single-issue PAC, endorsing on a single issue, across both parties.

2024: the scale. In the 2024 cycle the two committees together spent $95.1 million, according to FEC filings compiled by OpenSecrets, more than double the $44 million of 2022, and more than every other committee OpenSecrets classed as pro-Israel. [cite:pm_israelite_2024] The super PAC alone raised $87.1 million in that cycle and spent about $61.4 million. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] AIPAC's own account, published the day after the election, said its PAC had endorsed 361 candidates, that 326 had won their general elections, 120 Democrats and 206 Republicans, and that more than 10,000 members had given over $53 million to candidates through it. It credited the super PAC with helping to decide nine races, including the defeats of Representatives Jamaal Bowman of New York and Cori Bush of Missouri. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024] The win rate needs a caution: by the memo's own account, the endorsements covered 75 percent of House members and 84 percent of senators in the cycle, so a high rate of victory reflects whom AIPAC endorsed as much as what it spent. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024]

2026: more money, different names. By June 2026 the super PAC had raised about $104 million for the 2026 cycle, already more than in the whole of 2024, and had spent about $52.5 million. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] The change in 2026 was not only in size but in visibility. Politico reported that the super PAC had routed more than 40 percent of its primary spending that year through pop-up and pass-through committees, groups with names unrelated to Israel that received its money and spent it under their own. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] Counting spending by both committees, the super PAC and the PAC together, Sludge put AIPAC's 2026 election spending at $104.3 million by early August, a figure for money spent, separate from the super PAC's $104 million raised. [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026] Who received that money, and against whom it was spent, is set out in Parts Three to Five.

The turn of 2021 changed what AIPAC is. Until then it was a lobby surrounded by donors; afterwards it was a lobby with its own electoral machine. Its supporters describe the change as a natural extension of its members' civic rights, and point out that every major interest in American politics, from labor to industry, has PACs and super PACs of its own. Its critics argue that a lobby whose cause is a foreign country's security now decides, with sums no individual member of Congress can match, who among them faces a well-funded primary. Both descriptions rest on the same filings. What those filings show, cycle by cycle, is the subject of the next part.
