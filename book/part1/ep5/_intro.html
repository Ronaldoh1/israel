The news has told the story of the people around Epstein mostly through a single fact: that their name appeared in his papers. That fact on its own explains nothing. A reader cannot judge why it mattered that a man stayed close to Epstein without knowing who that man was: what he had built, what office he held, what he stood to gain or lose, and when and how the two of them met. Part Five supplies that. It is a biographical dictionary of the people this book names, rebuilt from the documented record rather than from headlines.

Every entry follows the same order. It begins with the person's life before and apart from Epstein, drawn from filings, official records and their own published words. It sets out the record between them, dated, with the kind of contact each document shows. It places the person's public statements beside what the files show, and says whether those statements were made under oath. It reports allegations only at the level of evidence they carry, with the person's answer. It records the consequences, if any. It says what the news got wrong, in both directions, and what remains unestablished.

Reporting on public people is protected speech, and the protection rests on the truth. That is why every statement of fact in these entries is tied to a document, why private people appear only where they are already on the public record in that role, and why the entries use plain verbs, emailed, flew, paid, and never imply an act the record does not show. Entries for living people will carry their response, or the date it was requested, before this book is final.
