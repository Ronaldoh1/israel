The Tests

The map comes with a set of prepared questions, each of which removes part of the documented network and reads the result. There are now 60 of them, together with nine that take a part of the record often denied. They are listed below from the lowest chance to the highest, as in the map's Tests panel, and anyone can run them there, change them, or remove any other combination of nodes. The figures are the chance of a founding on time, in 1948, and the chance of a founding ever, against about 93 percent for the full record.

Thirteen observations follow from the table, and each is a statement about the record rather than about what anyone intended. Rows marked as often denied take a part of the record that accounts of the founding commonly deny or play down; the map's panel gives, for each, the claim usually made beside the record. Rows marked as an assumption go further than the documents: they remove people or bodies the record does not show would have fallen, and are there to show how much a figure depends on that step.

The movement and its armed groups were the two things nothing else replaced. Remove the organized movement and no one is left to pursue the state; remove the armed groups and the arms channel is empty, with nothing outside it able to fill it. Every other removal leaves something to rebuild with.

The Rothschild figure depends on what is assumed to fall with the family. On the documented record, removing the family and its houses, 46 nodes in all, leaves about 30 percent on time; it was about 34 before the land the family's PICA held, about half a million dunams in 1934, was counted. Following everything that is then cut off from the founding bodies, PICA, the colonies of 1882 to 1896, Mikveh Israel, the Carmel wine company and two banks the family dealt with among them, leaves the same figure, because the map already counts what no longer connects. The lower figures come only from assumptions. If the Balfour Declaration could not have been issued without its addressee, the figure falls to about 16 percent; the record shows the Declaration was addressed to Walter Rothschild and that he worked on its draft, but the map holds the family's link to the British government only as a name on the same page, not as a stated tie. If everyone with a documented tie to the family is taken out too, it falls to about 9 percent, but that list includes Herzl, Weizmann, Balfour and the Jewish Agency, so the test comes closer to removing the movement than to removing a family. Thirty percent is the figure the record supports.

The network before Herzl mattered through its settlement money, not its ideas. Remove the whole network that existed before 1897, from Kalischer and Hess to the Lovers of Zion, the Odessa Committee and the Alliance Israélite Universelle, and the chance falls to about 70 percent, with about 16 years lost. Almost all of that comes from the Lovers of Zion and the donors and settlements around them. The thinkers and the persecutions that set the movement going score nothing, because no document states a tie from them to a later act in a form the map can weigh. Removed with the Rothschilds, the same network brings the chance to about 20 percent. Herzl, removed on his own, costs about two points. He founded the political movement; he did not originate the idea or build the first network, and the institutions he started outlived him. The network itself is told in [scene:rd0_network|the first network, 1836–1896].

Legitimacy mattered, and the survivors' case most of all. Take away the Holocaust as the map holds it, which is only through the case its survivors made in the displaced-persons camps, the Harrison Report and the inquiries that followed, and the chance falls from about 93 to about 87 percent. Take away American Christian and public support, the committees, Blackstone's memorials and the endorsement by Congress in 1922, and it falls to about 88. Both are floors. The map can measure only the legitimacy these ties lent to decisions; it cannot measure how the Holocaust changed everything else, from immigration and money to the will of the movement itself. What it does show is that the American case, already the heaviest single influence on the map, rested partly on that legitimacy: with the new channel, removing the United States lowers the chance to about 25 percent, from about 28.

Central banks were almost absent; the one that paid in was Nazi Germany's. Removing every central bank with a documented tie leaves the chance at about 93 percent. The only central-bank money on the record is the Reichsbank's share, about a third, of the Transfer Agreement of 1933, and removing the whole agreement lowers the chance to about 91 percent. [scene:rd7_central|Following the money to the central banks] sets out what the search found.

Two sources of money mattered more than any single leader. Edmond de Rothschild's colonies and the American appeals were the two largest sums, and they came at opposite ends of the period: his from 1882, when almost no one else was funding settlement, theirs in 1946 to 1948. Removing either makes the founding unlikely; removing both makes it impossible on this record.

Britain built the framework, and the framework was the Mandate. Without the Balfour Declaration, or without the British state that issued it and held the Mandate, immigration and settlement from 1920 to 1948 go at the Ottoman pace, and about 26 years of that work are lost. The stricter reading, under which the Declaration could not have been issued without its named addressee, Walter Rothschild, produces the same result because it removes the same document; the record shows that it was addressed to him and that he worked on its draft, not that it would have been withheld otherwise.

Britain also supplied the people. Since the people channel was added, removing the British government costs more than before, about 35 percent on time instead of about 45, because the Mandate's certificates let in most of the immigrants who made up the population of 1948, and its land law was the route for most of the land the Jewish National Fund bought. Removing those certificates alone, with everything else Britain did kept, lowers the chance to about 78 percent; removing the largest wave, the Fifth Aliyah of 1933 to 1939, to about 87. The earliest waves and the clandestine voyages, small in number, move it by less than a point. The limits at the end of this appendix explain why these figures are probably too gentle.

Two policies did more than any single person: the doors the Mandate opened. Article 6 of the Mandate obliged Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and close settlement on the land, and two instruments carried it out: the immigration certificates and the Land Transfer Ordinance of 1920, the legal route for about 914,000 of the 936,000 dunams the Jewish National Fund held by May 1948. [cite:pl_pij] Removing the land law alone lowers the chance to about 86 percent; removing it together with the certificates, an assumption marked as such, lowers it to about 73, far more than removing Truman or Herzl. The reversals mattered too. When the Passfield White Paper of 1930 limited immigration and land purchase, the prime minister's letter to Weizmann of February 1931 was treated as withdrawing it; even if its limits had held through the whole of the 1930s, the chance falls no lower than about 90 percent. [cite:pl_macdonald] The 1939 White Paper and the land regulations of 1940 pushed the other way, and four of the seven voting members of the League's mandates commission found the White Paper inconsistent with the Mandate, but the war prevented any ruling, and the Jewish National Fund still acquired 390,000 dunams between 1940 and 1946. [cite:pl_pmc1939] [cite:pl_stein]

The United States counted far more than the Soviet Union, and the difference was its bloc. In 1947 the two powers voted the same way. Remove the American government and its bloc follows it only at its measured independence; the vote becomes uncertain, American recognition is gone, and the founding is delayed. Remove Stalin, or the Soviet bloc whole, and the vote still clears two-thirds in most outcomes. The map now weighs the Czechoslovak arms by what arrived before 15 May 1948, about 14,700 rifles and 1,655 machine guns for a force of about 30,000, close to half the rifles in hand at the founding; on their own they cost about ten points, and with the other arms from abroad, much of it bought in breach of embargoes, they bring the chance down to about two in three. Their full weight fell in the war after the founding, which the next section explains. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel]

The men who decided mattered less than the decisions. Remove Truman and Henry Wallace decides in his place, a man on record as a supporter; the founding still happens on time. Keep Roosevelt alive and bound by his April 1945 pledge to Ibn Saud, and the American vote and recognition become uncertain, and the founding is delayed. The decisions were contested, and the record shows who pushed and who resisted. But in most of the tests someone else would have taken them, and the map can say who.

Who sat in the White House mattered through his own position, not through his party. Roosevelt's heart failure was diagnosed in March 1944, and in July his surgeon Frank Lahey wrote that he would not survive another term; the public was not told, and he ran and won. [cite:rd7_history_fdr_health] [cite:rd7_wiki_lahey] Had the diagnosis been public and the Republican Thomas Dewey won instead, little changes: Dewey had declared for a Jewish commonwealth in October 1944, after meeting Abba Hillel Silver, and in October 1946 asked for several hundred thousand immigrants, more than Truman did; the 1944 Republican platform had condemned Roosevelt for not pressing Britain. [cite:rd7_jta_dewey_1944] [cite:rd7_jta_dewey_1946] [cite:rd7_jvl_gop_1944] A Democrat with no recorded position delays the founding by about five years. Only a president bound by Roosevelt's own pledge to Ibn Saud lowers the chance materially, and even then to about 83 percent. American recognition on 14 May, removed on its own, costs only two points on the founding; its weight came afterwards, in arms, loans and membership of the United Nations.

The Holy Land did its work in the choice of the land, more than through organized Christian support. Remove the Christian Zionists the map holds, from Darby and Scofield to Blackstone's memorials and the American Christian Palestine committees, and the chance falls only to about 90 percent. But every other homeland the movement weighed, without either the Christian or the Jewish attachment to the land, scores between 0 and 2 percent on the same formula. The religious motives of Balfour and Lloyd George are recorded but not counted as a separate push, so the first figure is a floor.
