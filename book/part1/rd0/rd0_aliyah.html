The Jews of Palestine before Herzl

The small share of Russia's emigrants who chose Palestine did not arrive in a country without Jews. When Theodor Herzl published his case for a Jewish state in 1896, Jews had been living in Palestine, and arriving there, for the whole of the century. They are easy to lose from view, because the story of political Zionism is usually told from Vienna, Odessa and Basel. The record allows a rough count of them, and it shows that two quite different Jewish communities stood in the country by the 1890s: an older one centered on the holy cities, and a newer one of agricultural colonies founded from 1878 onward. They did not always welcome each other.

The older community, known as the Old Yishuv, had grown steadily through immigration well before any political program existed. Büşra Barın, in a 2014 study of Ottoman policy toward Jewish immigration, estimates that about 5,000 Jews came to Palestine between 1800 and 1840 and roughly 25,000 between 1850 and 1880. She puts the Old Yishuv at about 24,000 people in 1880, of whom 13,900 lived in Jerusalem [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014_pop]. A reference summary, citing Mordecai Elia (1971), gives a slightly lower figure for the same year, 23,000 [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. The two estimates are close; what they share is the point. On the eve of the first organized settlement movement, more than half of the country's Jews lived in one city, and Jerusalem's Jewish population was already counted in the thousands.

These figures matter for the argument that runs through this book. The strongest form of the Zionist case rested partly on continuity: Jews were not strangers to the country, and Jerusalem had a large Jewish population before any Zionist organization existed. The numbers above support that much. They do not, on their own, say anything about the political claims later built on them.

The Ottoman state that governed this community also permitted at least one Jewish institution from abroad to operate in it. On 5 April 1870 an Ottoman firman, an imperial decree, permitted the Alliance Israélite Universelle to establish an agricultural school in Jaffa. A copy of the firman is held by the Israel State Archives under the reference JM-ISA/AEB/83/32 [cite:rs_rd0_aiu_firman]. The document matters for what it does and does not say. It records an Ottoman grant of permission, through the regular instrument of imperial decree, to a foreign Jewish body to open an agricultural school in Palestine, eleven years before the pogroms in the Russian Empire set off the larger movement. The archival record of the firman does not name the school, and this book does not attach a later name to it.

The new community began before the pogroms and grew quickly after them. The First Aliyah, the first wave of Jewish immigration organized around settlement on the land, is conventionally dated 1881–1903. Its earliest colony had in fact been attempted before that. According to a reference summary, Petah Tikva was first settled in 1878 and then re-established in 1882 after that first attempt failed; Gei Oni was also founded in 1878, and was taken over in 1882 by the settlers of Rosh Pinna. Rishon LeZion and Zikhron Ya'akov also date from 1882, Ness Ziona from 1883, Gedera from 1884, Rehovot from 1890 and Hadera from 1891 [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. The settlers called these places colonies.

The violence of 1881–84 in the Russian Empire and the laws that followed it form the background to this wave, told in [scene:s02|the pogroms and the May Laws]. Within a year of the main foundings, at least one colony had turned to outside money. In 1883 the settlers' central committee transferred Zikhron Ya'akov's assets to Baron Edmond de Rothschild of the Paris banking house [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki], the first step in a patronage that is the subject of the section [scene:bc_s04|on Edmond de Rothschild's colonies].

The First Aliyah was not only European. Emigration from Yemen began in early 1881. A reference summary states that a few hundred Jews left Sanaa and nearby towns for Palestine in 1881–82, part of a movement that continued almost without break until 1914 [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. That summary gives no source of its own for the figure, and it should be treated as an estimate. The arrivals are nonetheless the point at which the Jews of the Arab world enter this story as participants rather than as background: their migration began in the same years as the Russian one, from a different country and under a different government, and the record does not show that the two movements were organized together.

How many Jews came in the First Aliyah as a whole, and how many stayed, is disputed, and the range is wide. Barın estimates about 20,000 to 30,000 arrivals [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014_pop]. A reference summary reports estimates running from 25,000, a Jewish Agency figure, to 60,000 in total, the latter from the historian Anita Shapira (2012), who also estimates that between 50 and 70 percent of those who came later left [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. The sources available here do not explain how each figure was reached, so the gap between them cannot be resolved in this book. If Shapira's estimate of departures is right, a large share of the newcomers did not remain.

The same disagreement carries into the count at the end of the period. Barın puts the Jewish population of Palestine at about 50,000 by 1903; a reference summary gives about 55,000 at the period's close, without a source; and Elia, as cited in the same summary, gives 80,000 for 1907 [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014_pop] [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki]. These dates fall after 1896, and they are given here only to show the direction of change. Read together with the 1880 figure, they suggest that the Jewish population roughly doubled, or more than tripled, in a generation, depending on whose estimate is used. That growth was what the Ottoman government was trying to limit in the same years, as [scene:rd0_limits|the next section] shows.

The newcomers also met opposition from Jews. According to a reference timeline citing an edited scholarly volume (Sobel and Beit-Hallahmi, eds., Tradition, Innovation, Conflict, 2012), in the 1880s the Orthodox community of Jerusalem regarded emerging Zionism as a threat and reported Zionist newcomers to the Ottoman authorities [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki_dissent]. That is a striking act: Jews in Jerusalem turning to the Ottoman state against other Jews over the new movement. In Europe the objection was made on other grounds. Adolf Jellinek, the preacher of Vienna's Jewish community, answered Leon Pinsker's Auto-Emancipation by denying that Jews had national characteristics as such. "We are at home in Europe," he answered, as the historian Robert Wistrich is cited quoting him [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki_dissent]. His objection went to the premise of Pinsker's pamphlet: that the Jews were a nation.

The record therefore shows a Jewish Palestine in 1896 that was older, more varied and more divided than the later national story usually allows. It held a long-established religious community concentrated in Jerusalem; a scattering of colonies, several of them already dependent on one Paris donor; a small migration from Yemen; and Jewish critics of the national idea both in Jerusalem and in Europe.

Much is left unsettled. The population figures are estimates from a thesis and a reference summary, and they disagree by tens of thousands at the end of the period. The material used here does not describe how the Old Yishuv supported itself, how it was divided internally, or how its institutions worked, and this section does not attempt it. The size of the Yemenite migration rests on an unsourced figure. Whether Jerusalem's reports to the Ottoman authorities in the 1880s were the act of the community's leadership or of particular individuals is not stated in the source available, and the matter needs the underlying study to settle it.
