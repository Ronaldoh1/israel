Who Held the Path

Sufism has had more kinds of keeper than any tradition on this shelf except Kabbalah, and its keepers have held very different kinds of power.

Three findings stand out. The first is the line around mystical speech. Al-Ghazali's Sufism, inside the law and the institutions, became orthodox; al-Hallaj's, which sounded like a claim to divinity and moved among those the state suspected of rebellion, ended on a cross. [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] [cite:sf_brit_hallaj] The same line ran around the inner sayings of the Christian canon. The second is that an inner path, once organised, becomes an institution with power of its own: the Safavid order took a throne and imposed a creed, and the Turkish Republic closed the orders because they were rival centres of loyalty. [cite:sf_brit_safavid] [cite:sf_brit_orders] The third is that the modern opponents examined here came from within the Muslim world, a secular republic and movements that claim to purify the faith, and that the victims and the protesters were Muslims too. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi] [cite:sf_wiki_rawda]

The continuity with the rest of the shelf is also clear. Like Enoch, the Gnostics and Kabbalah, Sufism has been described by its enemies: the Nizaris' story was written by Crusaders and Marco Polo. [cite:sf_iis_legends] Like Kabbalah, it has been borrowed and sold by outsiders who wanted its prestige without its religion. [cite:sf_pf_rumi] [cite:kb_wiki_xkab] And like every tradition here, it survived. The Brethren's burned encyclopedia was translated; and the closed lodges of Turkey left a living tradition.
