The Board in 1896: Who Could Act on Palestine Before Basel

When the First Zionist Congress met at Basel in August 1897, it did not build the structures that would later fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state. It walked into them. A common assumption, reading backward from the twentieth century, holds that the Zionist movement created the international power structures that later supported Israel. The record documents the reverse. By 1896 a sovereign state in Istanbul governed Palestine and had already written rules against Jewish settlement there; European powers held rights inside that state that let those rules leak; a British Parliament seated a lobby for finance; banking houses had long lent to governments and to the Pope; Protestant restorationists had wanted a Jewish Palestine for sixty years; a Vatican would refuse one; a Rothschild had already said no to Herzl; and in Palestine itself Arab notables and Jewish communities had their own institutions and quarrels. This section sets out those pieces as they stood at the end of 1896.

The state piece: Britain and the City. The British state that would issue the Balfour Declaration in 1917 governed through a Parliament in which the Corporation of the City of London held a business franchise and kept its Remembrancer, a Corporation official seated in the Commons and reviewing legislation for the City's interests. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation] Beneath that sat an older mechanism. Since 1694 the British Crown had borrowed from private subscribers through a chartered company in the City, the Bank of England, created to finance William III's war against France. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] Finance had both a seat inside the legislature and a standing channel into the Treasury. Neither made the City a sovereign. Parliament legislated for it, and its own description of its role, "rather like a trade body", is the documented finding. [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation]

The finance piece: lenders, not puppeteers. The Rothschild houses were documented creditors to Britain and Prussia, and from 1832 to the Holy See. [cite:wiki_nathan_mayer_rothschild] [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] The London house arranged a £5 million sterling loan for Prussia in 1818 and in November 1875 advanced the £4,000,000 with which Disraeli's government bought 44% of the Suez Canal shares, a purchase Disraeli defended as "a highway to our Indian Empire". [cite:jwatte_rothschild_myths_record] [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023] [cite:rs_rd0_disraeli_1876] The 1832 papal loan of £400,000 is documented, though the sources differ on whether the London or the Paris house was the counterparty. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] From 1882 the Paris house, through Baron Edmond de Rothschild, financed agricultural colonies in Palestine, roughly thirty of them by one count, on his own philanthropic terms. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] In July 1896 Edmond refused to back Herzl's political project. [cite:herzl_diaries_vol2_archive] Every connection in this piece is a contract or a loan. None supports the legend of a family directing governments from behind the scenes, a legend this chapter traces to an antisemitic pamphlet of 1846. [cite:wiki_dairnvaell_satan_pamphlet]

The money in the picture followed political alignment only in part. Edmond's money built settlements and refused the movement. Baron Maurice de Hirsch's money, £2,000,000 of authorized capital in the Jewish Colonization Association from 1891, chose Argentina and sought no state. [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] Herzl in 1896 had a program; the sources here record no fortune behind it. The two great philanthropic fortunes this chapter documents were therefore committed to resettlement on terms other than those of political Zionism, a year before its Congress met.

The church piece: a Protestant yes, a Catholic no. Protestant restorationists in Britain and the United States had pressed for a Jewish Palestine since 1839, when Lord Shaftesbury urged Palmerston to sponsor it. In March 1891 the Blackstone Memorial, signed by more than four hundred prominent Americans, asked President Benjamin Harrison to support a Jewish homeland in Palestine. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] Their motives were prophecy and empire, and most Jewish communal bodies of the period opposed or ignored them. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The papacy had lost the Papal States in 1870 and held a different theology. [cite:wiki_lateran_treaty] When Herzl went to Rome, in January 1904 by one source's date (another places the meeting between 1901 and 1903), Pope Pius X refused him on the ground that the Church could not support a Jewish restoration to a land central to its own theology while the Jews denied Christ. The Holy See did not recognize Israel until 30 December 1993, forty-five years after its founding. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] [cite:wiki_fundamental_agreement] That story belongs to later volumes and is told in [scene:vat_s01|the Vatican's refusal and its 45 years of non-recognition]. Two confessions held two positions, and both were political.

The sovereign piece: the Ottoman state. None of the pieces above held power in Palestine. The Ottoman Empire did, and it had already acted. In June 1882 it forbade the entry of foreign Jews and their purchase of land; in July 1882 it allowed Jews to settle anywhere in the empire except Palestine, provided they took Ottoman nationality. [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014] Through the late 1880s foreign Jews were admitted to Jerusalem only as pilgrims, for one month and later three. [cite:rs_rd0_cojs_moore1887] [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_may] An Ottoman note of 27 March 1894 barred Jewish immigrants from acquiring property in Palestine "for political reasons", for "the prevention of the permanent establishment in Palestine of Jewish immigrants". [cite:rs_rd0_frus1898_angell] When Herzl sought a charter in 1896, his diary records the Sultan's message that he could not sell even a foot of the land, because it belonged to his people. [cite:w_herzl]

The rules leaked because the empire was not sovereign in full over foreigners on its soil. Under the Capitulations and the protégé system, foreign Jews could live under European consular protection, and Britain had declared in 1847 that its consulates would protect Jews whose own states did not. [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014] Britain and the United States protested the restrictions on grounds of religious equality, Britain citing Article 62 of the Treaty of Berlin (1878), which declared that "in no part of the Ottoman Empire shall difference of religion be alleged against any person". [cite:rs_rd0_cojs_fo1893] [cite:rs_rd0_frus1888_may] By Barın's count, more than 50,000 Jews settled despite the bans; her study covers 1882 to 1920, but the passage that gives the figure does not say which years it counts. [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014] How the rules were made and broken is told in [scene:rd0_limits|the Ottoman limits and the first Arab responses].

The Palestinian piece: deputies, notables and a petition. Palestinian Arab society was not a blank on this board. In 1877 Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi of Jerusalem sat in the first Ottoman parliament as the sole deputy for the Jerusalem district and an opponent of Abdul Hamid II, until the Sultan suspended the chamber in February 1878 and ordered him out of Istanbul. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] The first reactions to Jewish settlement came from peasants, then from artisans and merchants whose livelihoods were touched. [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007] On 24 June 1891 Arab notables in Jerusalem asked the Grand Vizier to bar Russian Jews from entering Palestine and buying land; a 1978 review of Neville Mandel's work describes it instead as a telegram from leaders in Jerusalem and Jaffa. [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_djn_1978] In 1893 a "Commission for Sale" chaired by the mufti of Jerusalem, Muhammad Tahir al-Husayni, was blocking land sales to Jews. [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007] Whether this early opposition was political is disputed: a Jewish Agency overview doubts that the local opposition before 1891 had "any political connotations", while Beška finds that the mufti followed Jewish immigration from the 1880s and took an active stance against it. [cite:rs_rd0_jafi_opposition] [cite:rs_rd0_beska_2007]

The Jewish pieces: an old community, new colonies, a movement and its critics. The Jews of Palestine were not newcomers in 1882. Barın puts the Old Yishuv at about 24,000 in 1880, 13,900 of them in Jerusalem; another estimate gives 23,000. [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014_pop] [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki] Between 1882 and 1891 new settlements were founded from Rishon LeZion and Zikhron Ya'akov to Rehovot and Hadera. [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki] Behind them stood organized groups: the student pioneers of BILU from 1882, the Hovevei Zion societies that met at Kattowitz in 1884, and the word "Zionism", coined by Nathan Birnbaum in 1890. [cite:w_bilu] [cite:w_hovevei] [cite:w_birnbaum] Herzl's Der Judenstaat of February 1896 came last and gave these groups a program for a state. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Jewish critics stood on the same board. Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform of 1885 expected "neither a return to Palestine... nor the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state." [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] Vienna's preacher Adolf Jellinek answered Pinsker that "We are at home in Europe", and in the 1880s Jerusalem's Orthodox community reported Zionist newcomers to the Ottoman authorities. [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki_dissent]

Put together, the pieces show who could act on Palestine in 1896, and through what instrument. Only the Porte could decide who entered and who bought land. The foreign powers could blunt its decisions through consular protection and treaty. The Paris house and the Jewish Colonization Association had the money to settle people, the restorationists had a voice in London and Washington, and the Jerusalem notables could petition the Porte and, through the mufti's commission, slow sales on the ground. Herzl had a pamphlet.

The years in a ledger. Read year by year, the period from the pogroms to the Dreyfus conviction shows the same pieces moving. In 1881 pogroms broke out in the Russian Empire; of the roughly two million Jews who emigrated in the following decades, the great majority went to America, not Palestine. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] In 1882 Edmond de Rothschild began funding colonies. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] In 1885 Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform rejected a return. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] In 1891 Hirsch founded the Jewish Colonization Association with £2,000,000 of capital. [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] In 1894 Dreyfus was convicted, the event the ledger marks as Herzl's turn to political Zionism; his diaries show that turn developing over the following years. [cite:wiki_dreyfus]

The ledger's "Who objected" column records the emigrants who chose America and the Reform movement. It leaves blank two objections this chapter documents. In 1882, the year the ledger marks for Edmond's colonies, the Ottoman state forbade foreign Jews to enter and buy land. [cite:rs_rd0_barin_2014] In 1891, the year it marks for Hirsch, Jerusalem's notables petitioned against Jewish immigration and land purchase. [cite:rs_rd0_antizionism_wiki] Read with those two entries added, the ledger shows opposition from the sovereign in the first year of organized settlement, and from Jerusalem's notables within a decade.

What the board does and does not explain. To say the board existed is not to say it decided the outcome. Its pieces could move in different directions, and in 1896 several of the strongest were set against Herzl's project: the Sultan, Edmond de Rothschild, Reform Judaism and the Jerusalem notables, and the papacy would refuse Herzl after Basel. What the record supports is narrower and firmer: the structures that would fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state were older than the movement that asked for one. Some questions remain open in the sources used here. They give no decade-by-decade count of the Arab population of Palestine for this period. They do not total Edmond's spending on the colonies. They do not preserve the text or the signatories of the 1891 petition. And they cannot say how any of these pieces would have moved had the movement chosen Argentina instead.

The next part of this book opens at Basel in 1897 and follows how a political program became a state in fifty-one years, through Basel, Sykes-Picot, the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate, beginning with the First Zionist Congress. Zionism did not build this board. It entered a game whose pieces were already set, and the chapters that follow trace what it did with them.
