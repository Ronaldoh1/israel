The Jewish Colonial Trust: A Bank Owned a Pound at a Time

A movement seeking a charter from a great power also needed money. At the Second Zionist Congress in 1898 the delegates decided to create a bank, and on March 20, 1899, the Jewish Colonial Trust, in Hebrew Otẓar Hityashvuth Hayehudim, was incorporated in London under the English Companies Acts. Its stated aim was to raise the capital, and secure the "charter," that would make large-scale settlement in Palestine possible. Its authorized capital was £2,000,000, divided into £1 shares.[cite:jct_wiki]

The familiar account says Zionism was bankrolled by a handful of wealthy patrons such as Rothschild. The design of the Trust tells a different story. Its shares were priced deliberately low and could be paid for in installments, with plans running to as many as 55 to 80 payments. The aim was that ordinary people across Europe, working-class Jews as well as magnates, could become literal shareholders in the national project.[cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] [cite:bia_jewish_colonial_trust] Herzl bought the first 1,000 shares himself. The logic resembles a modern crowdfunding campaign: many small commitments instead of a few large ones.

Measured against its own target, the Trust fell far short. Roughly 250,000 shares were sold worldwide, raising about £250,000, or 12.5 percent of the authorized £2,000,000.[cite:jct_wiki] The installment design alone does not explain the gap, and the sources offer no full accounting of why the target was missed. But the sum was not the real innovation. The Trust proved that a political movement could be financed by a mass of small owners. That model would carry the movement for the next fifty years, through the Jewish National Fund's blue collection box, introduced two years later, and through Keren Hayesod.

Three founders stand out, each with a distinct role. Herzl supplied the political leadership. David Wolffsohn, a German-Jewish timber merchant and one of Herzl's closest associates, co-founded the Trust, chaired the Anglo-Palestine Company from its founding in 1902, and succeeded Herzl as president of the World Zionist Organization in 1905.[cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] Wolffsohn is remembered for something else as well: he designed the two-stripes-and-star flag adopted at Basel in 1897, which later became Israel's state flag. Jacobus Kahn, a Dutch-Jewish banker, was the third co-founder. His contribution was technical rather than political. He brought the banking expertise the new institution needed to work as a real financial body rather than a symbol.[cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] None of them acted alone; each institution had other colleagues as well.

A bank incorporated in London still had to reach Palestine. On February 27, 1902, the Trust founded the Anglo-Palestine Company as its banking arm on the ground.[cite:encyclopedia_jewish_colonial_trust] Here fundraising turned into settlement. The Company would finance land purchases directly and run a credit network offering long-term agricultural loans to settlers, a role traced in detail in [scene:s04j|the land purchase mechanism].

The later record complicates the picture of mass ownership. By 1899 the Trust had more than 100,000 shareholders, yet lasting control sat with a small founding bloc, through "founders' shares" carrying super-voting rights. Broad ownership and narrow control lived side by side inside the same institution. Nor was the Trust a failure in the longer run. It continued for decades and eventually became a major shareholder in Bank Leumi.

The Trust's importance, then, lies less in the sum it raised than in the structure it built. It gave political Zionism its first financial institution, a branch inside Palestine, and a funding model that spread the cost of the project across tens of thousands of ordinary participants while keeping its direction in few hands.
