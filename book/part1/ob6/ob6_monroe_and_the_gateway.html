Monroe and the Gateway

Robert Monroe, born in Indiana in 1915, was a radio broadcasting executive whose company produced network programmes from 1953. In 1958, after a series of strange sensations, he had what he described as his first experience of leaving his body. His 1971 book, Journeys Out of the Body, is credited with popularising the phrase out-of-body experience. [cite:cn_wiki_monroe]

He submitted himself to testing. In the mid-1960s the psychologist Charles Tart ran laboratory sessions in which subjects who said they could leave their bodies tried to read numbers placed out of their sight; Monroe did not succeed, though one of his reported experiences included accurate details. [cite:ob_psi_monroe]

Hemi-Sync and the Institute

Monroe then built a method. In 1975 he received the first of several patents for what he called Hemi-Sync, audio recordings that play two slightly different tones, one to each ear, so that the listener hears a slow beat matched to the brain-wave frequencies of relaxed and altered states. [cite:cn_wiki_monroe] [cite:ob_psi_monroe] The first Gateway programme ran in 1977 with forty participants, and the organisation became the Monroe Institute, an independent nonprofit from 1985. [cite:ob_psi_monroe] [cite:cn_wiki_monroe] How such beats produce altered states, the Psi Encyclopedia notes, remains unknown. [cite:ob_psi_monroe]

The Army's assessment, 1983

Military intelligence was interested. Joe McMoneagle, a leading remote viewer in the Army programme later known as Stargate, took a Gateway course in 1982 and said it made his results more reliable. [cite:ob_psi_monroe] [cite:fa_wiki_stargate] In 1983 an Army officer, Lieutenant Colonel Wayne M. McDonnell, wrote a 29-page "Analysis and Assessment of Gateway Process", which set out to "construct a scientifically valid and reasonably lucid model of how consciousness functions" and described reality as a hologram of energy and the Gateway's aim as moving awareness beyond time and space. [cite:ob_ia_gateway] [cite:ob_vice_gateway] [cite:ob_ifls_gateway] The report was declassified in 2003 and placed in the CIA's reading room, which is why it is often called a CIA report; page 25 was missing from the release and was later supplied to a reporter by the Monroe Institute. [cite:ob_vice_gateway]

The report went viral on social media in 2021. What it is matters: an officer's assessment of a training method, drawing on real research and on speculation, not a finding by an agency. A science writer who reviewed it concluded that it "shouldn't be treated as scientific". [cite:ob_ifls_gateway]

Loosh

In his second book, Far Journeys, published by Doubleday in 1985, Monroe went further than any laboratory could follow. He wrote that human beings produce an energy, most of all in states of fear, grief and despair, that non-human beings harvest; he called it "loosh". [cite:cn_wiki_monroe] [cite:cn_kirkus_monroe] He offered it as an account of his own experiences, not as a result he could test, and it should be read that way. The Canon and The Gnostics on this shelf have already set it beside the rulers of the Apocryphon of John. [cite:cn_wiki_monroe]
