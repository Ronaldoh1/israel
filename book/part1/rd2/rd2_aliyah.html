The Third Aliyah, 1919–1923

While Palestinian congresses petitioned against immigration and Britain wrote its policy into law, the immigration itself was under way. The wave of Jewish immigrants who arrived between 1919 and 1923 is known in Zionist history as the Third Aliyah. Its people founded the Histadrut, helped create the Haganah, and built new collective settlements. This section sets out who came, how many, under what rules, and what they built.

How many came is not a settled number. The Jewish Agency puts the Third Aliyah at about 35,000 immigrants. [cite:rs_ta_jafi] The Jewish Virtual Library puts it at approximately 40,000. [cite:rs_ta_jvl] A third figure can be built from the annual immigration statistics compiled in the United Nations study The Origins and Evolution of the Palestine Problem, which records Jewish immigration of 5,514 in 1920 (part of the year only), 9,149 in 1921, 7,844 in 1922 and 7,421 in 1923. [cite:rs_ta_un_origins] Those four years sum to about 29,900, but the series begins in September 1920 and so leaves out 1919 and the early months of 1920. [cite:rs_ta_un_origins] The gap between the official series and the larger estimates is at least partly a matter of those missing months; the sources do not explain the rest of the difference, and they do not say how the 35,000 and 40,000 estimates were reached.

For one year there is a primary count. The Mandatory government's report for 1922 records 8,128 immigrants in that year, "of these 7,844 were Jews," and emigration of 2,939. [cite:rs_ta_report1922] The 1922 figure in the UN series matches the government report exactly. The same UN tables record far smaller numbers of non-Jewish immigrants: 202 in 1920, 190 in 1921, 284 in 1922 and 570 in 1923. [cite:rs_ta_un_origins]

Where they came from is clearer. According to the Jewish Agency, 53 percent came from Russia and 36 percent from Poland, with about 800 from Western and Central Europe. [cite:rs_ta_jafi] Many were graduates of pioneering youth movements: He-Halutz in Russia and Poland, and Ha-Shomer ha-Tsa'ir in Galicia. [cite:rs_ta_jafi]

They came into a country whose population was counted by a British census in October 1922. The census recorded 757,182 people: 590,890 Muslims, 83,794 Jews and 73,024 Christians. [cite:rs_ta_report1922] By the end of the period, according to the Jewish Virtual Library, the Jewish community, the yishuv, numbered about 90,000. [cite:rs_ta_jvl] The census itself, its categories and its uses, is examined in [scene:cen_s01|the section on the 1922 census].

The rules governing their entry changed several times in these four years. In 1920 the Zionist Organization was permitted a quota of 16,500 immigrants a year, on conditions of self-support, health and a valid passport, according to the Center for Online Judaic Studies, which describes the 16,500 as heads of household. [cite:rs_ta_cojs_limits]

The first break came in May 1921. After the Jaffa riots, the High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel, temporarily prohibited Jewish immigration on 15 May 1921. The order caught about 150 immigrants who were already on their way. [cite:rs_ta_cojs_whitepaper] [cite:rs_ta_jweekly] In a speech on 3 June 1921 Samuel tied future Jewish immigration to the economic capacity of the country. The principle was written into the White Paper of 1922, which said that immigration must not exceed the country's "economic capacity to absorb new arrivals." [cite:rs_ta_cojs_whitepaper] That rule, and what it meant in practice, is the subject of [scene:s06a|the section on the Churchill White Paper]. The riots that prompted the suspension are covered in the section on the disturbances of 1920 and 1921.

What the immigrants built is the reason the Third Aliyah holds a large place in this history. Its members founded the Histadrut, the labor federation, and played a leading role in creating the Haganah. [cite:rs_ta_jafi] The Histadrut was founded at a convention in December 1920. [cite:rs_ta_ej_haganah] Its later history belongs to the next stage and is told in [scene:hl_s01|the section on the Histadrut]; the founding of the Haganah is told with the riots that prompted it.

The most distinctive creation of these years was Gedud ha-Avodah, the Labour Legion, also known as Gdud HaAvoda. It was founded in autumn 1920 by 80 Third Aliyah pioneers who were disciples of Joseph Trumpeldor, and it began with a contract to build the road between Tiberias and Tabgha. [cite:rs_ta_ej_gedud] From road work it moved to settlement: it founded Ein Harod in 1921 and Tel Yosef in 1923. At its height it had about 700 members, and more than 2,000 pioneers passed through it before it split, in 1923 and again in 1926. [cite:rs_ta_ej_gedud]

The collective settlement was not itself an invention of the Third Aliyah. Degania, often called the first kibbutz, was founded on 28 October 1910, during the Second Aliyah. [cite:rs_ta_degania] What these years added, in the record gathered here, was the Labour Legion's combination of contract labor and new settlement, and a labor federation founded alongside it.

Land was the other side of settlement. The Mandatory government's report for 1923 records Jewish land purchases of about 100,000 dunams in 1921, 45,000 in 1922 and 34,440 in 1923. [cite:rs_ta_report1923] The report gives the areas, not the buyers, the prices or the sellers, and the sources gathered for this section do not say who paid for these purchases or through which channels. The numbers show that purchases fell in each successive year of the period; they do not by themselves explain why.

The Third Aliyah looks different depending on where one stands. In Zionist history it is the wave of the pioneers who founded the Histadrut and helped create the Haganah. To the Palestinian congresses meeting in the same years, the immigration it represented was the thing they most wanted stopped: the Third Congress, in December 1920, demanded an end to Jewish immigration. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] British policy moved between these positions, setting a quota, suspending it after violence, and then tying it to an economic test. Both descriptions are accurate, and neither alone is the whole story.

Some questions the sources leave open. The size of the wave is uncertain within a range of roughly 30,000 to 40,000, depending on which years are counted and how. The sources do not say how many of the immigrants stayed, beyond the single year in which emigration was recorded, and they do not say whether the 16,500 quota was ever filled. Nor do they say how the shares of 53 and 36 percent were calculated against the competing totals. What they do establish is the shape of the period: a movement of organized pioneers, mainly from Russia and Poland, arriving under rules that Britain changed in response to events, and founding in those years institutions that later stages of this book return to.
