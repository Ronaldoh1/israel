Moscow Said Yes First

The Soviet vote for partition in November 1947 was not a last-minute surprise. It had been signaled six months earlier, before Washington had committed to anything. The familiar memory of these years casts Israel as a creation of American support, with the Soviet bloc hostile from the start. The diplomatic record runs almost the other way.

On 14 May 1947, the Soviet representative at the United Nations, Andrei Gromyko, addressed a special session of the General Assembly. He told the delegates that the USSR preferred a single Arab–Jewish state with equal rights for both peoples. If that proved impossible, he said, partition into two states was a justifiable alternative. [cite:israeled_gromyko_speech] [cite:israeled_gromyko_timeline] The statement reversed decades of Soviet opposition to Zionism. Analysts at the time and since have read the motive as a wish to remove British influence from the region. The USSR and its satellites voted for partition in November 1947.

The firmness of that commitment showed in the spring of 1948. In March, the United States moved to retreat from partition toward a trusteeship for Palestine. Moscow did not follow. At the Security Council the Soviet Union stood behind partition, and Gromyko argued that the rapid creation of two states was the only way to reduce bloodshed. [cite:yated_stalin_israel] [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel] At the moment American policy wavered, the Soviet position held.

Recognition followed the same pattern. Truman recognized the new state on 14 May 1948, the day of independence, but his recognition was de facto. On 17 May, three days later, the Soviet Union became the first state to grant Israel full legal, or de jure, recognition. [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel] In the same months, arms from Soviet-bloc Czechoslovakia reached the new state while an American embargo was in force, a supply line taken up later in this stage ([scene:cz_s01|the Czech arms]).

The convergence did not last. In December 1948 the Soviet bloc voted against UN Resolution 194 on the return of refugees, and from the 1950s Moscow turned toward the Arab states. [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] That later turn helps explain why the earlier record faded from popular memory.

The sources support a narrow conclusion, and it should be stated no more broadly than they allow. In 1947 and 1948 both Washington and Moscow backed the new state. Moscow did so earlier and, when the United States hesitated, more firmly, apparently in order to push Britain out of the region. This was a rare Cold War convergence of two rival powers acting for different reasons, not an alliance of shared purpose.
