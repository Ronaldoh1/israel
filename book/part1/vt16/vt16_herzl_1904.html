Herzl in Rome, January 1904

The record of the Holy See and Zionism begins with two conversations in Rome in January 1904, and both are known from one side only. Theodor Herzl, founder of the Zionist Organization, came to the Vatican to ask for support, or at least for the absence of opposition, at a time when the Holy See held no territory of its own and the popes had not left the Vatican since 1870. [scene:vt2_roman_question|Part Two] explains that position. [cite:vt2_wiki_prisoner] Palestine was an Ottoman province, and the interests the Holy See had there were old, specific and written down. This section sets out those interests, the two conversations as Herzl recorded them, and what the record can and cannot carry.

What Rome had in Palestine before Herzl

The Latin presence in Ottoman Palestine had three parts. The oldest was the Custody of the Holy Land, a Franciscan body founded in the fourteenth century that had long been the main Latin representative at the shrines. The second was the Latin Patriarchate of Jerusalem, re-established in 1847, in the historian Paolo Zanini's words, "to counter the influence of an Anglican-Lutheran diocese", which gave the Holy See a bishop in Jerusalem answering directly to Rome. The third was a foreign protector: "France had protected the Latin and Eastern Catholic communities since the sixteenth century." [cite:vt16_zanini_jq] The legal status of the Christian holy sites in Jerusalem was part of the wider Eastern Question that the European powers argued over throughout the nineteenth century. [cite:vt16_zanini_jq] The Ottoman background is set out in [scene:rd1_ottoman|Road to 1948].

Sergio Minerbi, whose English-language study was written from unpublished diplomatic archives, describes the Holy See before 1914 as balancing Ottoman rulers, rival Christian churches and the Catholic powers that claimed to protect the holy places. [cite:vt16_minerbi_book] Those were the interests in play when a Zionist leader asked for an audience: who held the shrines, who protected Catholic institutions, and who would govern Jerusalem.

The two conversations

Herzl's audience with Pius X was arranged by Count Berthold Dominik Lippay, an Austrian papal portrait painter whom Herzl had met in Venice. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] Before it, according to Minerbi, Herzl put his proposal to the Cardinal Secretary of State, Rafael Merry del Val, on 22 January 1904: the holy places would be given extraterritorial status. Merry del Val replied that the holy places could not be separated from the Holy Land. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] The audience with the pope followed on 25 January. [cite:dv1_ej_piusx] Herzl's diary entry dated 26 January 1904 opens "Yesterday I was with the Pope", and an editor's note gives the audience as about twenty-five minutes. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl]

In Herzl's account, he "recited my little piece about extraterritorialization, res sacrae extra commercium", the idea that sacred things should stand outside ordinary commerce and sovereignty. The pope's first answer was political: "We cannot give approval to this movement. We cannot prevent the Jews from going to Jerusalem—but we could never sanction it." When Herzl said "We are not asking for Jerusalem, but for Palestine—only the secular land", the pope answered "We cannot be in favor of it." [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] On the city itself Herzl recorded the pope as saying that "The soil of Jerusalem, if it was not always sacred, has been sanctified by the life of Jesus Christ", and that Jerusalem "must not get into the hands of the Jews." [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl]

The second answer was theological. Herzl recorded the pope as saying that "The Jewish religion was the foundation of our own; but it was superseded by the teachings of Christ", and that "The Jews have not recognized our Lord, therefore we cannot recognize the Jewish people." He closed with "Non possumus", the formula of papal refusal, and said that if settlers came to Palestine "we shall have churches and priests ready to baptize all of you." [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] Herzl also noted that Lippay knelt and kissed the pope's hand and had told him to do the same, and that he did not. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl]

Where the record is held, and how strong it is

The diary is Herzl's own, written the day after the audience and published in English in 1960 in Raphael Patai's English edition of the complete diaries, where the entry runs to pages 1601–1605. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] It is a primary document of what Herzl wrote. As evidence of what Pius X said, it is one party's account of a private conversation, and the reference works that report the audience say that it rests on Herzl's diaries. [cite:dv1_ej_piusx] The Merry del Val meeting is reported by Minerbi from his research; the volume has not seen a Holy See minute of either conversation. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] What strengthens the diary is that nothing in it flatters its author: Herzl recorded a refusal, not a success, and the refusal matches the line Merry del Val took three days earlier and the line the Holy See took in writing in the decades that followed (see [scene:vt16_benedict_mandate|the next section]).

Two refusals in one audience

The audience fused two different claims, and keeping them apart is the first discipline of this part. One was a political judgment about a movement and about who should govern Jerusalem and the shrines. The other was a theological statement about the Jewish people. The political judgment had a long life: it reappears in the Holy See's written diplomacy from 1917 to 1949, always tied to the holy places, the rights of Catholic institutions and the Catholic communities of Palestine. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] The theological statement did not last in that form. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate of 1965 and the Holy See's 1985 Notes separated the Church's teaching on the Jewish people from its position on a state, the latter saying that Israel's existence and political options were to be seen "not in a perspective which is in itself religious" but by "the common principles of international law." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] [scene:vt17_nostra_aetate_notes|Part Seventeen] follows that change. This volume does not read the 1904 theology as the Holy See's teaching today, and it does not read the Holy See's later political position on Israel as a judgment about Jews.

Minerbi's reading of the period is blunter than the documents alone require. His publisher's summary says the Holy See preferred British rule to a Jewish state and that its opposition drew both on theological doctrines about Judaism and on Christian antisemitism of the time. [cite:vt16_minerbi_book] The second half of that reading is an interpretation of motive by a named historian; the first half is visible in the documents set out in the next two sections. Other studies used in this part are Andrej Kreutz's Vatican Policy on the Palestinian-Israeli Conflict (1990), Silvio Ferrari's study of the Vatican and Israel from the Second World War to the Gulf War (1991), and, for the Israeli side after 1948, Uri Bialer's Cross on the Star of David (2005), built on declassified Israeli files. [cite:vt16_kreutz] [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] [cite:vt16_bialer]

What 1904 contributes to the front test

Measured against the tests set out in [scene:vt1_front_test|Part One], the 1904 record is the first entry on the side of a real refusal. The private conversation, as recorded by the man who had every reason to report success, matched the public position; nothing passed from the Holy See to the movement; and the refusal cost Herzl the support he had come to ask for. It is also a thin record: one diary and one historian's archival summary. Its weight lies in how closely the later documents, written by different officials for different audiences over forty years, repeat its political half. Those documents begin with the next Zionist envoy to Rome, Nahum Sokolow, in the spring of 1917. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] [cite:vt16_ej_vatican]
