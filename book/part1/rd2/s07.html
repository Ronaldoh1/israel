The Mandate for Palestine: The Text and the Polity It Created

The document that wrote a British wartime letter into a League of Nations instrument ends with a place and a date: "Done at London the twenty-fourth day of July, one thousand nine hundred and twenty-two." [cite:rs_md_avalon] Behind that line lay two years of diplomacy. At the San Remo Conference in April 1920 the victorious Allies assigned Palestine to Britain, and in July 1922 the Council of the League of Nations approved the text under which Britain would hold it. [cite:san_remo_wiki] [cite:mandate_text] How a letter of policy became an instrument of the League, and the argument over whether that instrument honored the League's own Covenant, is told in [scene:blg_s01|By What Right?]. This section reads the instrument itself: what its articles said, when it actually took effect, what kind of state apparatus it brought into being, and what it gave and withheld.

The League of Nations, founded in 1920 under the Treaty of Versailles, was the authority in whose name the arrangement was made. [cite:san_remo_wiki] The mandate system was Woodrow Wilson's attempt to separate a temporary trusteeship, meant to lead toward self-government, from outright colonial annexation. In practice, mandates were administered essentially as colonies. [cite:san_remo_wiki] [cite:mandate_text] That judgment concerns Palestine; the record summarized here does not show that every mandate across the world was run the same way. The League itself did not survive the Second World War, and its successor, the United Nations, inherited its records. [cite:san_remo_wiki]

What the articles said. Article 1 gave the Mandatory, Britain, "full powers of legislation and of administration." [cite:rs_md_un_origins] Everything else in the text operated inside that grant.

Article 2 set out the Mandatory's core obligations, and it set out three of them in one sentence. Britain was responsible for conditions that would secure the Jewish national home; for the development of self-governing institutions; and for "safeguarding the civil and religious rights of all the inhabitants of Palestine." [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_md_un_origins] The national home and the protection of rights were both binding. The text did not say how they were to be weighed against each other if they collided.

Article 4 provided that "an appropriate Jewish agency shall be recognized as a public body" to advise the Administration, and it recognized the Zionist Organization as that agency for as long as its constitution was deemed appropriate. [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_md_un_origins] The League later recorded that in 1929 the Zionist Organisation was replaced in this role by the "Jewish Agency for Palestine." [cite:rs_md_lon1945] The articles summarized here name no equivalent public body for the Arab majority. Britain's later offer of an "Arab Agency" analogous to the Jewish Agency, with members chosen by the High Commissioner, and its rejection in October 1923, belong to the story of the Legislative Council of 1922–23. [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_arabagency]

Article 6 turned the national home from a principle into a working instruction. The Administration was to "facilitate Jewish immigration under suitable conditions" and to encourage close settlement by Jews on the land, while ensuring that other sections of the population are not prejudiced. [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_md_lon1945] Read closely, in the article that governs immigration and settlement the majority of the country's people appear in that form: as other sections of the population, protected against prejudice but not named.

Article 7 provided the basis for a Palestinian citizenship, and part of it concerned the acquisition of Palestinian citizenship by Jews. [cite:rs_md_transjordan] Three years later, that article became the legal ground of the Palestinian Citizenship Order, discussed below.

Article 11 dealt with the country's wealth. It allowed the Administration to place natural resources and public works under public ownership or control, and to make arrangements with the Jewish agency for such works, with the profits that could be distributed capped. [cite:rs_md_avalon] The clause named the Jewish agency as a body that could be brought in to develop public resources, and it limited the profits that could be taken out of them.

Article 22 made English, Arabic and Hebrew the official languages of Palestine, and required that any Arabic inscription on stamps or money be repeated in Hebrew, and any Hebrew inscription in Arabic. [cite:rs_md_avalon] The rule is small, but it fixed in law that the coins and stamps of the new polity would speak to both communities at once.

Article 25 concerned the lands east of the river. It let the Mandatory, with the consent of the League Council, postpone or withhold the application of provisions of the Mandate in the territories between the Jordan and Palestine's eastern boundary, so long as nothing was done that conflicted with Articles 15, 16 and 18. [cite:rs_md_avalon] Britain used that power within two months of the text's approval. The memorandum of September 1922 that removed the national-home provisions from Transjordan, one stage in a separation completed by 1923, is the subject of its own section, with the long political afterlife of that separation.

Set beside the letter it absorbed, the Mandate shows what changed in five years. The 1917 declaration expressed a government's favour and promised its best efforts. The 1922 text made the national home a legal responsibility of the governing power, added an immigration duty and a recognized Jewish public body, and widened the protected group from "existing non-Jewish communities" to "all the inhabitants." It also added a promise the letter had not contained: self-governing institutions, with no date and no form specified.

When the Mandate took effect, and the dispute over it. The approval of July 1922 was conditional. The League Council approved the terms of the Palestine and Syria mandates together, pending an agreement between France and Italy over Syria. [cite:rs_md_lon1923] At its meeting in Geneva on 29 September 1923, the Council took note that the France–Italy agreement had been reported and that the two mandates had "automatically and simultaneously entered into force." [cite:rs_md_lon1923] The United Nations later used that date. [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj]

That date has been challenged. John Quigley, writing in July 2026, argues that the Council only noted, in the future tense, that the mandates would enter into force, and that the Secretary-General, Drummond, changed the tense in the circular that reported the meeting. [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] Quigley adds a second argument: Britain lacked title to the territory at that point, because the Treaty of Lausanne, signed on 24 July 1923, entered into force only on 6 August 1924. [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] The case for the conventional date rests on the League's own record, circular C.L.101.1923.VI, which states that the mandates had "automatically and simultaneously entered into force," and on the United Nations' later reliance on it. [cite:rs_md_lon1923] [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] The case against rests on the minutes' wording and on the sequence of the Ottoman peace treaty. The dispute is about the legal moment the Mandate began, not about whether Britain governed: British administration of Palestine was already in place, under a High Commissioner, from 1920.

The government the text created. Britain administered Palestine under the Mandate until 1948. It issued passports, minted the currency, printed the stamps, ran the police and the courts, operated the railways and the ports, and staffed a civil service that employed Arabs and Jews alike, all under a High Commissioner headquartered in Jerusalem. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] Beneath him sat a postal system and a customs and taxation apparatus that functioned for the whole of the Mandate's span. The Mandate had a legal text, published borders and recognized standing in international law. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki]

The first High Commissioner, appointed in 1920 by the Cabinet that had issued the Balfour Declaration, was Sir Herbert Samuel, a British Jew and a committed Zionist. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel_hc] [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] One reading holds that his appointment embedded the declaration's contradiction in the administration itself: a single government was charged at once with fostering a Jewish national home and safeguarding the rights of an existing Arab majority, two obligations that, on this reading, could not both be honored. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] His tenure and those of the six men who followed him are traced in [scene:s07a2|the High Commissioners].

Citizens, passports, money. The Palestinian Citizenship Order of 1925, which came into force on 1 August 1925, gave legal form to a Palestinian citizenship under Article 7. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] Under it, about 70,000 brown passports were issued over the years of the Mandate, each stamped "BRITISH PASSPORT / PALESTINE." [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] The word "British" on the cover is what later led casual observers to misremember them. The holders were citizens of Palestine, Arab and Jewish. They used the documents to cross borders, conduct trade, study abroad and perform the hajj. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] The Palestine pound was minted from 1927 and circulated as legal tender; Palestine issued its own postage stamps, ran its own courts, and registered its own births, deaths, marriages and land titles. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki]

The historian Lauren Banko's study The Invention of Palestinian Citizenship, 1918–1947 traces how this status was built. It was not an informal identity but a specific legal status created by British administrative order, with its own bureaucratic history, population records, and legal disputes over who qualified. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] Many of the passports survive. They are held today by the descendants of Palestinian families in Ramallah and Haifa, in the refugee camps of Lebanon and Jordan, and across the diaspora, as evidence that their grandparents were citizens of a recognized polity. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship]

The people of the polity. By one general account of the Mandate, its population at the start was roughly 90 per cent Arab and 10 per cent Jewish, about 600,000 Arabs and 60,000 Jews. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] An account of the 1922 White Paper puts the Jewish population at about 80,000 in 1922. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] Both are round estimates. The one count from these years is the British census of October 1922, which recorded 757,182 people: 590,890 Muslims, 83,794 Jews and 73,024 Christians. [cite:rs_ta_report1922] Its figures are set out with [scene:rd2_aliyah|the Third Aliyah]. The census total is well above the first estimate, and its Jewish count is close to the second. By 1947, the last full year of the Mandate, the figures had moved to roughly 1.3 million Arabs and 600,000 Jews. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] Jewish growth came substantially through waves of immigration from Europe between the wars, heaviest in the 1930s as antisemitism in Europe intensified. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] The start and end points are documented here; the year-by-year course of that change, and the immigration and land machinery that drove it, belong to later chapters.

Land ownership moved more slowly than population. The Jewish National Fund, founded in 1901, and the other Zionist institutions had acquired about 9.8 per cent of Mandate Palestine by purchase by 1948. [cite:jnf_wiki] The same source's further point is that most of the territory of the state that emerged in 1948 came not through purchase but through the war and displacement of that year. [cite:jnf_wiki] The two measures are taken against different wholes, Mandate Palestine in one case and the territory of the new state in the other, so they should not be read as a single sum. What the comparison does establish is that fifty years of organized, funded purchase left Jewish institutions owning under a tenth of the country's land on the eve of statehood.

The claim and the record. A familiar line holds that Palestine was an empty desert until Jewish pioneers made it bloom. The administrative record of the Mandate answers the first half of that sentence directly. The territory Britain governed had a High Commissioner, a treasury, a passport office, courts, a postal service, a registry of births and land titles, a currency, and a population numbering in the hundreds of thousands. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] [cite:san_remo_1920] The Mandate text itself legislated for "all the inhabitants of Palestine" and for "other sections of the population" whose rights were not to be prejudiced, wording that presupposes people already living there. [cite:rs_md_avalon] None of this asserts a modern nation-state before 1948, or settles any present political claim. It establishes something narrower: that a functioning civic polity called Palestine, with a documented citizenry, existed for a quarter-century before 1948. That matters for what followed. If the land was empty, no one was displaced; if it had citizens, the events of 1948 require a different accounting.

The record also supports the strongest form of the Zionist reading of the same document. The Mandate was not a British favour that could be quietly withdrawn. It was an instrument approved by the Council of the League of Nations that wrote the national home into binding articles: Britain was responsible for securing it, was obliged to facilitate Jewish immigration and close settlement, and was required to work with a recognized Jewish public body. [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_md_un_origins] For the Zionist movement, that was international legitimacy in the most formal sense then available. The Arab leadership's reading pointed to what the framework did not give: in February 1922 the Palestine Arab Delegation demanded a "national independent Government" and said the Balfour Declaration could not be a basis for discussion, and the Colonial Office replied that "the creation at this stage of a national Government would preclude the fulfilment of the pledge." [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] Both readings rest on the same articles. On one reading, the disagreement is over which of Article 2's obligations governed when they could not all be met at once.

What the record leaves open. The passport count is an approximation, and the record does not give the number that survive today. It does not resolve present arguments about citizenship, return or statehood; it shows only that a formal, legal Palestinian citizenship existed under British administration decades before 1948. The two estimates of the Jewish population at the Mandate's start, 60,000 and about 80,000, are not reconciled with each other or with the census count of 83,794. Whether the Mandate entered into force on 29 September 1923 remains, as of the scholarship cited, contested. And the text never answered its own central question. It bound Britain to a national home, to self-governing institutions and to the rights of all inhabitants, and it named no rule for choosing among them. The documents of 1922 that tried to supply such a rule, the Churchill White Paper and the Transjordan memorandum, are where the next sections turn.
