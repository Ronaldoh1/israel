Independence, the Nakba and the Czech Guns

At 4 p.m. on May 14, 1948, hours before the British Mandate expired at midnight, David Ben-Gurion declared the establishment of the State of Israel. [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal] The United States granted de facto recognition within eleven minutes, a timing arranged by Clark Clifford over State Department objection. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The Haganah became the Israel Defense Forces, and the Mossad was established the following year. War with the neighboring Arab states began. Two familiar accounts of what followed have shaped memory of 1948: that Palestinians left voluntarily on the instructions of Arab radio, and that America created and armed Israel. The record contradicts both.

The second can be dealt with briefly, since the arms pipeline is traced earlier in this stage ([scene:cz_s01|the Czech arms]). The United States imposed an arms embargo on all parties to the 1948 war. Truman's recognition was political; the embargo stayed in place. [cite:morasha_czech_arms] The weapons came from communist Czechoslovakia, with Soviet permission: ex-German Mauser rifles, Messerschmitt Bf 109G fighters and ZK 383 submachine guns, flown by C-54 airlift from Žatec airfield in Operation Balak. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_shipments] Ben-Gurion credited them with saving the state. [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal] Moscow's motive was the same anti-British calculation that had shaped its vote on partition, and Soviet-Israeli relations shifted markedly in the following years.

The first account requires more. Between 700,000 and 750,000 Palestinians were displaced in the war of 1947 to 1949; Benny Morris estimates 700,000 and Walid Khalidi 750,000. About 500 villages were depopulated or destroyed, and Khalidi's All That Remains (1992) documents each one by name. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] [cite:walid_khalidi_all_that_remains] Palestinians call these events the Nakba, Arabic for "catastrophe." Much of the documentation comes from Israeli historians, among them Morris, Ilan Pappé, Tom Segev and Avi Shlaim.

The record names specific episodes. At Deir Yassin in April 1948 ([scene:ma_s01|the massacres of 1948]), according to Yehuda Slutsky's official Haganah history, the attackers announced the killings by radio to nearby villages to induce panic, and many families in those villages fled in the weeks that followed. Ben-Gurion later noted in his diaries that Deir Yassin "had a decisive effect" on Arab flight. In July 1948, at Lydda and Ramla, an expulsion of approximately 50,000 civilians was carried out on Yitzhak Rabin's written orders, without Irgun or Lehi involvement. [cite:morris_1948] The broader scholarly record describes a mix of causes, village by village: direct military expulsions, massacres and the fear they spread, and the collapse of Palestinian society under war. Ilan Pappé argues in The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine (2006) that Plan Dalet meets the UN definition of ethnic cleansing; Morris, who is politically to Pappé's right and does not use the same terminology, nevertheless documents the same operational facts. [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] On that comparison, one account in the sources holds that the debate is over the term, not the facts. The sources also record a disagreement about intent, discussed in the section on Plan Dalet: Walid Khalidi and Pappé read the plan as a blueprint for deliberately removing the Arab population, while Morris reads it as a military plan whose village provisions left expulsions to local commanders rather than ordering them centrally. Both readings are attributed to their authors here; neither is settled.

The radio story itself was tested in 1961. The Irish journalist Erskine Childers examined the BBC monitoring records, which had transcribed Middle Eastern broadcasts throughout 1948, looking for the alleged evacuation orders. He published his findings in The Spectator: [cite:spectator_childers_1961]

There was not a single order, or appeal, or suggestion about evacuation from Palestine from any Arab radio station, inside or outside Palestine, in 1948. There is a repeated monitored record of Arab appeals, even flat orders, to the civilians of Palestine to stay put.

Morris, reviewing archives decades later, found no evidence for the broadcasts in Israeli, Arab or British records. [cite:decolonizepalestine_childers] Childers's finding is narrow: it does not explain every individual departure, but it removes a blanket explanation that the broadcast record does not support.

The territorial figures show what the war decided. Before 1948, Jewish ownership, purchased parcel by parcel over decades, amounted to under a tenth of Mandate Palestine; the sources give figures of roughly seven and 9.8 percent. By the 1949 armistice, about 78 percent of Mandate territory was under Israeli control, against the roughly 55 percent allocated by Resolution 181. The difference was made not by purchase but by war and displacement.

Ben-Gurion stands at the center of both halves of this record. He led a community that included Holocaust survivors to statehood against invading armies, and he was the executive authority over a founding that included the expulsions. Both are documented, much of it in Israeli sources. The same holds for the wider frame. The Zionist claim that Europe's Jews needed a place of safety after 1945 was not false; the cost of meeting it fell on Palestinians, who bore no responsibility for Europe's crimes.

Two further details complete the founding year. Jewish communities had lived alongside Arab populations across the Muslim world for over a millennium, from Baghdad's community of 140,000 to Damascus, Cairo, Sana'a and Fez; this was not a utopia, and pogroms occurred. The exodus of roughly 850,000 Jews from Arab lands between 1948 and 1972 had multiple documented causes, including active Israeli and Jewish Agency recruitment operations. [cite:morris_righteous_victims] And on October 28, 1948, the Provisional Council adopted as the state flag the Magen David between blue stripes, the design of the 1897 Zionist Congress at Basel, placing the ancient Menorah on the state seal. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] [cite:wjc_flag_unveiled_1948]

The displacement was not resolved. UN Resolution 194 of December 11, 1948 affirmed a Palestinian right of return; it has never been implemented, and Israel has refused it on the grounds that it would end the Jewish demographic majority. The descendants of the displaced, registered with UNRWA, number some 6 million today, most of them stateless. [cite:morris_1948]
