The Other Homelands

Palestine was not the only land the movement and its benefactors considered. Two alternatives reached the stage of land and settlers, and both are documented well enough to be measured on the same six things the state needed. The question each answers is the one that is often asked: with more room elsewhere, could a Jewish home have been built more easily and at less cost?

Argentina. In Der Judenstaat, published on 14 February 1896, Herzl named two possible lands, Argentina and Palestine. He warned that either would fail if settled by slow "infiltration," because immigration was pointless without a sovereign right to keep it going. [cite:a_wp_judenstaat] Argentina already had a Jewish settlement project. Baron Maurice de Hirsch had founded the Jewish Colonization Association in 1891 to move Jews out of the Russian Empire and settle them as farmers there, and spent nearly his entire fortune on it. When he died in 1896 there were 6,757 colonists. [cite:a_wp_hirsch_arg] By 1923 the Association had settled 30,725 people on 590,000 hectares. [cite:a_jta_ica24] That was more than three times all the land Jews held in Palestine on the eve of statehood, about 173,000 hectares, of which the Jewish National Fund held 936,000 dunams. [cite:a_wp_jnf] Land was not the limit in Argentina. Its colonies peaked at over 20,000 colonists in 1930. Their progress was "hampered by insufficient land for extensive cultivation and the unfavorable location of many of the colonies," and by 1966 only 8,000 Jews remained in them. [cite:a_jvl_ica]

What Argentina never offered was what Herzl said was essential. It was an independent republic that welcomed European immigrants as citizens. No Argentine government offered the colonies self-rule, and none was sought. Nor was Argentina free of the violence the colonists had fled. In January 1919, during the suppression of a general strike in Buenos Aires, the Argentine Patriotic League singled out the Jewish neighbourhood of Once, in what has been called the first pogrom in the Americas. [cite:a_jc_tragic]

British East Africa. In 1903, after the Kishinev pogrom, the Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain offered land for Jewish settlement in British East Africa. It was later remembered as the "Uganda" offer, but it lay on the Uasin Gishu plateau, in what is now Kenya, between Lake Nakuru, Kisumu and Mount Elgon. It covered about 16,300 square kilometres. [cite:a_ea_kenya] That is smaller than Mandatory Palestine, about 26,000 square kilometres. [cite:a_wp_mandpal] On 26 August 1903 the Sixth Zionist Congress voted 295 to 178 only to send a commission to look at it. Herzl presented it as a temporary refuge for Jews in danger in Russia, not a replacement for the Land of Israel. [cite:a_jvl_uganda] The commission split two to one against. British settlers opposed the plan, and the East African Standard denounced "pauper alien Jews" turning the highlands into "Jewganda." [cite:a_ea_kenya] The plateau was Maasai grazing land. Under agreements of 1904 and 1911 the British moved the Maasai off much of the Rift Valley and the Uasin Gishu to make room for European settlers; over a million acres had been leased or sold by the end of 1905. [cite:a_unpo_maasai] The Seventh Zionist Congress rejected the offer in 1905. Israel Zangwill led the minority out and founded the Jewish Territorial Organization. It searched for a territory elsewhere, in Angola, Galveston and other places, found none, and dissolved in 1925. In 1944 Australia vetoed the last such plan, in the Kimberley. [cite:a_wp_ito]

The comparison answers the question differently than it is usually asked. Where land was plentiful, there was no sovereignty to be had: Argentina offered citizenship, and the result was a large community, not a state. Where sovereignty was offered, by the one power able to offer it, the land was smaller, already inhabited, and contested by the offering power's own settlers. The movement did not choose Palestine over an empty and willing alternative. On the record no such alternative existed. Those who went looking for one, the territorialists, spent twenty years and found no ruler willing to give land.

Nor would either alternative have been free of the conflict that Palestine produced. Palestine in 1945 had about 1,061,000 Muslims, 136,000 Christians and 554,000 Jews. [cite:a_wp_mandpal] In February 1947 Bevin told the Commons that the two sides' positions were an "irreconcilable conflict of principles." [cite:a_cojs_47] East Africa's land was the Maasai's, and its settlers fought the offer before a single colonist arrived. Argentina avoided a war over sovereignty only because sovereignty never changed hands.

A last question sits underneath the choice of Palestine: whose land it was to return to. The record holds two facts that are often set against each other, though both are true. A Jewish community never left. About 24,000 Jews lived in Palestine in 1882, among them Arabic-speaking Jews, the Musta'arabim, whose communities predated the coming of Islam, alongside Sephardic communities from the late Mamluk and early Ottoman periods and Ashkenazi immigrants of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [cite:m_wp_oldyishuv] Most of those who built the state, though, came from Europe, and genetic studies describe the largest group among them, the Ashkenazim, as a mixture of Middle Eastern and European ancestry. The Middle Eastern share is most likely Levantine and is estimated at anywhere from about a fifth to over half, depending on the method. The same studies find Ashkenazi, Sephardi and Mizrahi Jews closer to one another than to the peoples they lived among, and count Palestinians, Bedouin and Druze among their closest genetic neighbours. [cite:m_wp_genetics] Ancestry does not decide a claim to land, and this book does not use it to. What the record shows is narrower: neither the community that stayed nor the diaspora that returned was a stranger to the region, and the people already living there in 1947 had their own ties to it.
