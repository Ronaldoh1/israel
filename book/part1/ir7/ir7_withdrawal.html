The Archive

On the evening of 30 April 2018, at the Defence Ministry in Tel Aviv, Netanyahu gave a televised presentation. "Tonight, I'm here to tell you one thing: Iran lied. Big time," he said. He described "Fifty-five thousand pages. Another 55,000 files on 183 CDs," material he said Israel had obtained "a few weeks ago" from vaults in Tehran, and said they documented Project Amad, a secret Iranian weapons programme meant to produce the equivalent of "five Hiroshima bombs to be put on ballistic missiles." He said Iran had "lied again in 2015, when it didn't come clean to the IAEA," that "the Iran deal ... is based on lies," and that "in a few days' time, President Trump will decide" what to do with it. [cite:ir7_pmo_archive]

The US Secretary of State, Mike Pompeo, said the documents showed "that Iran had a secret nuclear-weapons program for years." The British government said it had "never been naive about Iran," and a German spokesman said Iran's past work was the very reason the deal had been signed. The IAEA said that its 2015 report had already found "no credible indications of activities in Iran relevant to the development of a nuclear explosive device after 2009," and that its Board had closed the matter. Iran's foreign minister, Mohammad Javad Zarif, called the presentation "a rehash of old allegations" and noted its "coordinated timing." [cite:ir7_rferl_archive] The archive was genuine intelligence about a weapons effort that the IAEA and US intelligence already believed had existed. Part Four sets out that history. [scene:ir4_programme|The atom] What the presentation did not show was any breach of the JCPOA's limits, which the IAEA was reporting Iran had kept. [cite:ir7_iaea_may18]

The Withdrawal and Maximum Pressure

Eight days later, on 8 May 2018, President Donald Trump announced "that the United States will withdraw from the Iran nuclear deal." He said that "we cannot prevent an Iranian nuclear bomb under the decaying and rotten structure of the current agreement," that "At the heart of the Iran deal was a giant fiction," and that its sunset clauses were "totally unacceptable." He also said that it "fails to address" Iran's ballistic missiles. He cited the documents Israel had just published and promised "the highest level of economic sanction." [cite:ir7_toi_trump] Netanyahu called the decision "correct, wise and courageous." [cite:ir7_ynet_praise] None of his stated reasons was a finding that Iran had broken the deal's limits.

On 21 May 2018, at the Heritage Foundation, Pompeo set out twelve demands. Iran was to stop all enrichment, give the IAEA "unqualified access to all sites throughout the country," and end its support for Hezbollah, Hamas and the Houthis. He promised that "These will be the strongest sanctions in history by the time we are done." President Hassan Rouhani replied, "Who are you to decide for Iran and the world?" [cite:ir7_voa_pompeo] The sanctions came back in two waves. The first, in August 2018, covered dollar purchases, gold, metals and the car industry. The second, on 5 November 2018, covered oil, the National Iranian Oil Company, shipping, insurance and foreign banks dealing with Iran's central bank. [cite:ir7_ac_waves] On 22 April 2019 Pompeo announced that the waivers allowing eight countries to keep buying Iranian oil would end on 2 May. "Our goal has been to get countries to cease importing Iranian oil entirely," he said. [cite:ir7_ap_waivers]

Iran's Breaches, Step by Step

For a year after the withdrawal Iran stayed within the limits, as the IAEA reported. [cite:ir7_ap_may19] On 8 May 2019, the anniversary of the withdrawal, Iran's Supreme National Security Council announced that it would no longer observe the caps on its stocks of enriched uranium and heavy water. It cited paragraphs 26 and 36 of the JCPOA, the clauses on reimposed sanctions and dispute resolution, and gave the remaining parties "sixty days to fulfil their obligations, especially in banking and oil fields." [cite:ir7_snsc_may19] On 1 July 2019 Zarif said Iran had "crossed the 300-kilogramme limit," and the IAEA confirmed it. Zarif said Iran was responding to the American withdrawal and would reverse the step once the European parties met their obligations. The White House answered that "maximum pressure" would continue and called for "no enrichment for Iran." Netanyahu called the step a "significant step toward making a nuclear weapon." [cite:ir7_aje_300kg]

Iran then began enriching to 4.5 per cent in July 2019 and restarted enrichment at Fordow in November. On 5 January 2020 it said it would observe no limit on the number of its centrifuges. Zarif described every step as "reversible upon effective implementation of reciprocal obligations." Iran's demand throughout was that the remaining parties find a way for it to sell oil and use the banking system. [cite:ir7_aca_jan20] In February 2021 Iran stopped applying the Additional Protocol and the deal's extra monitoring. [cite:ir7_crs_snapback] On 16 April 2021, days after an act of sabotage at Natanz that Tehran blamed on Israel and that Israel did not claim, Iran began enriching to 60 per cent, its highest level ever, and said the purpose was medical. [cite:ir7_aje_60pc] Talks in Vienna to restore the deal began that same month. They produced a European "final" text on 8 August 2022, to which Iran responded with demands for guarantees on sanctions and on the IAEA's open safeguards questions. [cite:ir7_afp_vienna] The deal was never restored. In August 2025 Britain, France and Germany triggered the snapback, and UN sanctions were reimposed on 27 September 2025. Iran, Russia and China rejected the step as invalid. [cite:ir7_crs_snapback]

Iran's stated reason for the breaches is a matter of record: it said the other parties had not delivered the sanctions relief the deal promised once the United States reimposed sanctions. The deal's own text gave each side a remedy if the other failed to perform, and that is the remedy Iran claimed to be using. The breaches were also real, and they went well beyond the first step, to 60 per cent enrichment, a level for which the European Union saw no civilian need. [cite:ir7_aje_60pc] Both facts stand.

By May 2025, a month before Israel's strikes, the IAEA counted 408.6 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 per cent and a total enriched stockpile of 9,247.6 kilograms. Some 21,900 centrifuges were installed, about 14,689 of them advanced models, and Fordow had been producing 60 per cent uranium since November 2022. The agency said it had lost continuity of knowledge over Iran's production of centrifuges and other items, and that this could not be restored. [cite:ir7_isis_may25] After the June 2025 strikes its inspectors withdrew, and as of the Congressional Research Service's July 2026 update the agency had not been able to inspect the attacked sites. [cite:ir7_crs_snapback]

Was It a Mistake? What Officials Said Later

Within a few years several of Israel's own former security chiefs said in public that the withdrawal had harmed Israel. Gadi Eisenkot, chief of staff of the Israel Defense Forces from 2015 to 2019, told Maariv in an interview reported on 28 January 2022 that the withdrawal was "a strategic mistake." He said "No one spoke with the security establishment," and that only Netanyahu, the Mossad director Yossi Cohen and the ambassador Ron Dermer had handled the question. He said he knew the Mossad was pushing the Americans to leave, and that the deal, though flawed, had slowed Iran's programme. [cite:ir7_toi_eisenkot] [cite:ir7_jpost_eisenkot] Moshe Ya'alon, defence minister when the deal was signed, told Haaretz in November 2021 that "the main mistake was the withdrawal from the agreement." [cite:ir7_rs_yaalon] Neri Zilber reported in New Lines that the former Mossad director Tamir Pardo called the 2018 decision a "disaster." He also reported that Amos Yadlin, a former head of military intelligence, said on Channel 12 in August 2021 that "The path that Netanyahu led got Iran extremely close to a bomb." [cite:ir7_nl_zilber] In the United States, Joe Biden, as a candidate, called Trump's policy "a dangerous failure" in a CNN article of 13 September 2020 and pledged to rejoin the deal if Iran returned to its terms. [cite:ir7_iranintl_biden] As president he did not rejoin it; the deal was never restored. [cite:ir7_crs_snapback]

The other side of the argument was made by the officials who took the decision. Trump said the deal's sunsets were unacceptable and that it could not prevent an Iranian bomb, and Pompeo set out sanctions and demands meant to force a broader settlement. [cite:ir7_toi_trump] [cite:ir7_voa_pompeo] Netanyahu hailed the exit in May 2018 as a "historic move." [cite:ir7_nl_zilber] Naftali Bennett, prime minister from June 2021, publicly thanked Trump for leaving. [cite:ir7_nl_zilber] The thesis this volume tests, that Israel pushed the United States into a choice that left Iran closer to a bomb, can be split into parts. That Netanyahu campaigned for the exit and that the Mossad pressed for it are documented, the second on Eisenkot's account. That Iran's stockpile and enrichment level grew far past the deal's limits after the exit is documented by the IAEA. That the exit caused the growth, rather than Iran's own choices or the Europeans' failure to deliver relief, is an argument. Iran's government chose each breach and said so. Israeli officials who once opposed the deal made that argument themselves, and those who took the decision rejected it.
