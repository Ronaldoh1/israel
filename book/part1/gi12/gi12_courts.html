The Courts

Israel's Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, hears petitions from Palestinians in the West Bank as well as from Israelis, and it has repeatedly been the place where the settlement enterprise met a legal limit. It has also approved much of that enterprise and left its foundations untouched. This section takes the rulings most often cited on each side. The West Bank volume covers the court's wider record on the territory. [scene:wb7_courts|The West Bank, Documented: the courts]

Elon Moreh, 1979: private land and military need

In the first decade of occupation the army requisitioned private Palestinian land for settlements on grounds of "military needs." In 1979 the landowners of Rujeib, near Nablus, challenged the seizure of their land for the Gush Emunim settlement of Elon Moreh, which Begin had welcomed after his election two years earlier. [cite:gi5_akevot_tzipori] The court ruled that the requisition was unlawful under Article 52 of the Hague Regulations. Political and ideological considerations, including the movement's pressure, had outweighed military need, and a military administration could not create facts meant to outlast military rule. [cite:wb_dweikat] The ruling closed one route to settlement and the government took another. Between 1979 and 1992 the authorities declared about 908,000 dunams "state land" under a strict reading of the Ottoman Land Code of 1858, about 16 percent of the West Bank by B'Tselem's count. Israel holds that such land is public land that it may administer in trust. [cite:wb_wiki_stateland] Part Five and the West Bank volume follow that mechanism. [scene:wb4_land|The West Bank, Documented: the land]

Amona, 2014–2017

Amona, an outpost near the settlement of Ofra, was built on land whose Palestinian owners, from the village of Silwad, petitioned with the legal aid group Yesh Din. A High Court ruling in 2014 held that part of the land was privately owned, and the court later ordered the outpost removed by 25 December 2016. When the government tried to move the residents to a neighbouring hilltop, the court blocked the move, because that land was also privately owned. Security forces evacuated 42 families in early February 2017, and dismantling began on 6 February. [cite:gi12_toi_amona] The government's response is part of the record. Netanyahu ordered a new settlement to replace Amona, described as the first new official settlement in the West Bank in about 25 years. [cite:gi12_toi_amona]

The Regulation Law, 2017–2020

The Knesset's answer to Amona was the Regulation Law, passed in February 2017 by 60 votes to 52. It would retroactively legalise about 2,000 homes built on privately owned Palestinian land. Use of the land would pass to the state's Commissioner of Government Property, and the owners would receive compensation above market value or another plot. [cite:gi12_ynet_reglaw2018] The government's own chief legal adviser would not defend it. Attorney General Avichai Mandelblit called it "a harmful law which would not stand up to the test of proportionality," and his representative told the court in June 2018 that "the Regulation Law is null and void." The government hired a private lawyer to argue for it. [cite:gi12_ynet_reglaw2018]

On 9 June 2020 the High Court of Justice ruled by eight justices to one that the law was unconstitutional and void. Experts cited by the Times of Israel put the number of settler homes on private Palestinian land at about 4,000. [cite:gi12_toi_reglaw2020] Reactions divided along the lines this volume has traced. Likud called the ruling "unfortunate" and promised a new law, and Knesset Speaker Yariv Levin said the judges had "trampled on Israeli democracy." The settlements minister, Tzipi Hotovely, called it "a declaration of war on the rights of Jews to settle the Land of Israel," and Naftali Bennett urged the government to answer with annexation. Benny Gantz, then defence minister, said the law should never have passed and that the ruling would be respected. Peace Now, one of the petitioners, welcomed the decision. B'Tselem said it did little to stop the settlement project. [cite:gi12_toi_reglaw2020]

The limits of the legal check

The court's rulings against the government have been about private Palestinian land and the means of taking it, not about settlement as such. It has not ruled the settlements themselves unlawful. In July 2021 it upheld the Nation-State Law by ten votes to one, including the clause declaring Jewish settlement a national value. The majority held that the clause was not meant to permit discrimination or the exclusion of non-Jews from state land, as the state had also clarified, and Justice George Karra dissented. [cite:gi5_adalah_hcj] [scene:gi5_nationstate|Part Five: the Nation-State Law] In May 2022 the Supreme Court rejected the petitions of the residents of Masafer Yatta, south of Hebron. It accepted the state's case that they had not proved permanent residence before the area was declared a firing zone, and treated the ban on forcible transfer as a treaty norm that Israeli courts do not enforce. [cite:wb_sabrang_masafer] It approved the demolition of Khan al-Ahmar in 2018, a demolition successive governments then postponed under international pressure. [cite:wb_toi_khan]

The E1 plan east of Jerusalem shows both sides of the check as of October 2026. The Jerusalem District Court refused on 8 September 2026 to freeze the tender for the first homes, without ruling on the legality of the plans, and three petitions remain pending. [cite:gi6_jpost_e1court] On 26 September the Attorney General's Office ordered the bid deadline moved past the 27 October election, citing diplomatic sensitivity and the risk that the tender could be used as election propaganda. [cite:gi6_ih_e1postpone] Part Six follows the case. [scene:gi6_ground|Part Six: on the ground]

The legal record supports a narrower finding than either side tends to draw from it. The court and the attorney general have stopped specific measures: seizure of private land on ideological grounds, an outpost on privately owned land, and a law to legalise such building across the territory. Each time, the government's own legal officers or Israeli petitioners brought the case, and some ministers answered by calling for annexation. The court has not blocked settlement on land the state declares its own, and it has left the larger question, whether Israel may keep the territory, to the government and the Knesset.
