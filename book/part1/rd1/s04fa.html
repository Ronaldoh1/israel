The Uganda Test: Could the Name Have Moved?

Suppose the Sixth Zionist Congress had voted the other way in 1903, and the Jewish state had been built in East Africa, or in Argentina. What would it have been called? And would the structures later built on that name still have worked? This is a counterfactual, and it should be read as one. No one can document a history that never happened. But the question can still be put to the documented record, and the record answers more clearly than one might expect.

A common assumption holds that geography was incidental, that the name and the theology later gathered around the state could have attached themselves to whatever territory the movement settled. The evidence points the other way. The theology behind later Christian Zionist support for the state was tied, on its own terms, to one specific piece of land.

Scripture names that land. Genesis 15:18 reads:

"On that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying, 'To your offspring I give this land, from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates.'" [cite:genesis_15_18]

The verse names a place, bounded by rivers. It does not promise a homeland wherever a people happens to settle. That precision does not settle how the verse should be read; allegorical, non-literal readings of scripture form an ancient and formally recognized tradition. But the literal reading, the one later Christian Zionism adopted, depends on the place as much as on the people.

Cyrus Scofield's dispensationalism, a scheme dividing history into prophetic stages, makes that dependence concrete; this book follows it in [scene:s05|the section on the Scofield Bible]. Its sequence runs in order: Israel dispersed, Israel regathered, tribulation, Israel turns to Christ, the Second Coming, and a millennial kingdom centered on Israel. Every step after the regathering assumes the return happens at the site the earlier covenant named, Zion and Jerusalem. A Jewish state in Uganda, even one calling itself Israel, would not have been "Israel regathered" in the sense the prophecy required. On this argument, the 30 to 40 million Christian Zionists who later grew out of Scofield's framework exist because the state stood where their theology pointed. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] Move the state, and there is a serious case that this constituency, and the sustained American political support it supplied, would never have formed. The point concerns what the theology itself requires. It does not claim that every believer has consciously reasoned through each link.

The strongest evidence that place mattered comes from the movement's founder, writing before the Uganda question ever arose. In 1896 Theodor Herzl set the two candidate territories side by side. Argentina offered practical advantages: fertile land and a sparse population. Palestine, by contrast, was "our ever-memorable historic home... a force of marvelous potency." [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Herzl named the difference seven years before the Uganda vote. When the movement finally rejected the Uganda offer in 1905, it chose the site its symbolic and theological weight required. That is Herzl's own stated reasoning, borne out by a later decision. It does not prove that every delegate or later leader shared his reasons.

A very different reading arrives at the same point. This book's opening sections set out a symbolic interpretation of the name "Israel" as IS-RA-EL: a state of consciousness a person arrives at, rather than a bloodline or a border. That is an interpretation, not a documented finding. Yet put to the same counterfactual, even this reading draws its resonance from places named in the text: Jerusalem, the Temple, and Peniel, where Jacob wrestled. A political entity in Uganda that borrowed the word would not carry that resonance. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] The literalist theology and the symbolic reading disagree about almost everything else. On this one narrow point they agree: the place mattered, not only the word. The agreement goes no further.

Fairness requires saying what would have survived the move. The movement's financial and political institutions, among them the Jewish Colonial Trust and the Jewish National Fund, were built to fund and organize settlement anywhere. Before Herzl, real settlement infrastructure had in fact been built in Argentina under the JCA. That machinery would have adapted to any territory. What would not have travelled was the theological and symbolic layer: Scofield's prophecy, the Christian Zionism built on it, and the scriptural resonance Herzl himself had named.

The result is a structural argument assembled from documented pieces: Herzl's 1896 words, the real Uganda vote, Scofield's prophetic sequence, and the land-promise of Genesis 15:18. It is not an account of an alternate past. What it suggests is that Palestine was more than the sentimental favorite among roughly equal choices. For at least one major source of the movement's eventual support, the land came close to being a structural requirement.
