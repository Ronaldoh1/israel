What a Canon Controls

The Greek word kanōn meant a measuring rod, a straight edge used by builders. Applied to books, it came to mean the list against which every other writing was measured. The ordinary account of the Bible treats that list as a matter of faith and scholarship: which texts were old, which were apostolic, which were read in the churches. Those questions were real, and the men who argued them took them seriously. But a list of authoritative books is never only a list. Whoever fixes it also fixes three other things, and each of the three has a price.

The first is who may speak for God. A closed list means that revelation is finished, and that no teacher, prophet or visionary can add to it. The earliest bishops who argued for a fixed set of gospels made exactly this point against rival teachers whose followers read other books. Irenaeus of Lyon, writing around 180, rejected a Valentinian "Gospel of Truth" and accused his opponents of producing "an unspeakable number of apocryphal and spurious writings, which they themselves have forged". [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_1_20] A list is a boundary around who may claim new knowledge.

The second is who may interpret the list. Irenaeus did not stop at naming four gospels. In the same work he set out a second chain of authority: the bishops "instituted" by the apostles and their successors, which he traced in Rome through twelve names down to Eleutherius, "in the twelfth place from the apostles". He concluded that "it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its preeminent authority". [cite:cn_iren_3_3] From its first full statement, then, the argument for a canon was bound to an argument for an office. The books were authoritative, and the bishops in the line of succession were their authorised readers.

The third is who collects. Once an office holds the scriptures and their interpretation, it holds the sacraments, the forgiveness of sins and the rites of the dead that those scriptures were read to support. Over the centuries that followed, the office acquired tax immunity, courts, estates and, in the end, a market in remitted punishment that financed the largest church in Christendom. [cite:vt21_hist_x7] [cite:vt21_bateman] [cite:cn_nwe_indulgence] None of this was written into the canon itself. All of it depended on the canon being closed and on one institution being its recognised guardian.

This is the lens of the book. It does not assume that the men who fixed the canon were cynics. Many of them were sincere, some were brave, and several were exiled or killed for what they believed. The lens asks only what the documents show at each step: who decided, by what authority, with whose money, against whom, and with what result for the people outside the decision. When the answer is theology, the book says so. When it is money, property or a family's place, the book says that too.

Two cautions run through everything that follows. The first is that the word "the Church" hides a great deal. There was no single body deciding the canon for most of this history. There were bishops in Lyon, Alexandria, Caesarea, Carthage and Rome, emperors in Constantinople, councils that disagreed and churches, from Syria to Ethiopia, that never accepted the Latin lists at all. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon] [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] Where a decision was made by a named person, this book names the person. The second caution is that no surviving decree ordered the Gnostic gospels burned. The documented burning orders of the early centuries named other targets, above all the books of Arius, and the record of how the excluded gospels were lost is a story of neglect, warning and replacement as much as of fire. [cite:vt21_fc_laws] [cite:vt21_socrates19] [scene:vt23_left_out|What was left out, and by whom] The power in this story is real enough without an invented bonfire.
