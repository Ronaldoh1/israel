The Chariot, the Orchard and the Book of Creation

Two subjects were set apart from the start. The rabbis of the Mishnah called them the "work of creation" and the "work of the chariot": the secrets of how the world was made, and the vision of God's throne-chariot that the prophet Ezekiel saw. The Mishnah ruled that the account of the chariot was not to be expounded even before one student, unless he was wise and understood it himself. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] Between about 200 and 700 the practitioners of chariot mysticism wrote the Hekhalot, or "palaces", texts, which describe an ascent through seven heavens past angelic guards to the throne. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] The Ethiopian Bible traced this tradition back to Enoch's vision; here it becomes the root of Kabbalah.

The Talmud gives the warning a story. Four sages, it says, "entered pardes", the orchard, commonly understood as a mystical encounter with God. One died, one went mad, and one "cut off the shoots", abandoning the faith. Only Rabbi Akiva came out unharmed. [cite:kb_mjl_danger] The story became the standard reason for caution: the knowledge was real, and it could destroy those not ready for it. The philosopher Maimonides, who identified the orchard with the work of creation and the work of the chariot, wrote that one should not stroll in it "except if his stomach is full with bread and meat", meaning that the student must first know well what is forbidden and what is permitted. [cite:kb_ou_age40]

The work of creation has its own text. The Sefer Yetzira, the "Book of Creation", is short and hard to date, and it is the first source for the idea of the sefirot, which it presents as "ideal numbers". Later Kabbalists turned them into the ten stages of emanation from En Sof, the unknowable Infinite, through which God becomes manifest. [cite:gn_brit_sefira] Together with the Bahir, the Book of Creation is among the earlier Jewish mystical writings on which the Zohar later drew. [cite:kb_wiki_zohar] The Gnostics set the sefirot beside the Gnostic aeons as two ladders of emanation; this book follows the ladder's own history.

From these roots three features run through everything that follows. The teaching was oral and personal, passed from a master to a few. It was guarded by conditions: wisdom, learning, later age and marriage. [cite:kb_mjl_danger] And the reason given for the guard was not that outsiders were unworthy, but that the knowledge was dangerous to the one who received it. The story of the four in the orchard says three of them were harmed. That reason would be tested, at the scale of a whole people, in 1666.
