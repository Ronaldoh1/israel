Citizens by Office, Courts by Delegation, Prisons in Italy

Most states make citizens by birth or descent and keep them for life. Vatican City State makes citizens by appointment and lets them go when the appointment ends. It has a full set of courts but almost no criminal caseload, and it has never needed a prison of its own, because the Lateran Treaty lets it hand the work of punishment to Italy. This section sets out those three arrangements from the laws that create them and from the few cases that have tested them.

Who is a citizen

The Lateran Treaty laid down the base rule in 1929. Article 9 makes everyone with stable residence in Vatican City subject to the Holy See's sovereignty, and Article 21 adds that cardinals resident in Rome, even outside the city, are Vatican citizens for all purposes. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The details were set by a citizenship law of 1929 and, since 22 February 2011, by Law No. CXXXI on citizenship, residence and access, issued by Benedict XVI. [cite:vt4_loc_citizenship]

The 2011 law creates two routes. Citizenship follows automatically, by right, for three groups: cardinals who live in Vatican City or Rome, the diplomats of the Holy See, and people who live in the state because of their office or service there, a group that includes the Swiss Guards. It may be granted by administrative decision to three further groups: residents authorised to live in the state because of their work, people the Pope personally authorises to reside there, and the spouses and children of citizens who are themselves authorised to live in the state. [cite:vt4_loc_citizenship] Nobody becomes a Vatican citizen by being born there.

Citizenship ends the same way it begins. Except for the Pope's, every grant is temporary. It lapses when the holder leaves the office that justified it, when he or she stops living in the state, or, for a cardinal, when he moves his residence outside Vatican City and Rome. Family members lose it at the same moment as the holder, and children lose it at eighteen unless they apply for permission to remain. A person who loses Vatican citizenship and has no other becomes an Italian citizen automatically, under Article 9 of the Lateran Treaty. [cite:vt4_wiki_citizenship] The rule prevents statelessness and explains why the Vatican can afford a citizenship that lasts only as long as a contract.

The result is the population described in section one: 673 citizens, of whom 458 live inside the walls, including 120 Swiss Guards, with about 30 per cent abroad, mostly as diplomats; and 882 residents of any citizenship at the end of 2024. [cite:vt4_vcs_population] Many residents, such as employees' relatives without a grant, members of religious communities or staff housed in the city, are not citizens, and many citizens, such as nuncios abroad and cardinals living elsewhere in Rome, are not residents. The Law Library of Congress, writing in 2012, gave a figure of about 800 people and 450 citizens, which shows how little the numbers move. [cite:vt4_loc_citizenship]

The courts

Judicial power in the state is exercised in the Pope's name by a single judge (the Giudice Unico) for minor matters, a Tribunal of first instance, a Court of Appeal and a Court of Cassation. Investigation and prosecution belong to the Office of the Promoter of Justice. The judges are appointed by the Pope and are, in the Governorate's words, subject only to the law; the Pope may nonetheless transfer any civil or criminal case to a particular court at any stage, and the power of clemency is his. [cite:vt4_vcs_judicial] These courts are civil and criminal courts of the state. They are not the Church's canon-law tribunals, which judge clergy under church law and are a separate system.

The present structure comes from a run of reforms under Francis. On 13 March 2020 he signed Law CCCLI on the judicial system, replacing John Paul II's law of 1987. It described the judges as "hierarchically dependent on the Supreme Pontiff", required them to be drawn from university professors or recognised jurists, barred curial officials from serving because of the conflict of interest, gave the judicial offices their own spending autonomy within the Governorate's budget, and placed the judicial police, a section of the Gendarmerie, under the Promoter of Justice. [cite:vt4_cwr_judicial2020] On 30 April 2021 he ended a privilege: until then cardinals and bishops accused of crimes could be tried only by the Court of Cassation, presided over by a cardinal; from then on they would be tried by the ordinary Tribunal, through the same three levels as anyone else, though "with the prior consent of the Supreme Pontiff". [cite:vt4_vn_cardinals2021] A motu proprio in force from 13 April 2023 defined the Promoter's powers more closely, allowed the Promoter to ask for a case to be dropped where the offence was minor or reparation had been made, removed the requirement that one judge of the Tribunal serve full-time, and provided for substitute judges on long trials. [cite:vt4_vn_judicial2023]

The reason for the 2021 change was the largest case the courts have heard: the trial over the Secretariat of State's investment in a London building, in which Cardinal Giovanni Angelo Becciu was convicted on 16 December 2023. In March 2026 the Court of Appeal declared the first-instance proceedings null and ordered a retrial, directing the Promoter of Justice to file the complete record of the investigation. As of 19 September 2026, the appeal court had set a hearing for 29 October 2026, and Becciu's lawyer, Fabio Viglione, said the defence was confident of "the cardinal's complete innocence". [cite:vt4_unione_becciu] Becciu is therefore, as of October 2026, a defendant whose conviction no longer stands. The case and its money are the subject of Part Ten. [scene:vt10_london_trial|Part Ten: the London trial]

Before that trial, criminal cases were rare. In September 2012 Giovanni Giacobbe, the Promoter of Justice at the Court of Appeal, told reporters that Vatican criminal trials were "extremely" rare, and that the only recent comparison was a drug-possession case about ten years earlier. [cite:vt4_cns_tribunal2012] The case he was preparing for was that of Paolo Gabriele, the papal butler who leaked private documents. A three-judge panel convicted him of aggravated theft on 6 October 2012 and sentenced him to eighteen months. [cite:vt4_aj_gabriele]

No prison: the Italian arrangement

The Gabriele case also showed what the state lacks. At his conviction the Vatican said that a sentence would be served in an Italian prison, because it "does not have any long-term prison facilities". [cite:vt4_aj_gabriele] Giacobbe had already explained that this would mean invoking, for the first time, a long-standing agreement with Italy that had never been used. [cite:vt4_cns_tribunal2012] It was not used in the end. Gabriele was held in the Vatican police barracks, and on 22 December 2012 Benedict XVI visited him there, told him in person that he accepted his request for pardon, and commuted the sentence. [cite:vt4_spokesman_pardon]

The treaty basis is Article 22 of the Lateran Treaty. At the Holy See's request, or by a delegation it grants, Italy will punish in its own territory crimes committed in Vatican City; if the offender flees into Italy, Italian law applies directly. In the other direction, the Holy See will hand over people who take refuge in Vatican City after committing acts in Italy that are crimes under the laws of both states, and the same applies to suspects who shelter in the immune buildings listed in Article 15, unless the officials there prefer to invite Italian police in to make the arrest. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The best-known use of Italian jurisdiction came after 13 May 1981, when Mehmet Ali Ağca shot John Paul II in St Peter's Square: he was handed to the Italian police and tried and convicted by an Italian court, not by the Vatican. [cite:vt4_cns_tribunal2012]

Put together, these arrangements answer a common question about the state's independence. Vatican City does have its own laws, judges and police, and in the London trial it showed that it can prosecute its own senior officials. [cite:vt4_vn_cardinals2021] [cite:vt4_unione_becciu] But the system was built on the assumption that serious crime would be rare and that Italy would do the heavy work of prisons and fugitives when it occurred. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] [cite:vt4_cns_tribunal2012] The Holy See's position, stated in each reform, is that the changes bring the courts closer to equality before the law; Francis described the 2021 change as answering "the overriding need" for the procedural system to ensure equality among the Church's members. [cite:vt4_vn_cardinals2021] The courts remain, in the end, the sovereign's courts, appointed by him and answerable to him, as the Fundamental Law provides. [cite:vt4_vcs_judicial]
