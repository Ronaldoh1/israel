Partition and the Catastrophe, 1947–1949

When the question of Palestine went to the United Nations, the people who lived there had no vote on it. The Arab majority of Mandate Palestine had no seat in the General Assembly and no direct voice in the decision to partition the country. [cite:britannica_res181] The Mandate's figures counted about 1.3 million Arabs and 630,000 Jews; Jews were roughly a third of the population. [cite:britannica_res181] Resolution 181 gave the proposed Jewish state about 55 to 56 percent of the territory, at a time when land in Jewish ownership was about 7 percent. [cite:britannica_res181] [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] By one count, the Jewish state as drawn would have held about 498,000 Jews and about 494,000 Arabs. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002]

The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab League rejected the plan in its entirety. Jamal Husseini told the United Nations that the partition line would be "nothing but a line of fire and blood." [cite:wiki_un_partition_plan] [cite:palquest_un_partition_1947] The Jewish Agency accepted it, with misgivings. [cite:wiki_un_partition_plan] On the narrow question of who said yes and who said no, the record is one-sided. On the question of why, it is not: the people asked to accept the plan were two-thirds of the population, in a country where Jews owned under a tenth of the land, and were being asked to accept a state in which half the residents of one part would be Arabs governed by others.

What followed came in two waves. From December 1947 there was civil war, with attacks in both directions. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] From April 1948, Walid Khalidi writes, the Zionist forces began "to seize and hold on to Arab territory," under Plan Dalet. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] On 9 April Irgun and Lehi forces attacked the village of Deir Yassin west of Jerusalem; current scholarship puts the dead at about 110. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] In July about 50,000 to 70,000 inhabitants of Lydda and Ramla were expelled, under written orders signed by Yitzhak Rabin. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda]

By the end, between 700,000 and 750,000 Palestinians had been displaced, and about 500 villages depopulated or destroyed. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] The UN's own technical estimate in 1949 was about 711,000 refugees from the territory under Israeli control. [cite:rs_rd5_unccp_1949] Why they left is still argued: Benny Morris holds that most of the flight followed fighting and that no general order to expel has been found, while deliberate expulsions grew from June; Walid Khalidi holds that it was produced by design. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Why they could not return is plainer. "As early as June 1948," Zachary Lockman writes, "the cabinet had reached a consensus against allowing the refugees to return to their homes." [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] Palestinians call these events the Nakba, the catastrophe. [cite:npr_hamashistory]

The figures that close the period are the measure of everything this part has described. Land bought over sixty years came to under a tenth of the country. The UN plan offered the Jewish state a little over half. By the armistices of 1949 about 78 percent of Mandate Palestine was under Israeli control. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:morris_1948] The difference between the first figure and the last was not bought. It was made by the war, and by the flight and expulsion of the people who had warned, petitioned, gone to court and risen against exactly this outcome since 1899.

Seen from their side, the half-century has one shape. A people living on their land under Ottoman rule were told by a notable of their own that the land would be taken, and by the founder of the movement that no one would think of sending them away. They were promised independence by one British official and left out of another's promise. They asked for an elected government and were refused. They watched the land sold from under their tenants by absentee landlords, lost their case in the courts, rose in revolt and were crushed. When the world decided their country's fate, they had no vote, and when the war came, most of them lost their homes. The record also shows their own leaders' failures, their divisions, and the violence some of them did to Jews and to each other. It does not show a people who did not see it coming. They saw it from the first, and said so.
