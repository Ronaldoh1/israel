The Arms Race: Prague, Washington and Paris, 1954–1956

The question of who would arm Egypt, and who would arm Israel, was settled in the twelve months after September 1955. Egypt found its weapons in the Soviet bloc. Israel asked Washington and was refused, and found its aircraft in France. The documents of those months show each government's reasons in its own officials' words, and they show that the outcome was produced by several decisions taken in several capitals, each for its own purposes.

Egypt's approach to Moscow began well before the deal became public. On 15 June 1954, more than a year earlier, Nasser had told the Soviet ambassador that Britain had stopped arms shipments and blocked supplies from other European states, that Egypt could not meet "a hundredth of its army's needs", and had formally asked the Soviet position on selling arms to Egypt. [cite:rs_cw2_wilson_solod] That conversation, and the revolution that brought Nasser to power, are told in [scene:cw2_free_officers|Egypt's revolution]. Its date matters for what follows, because it predates by eight months the Gaza raid that some accounts treat as the deal's cause.

On 27 September 1955 Nasser announced that Czechoslovakia would supply Egypt with Soviet heavy weapons: tanks, MiG-15 fighters and bombers. The Jewish Virtual Library values the deal at "more than $83 million"; no primary figure for its value appears in the sources used here. Nasser called the arrangement "unconditional". [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_czech] [cite:rs_cw2_adst_czech] The American embassy in Cairo had already grasped its form. Byroade reported on 29 September that the "actual agreement on arms was with Czechoslovakia": the weapons were Soviet, but the contracting party was Prague. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tel632]

Around 1 October 1955, Assistant Secretary George Allen and Byroade met Nasser, and he told them how the deal would be paid for. Egypt had already sent one million Egyptian pounds to Prague "on account". Cotton and rice would be shipped "more or less parallel" with the arms. And the agreement, he said, was "without any conditions whatsoever". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tel632] The payment mechanism was barter backed by a cash deposit. Egypt would pay largely in its own crops, and the deliveries of goods and weapons would move together.

Why had Egypt not bought from the United States instead? Allen gave his account to Ambassador Abba Eban on 11 October 1955. The deal, he said, "would go through", and it would not be "of token proportions". Nasser had first presented it to the Americans as trading "cotton which Egypt could not sell elsewhere for arms which Egypt could not obtain elsewhere". And the sticking point in the American arms talks with Egypt, Allen said, had been the American requirement that a military mission accompany any sale, not a demand for a non-aggression pledge. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_allen_czech] Allen and Eban also disagreed that day about what had turned Nasser toward Moscow, the Gaza raid or the Turkish–Iraqi pact; that dispute, with the evidence on each side, is set out in [scene:cw2_gaza_raid|the Gaza raid and what followed].

Israel's answer was to ask Washington for arms of its own. On 16 November 1955 Eban, with Shiloah and Salmon, met Hoover, Murphy and Allen and presented a confidential list of arms "solely for defensive purposes". The request, he said, "sprang directly" from the imbalance created by the Czech deal. He added that, despite press reports, Israel had not obtained any Mystères from France. Hoover replied that the United States had to consider the other signatories of the Tripartite Declaration. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_hoover] That reply named the constraint Washington would invoke: it did not act on arms to the region alone, but with regard to the other governments that had signed the declaration.

According to Avi Shlaim, Dulles had decided, on the eve of the raid at Lake Tiberias in December 1955, to sell Israel defensive arms. In Shlaim's account, the raid changed the decision. On 13 December 1955 Eban was told it had been postponed. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] The raid itself, Israel's attack on Syrian positions east of Lake Tiberias on the night of 11–12 December, and the Council's response to it, are told in [scene:cw2_border_un_1956|the border and the United Nations]. Its effect on the arms question was immediate. Sharett had been in Washington seeking those arms when, Shlaim writes, Ben-Gurion alone authorized the raid. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_kinneret] Sharett recorded his reaction in words Shlaim quotes: "My world became black, the matter of arms was murdered." [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms]

Sharett pressed again at the end of the month. In late December 1955 he stressed to the American Embassy Israel's "almost complete lack of defensive facilities" against Egyptian jets. Israel wanted twelve Mystère IVs that it had ordered from France, and the French had said their release rested "entirely with US Government". The first 15 MiGs had reached Egypt about two months earlier. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tlv_jan56] The documents thus show a chain of dependence. Israel had ordered French aircraft; France would not release them without Washington's approval; and Washington had postponed its own decision on arms for Israel. The final American refusal of Israel's request came in April 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_alpha]

The policy behind these refusals had a longer history. The aid relationship as summarized in the panel below began with economic aid and loans only, from 1948 to 1961, and the panel gives the reason as Washington's wish to avoid selling arms for fear of alienating the Arab oil states; the first American military aid to Israel came in 1962, and then as loans.

The documents of 1955 and 1956 fill in that general rule with particular reasons, and they are not quite the same reason. Hoover cited the Tripartite partners. The postponement followed the Kinneret raid. Neither of those documented statements mentions oil. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_hoover] [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] The panel's reason describes the policy across fourteen years; the records of these months show what officials said when they applied it to a specific request.

Refused in Washington, Israel turned to Paris. Shlaim presents the French orientation as the result of the American refusal. Peres, he writes, went directly to the French defence establishment. Shlaim's reading of the two Western governments' motives is sharply drawn: "France offered arms in the hope of inducing Israel to go to war against Egypt," while "America allowed its allies to supply arms to Israel on condition that Israel did not go to war." [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms] That is a historian's interpretation of motive, not a document's statement of it, and it should be read as such. The American records used here show Washington's role in the French channel as a veto rather than a sponsorship: the French said release of the first twelve Mystères depended on the United States, and in September 1956 Washington judged a further French sale undesirable. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tlv_jan56] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_mystere]

By September 1956 Israel was believed to hold French jets. A State Department memorandum from Rountree to Hoover, dated 20 September 1956, said that Israel was believed to have 24 Mystères and that it "appears possible" it had 48. British sources reported "forty-eight Mystere IVs from France". Eighteen aircraft seen passing through Brindisi may have been heading to Israel. And France had proposed selling 24 more through the Near East Arms Coordinating Committee, a proposal Washington called undesirable. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_mystere] The memorandum's own uncertainty is part of the record: the State Department did not know, in September 1956, whether Israel had 24 French jets or twice as many.

Laid side by side, on the record used here, the governments' positions explain the outcome without any need for a hidden design. Cairo had asked Moscow for arms in June 1954, its leader saying that Britain had cut off supplies, and its talks with Washington had stalled, on Allen's account, over an American military mission; it paid in cotton and rice for weapons that came without conditions. Washington would not supply Israel directly, cited its Tripartite partners, postponed its decision after an Israeli raid, and refused in April 1956, while keeping a say over what its allies sold. Paris, on Shlaim's reading, had reasons of its own for arming Israel against Egypt. In Jerusalem, the leadership was divided: Sharett sought arms through Washington and saw his effort undone by a raid authorized while he was there, and Peres went to the French. Each capital acted on its own interest. The result was that by September 1956 Egypt held Soviet-bloc arms and Israel held French jets, and the United States had supplied neither.

Several things remain open in the sources used here. The value of the Czech deal rests on a secondary figure, "more than $83 million", with no primary confirmation. The number of Mystères in Israel by September 1956 was uncertain even to the State Department, at 24 or possibly 48. Allen's explanation of why the American talks with Egypt stalled is one official's account, given to Israel's ambassador, and no Egyptian record of those talks is before this book. Shlaim's account of Dulles's decision on the eve of the Kinneret raid, and his reading of French motives, are a historian's reconstruction rather than documents quoted here; whether the United States would have sold Israel arms had the raid not happened cannot be settled from these sources. What the record does establish is the sequence: an Egyptian request in 1954, a Soviet-bloc deal in 1955, an American refusal in 1956, and a French supply line open by the autumn of that year.
