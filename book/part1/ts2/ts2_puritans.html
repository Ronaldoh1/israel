The Puritan Root, 1609–1815

The belief that the Jewish people would one day return to the land of Israel, and that Christians should welcome and even help that return, is older than every name usually attached to it. It is older than Cyrus Scofield's Bible of 1909, older than John Nelson Darby's system of the 1830s, and older than the Zionist movement itself. Historians call the earlier form "restorationism". Andrew Crome, writing in the Critical Dictionary of Apocalyptic and Millenarian Movements, defines it as "the pre-twentieth-century Christian belief that the Jewish people would return" to Palestine, and notes that it "is not synonymous with either premillennialism or dispensationalism" and "significantly predates Darby's innovations". [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] That is the first fact this part establishes, and it corrects a claim repeated in earlier volumes of this library and in Book One of the series: that support for a Jewish return was "substantially non-existent" in Protestant thought before 1909, or "younger than the automobile". The 1909 Bible and its system are the subject of [scene:ts5_notes|Part Five] and Darby of [scene:ts3_darby|Part Three]. This section goes back three centuries further.

Reading the prophets literally

For most of Christian history the dominant reading of the Hebrew prophets held that their promises to Israel had passed to the Church. Restorationism began when a group of English Protestant scholars started to read some of those promises as applying to the Jewish people themselves, in a future that would include a return to the land. The earliest influential figure was Thomas Brightman (1562–1607), a Puritan commentator who leaned towards Presbyterian church government but remained a serving minister of the Church of England until his death. In commentaries on Daniel, Revelation and the Song of Songs, published after he died, he argued that prophecy concerned the Jews' earthly future: they would convert to Christianity and then rule the world from Palestine. His ideas first appeared in print in the United Provinces, the Dutch Republic, in 1609. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] Brightman and other commentators of the period suggested 1656 as the year of the Jews' restoration. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm]

The next step brought the idea into conflict with the Crown. In 1621 Henry Finch, a lawyer and Member of Parliament, published The World's Great Restauration, or Calling of the Jews, and with them of all Nations and Kingdoms of the Earth to the Faith of Christ. Finch appears to have predicted that the Jews would regain temporal dominion, and the book applied the prophets' sayings to the Jews themselves rather than to the Church in general. [cite:ts2_wiki_finch] James I treated it as a libel. Crome's account is that Finch had suggested kings would lay their crowns before the Jews in Jerusalem. Finch was arrested in April 1621 and released after he disavowed passages that could be read as derogatory to the sovereign and apologised for having written "unadvisedly"; William Laud attacked the book in a sermon in July 1621 and it was suppressed. [cite:ts2_wiki_finch] [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] The episode shows the limit of the early form. Finch's restoration was an act of God inside a Christian scheme, ending in conversion, and a king could read it as a claim against his own authority. It was not yet a policy anyone in government would carry.

Cromwell, Menasseh ben Israel and New England

The prophetic reading touched English law once in the seventeenth century, though not in Palestine. Jews had been expelled from England in 1290. Oliver Cromwell had hoped that the Barebones Parliament of 1653 might advance the Jews' return, and in December 1655 he called a conference at Whitehall on readmitting them. Menasseh ben Israel, a Sephardi rabbi from Amsterdam, made the case on two grounds at once: commercial benefit, and a prophetic argument that the Jews had first to be scattered to "the ends of the earth", England included, before their redemption. The conference ended without a resolution. In 1656 Cromwell quietly recognised a small Jewish community in London. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] The pairing of prophecy with commerce at Whitehall in 1655 recurs in every later episode in this part, down to Palmerston's dispatch of 1840 in [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|the next section].

The same reading crossed the Atlantic with the Puritans. Increase Mather, the Boston minister, argued in The Mystery of Israel's Salvation (1669) that God would use the Jews to overcome the Roman Church and the Ottoman Empire, and that they would gain earthly pre-eminence and return to Palestine before their conversion; he restated the view in a Dissertation of 1709. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] An American line of restorationist thought therefore existed more than two centuries before William Blackstone's petition of 1891, which is taken up in [scene:ts6_blackstone|Part Six].

Not a gap but a continuous strand

A common telling has restorationism flaring in the 1650s, going quiet for over a century, and reviving only with the French Revolution. Crome rejects that picture. He writes that restorationist thought "did not go into hibernation between the late 1650s and the French Revolution", and that it remained "an established part of Protestant discourse", carried in the commentaries of eighteenth-century churchmen such as Thomas Newton and the Baptist John Gill. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] The American and French Revolutions then increased prophetic speculation; in 1795 Richard Brothers, a former naval officer, claimed to be "king of the Hebrews" and promised a restoration by 1798. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] Brothers is a reminder that the strand had a fringe as well as a learned mainstream, and that both read the same verses.

The London Society, 1809

The strand acquired an institution in 1809. The London Society for Promoting Christianity amongst the Jews was founded that year, at first on a non-denominational basis, through the work of Joseph Frey, a German Jewish convert. It ran into large debts, and in 1815 it was reconstituted as an Anglican society, financed by Lewis Way, a wealthy lawyer. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] Its purpose is in its name: it was a mission to convert Jews. The Society itself denied any particular focus on prophecy, but Crome finds in its sermons, library and publications strong restorationist interests. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] The Society matters for the rest of this part because it supplied people and money. Its later president was Lord Shaftesbury, from 1848; its missionary and convert Michael Solomon Alexander became the first Protestant bishop in Jerusalem in 1841; and it helped fund that bishopric. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] [cite:ts2_wiki_bishopric]

What the restorationists wanted, and how Jews saw it

The documents support a precise statement of aim, which should not be read back into the stated aims of later Christian Zionist organisations; what those organisations say about conversion is set out in [scene:ts8_cufi|Part Eight]. Crome concludes that "the vast majority of restorationists attempted to effect the conversion of Jews to Christianity", and that this call to give up Jewish identity "was often seen by Jews themselves as a form of both personal and corporate attack". [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] Brightman's Jews convert and then rule; Finch's are called "to the Faith of Christ"; Mather's return before converting; the London Society existed to make converts. The restoration these writers expected was part of a Christian story whose ending was a Christian one.

The historian Donald M. Lewis, in The Origins of Christian Zionism (Cambridge University Press, 2010), offers a more generous frame. On the summary of his reviewer David Fox Sandmel, Lewis describes a Protestant "Teaching of Esteem", set against the older Catholic "Teaching of Contempt", in which Jews were honoured as God's chosen people; he ties it to Britain's view of itself as an elect nation and to evangelical anti-Catholicism. [cite:ts2_sandmel_lewis] Sandmel, writing in Studies in Christian-Jewish Relations in 2011, accepted that this philosemitism had been under-studied, but faulted the book on two counts that bear on this volume: Lewis does not explore the tension between esteem and the goal of conversion, and actual Jews, apart from converts and Moses Montefiore, are largely absent from his story. [cite:ts2_sandmel_lewis] Both readings can be held together. The restorationists of this period were, by their own lights, friends of the Jewish people, and the friendship they offered was conditional on a change of faith that most Jews refused.

The finding of this section is therefore narrower than either side of the modern argument usually allows. Christian belief in a Jewish return to the land did not begin with Darby or Scofield; it ran from Brightman's commentaries of 1609 through Finch, Mather and the eighteenth-century commentators to an organised missionary society in 1809. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] But it was a theology of conversion, not a programme for a Jewish state, and until the 1830s no government acted on it. How it reached a Foreign Secretary is the subject of [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|the next section].
