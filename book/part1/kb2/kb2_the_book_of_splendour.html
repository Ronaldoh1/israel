The Book of Splendour and Its Author

The Zohar is "a foundational work of Kabbalistic literature": a mystical commentary on the Torah, with teaching on the origin of the world and the structure of the soul, written mainly in Aramaic. [cite:kb_wiki_zohar] It was first made public by Moses de León, a Spanish Jewish scholar born in León in 1250, who had been circulating copies "since the 1280s" and who presented it as the work of Simeon ben Yohai, a rabbinic teacher of the second century in the land of Israel. [cite:kb_brit_deleon] [cite:kb_wiki_zohar]

Doubts began at once. In about 1305 the kabbalist Isaac of Acre, a survivor of the fall of Acre to the Mamluks, travelled to Spain to find the original. He met Moses de León at Valladolid, who swore that he owned an ancient copy kept at his house in Ávila. De León died before he could show it. In Ávila, Isaac was told that de León's wife and daughter had said that "Moses of Leon had written the book himself." [cite:kb_wiki_isaacacre] [cite:kb_brit_deleon] Isaac's testimony was printed in a chronicle, removed from its 1580 edition and restored only in 1857. [cite:kb_wiki_isaacacre]

Modern scholarship agrees with that account in substance. The historian Gershom Scholem became convinced that the Zohar was medieval and identified de León as its author. He showed that its "strange, artificial, literary Aramaic" was written by someone whose "native language was Hebrew", and he suggested that de León wrote it to counter rationalism and lax observance among Spanish Jews. [cite:kb_brit_deleon] Scholem's view is "widely held as accurate among historians of Kabbalah"; later scholars, among them Yehuda Liebes and Daniel Matt, have proposed that a circle of medieval authors contributed, perhaps with a little older material. [cite:kb_wiki_zohar]

The pattern is one this shelf has met before. The Book of Enoch was written in Enoch's name centuries after any Enoch could have lived; the Kebra Nagast, described in The Ethiopian Bible, gave a dynasty an ancient pedigree. [cite:ds_bas_enoch] [cite:et_wiki_kebra] A text gains authority by being placed further back in time and in a greater name, and the Zohar is a medieval masterpiece presented as an ancient one. That is a finding about its authorship, not about its value; it became, for many communities, among the most revered of Jewish books.

Acceptance was never universal. The legal authority Joseph Karo accepted the Zohar's authenticity; some communities, including Yemenite, Andalusian and some Italian ones, "never accepted it as authentic", and later authorities such as Jacob Emden doubted it. [cite:kb_wiki_zohar] Printing brought it to a wider public. After what one account describes as a ninety-year rabbinic ban on printing the Zohar, rival editions appeared in Italy: a one-volume edition in Cremona in 1558 and a three-volume edition in Mantua from 1558 to 1560. [cite:kb_jc_zohar] [cite:kb_wiki_zohar] A book that had moved from master to student in manuscript was now for sale.
