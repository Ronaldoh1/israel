The Historic Churches: Rome, Jerusalem and the Mainline

Christian Zionism grew up inside one branch of Protestantism, and most of the world's Christians belong to churches that never adopted it. The largest of them, the Catholic Church, met political Zionism at its start with a refusal; it later revised its teaching about the Jewish people at its root, while still declining to read the modern state as the fulfilment of prophecy. The ancient churches of Jerusalem, Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant, have twice in twenty years named Christian Zionism directly as a teaching they reject. Several American mainline denominations have gone further and moved money. This section sets out what each body said and did, in its own documents, and gives the answers of those who disagreed.

Rome: from "non possumus" to Nostra Aetate

The first meeting between political Zionism and the papacy was short. In January 1904 Theodor Herzl was received by Pope Pius X in an audience arranged by the Austrian painter Count Berthold Dominik Lippay. Herzl's diary records the pope's answer to his request for support as "Non possumus" ("We cannot"), followed by: "The Jews have not recognized our Lord, therefore we cannot recognize the Jewish people." If Jews settled in Palestine, the pope added, "we shall have churches and priests ready to baptize all of you." [cite:ts11_ccjr_herzl1904] [cite:dv1_ej_piusx] The account is Herzl's own diary. The refusal was theological, not political: it rested on the old teaching that the covenant had passed to the Church, the view that Protestant restorationists, from the Puritans onward, had already broken with. [scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two: the Puritans]

After 1945 the Church's position changed in its foundations. In 1949 Pius XII asked that Jerusalem be given a legally guaranteed "international" status. [cite:dv1_rnc_1949] In 1965 the Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate stated that "God holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers," that the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive," nor against Jews today, and that Jews "should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God." It decried "displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone." [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] The declaration says nothing about the land or the state. Twenty years later, in 1985, the Holy See's commission for relations with the Jews asked Catholics to understand the Jewish attachment to the land without adopting "any particular religious interpretation" of it, and to see the State of Israel's existence in terms of international law rather than of religion. [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] The Holy See signed a Fundamental Agreement with Israel on 30 December 1993 and opened full diplomatic relations in June 1994. [cite:rs_cw2_holysee_israel_protocol]

Catholic teaching also rejects the end-times system that underlies much Christian Zionism, though not by name. Paragraph 676 of the Catechism of the Catholic Church warns that the Antichrist's deception "already begins to take shape in the world" whenever it is claimed that the messianic hope can be realised within history, and states that the Church has rejected even modified forms of this falsification under the name of millenarianism, especially in the political form of a secular messianism. [cite:ts11_usccb_ccc676] The text addresses millenarian expectation in general, not dispensationalism or Scofield in particular; claims in earlier library material that Rome issued a ruling against "Scofield's entire prophetic system" go beyond what the documents checked for this book say. [scene:ts14_myths|Part Fourteen: myths and contested claims]

The Holy See has applied the same law-not-prophecy approach to Palestine. It signed a Basic Agreement with the PLO on 15 February 2000 and, on 26 June 2015, a Comprehensive Agreement with the "State of Palestine," which entered into force on 2 January 2016 and treated Palestine as a state. Israel's Foreign Ministry called the agreement one-sided, said it ignored Jewish rights and holy sites in Jerusalem, and argued that recognition "damages the peace process by strengthening Palestinian refusal to negotiate." [cite:ts11_wiki_hspal] The Church thus recognises both states and grounds neither in scripture.

Jerusalem: the patriarchs and heads of churches

The churches with the oldest presence in the land have spoken most directly. On 22 August 2006 the Latin Patriarch Michel Sabbah, the Syriac Orthodox Archbishop Swerios Malki Mourad, the Anglican Bishop Riah Abu El-Assal and the Lutheran Bishop Munib Younan issued the Jerusalem Declaration on Christian Zionism. It called the movement "detrimental to a just peace within Palestine and Israel" and stated: "We categorically reject Christian Zionist doctrines as false teaching." [cite:ts1_wiki_jerdecl] [cite:ts11_osv_heads2026] Representatives of the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem, Bridges for Peace and Christian Friends of Israel published a response that denied Christian Zionism is a heresy, grounded their position in the Bible, including God's gift of the land, and described it as "not a threat to anybody, but instead seeks to be a blessing." [cite:ts1_wiki_jerdecl] The declaration, not the later Kairos Palestine document, is the text that names Christian Zionism as false teaching; library material that attributes the word "heresy" to Kairos is not supported by the passages checked. [scene:ts11_palestinian|Palestinian Christians]

Twenty years later the Patriarchs and Heads of Churches of the Holy Land, as a body, returned to the subject. In a statement dated 17 January 2026 and published by the Patriarchate of Jerusalem the next day, the first day of the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity, they named Christian Zionism among "damaging ideologies" whose recent activities "mislead the public, sow confusion, and harm the unity of our flock," described them as "interference in the internal life of the churches," and objected that their promoters had been "welcomed at official levels both locally and internationally." [cite:ts11_osv_heads2026] [cite:ts11_aje_heads2026] The statement followed a reported visit by about 1,000 evangelical pastors to Israel in early January, and a meeting on 31 December 2025 at which Prime Minister Netanyahu called evangelical Christians Israel's "closest allies." [cite:ts11_osv_heads2026]

The replies came from both an office of state and the academy. The US ambassador to Israel, Mike Huckabee, wrote on 20 January 2026 that he respected "the traditional, liturgical churches" but disagreed that "any sect of the Christian faith should claim exclusivity in speaking for Christians worldwide," and said the term "Christian Zionism" is used "in a pejorative manner to disparage free-church believers." [cite:ts11_osv_heads2026] Theologians published by the Middle East Forum, a pro-Israel think tank, answered on the merits: André Villeneuve of Sacred Heart Major Seminary argued that Zionism "is not the product of nineteenth-century Protestant dispensationalism"; Gavin D'Costa called the election of the Jewish people "the theological foundation of Catholic Zionism"; and A.J. Nolte of Regent University said Arab Christians are "expected to actively promote and support the Arab cause, or else." [cite:ts11_mef_theologians] Huckabee's own role is treated in Part Twelve. [scene:ts12_officials|Part Twelve: officials]

The American mainline: statements and money

The Presbyterian Church (USA) has gone furthest. On 20 June 2014 its General Assembly voted 310 to 303 to sell its holdings, about $21 million, in Caterpillar, Hewlett-Packard and Motorola Solutions, as a protest against the use of their products in the occupied territories; a similar proposal had failed by two votes in 2012. The measure reaffirmed Israel's right to exist, and supporters said it was not part of the boycott, divestment and sanctions movement; the moderator, Heath Rada, said it was "in no way" a reflection of "lack of love for our Jewish brothers and sisters." [cite:ts11_csm_pcusa2014] The Israeli Embassy in Washington called the resolution "shameful," the American Jewish Committee said the vote was "driven by hatred of Israel," and Rabbi Rick Jacobs of the Union for Reform Judaism said the church was no longer "a partner" for joint work on peace. [cite:ts11_csm_pcusa2014] In 2024 the same assembly adopted an overture "On Confessing our Complicity in Christian Zionism," and in August 2026 a church gathering in Louisville met under the title "Christianity without Christian Zionism." [cite:ts1_pcusa_2026]

The United Methodist Church has acted in smaller steps. In January 2016 Wespath, its pension agency, with more than $20 billion under management, excluded five Israeli banks, Hapoalim, Leumi, First International, Israel Discount and Mizrahi Tefahot, from its portfolios as part of a screen on "high-risk" countries. [cite:ts11_jta_wespath2016] On 30 April 2024, at the General Conference in Charlotte, delegates approved by consent calendar a resolution, written by the advocacy group United Methodists for Kairos Response, asking church institutions not to buy the government bonds of Israel, Turkey and Morocco because of their long-term occupations of Palestinian territory, northern Cyprus and Western Sahara. The resolution is advisory and does not bind Wespath, which said it "takes the call seriously"; at the end of 2023 it held about 0.12 per cent of its investments in those three governments' debt. [cite:ts11_umnews_bonds2024]

The documented pattern is that the churches outside the dispensational tradition have rejected its reading of prophecy in their own teaching documents, and that some have turned that rejection into votes on investment. What the record does not show is a single Christian position against Israel: the Catholic Church recognises Israel and condemns antisemitism in its highest teaching, the Presbyterian vote passed by seven votes out of 613, and Catholic and Anglican theologians publicly defend forms of Christian Zionism.
