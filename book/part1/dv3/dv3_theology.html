Whose Theology? The Vatican, Scofield and the Return to Zion

The title of this volume calls the Vatican the theological parent of Israel. That claim can mean two different things, and the record answers them differently. It can mean that Christian theology made the return of Jews to Palestine thinkable and politically popular in the countries that mattered. Or it can mean that the Vatican's theology, the teaching of the Catholic Church, did so. The first is well documented. The second is very nearly the opposite of what the documents show, and the reason lies in a division inside Christianity that is four and a half centuries old.

What the Vatican can and cannot bind. The Catholic Church's teaching authority, its magisterium, binds Catholics: Catholic doctrine, Catholic clergy, and Catholic religious publications, which need its approval, the imprimatur, before they are printed. [cite:catholic_imprimatur_explained] It has no authority over Protestant churches, their doctrines or their books. That is not an oversight; the Protestant Reformation of the sixteenth century was, at its root, a refusal of the Vatican's authority to bind believers, and the churches that came out of it have never recognized it since. When the theology that made millions of Christians into supporters of a Jewish state took shape, it took shape on the Protestant side of that line, and the Vatican had no means of stopping it, and no wish to adopt it.

The theology that did the work. The decisive text was an American Protestant Bible. The Scofield Reference Bible, edited by Cyrus I. Scofield, was first published in 1909 by Oxford University Press and revised in 1917; it printed the King James text with notes that taught dispensationalism, the view that God deals with humanity in a series of distinct eras running to a final judgment, and it had sold more than two million copies by the end of the Second World War. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] In its scheme the restoration of the Jews to their land was a step in the prophetic calendar, and when a Jewish state was founded in 1948 many readers took it as proof that the scheme was true. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] Its note on Genesis 12:3 is still quoted by Christian Zionist leaders such as John Hagee, founder of Christians United for Israel, in the form that a nation that raises its hand against Israel "invites the wrath of God." [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] Volume II traces this current from its British beginnings, through Lord Shaftesbury and the Protestant restorationists of the nineteenth century, to the American evangelicals who became one of Israel's most reliable constituencies. [scene:bc_s05|Protestant restorationism] It is a real theological parent of political support for Israel. It is not the Vatican's.

What the Vatican taught instead. On the central point the Catholic Church had already ruled against that theology. In 1944 the Holy Office, the Vatican's doctrinal tribunal, judged that even a mitigated form of millenarianism, the belief in a literal earthly reign of Christ before the end, "cannot safely be taught." [cite:catholic_answers_rapture_tract] The Catechism of the Catholic Church still carries that ruling. Paragraph 676 says that the Church "has rejected even modified forms of this falsification of the kingdom to come under the name of millenarianism," and its footnote points to the 1944 decree. [cite:dv3_usccb_ccc676] [cite:catholicculture_five_myths_rapture] The prophetic machinery that turned Scofield's readers into supporters of a Jewish state was, for Catholics, forbidden doctrine.

Older Catholic teaching ran against the Zionist project from the other direction, through the Church's view of the Jewish people themselves. In January 1904 Pius X gave Theodor Herzl its practical conclusion in a single sentence: "We cannot prevent the Jews from going to Jerusalem but we could never sanction it." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] Herzl's diary records the reason the pope gave, that the Jews had not recognized Christ, and that the ground of Jerusalem had been made holy by his life. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The record pope by pope, in the next section, shows that refusal holding for most of the century.

The change, and its limit. Catholic teaching about Jews changed sharply after the Holocaust. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate, promulgated on 28 October 1965, stated that what happened in Christ's passion "cannot be blamed upon all the Jews then living nor the Jews of today." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] Fifty years later, on 10 December 2015, the Holy See's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews published "The Gifts and the Calling of God are Irrevocable," which said that the Church "neither conducts nor supports any specific institutional mission work directed towards Jews." [cite:dv3_jta_gifts_2015] The theological ground that had made Pius X's refusal automatic was gone.

What did not change was the Church's refusal to give the modern state a religious meaning. The same commission's guidance of 24 June 1985, written for Catholic preachers and teachers, put the point precisely: "The existence of the State of Israel and its political options should be envisaged not in a perspective which is in itself religious, but in their reference to the common principles of international law." [cite:dv3_jcr_notes_1985] A Jewish organization criticized the document at the time for failing to consider Israel's religious significance. [cite:dv3_jta_notes_1985] That sentence is the clearest statement in the record of how the Catholic Church positions itself. It came to accept the Jewish people's covenant as living and irrevocable, and it came to recognize the State of Israel as a state. It has not treated that state as the fulfilment of anything.

Answering the question. If "theological parent" means the Christian theology that made Zionism attractive to the governments and voters of Britain and the United States, the parent was Protestant: the restorationists of nineteenth-century England and the dispensationalists of twentieth-century America. If it means the Vatican, the record shows a church whose teaching first ruled out a Jewish return as a matter of doctrine, then condemned the prophetic scheme that made Protestants enthusiasts for it, and finally accepted the state on the same legal footing as any other. The Vatican's real theological contribution is of a different kind, and it is taken up in the parent test at the end of this part: by changing what Catholics were taught about Jews in 1965, it removed one of the oldest religious arguments against Jewish life, in Palestine and everywhere else.
