The Patron and the Translator

In 1460 a Greek manuscript of the Hermetic texts was found in Macedonia and brought to Cosimo de' Medici in Florence. In 1463 Cosimo commissioned the young scholar Marsilio Ficino to translate it, "interrupting the latter's work on Plato". [cite:ht_enc_hermeticism] Cosimo was Ficino's most important patron. In 1463 he also gave him a small property at Careggi, near the Medici villa, where Ficino said he devoted himself to "the Academy you arranged for us on the estate in Careggi". [cite:ht_sep_ficino] In 1464, as Cosimo was dying, Ficino read him new translations of Plato. [cite:ht_sep_ficino]

The order of work was a judgement about authority. Ficino believed the Hermetic writings had been written by ancient Egyptian priests, and he placed them at the head of a chain of sages: Hermes, Orpheus, Aglaophemus, Pythagoras, Philolaus and then Plato; after 1469 he put Zoroaster first. In his preface to Hermes he described "a single system of ancient theology, harmonious in every part". [cite:ht_sep_ficino] If Hermes was older than Plato and agreed with Moses, then pagan wisdom and Christian revelation were one teaching, and the oldest witness should be read first.

The money and the theology were tied together. A banking family paid for the translation, gave the translator a house and set his priorities, and the translation in turn gave Florence's leading family a claim to be the restorers of the oldest wisdom in the world. [cite:ht_enc_hermeticism] [cite:ht_sep_ficino] Ficino followed his patron's order: Hermes first, Plato second.

The ideas that came with Hermes were not all safe. Ficino's Three Books on Life, printed in 1489, dealt in its third book with drawing life from the heavens, including astrological and occult ideas such as "statue animation". It was the only one of his works to draw the negative attention of church authorities; in 1490 he asked friends at the court of Pope Innocent VIII for help, and by August he was assured that his reputation there was favourable. [cite:ht_sep_ficino] His younger Florentine contemporary Pico della Mirandola was less fortunate: Innocent VIII condemned thirteen of his nine hundred theses in 1487, and Pico fled to France, where he was arrested at the pope's request. [cite:kb_sep_pico] The line around Hermetic learning in Florence ran where it ran everywhere on this shelf: a scholar with a powerful patron and an orthodox defence could pursue it; one who challenged all comers in Rome could not.
