Content Moderation as a Contested Ground

This section picks up where [scene:s00a|the account of how the story is taught] leaves off, in an arena that is entirely contemporary: what happens to Palestinian and pro-Palestinian content on the major platforms. Two simple stories compete. One says moderation is neutral. The other says it is a secret, coordinated effort to silence one side. The record for 2021 to 2026 supports neither. It is drawn from formal investigations, from reviews the platforms commissioned themselves, from the platforms' own denials, and from findings that run the other way.

The weightiest finding comes from two separate inquiries. In 2021 Meta commissioned an independent investigation by Business for Social Responsibility. It concluded that the company's content moderation "appear[s] to have had an adverse human rights impact on the rights of Palestinian users." In December 2023 Human Rights Watch published a 51-page report, "Meta's Broken Promises: Systemic Censorship of Palestine Content on Instagram and Facebook," documenting 1,050 cases of removal or suppression across more than 60 countries. After the report, Senator Elizabeth Warren wrote to Meta's chief executive, Mark Zuckerberg, demanding accountability. [cite:hrw_meta_broken_promises] One inquiry was begun by critics; the other was initiated and paid for by the company. When a company's own audit reaches the same conclusion as its critics, the finding carries particular weight. It describes a pattern, not a claim that every removal was politically motivated. Moderation at global scale produces errors of every kind.

A broader dataset brings in other platforms. Sada Social's Digital Rights Index 2024 documented more than 25,000 violations against Palestinian digital content: Instagram 31 percent, TikTok 27 percent, Facebook 24 percent and X 12 percent. [cite:sada_social_digital_rights_index_2024] The Human Rights Watch report, which concentrated on Meta, noted that it received fewer reports about TikTok. Differences of this kind may reflect moderation policy. They may equally reflect the size of each platform's user base, its reporting tools, or the reach of the monitoring organization. The data does not say which, and this book does not decide.

One named case followed a change of ownership. In 2025–26 an Oracle-led group acquired TikTok's American operations, in a deal involving Larry Ellison, whose record of major personal donations to Israeli military-support causes is extensively documented. After the acquisition, a Palestinian journalist and influencer known as Owda was banned from the platform. TikTok's new ownership has not publicly addressed the case. [cite:meo_us_tiktok_ownership_bans] Observers have discussed a link between the ownership change and the ban. That link is plausible but not independently documented. The two facts stand side by side; no causal connection between them is established.

The record also runs the other way. In November 2023 the Anti-Defamation League found specific loopholes in TikTok's moderation, including Photo Mode slideshows and hashtag manipulation, that let antisemitic content slip past automated detection. [cite:adl_tiktok_antisemitism_loopholes] In January 2025 the watchdog CyberWell praised TikTok for improved removal of Holocaust-denial content compared with other platforms. TikTok has formally denied bias, and Reuters reported the company calling Malaysian claims that it suppressed pro-Palestinian content "unfounded." Calcalist, an Israeli technology publication, examined the Human Rights Watch report and identified possible biases in its sampling and choice of platforms. [cite:calcalist_hrw_methodology_critique] That critique deserves to be read alongside the report. None of this cancels the earlier findings. Moderation that fails Palestinian users and moderation that lets antisemitic content through are not mutually exclusive, and on this record both occurred.

Users adapted. Palestinian and pro-Palestinian users began posting images of watermelons, whose red, green, black and white match the colors of the Palestinian flag, because keyword and image filters that flagged the flag did not flag the fruit. That origin is why the watermelon became a globally recognized shorthand for solidarity with Palestine. [cite:wagingnonviolence_watermelon_algorithms] The workaround shows that moderation of flag-related content was something people met often enough to route around. It does not measure how widespread that moderation was.

Held together, the record looks like this. A human rights investigation and a platform's own commissioned review both found an adverse pattern affecting Palestinian users. A credible watchdog found antisemitic content evading moderation on another platform. A platform formally denied bias. A named methodological critique of the most prominent report exists. Several independent and sometimes adversarial sources, among them Human Rights Watch, Meta's own reviewers, Sada Social, the ADL and CyberWell, converge on real but partial and sometimes conflicting findings. That does not mean no pattern exists. It means the honest account ends in complexity, not a clean verdict in either direction.
