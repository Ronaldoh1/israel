What Counts as Pro-Israel Money

Every argument about pro-Israel money in American politics begins with a number, and most arguments go wrong at the same place: before the number, in the definition of what was counted. A figure can describe money given to a candidate or money spent attacking one. It can cover one election or a thirty-year career. It can come from a single organization or from a tracker that adds together every group with "Israel" in its mission. Two honest people can quote figures that differ by a factor of twenty and both be right, because they are counting different things. This part of the volume sets out the definitions the rest of it uses, the legal channels through which the money moves, and the rules for reading any figure that follows. It is the shortest part of the book and the one the others depend on.

A definition by organization, not by identity. In this volume, "pro-Israel money" means money that moves through organizations whose stated purpose is to support Israel or the relationship between the United States and Israel: political action committees, super PACs, lobbying organizations, charitable foundations and the registered agents of the Israeli government. It does not mean money given by Jewish Americans, and nothing in this volume treats a donor's religion, ancestry or ethnicity as evidence of anything. Many Jewish Americans oppose the organizations studied here, and many of the largest donors to those organizations are not Jewish. Individual donors appear in this book only where the public record names them as major donors to the vehicles being studied, and they appear for what they gave, not for who they are. The same rule applies in Part Nine to every other country.

American money, a foreign-policy cause. The second definition concerns where the money comes from. Federal law bars foreign nationals, including foreign governments, foreign political parties and foreign corporations, from making contributions or expenditures, directly or indirectly, in any federal, state or local election in the United States; lawful permanent residents are not covered by the ban. [cite:fec_foreign_nationals] The campaign records studied in this volume therefore do not show money from the government of Israel. They show money from American citizens and residents, given through American organizations, in support of a foreign-policy outcome. That is the accurate frame, and it cuts in two directions at once. It rules out the claim, common online, that Israel pays American politicians through their campaign accounts; the records do not show that. It also rules out the opposite claim, that the money is too small or too scattered to matter; the records show sums that, in some years and some races, are among the largest in American politics.

There is one channel in which a foreign government does pay, openly, and it is not a campaign channel. Under the Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938, a person or firm working in the United States for a foreign principal must register and disclose the work. Israel's government uses that channel. In September 2025 two firms registered as its agents for paid messaging campaigns in the United States, one of them run by a former manager of Donald Trump's presidential campaign. [cite:pc_fara_complaint] [cite:dc_parscale_fara] Those registrations, and what they disclose, are set out in [scene:ip_s02|the simulation's account of Clock Tower X] and in Part Nine. AIPAC, the largest of the American organizations, is not registered under the act. It says it represents Americans and receives no money from any foreign government; its critics argue that its alignment with Israel's policy priorities should bring it within the law. The history of that dispute, from the Justice Department's 1962 order to AIPAC's parent body to the Senate's inquiry of 1963, is told in [scene:s11a|the order of 21 November 1962] and [scene:cw5_fulbright_inquiry|the Fulbright inquiry].

The long record. Pro-Israel money is not new, and it was not always large. The Center for Responsive Politics, which publishes OpenSecrets, has kept a pro-Israel category for decades. Its figures for 1990 to 2012 show $108.6 million in contributions to federal candidates over twelve election cycles: $71.5 million from individuals, $34.5 million from political action committees and $2.5 million in "soft money" given to parties before that practice was banned. About two-thirds went to Democrats and one-third to Republicans. The cycle totals ranged from $5.6 million in 1996 to $14.0 million in 2008. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs] For most of that period the money moved through several dozen independent PACs and through individual donors, not through AIPAC. As OpenSecrets noted in 2019, AIPAC itself did not contribute to candidates at all. [cite:opensecrets_aipac_2019]

Those figures look modest beside what followed. In December 2021 AIPAC announced that it would form its own PAC and a super PAC, [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] and from the 2022 cycle onward the scale of pro-Israel spending changed by an order of magnitude, as Part Three shows cycle by cycle. The change was not only in size. It was in kind. A super PAC does not give money to candidates; it spends money to elect or defeat them. The largest sums in this volume are of that second kind, and the most consequential of them were spent in primaries against sitting members of Congress.

What this volume does not claim. Three limits apply to everything that follows. First, receiving money is legal and does not by itself show why any member of Congress votes as he or she does. Donors often give to candidates who already agree with them, and the direction of cause between money and position is a question the campaign records alone cannot settle. Second, pro-Israel organizations disagree with one another. J Street, which calls itself "pro-Israel, pro-peace" and campaigns for a two-state solution, is counted as pro-Israel by most trackers, and in 2018 it described itself as the largest pro-Israel PAC in the country; [cite:pm_jstreet_2018] so is the Republican Jewish Coalition; so is AIPAC. Adding them together, as some trackers do, produces a large number that describes no single interest. Third, other interests spend more on some channels. In 2018, the last year before AIPAC entered campaign giving, AIPAC spent about $3.5 million on lobbying and all pro-Israel groups together about $5.0 million, against $94.8 million by the US Chamber of Commerce; by OpenSecrets' count pro-Israel donors ranked thirty-fourth among interest groups giving to members of Congress that year. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] Part Three sets the comparison out for the years since.

What the volume does claim is narrower and better documented. It is that a network of American organizations, built over seventy years, now moves money through every legal channel open to it, at every level of American government, in amounts that are public and can be counted; that the money has been concentrated, at its largest, against members of Congress who broke with the network's positions; and that similar organizations, smaller and less documented, operate in other democracies.
