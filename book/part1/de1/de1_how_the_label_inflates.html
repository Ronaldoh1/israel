How the Label Inflates

When Klint Finley introduced neoreaction to the readers of TechCrunch on 22 November 2013, he described it as "a community of bloggers" who wanted to turn the dial back to "the days before the French Revolution." He named Yarvin as its starting point and Land as the man who coined "dark enlightenment," and he mentioned Peter Thiel, carefully, as a PayPal founder who "has voiced similar ideas," quoting the 2009 sentence about freedom and democracy. [cite:de1_finley_tc] Thirteen years later the same label is attached, in newspapers, academic journals and online commentary, to billionaires, a vice president, an administration and much of Silicon Valley. Some of that spread follows documented ties. Much of it does not. This section shows how the label grows, sorts who has actually claimed it and who has denied it, and then sets out the working hypothesis the book tests.

Two worked examples from 2025

The clearest way to see inflation is to read claims against their own footnotes. In August 2025 the peer-reviewed journal Politics and Governance published an article by Danica Fink-Hafner of the University of Ljubljana comparing how liberal democracy, illiberal democracy and the Dark Enlightenment treat elections. It said the Dark Enlightenment had been "significantly empowered" by the world's richest individuals, "particularly Elon Musk," who "have adopted Dark Enlightenment ideas." Its source for that was a 2025 magazine article, not a statement by Musk. It said that Steve Bannon "also supports the Dark Enlightenment," citing a 2017 Politico list of books Bannon recommended. And it said that the ideology's "followers damage free and fair elections." [cite:de1_fh_2025] Each step moves from a text, a reading list or a news story to a belief held by a named person, and then to a group of "followers" who are never named at all.

A second 2025 paper, by Bahram Sarmast in the journal Interdisciplinary Studies in Society, Law, and Politics, went further. It said that J.D. Vance "has explicitly praised Yarvin's work," that the Trump administration's cuts to the civil service showed neoreactionary critiques "operationalized," and that a proposal to turn Gaza into a "Riviera of the Middle East" embodied "Yarvin's concept of neocameralist management." [cite:de1_sarmast_2025] The documented version of the Vance claim is narrower. In 2021, on a conservative podcast, Vance said "there's this guy, Curtis Yarvin, who has written about some of these things," and called for a president to fire "every single mid-level bureaucrat, every civil servant in the administrative state." [cite:de1_vox_vance] [cite:de1_nextgov_vance] That is a citation by name, the third rung on the ladder in the previous section. It is not praise of Yarvin's work as a whole, and it says nothing about neocameralism. The Gaza example has no document linking it to Yarvin at all; it is resemblance offered as provenance.

Not all commentary inflates. A 2022 explainer in The Conversation on neoreaction in Republican politics laid out Yarvin's ideas from his own writing, recorded Vance's podcast remark and Thiel's funding of candidates, and then noted that neither Vance nor Blake Masters had explicitly called for dismantling American democracy. [cite:de1_conv_2022] That is the standard this book holds: what was said, by whom, and what was not said.

Who has said they hold it, and who has denied it

Only two people stand on the top rung, and both are authors. Land named the current in 2012 and wrote of it from the inside, calling Yarvin the "supreme Sith Lord of the neoreactionaries." [cite:de1_land_gwern] Yarvin has used several labels for himself. In 2008, in his "Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives," he wrote that if he had to choose one word he would pick "restorationist," and that if he had to concede one pejorative that fair writers could apply, he would accept "reactionary." The same year he first used "neoreactionary" for his project. [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] In January 2025, when his interviewer at The New York Times used the word monarch for what he proposes, Yarvin interjected, "Monarch is good," adding, "It's a neutral term." [cite:de1_nyt_interview]

Yarvin's denials are as much part of his record as his claims, and they are often qualified in the same breath. In a 2007 post titled "Why I am not a white nationalist" he wrote that although he was not one, "I am not exactly allergic to the stuff." In a published response quoted by the Boulder Daily Camera, he wrote that he was not "an outspoken advocate for slavery, a racist, a sexist or a fascist." [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] When reports circulated in 2017 that he was advising Steve Bannon, he denied them. [cite:de1_tablet] Asked in 2025 about his friendship with Vance, he said it was "definitely overstated," and said his ideas spread "mostly through the staffers and the younger people." An adviser to Vance has said the two met "like once." [cite:de1_nyt_interview] [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] Asked about a 2016 email in which he described Thiel as "fully enlightened," Yarvin said that for him the phrase means "fully disenchanted." [cite:de2_pein2017] [cite:de1_nyt_interview] These statements are recorded here as what he said, not as settled facts; [scene:de6_claims_vs_record|Part Six] sets them against the documents.

No one else in this book has, in their own words, claimed the label. Thiel's own comment on Yarvin, as reported from a 2023 interview with The Atlantic, was that his ideas would not "work." [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin] Marc Andreessen's 2023 "Techno-Optimist Manifesto" listed Land among its "patron saints," which is a citation of Land, documented, and not an adoption of neocameralism. [cite:de1_a16z_wiki] Vance's documented citation of Yarvin by name is the 2021 podcast remark. Everyone else to whom the label is applied sits on the lower rungs, and the book says which.

Neighbours, not the same thing

The label also inflates sideways, absorbing other currents that share a stage or a funder. The Dark Enlightenment is distinct from conservatism in general, which defends inherited institutions that these texts propose to replace; from the Republican Party, a mass electoral organisation; from Christian nationalism and Catholic integralism, which are religious and, in the first case, majoritarian, where these texts are secular and elitist; from pronatalism, a single policy aim; from the rationalist community, one of whose best-known writers, Scott Alexander, published a long rebuttal of neoreaction in 2013; and from "effective accelerationism," a technology-optimist current. It is, above all, distinct from the violent "accelerationism" of neo-Nazi terror groups, a doctrine of attacks meant to hasten collapse, which shares a word with Land's philosophy and must not be confused with it. [cite:de2_ssc_faq] [cite:de1_a16z_wiki] [scene:de16_neighbours_not_the_same|Part Sixteen] maps each neighbour and its documented touchpoints, on equal terms.

The working hypothesis, and how it is graded

The Sophia platform proposes a reading of the period that this book adopts as its working hypothesis, not as a finding. Stated in its worst case: that immigration enforcement and government cuts in 2025 and 2026 were the occasion, and that a sovereign executive without a veto, the end state the Dark Enlightenment texts describe, is the destination. The book reads every mechanism it examines in that worst case, and then tests the reading against the record.

Every part ends with a passage headed "The worst-case reading, tested," which grades what that part's material shows in three registers. Documented means a primary record or independent named reporting shows it. Probable means several documented facts point the same way and no documented fact points against, but the link itself is not on paper. Not supported means the record does not show it, or shows the opposite; where the record runs against the hypothesis, the passage says so plainly. The biography parts add a second scale for public claims set against the record: red where a claim is contradicted by the person's own documents or sworn record, orange where a promise is undelivered or unverifiable by the official auditor, yellow for a reversal or concealment shown with the person's own explanation, and grey where credible accounts conflict. The word "lie" is used only where the record shows a person knew a claim was untrue when making it. The same scales apply to every person, whatever their party. [scene:de40_worst_case_scorecard|Part Forty] gathers every grade in one place.

The worst-case reading, tested

This part bears on the hypothesis in three ways. First, the destination is documented as a stated aim of the texts. Land's serial and the Yarvin passages it quotes describe a state owned and run like a company by a chief executive, with residents who have "no voice," and Yarvin said in 2025 that he does not believe in voting at all. [cite:de1_land_ebook] [cite:de1_nyt_interview] A sovereign executive without a veto is not an inference about these authors; it is what they wrote. Second, the claim that people in power hold this aim is, on this part's material, not supported beyond one documented link: Vance's 2021 citation of Yarvin by name. The other attributions examined here rest on adjacency or resemblance, and some are contradicted by the people concerned. Third, coordination is not supported: no document in this part shows a plan connecting the texts to the occasions the hypothesis names. The record also runs against the hypothesis in one respect worth noting at the start. The early texts, and Thiel's 2009 essay, framed the goal as exit, leaving existing states, rather than seizing one. Whether and when that changed is the question [scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two] and the parts after it take up.
