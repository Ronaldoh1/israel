Recognition and the Coup

The alliance between Israel and Iran began in a vote against Israel's creation. When the United Nations General Assembly voted on the partition of Palestine in November 1947, Iran, then ruled by the young Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi, voted against it. [cite:ir1_wiki_embtehran] Two and a half years later the same government recognised the state it had voted against. On 6 March 1950, while the Majlis, Iran's parliament, was in recess, the government extended de facto recognition to Israel "without formal announcement," according to the Encyclopaedia Iranica entry on the two countries' relations by the historians David Menashri and Trita Parsi. The decision drew strong opposition inside Iran and in Arab capitals. Iran never extended de jure, or full legal, recognition, and that distinction shaped the whole relationship that followed: real but unannounced, with no ambassadors formally accredited. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] Iran was the second Muslim-majority state, after Turkey, to recognise Israel, and Israel kept what amounted to an embassy in Tehran under the name of a political representation. [cite:ir1_wiki_embtehran]

The date matters because it is often given wrongly. Some accounts, including an earlier volume of this library, list Iran among the first states to recognise Israel in May 1948, immediately after the United States. The record does not support that. Iran voted against partition in 1947 and recognised Israel de facto in March 1950. [cite:ir1_wiki_embtehran] [cite:ir1_iranica_israel]

Mosaddegh and the oil

The recognition soon ran into Iranian politics. On 29 April 1951 Mohammad Mosaddegh, leader of the National Front, became prime minister, and two days later his government nationalised the oil industry, which until then had been run by the British-owned Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. Britain answered with economic sanctions, naval and military manoeuvres in the region and covert efforts to replace him. [cite:ir1_iranica_coup] On 7 July 1951, shortly after Mosaddegh took office, his government closed Iran's consulate in Jerusalem, citing "financial difficulties." It did not withdraw the de facto recognition. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] The record examined for this book does not show Mosaddegh's government taking further steps against Israel; the consulate closure is the documented act, and its stated reason is the government's own.

The coup of August 1953

The British government failed twice to replace Mosaddegh with a prime minister of its choosing, in 1951 and 1952, and in October 1952 he broke diplomatic relations with Britain. President Truman declined to join a plan to remove him. The Eisenhower administration did not. On 3 February 1953 American and British officials agreed on a plan, code-named AJAX by the Americans, to be run by the CIA under Kermit Roosevelt with British support; it was given final approval at a State Department meeting on 25 June 1953. [cite:ir1_iranica_coup] The first attempt, around 15 August, failed: the officer carrying the Shah's decree dismissing Mosaddegh and naming General Fazlollah Zahedi in his place was arrested on arrival, and the Shah left the country for Baghdad and then Rome. On 19 August a crowd paid by the CIA, apparently raised through the clerics Ayatollah Abol-Ghasem Kashani and Mohammad Behbahani, marched on the bazaar and Radio Tehran; army and police units joined it, and tanks attacked Mosaddegh's house. He surrendered the next day. Zahedi became prime minister, the Shah returned, and Mosaddegh was convicted of treason and held under house arrest until his death in 1967. [cite:ir1_iranica_coup] The Shah was restored, not installed: he had been on the throne since 1941.

For decades the United States did not formally acknowledge its role. In August 2013 the National Security Archive at George Washington University published a CIA internal history, "The Battle for Iran," released under the Freedom of Information Act, which stated: "The military coup that overthrew Mossadegh and his National Front Cabinet was carried out under CIA direction." The same history gave the agency's reason: "The risk of leaving Iran open to Soviet aggression compelled the United States" to act. An earlier release of the document, in 1981, had blacked out the section on the coup. [cite:ir1_afp_cia2013] [cite:ir1_npr_cia2013]

How much the CIA decided: the historians' debate

That the CIA planned, paid for and directed the operation is documented in the agency's own history. How decisive it was is argued among historians. Mark Gasiorowski, author of the Iranica entry and the most detailed scholarly accounts, describes a joint British and American operation in which Iranian actors were central: Zahedi, the Rashidian brothers, whom he describes as British agents, and figures such as Kashani who wanted Mosaddegh gone for their own reasons, while Mosaddegh's support had been worn down within the National Front, the Majlis and the army. [cite:ir1_iranica_coup] The historian Ray Takeyh argued in Foreign Affairs in 2014 that after the first attempt failed Washington had largely given up, and that the second, successful attempt was "very much an Iranian initiative," because "Iranian royalists had more at stake and more to lose than the Americans." [cite:ir1_takeyh_fa] The historian Gregory Brew, reviewing the literature in 2019, groups the explanations for the American decision into those that stress the fear of communism (Gasiorowski), those that stress control of oil (Ervand Abrahamian), and revisionists who play down the foreign role (Darioush Bayandor, Takeyh); he notes Fakhreddin Azimi's detailed reply to the revisionists, and argues himself that Washington acted on a "collapse narrative," a belief that Iran was about to fall apart, without strong evidence of an imminent communist takeover. [cite:ir1_brew_tnsr]

The debate is about weight, not about the documented facts. No serious account disputes that the CIA and British intelligence organised the operation and that it removed an elected prime minister. What is argued is whether it would have succeeded without Iranian allies who wanted the same result, and why the United States chose it.

What the coup did for the alliance

The coup did not create the relationship with Israel, which predated Mosaddegh. What it did, on the documented sequence, was return power to a monarch who over the next decade concentrated it in his own hands, and it was under that monarch that the alliance grew. The Shah's new security service, SAVAK, was founded in 1957, and by the account of the historians Ervand Abrahamian and Mohsen Milani, Mossad helped found it alongside the CIA. [cite:ir1_irb_savak] Iran's crude sales to Israel accelerated from 1958, and on 24 July 1960 the Shah publicly reaffirmed Iran's recognition of Israel, a statement that led Egypt's President Gamal Abdel Nasser to break relations with Iran. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] The next section describes that partnership. Whether a Mosaddegh government, had it survived, would have kept or ended the relationship cannot be known; the record shows only that his government closed the consulate and kept the recognition. The connection between the coup and Iran's later hostility toward Israel is an argument made by some writers; Part Two examines it. [scene:ir2_revolution|The revolution]
