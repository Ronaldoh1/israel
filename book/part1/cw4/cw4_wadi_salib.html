Wadi Salib, 1959: "Is Our Blood Cheap?"

The crises of 1958 turned on Israel's place among states. The crisis of 1959 turned on the place of its own citizens. The mass immigration of 1948–52, and the transit camps that received hundreds of thousands of newcomers, are told in Volume II, in [scene:rd6_ingathering|the mass immigration]. By the summer of 1959 most of those immigrants had been in the country for years, and the question was no longer how to house them in the first months but what kind of place they had been given in the state. In July of that year the question was put in the street, in one neighbourhood of Haifa, by a union of immigrants from North Africa. Its causes were argued over at the time and have been argued over since, in two accounts: the one the protesters wrote and the one the state's commission of inquiry gave.

A neighbourhood twice settled. Wadi Salib had a history before 1959 that shaped its geography. Before 1948 it was a mainly Muslim quarter of Haifa, home to families such as that of the former mayor 'Abd al-Rahman al-Hajj. In the war of 1948 its Palestinian residents were uprooted and became refugees, and Jewish migrants, most of them from North Africa, were settled in the houses they had left. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] [cite:rs_cw4_cie_wadisalib] The neighbourhood that protested in 1959 was therefore one built on an earlier displacement: the people who lived there had arrived as the state's newest citizens in the houses of people who had become refugees. The scholarship used here does not record whether the 1959 protesters or the authorities spoke of that history at the time.

The shooting, 8 July 1959. On the night of 8 July 1959 police shot and wounded Akiva Yaakov Elkrif, a resident of the neighbourhood who had been born in Morocco. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] [cite:rs_cw4_cie_wadisalib] How the shooting came about is disputed, and the protest began from it. The Center for Israel Education says that the police were trying to arrest Elkrif for being drunk and disorderly. It also says that witnesses disagree on whether he provoked them. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_wadisalib] The neighbourhood's own reading was that the shooting was unjustified, and the union that organized the protest put that reading into the title of its broadsheet. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] None of the sources used here settles which version is correct; they establish that a man was shot and wounded by police, and that what he did before the shot was fired is contested.

The procession, 9 July. The next day the protest acquired a leader and an organization. David Ben-Harush, a resident born in Casablanca, had founded the Union of Migrants from North Africa early in 1959. On 9 July he led a procession from the Rambam Synagogue to police headquarters. The union's broadsheet that day was titled "Is Our Blood Cheap?" According to Moshe Naor's study of the protest, published in the International Journal of Middle East Studies in 2024, the protesters damaged cafés, a bank, and buildings belonging to Mapai and to the Histadrut. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] The choice of targets is part of the record: the crowd turned on Mapai and the Histadrut, not only on the police.

The spread, July 1959. On 10 July rumours spread that Elkrif had died. In the clashes that followed, 13 police and 2 demonstrators were hurt, and 34 people were arrested. During July protests spread beyond Haifa, to Beersheba, Acre, Tiberias and Migdal HaEmek. After the union's leaders were arrested, the protest in Wadi Salib resumed from 31 July. Ben-Harush was jailed and tried. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_wadisalib] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_wadisalib] [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] These figures rest on the Center for Israel Education and an encyclopedia summary as well as on Naor, and they are given here as those sources report them. The sources used here do not record the outcome of Ben-Harush's trial or his sentence.

The commission, 19 July–17 August. The government answered with an inquiry. On 19 July 1959 it set up a commission under Judge Moshe Etzioni. The commission heard 46 witnesses in public sessions and reported on 17 August 1959. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] Its members, according to a Hebrew encyclopedia's summary of the report, included Rabbi Yitzhak Abuhatzira and the sociologist S. N. Eisenstadt. [cite:rs_cw4_hamichlol_wadisalib] One finding is quoted directly in Naor's study: "in most instances, half of [the protestors] were children, women, and adolescents." [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] The title of Naor's article, "The Children of Wadi Salib and the 1959 Moroccan Jewish Protest in Haifa," carries the same emphasis.

The protesters' account. The protesters left their own record, in the union's broadsheets. Naor's reading of them is that they framed the protest around poverty, jobs, housing and education. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] The first broadsheet's question, "Is Our Blood Cheap?", can be read as joining the shooting to that wider claim: that a life from this neighbourhood was held cheap, as its people felt they were treated in work, housing and schooling. In the protesters' reading, and in the reading of many researchers since, Wadi Salib was a protest against the marginalization of Jews from North Africa by an establishment led by Jews of European origin. That is how the Center for Israel Education summarizes it, and it is the reading Romanenko surveys in the research literature. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] [cite:rs_cw4_cie_wadisalib] [cite:rs_cw4_romanenko]

The state's account. The state's account came from three directions. The first was the city. Haifa's mayor, Abba Khoushi, said that the riots were mainly led by women and children. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] The second was Mapai. A Mapai broadsheet in August claimed that the protest had support from Herut, the opposition party. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] That was a political explanation, not a social one: it located the cause in a rival party rather than in the neighbourhood's conditions, and it was made in the same year as a general election. The third and most formal was the commission. According to a summary of sections 9–14 of its report, the Etzioni commission found no deliberate discrimination by state institutions. It found a "sincere will" at the level of policy to attend to the Mizrahi communities, and discrimination at the lower levels where policy was carried out. Etzioni wrote, in that summary's rendering, "here we all sinned." [cite:rs_cw4_hamichlol_wadisalib] The finding was therefore not an acquittal. It placed the failure in implementation rather than intention, at the lower levels where policy was carried out rather than the level where it was made, and its chairman described the failure as a shared one.

The two accounts are not as far apart as their tone. The commission did not deny that discrimination happened; it denied that it was deliberate policy. The protesters' broadsheets, as Naor summarizes them, spoke of poverty, jobs, housing and education: of results, in their streets and schools, rather than of a written policy of exclusion. Where they divided most sharply was on responsibility: whether failures at the lower levels of the state, repeated across a whole population of immigrants, could be separated from the policy that set those levels to work. The Mapai broadsheet's charge about Herut was of a different kind from either, and neither the commission's summary nor Naor's study records evidence for or against it.

The scholarship. Two recent studies take the argument further, and they agree on more than they disagree. Naor argues that poverty, housing and school dropout were central causes. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] R. E. Romanenko, writing in 2025, argues that the causes lay less in deliberate discrimination than in an absorption policy that neglected the economic integration of Mizrahi immigrants, and that this neglect produced a sense of grievance and of discrimination as the immigrants perceived it. [cite:rs_cw4_romanenko] Romanenko's position sits close to the commission's on the question of intent, and close to the protesters' on the question of outcome: the immigrants were left behind, whether or not anyone had designed it. Naor's emphasis on children and school dropout gives the protest a concrete social base that neither the mayor's remark nor the party's broadsheet allowed it.

The election and the laws. The protest entered politics directly. Ben-Harush ran for the Knesset in the 1959 election from prison, on the list of the Union of North African Immigrants, and the list did not pass the threshold. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_wadisalib] [cite:rs_cw4_hamichlol_wadisalib] (Naor names his organization the Union of Migrants from North Africa; the encyclopedias name the electoral list the Union of North African Immigrants. The sources do not say whether these are translations of one name.) Its effect on policy was slower and less visible than its effect in the street. Naor argues that the protests led to government measures, among them the Youth Law (Care and Supervision) of 1960 and the Child Benefit Law. [cite:rs_cw4_naor_ijmes] Both answered, on paper at least, the conditions that his study places at the centre of the protest: children out of school and families without enough to live on.

Wadi Salib stands at one end of a longer history of how the young state received the Jews of the Arab world, a history that includes [scene:s00e2|the exodus and what followed it] and the families' accounts of infants taken from the immigrant camps, told in [scene:mz_s02|the Yemenite children affair]. It should be described for what the documents show it was: a protest by a named union, in a named neighbourhood, after a named man was shot, answered by a named mayor, a named party and a named commission. It was not the voice of a whole community, and the commission's finding was not the voice of a whole state.

What the record leaves open. Several questions cannot be answered from the sources used for this section. The exact wording of the Etzioni report on discrimination is known here only through a secondary summary of its sections 9–14, not from the report itself, so the phrases "sincere will" and "here we all sinned" are given as that summary renders them. How many votes Ben-Harush's list won, and what sentence he received, are not recorded in these sources. Whether Elkrif provoked the police on 8 July is still disputed by the witnesses whom the Center for Israel Education cites. And the Mapai claim that Herut supported the protest is documented only as a claim.
