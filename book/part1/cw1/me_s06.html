Iran's Oil Vote and the Anglo-Iranian Crisis, 1949–1952

While Israel and its neighbours were working out where they stood between Washington and Moscow, a major Cold War crisis of the region was unfolding further east, in Iran, and it was about oil before it was about anything else. A familiar account holds that Iran has always been governed by extremists, and that the West has only ever dealt with hostile regimes there. The record of 1941 to 1953 does not fit that account. It shows an elected parliament that recovered its voice after a foreign invasion, a nationalist prime minister it chose, a long legal and diplomatic contest with Britain over the country's largest industry, and a government whose own conduct became contested before a foreign-organized coup ended it. All of these belong to the record, and this section follows them to October 1952.

The starting point is 1941. In August of that year British and Soviet forces invaded Iran and forced Reza Shah to abdicate in favour of his son, the young Mohammad Reza Shah. [cite:loc_mossadeq] The invasion was an act of wartime occupation, but one of its consequences ran the other way: with the old Shah gone, parliamentary politics revived. The Majlis, Iran's parliament, became once again a place where the country's largest questions were argued, and by the end of the decade the largest of them was the oil concession held by the British-owned Anglo-Iranian Oil Company.

Oil was the state's development plan as well as its grievance. In 1949 the Majlis approved the First Development Plan, covering 1948 to 1955, which was to be financed largely from oil revenues; the oil question dominated that year's elections. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] The nationalist National Front won seats in 1950, and Mohammad Mossadeq chaired the Majlis committee on oil. [cite:loc_mossadeq] In November 1950 that committee, under his chairmanship, rejected a draft agreement with the company because it lacked fifty-fifty profit-sharing. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] The demand was specific: not yet the end of the concession, but an equal division of its profits.

The government of the day tried to hold a middle line. General Ali Razmara had become prime minister in June 1950. In February 1951 the company offered the fifty-fifty sharing the committee had asked for. It came too late. In March 1951 Razmara, who had advised against nationalization, was assassinated by Khalil Tahmasebi of the Fadayan-e Islam. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] On 15 March 1951 the Majlis voted to nationalize the oil industry. [cite:w_iran_oil_nationalization] [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] In April 1951 the Shah, yielding to the Majlis and to the street, named Mossadeq prime minister. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study]

The mechanism matters for what followed. The decision to nationalize was taken by the elected parliament, by vote; the appointment of the prime minister remained the Shah's, and he made it under pressure from parliament and from crowds rather than by his own preference. Mossadeq therefore held office on the strength of the Majlis while the Shah kept the formal power of appointment. That division of authority would become the hinge of the crisis in 1952 and again in August 1953.

Britain answered on two tracks, legal and economic. On 26 May 1951 it filed a case against Iran at the International Court of Justice. On 5 July 1951 the Court indicated provisional measures. [cite:rs_cw1_icj_aioc] Meanwhile Washington tried mediation. The Harriman mission left Washington on 13 July 1951 and reached Tehran on 15 July, where it was met by an estimated 10,000 pro-Communist demonstrators outside the United States Embassy. A British mission under Stokes was in Tehran from 4 to 23 August, when the Anglo-Iranian talks were suspended. [cite:rs_cw1_frus_harriman] The crowd at the embassy gates is worth noticing: the demonstrators greeting the American envoy were described in the State Department's published record as pro-Communist, and that fact, set beside a nationalist government that was not itself Communist, is the background to the American debate that followed.

With the talks broken off, Britain moved to economic pressure. In September 1951 it froze Iran's sterling assets and banned the export of goods to Iran. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] Britain also imposed a worldwide embargo on Iranian oil. [cite:w_iran_oil_nationalization] The cost fell on Iran's production and on the revenues the development plan depended on. Iran's oil output collapsed from 242 million barrels in 1950 to 10.6 million in 1952. [cite:w_iran_oil_nationalization] In money terms, the embargo cut the channel through which the 1949 plan was to be paid for; in political terms, it tested whether a government that had nationalized its oil could survive without selling it.

The legal track ended in July 1952, and how it ended is commonly misreported. On 22 July 1952 the Court decided that it had no jurisdiction to deal with the dispute. Its reasoning was narrow. Iran's 1932 declaration covered only disputes over treaties concluded after that date, and the 1933 concession was not a treaty to which Britain was a party. Four judges dissented: Alvarez, Hackworth, Read and Levi Carneiro. [cite:rs_cw1_icj_aioc] The Library of Congress country study describes the outcome as a finding in Iran's favour. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] Both descriptions refer to the same judgment, and each has its basis. On one reading, in practical terms Iran won: Britain's case failed and the nationalization stood unchallenged in that forum. In legal terms the Court decided nothing about the merits, neither the lawfulness of nationalization nor the company's claim to compensation; it decided only that the dispute was not one it could hear. On that reading the country study's phrasing describes the result, while the Court's own record shows its limits.

That summer the crisis turned inward. In the summer of 1952 Mossadeq demanded the right to name the minister of war, and the Shah refused. Mossadeq resigned. Three days of pro-Mossadeq rioting followed, and the Shah reappointed him. In August 1952 the Majlis granted him full powers for six months, a grant later extended. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study] The episode showed where power lay in mid-1952: the crowd and the parliament could restore a prime minister the monarch had let go, and the parliament could then hand him powers beyond the ordinary. It also showed the cost of that route. A government that ruled by full powers, renewed, was a different thing from the parliament that had voted nationalization in March 1951.

Washington's position moved during the same months, and the State Department's own papers show how. A United States aide-mémoire of 31 July 1952 proposed "an immediate grant of $10 million to the Iranian Government," together with British purchases of stored oil and arbitration on compensation. On 30 August 1952 Truman and Churchill sent Mossadeq a joint message, which he first called "flatly unacceptable." [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_byroade] The joint message mattered as a signal as much as for its terms: the two governments now addressed Tehran together.

Inside the State Department, however, Assistant Secretary Byroade argued for a different course. In September 1952 he judged that "a nationalist government will neither bring in the communists nor fall victim to a communist revolt before next March." He recommended telling Britain that keeping Iran out of the communist world outweighed legal considerations, consulting American oil companies about buying 100,000 to 150,000 barrels a day, and telling Mossadeq plainly that "the United States Government is not in the oil business." [cite:rs_cw1_frus_iran_byroade] Byroade's memorandum is a useful corrective in two directions. It shows that in the autumn of 1952 a senior American official did not regard Mossadeq's government as a Communist danger in the short term. And it shows that the American interest, as he framed it, was strategic, keeping Iran out of the Soviet camp, rather than the defence of a British company's legal claim, which he was prepared to subordinate. Whether that view prevailed is the subject of the next section.

In October 1952 Mossadeq cut diplomatic relations with Britain, according to diplomats' oral histories collected by the Association for Diplomatic Studies and Training. [cite:rs_cw1_adst_iran] It ended the formal diplomatic channel between the two governments at the height of their dispute.

One image of Mossadeq from these months needs correcting. He is often remembered as Time's "Man of the Year" for 1951, and the title is sometimes cited as proof of the world's esteem. Time did name him Man of the Year, in its issue of 7 January 1952. But the magazine's own text described him as "an appalling caricature of a statesman" and "the strange old wizard." [cite:rs_cw1_time_1952] The title marked his importance in the year's news, not approval; the magazine's words were openly hostile. That hostility is itself part of the record of how Western opinion saw the crisis while it was under way.

What followed belongs to the next section. In August 1953 Mossadeq dissolved the parliament by a plebiscite that his opponents called rigged, and on 19 August 1953 a coup organized by the CIA and MI6 removed him. [cite:loc_mossadeq] [cite:w_1953_coup] The Shah then ruled as an autocrat for 26 years. [cite:w_1953_coup] The planning, the approval, the money and the long concealment of that operation are told in [scene:s11|the coup of August 1953].

Seen whole, the record of 1941 to 1952 answers the familiar claim plainly. Iran in these years had an elected parliament that chose its own course on oil and a prime minister who held office on that parliament's strength; it also had political violence, including the assassination of a sitting prime minister, and a government that came to rule by delegated full powers. Both halves are documented, and neither cancels the other. What these sources do not settle is just as important. They do not give the company's profits, the revenue Iran lost year by year, or the terms of the 1933 concession beyond the Court's description of it; they do not show how the full powers were used between August 1952 and the end of the year; and they do not establish what the British and American governments were already planning when relations were broken in October 1952. On that last question the documents begin in 1953, and they are taken up in the next section.
