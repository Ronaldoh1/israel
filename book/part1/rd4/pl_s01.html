"Transfer" Enters the Record

In July 1937 a British royal commission, the Peel Commission, published a partition map for Palestine. Alongside the map it made a recommendation about people. It proposed an "exchange of population," modelled explicitly on the Greek-Turkish exchange of 1923. Approximately 225,000 Arabs would be moved out of the territory allotted to the Jewish state, and about 1,250 Jews out of the territory allotted to the Arab state. In the last resort, the report said, the exchange should be compulsory. It was the first official British document to put population transfer in writing. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

The idea of removing the Arab population from a future Jewish state is often assumed to have begun with Israeli extremists after 1948. The record places it earlier, and higher. It appeared first not in the program of a fringe group but in the report of a British royal commission, a decade before the war of 1948. The numbers show how lopsided the proposed exchange was: roughly 225,000 people on one side of the ledger and roughly 1,250 on the other.

How Zionist leaders reacted in private is documented in their own papers, and here the record becomes genuinely contested. In October 1937 David Ben-Gurion wrote to his son Amos. In that letter he welcomed partition as a stage toward a larger state and discussed the Arab population. His diary and letters from the same period discuss compulsory transfer as a possibility. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] One sentence in the Amos letter, however, has become the subject of a long dispute. Some have quoted it as support for expelling Arabs; others, reading the handwritten Hebrew differently, have taken it to say the opposite. Historians have disagreed over the wording, and some, including Benny Morris, have changed their positions on it over time. This book does not resolve that dispute. The meaning of that sentence remains a matter on which serious historians differ.

The response of the movement's highest body is not in dispute. The Twentieth Zionist Congress, meeting in Zurich in August 1937, accepted partition in principle as a basis for negotiation, and it did not reject the transfer clause. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] The Arab Higher Committee, for its part, rejected the Peel report outright. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

The difference between these two kinds of evidence matters. Whether any particular leader's private words amounted to an endorsement of compulsory transfer is a question on which the sources do not speak with one voice, and it should be left open. Whether the concept itself was on the table is a different question, and the answer is clear. Compulsory transfer entered the official record in a British royal commission report in 1937, and the Zionist movement's own Congress discussed it rather than rejecting it. It was a live subject at the top of the movement eleven years before the displacement of 1948.

The finding is limited, and it should be read that way. It does not establish what any individual intended to do a decade later, nor does it by itself explain what happened in 1948. What it establishes is narrower and firmer. The idea of moving a population to fit a partition map was not invented in the fighting of 1948. It had been written down, in an official document, by the power that governed the country, and it had been debated by the leadership of the movement that would found the state.
