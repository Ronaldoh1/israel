How It Began, 1948–2016

In its first years Israel received American economic aid and loans rather than weapons. Kennedy approved the sale of Hawk anti-aircraft missiles over the objections of the State Department, and Johnson's sale of Phantom jets made the United States Israel's principal arms supplier. [cite:dv4_jvl_special_alliance] Large grants of economic aid began in 1971. [cite:aa_crs_2023]

The war of October 1973 changed the scale. Congress passed an emergency appropriation of $2.2 billion for Israel, $1.5 billion of it in grants and $700 million in loans. [cite:aa_crs_2023] In February 1987 Israel was designated a major non-NATO ally. [cite:aa_crs_2023] [cite:dv4_jvl_special_alliance]

From 1999 the aid was set in ten-year memoranda of understanding. The first, the "glide path" agreement, covered at least $26.7 billion over FY1999–2008 and phased out economic aid while raising military aid; the last bilateral economic grants were made in FY2008. The second, for FY2009–2018, pledged $30 billion. The third, signed on 14 September 2016, pledged $38 billion for FY2019–2028: $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing and $5 billion for missile defence. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] [cite:aa_crs_2023] [cite:israeled_mou_text]

By the Congressional Research Service's count, the total through FY2025 is about $175 billion in current dollars, of which $124.5 billion was military aid, $34.3 billion economic aid and $16.1 billion missile defence; adjusted for inflation, the total for 1946 to 2024 is about $298 billion. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] Figures quoted elsewhere, from $260 billion to over $300 billion, differ because they use different dollar years and end dates. [cite:aa_crs_2023] [cite:aa_usafacts]
