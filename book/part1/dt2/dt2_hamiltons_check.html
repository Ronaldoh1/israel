Hamilton's Check

The argument most often made that the Electoral College "failed" in 2016 rests on a single founding text. It is worth separating into two claims, because they carry very different weight. The first is a claim about design: that the electors were meant to exercise judgment, and that this function no longer operates. That claim can be checked against documents, and it holds. The second is a claim about outcomes: that the country has been worse off because the function did not operate in 2016. That is a judgment, contested along party lines, and this section sets out the measures each side uses rather than settling it.

What Hamilton promised

Alexander Hamilton defended the method of choosing a president in The Federalist No. 68, published in the New York Independent Journal on 12 March 1788. He wanted the choice made by "men most capable of analizing the qualities adapted to the station," meeting in their own states, so as to leave "as little opportunity as possible to tumult and disorder." [cite:dt2_fed68] The electors were to be a barrier. "Nothing was more to be desired," he wrote, "than that every practicable obstacle should be opposed to cabal, intrigue and corruption," and the most dangerous of these came "chiefly from the desire in foreign powers to gain an improper ascendant in our councils." He promised that the process would rarely fail to produce a qualified president, because "Talents for low intrigue and the little arts of popularity may alone suffice" to win a single state but not the confidence of the whole Union. [cite:dt2_fed68]

Two things about this defence matter for what follows. It describes electors as people who deliberate, not as messengers. And it is openly a check on popular choice. Hamilton's screen was meant to stand between the voters' preferences, or the legislatures' choices, and the office; its purpose was to allow a small body of electors to refuse someone the public might favour. Whatever its merits, it is not a democratic device, and its absence cannot be counted as a loss of democracy.

How quickly practice departed from it

The deliberating elector did not survive the first contested elections. When the Supreme Court reviewed the history in 2020, Justice Elena Kagan wrote for the Court that electors had declared their loyalty to party candidates since the election of 1796, and that by the early 1900s states were requiring them to pledge. The Twelfth Amendment, she wrote, was adopted partly to accommodate this practice: "By allowing the electors to vote separately for the two offices, the Twelfth Amendment made party-line voting safe." Over the whole of American history, about one-half of one per cent of electoral votes went to someone other than the winner of the elector's home state, and none of those votes came close to changing an outcome. [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo]

An elector who votes against the candidate he or she was pledged to support is called a faithless elector. Before 2020, whether states could stop or punish them was unsettled. The Constitution and federal law are silent on the point; some states required pledges, and some attached penalties. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] In 2020, 32 states and the District of Columbia required electors to pledge to their party's nominees, and 15 states imposed some sanction for breaking the pledge. [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo]

2016: the check invoked

In 2016 a group of electors tried to use the function Hamilton described. On 30 November that year, Democratic electors from Washington and Colorado, calling themselves the "Hamilton Electors" and citing Federalist No. 68, asked Republican electors in other states to vote for a Republican other than Donald Trump. They needed 37 to defect: enough to keep Trump below 270 and send the choice to the House. One of them, Levi Guerra, a 19-year-old Democratic elector from Washington, said it was "not an easy thing" for her to pledge her vote to a Republican alternative. The group said itself that the plan was unlikely to succeed. [cite:dt2_knkx_hamilton]

It did not. When the electors met in December, seven cast faithless votes, but five of the seven came from Hillary Clinton's own electors: one in Hawaii voted for Bernie Sanders, three in Washington voted for Colin Powell and a fourth for Faith Spotted Eagle. Two Texas electors pledged to Trump voted for John Kasich and Ron Paul. The final count was Trump 304, Clinton 227. [cite:dt2_cbs_faithless] Washington fined its three electors who voted for Powell $1,000 each, and the case of one of them, Bret Chiafalo, went to the Supreme Court. [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo]

Chiafalo v. Washington

On 6 July 2020 the Supreme Court decided Chiafalo v. Washington unanimously. Kagan wrote for eight justices; Justice Clarence Thomas, joined in part by Justice Neil Gorsuch, agreed with the result but would have grounded it in the Tenth Amendment rather than Article II. The holding was that a state may penalise an elector who breaks a pledge to vote for the winner of the state's popular vote. In a companion case from Colorado, the Court reversed a lower court that had sided with a faithless elector. The opinion's summary line was plain: "electors are not free agents; they are to vote for the candidate whom the State's voters have chosen." [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo]

This is the documented core of the argument about 2016. Federalist No. 68 describes a screen that depends on electors exercising judgment. Party pledges removed that judgment in practice within a decade of ratification, and Chiafalo confirmed that states may remove it in law. Where a state binds its electors, the screen does not exist; where a state leaves them free, it exists on paper. The Electoral College that chose the president in 2016 was therefore not the deliberating body Hamilton defended, and had not been for more than two centuries.

Worse off since 2016: the measures and both readings

Whether the outcome in 2016 was the kind Hamilton's screen was meant to prevent, and whether the country is worse off for it, are not questions documents can settle. Critics of the 2016 result, the Hamilton Electors among them, argued that the election presented exactly the case Federalist No. 68 anticipated, and that the check failed when needed. [cite:dt2_knkx_hamilton] The opposing reading holds that the voters in each state had chosen under rules known in advance, and that 538 electors overriding them would have been a far greater departure from democracy than anything they were correcting; the Supreme Court's later reasoning in Chiafalo, that electors are bound to "the candidate whom the State's voters have chosen," states the same principle as law. [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo]

The measures that each side cites cut both ways. The Economist Intelligence Unit moved the United States into its "flawed democracy" category for 2016, but said the score would have fallen below the threshold "even if there had been no presidential election." [cite:dt1_eiu2016] Freedom House has scored the country below 90 every year since 2016, but its score had already fallen from 94 in 2010. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] [cite:us3_fh_fiw2021] The V-Dem Institute's downgrade to "electoral democracy" came in 2025, during Trump's second term, which he won with a plurality of the national vote. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] [cite:dt2_bp_2024] And public verdicts follow party: the share of Republicans calling American democracy a good example for other countries rose from 17 per cent in 2024 to 38 per cent in 2026, while the Democratic share fell from 22 per cent to 12. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

For this book's question, the design finding matters more than the outcome dispute. Hamilton's screen was an elite check on popular choice, not a part of democracy that has been lost. Its absence shows that the presidency is now chosen by voters through a state-by-state arithmetic with no deliberative stage, which is what the next section measures. A system that wanted a safeguard against an unfit candidate while staying democratic would have to put it somewhere else: in how parties choose nominees, in how ballots count second choices, or in the checks on a president after election. Those are the subjects of [scene:dt3_primaries_and_the_two_party_trap|Part Three], [scene:dt6_how_it_counts|Part Six] and, for the texts' own answer to who removes a ruler, the companion volume's [scene:de35b_how_real_systems_choose|survey of how real systems choose].
