Basel, 1897: The Program and Its Opponents

Political Zionism as an organized movement has a precise birthplace and date. It was founded at the First Zionist Congress, which met in Basel, Switzerland, from 29 to 31 August 1897. Its convener and chairman was Theodor Herzl, a journalist born in Budapest and a subject of Austria-Hungary, who had published Der Judenstaat the year before. The Congress adopted a platform, the Basel Program, and founded the Zionist Organization with Herzl as its first president. Its founding leadership was overwhelmingly European, and so were its funding and its location.[cite:zionism_founding_1897] The familiar claim that the State of Israel grew from an ancient national presence with no meaningful break therefore needs a qualification at the start. The movement that set out to build a state there was new, and it was organized in Europe in 1897.

A second familiar claim, that Jews embraced Zionism from the start, does not survive the record of that same summer. The Congress was opposed before it opened, it was opposed by rabbis in Germany and Britain, and in the same season a rival Jewish movement was founded on the opposite premise. The contest inside Jewish life over Herzl's project is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that Zionism and Judaism were not the same thing, and that Jewish opponents, religious and secular, were the first to insist on the difference.

Herzl had planned to hold the Congress in Munich. It never met there. On 5 June 1897 a notice in the Münchner Neueste Nachrichten rejected holding his congress in the city, and in mid-June, in letters dated 13 June, the board of the Munich Jewish congregation wrote to Herzl rejecting it as well.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_basel] The venue moved to neutral Switzerland. In one account, the protest of local Orthodox and Reform rabbis helped push the Congress to Basel, and the German Rabbinical Association refused to support holding it in Munich.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] The documents of June 1897 name the congregation's board and the press notice as the first refusals.

The rabbis' formal protest followed. On 6 July 1897 the board of the Allgemeiner Rabbiner-Verband, the Union of Rabbis in Germany, published a resolution protesting Zionism, on the grounds of loyalty to the state and of religious messianism. Herzl gave its authors a name that stuck: the Protestrabbiner, the "protest rabbis".[cite:rs_rd1_grill_basel] The declaration appeared in English in the London Jewish Chronicle on 9 July 1897, on page 9. It was signed by the Union's executive committee: Maybaum of Berlin, Horovitz of Frankfurt, Guttmann of Breslau, Auerbach of Halberstadt and Werner of Munich. It called the Zionists' efforts toward a state "antagonistic to the messianic promises of Judaism", and it urged Jews to "hold aloof" from the movement and from its Congress.[cite:rs_rd1_posen_protest] The document is more precise than the label attached to it. The rabbis did not object to Jewish agricultural colonization of Palestine. What they rejected was a political state.[cite:rs_rd1_posen_protest] In Britain, the Orthodox Chief Rabbi Hermann Adler sided with the German rabbis and called Herzl's plan völlig unheilbringend, "completely disastrous".[cite:rs_rd1_grill_basel]

The Congress met in the Basel Municipal Casino. The figure most often given for its size is 208 delegates from 17 countries, among them 14 women who attended without a vote.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] Herzl presided in top hat and morning coat, and a photograph of him leaning over the balcony of the Drei Könige hotel became one of the movement's most familiar images.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] The size of the gathering is less certain than the famous number suggests. The Posen Library describes some two hundred delegates from seventeen countries.[cite:rs_rd1_posen_congress] The Jewish Virtual Library gives "some 200 participants" without naming the number of countries.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_a] The historian Tobias Grill writes that the delegates came from fourteen states.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_basel] The sources agree on roughly two hundred people; they differ on the exact count and on whether the delegates came from fourteen states or seventeen countries.

The program that the Congress adopted unanimously on 30 August 1897 was short. Its aim, in a standard English translation, read:[cite:rs_rd1_basel_wiki][cite:rs_rd1_basel_palquest]

Zionism seeks to establish a home in Palestine for the Jewish people, secured under public law.

Another published translation renders the same sentence as "the establishment of a publicly and legally secured home in Palestine for the Jewish people". Both versions turn on one word. The German original uses Heimstätte, a home or homestead, and not Staat, a state.[cite:rs_rd1_basel_palquest] The wording was revised during the Congress itself. According to one reference account, the draft first read rechtlich, "legally" secured, and was changed to öffentlich-rechtlich, "secured under public law"; that account does not give the reason for the change.[cite:rs_rd1_basel_wiki]

The program then named four means to the aim. The first was the promotion of settlement in Palestine by Jewish agriculturists, artisans and tradesmen. The second was the organization of all Jews through local and general institutions "according to the laws of the various countries". The third was the strengthening of Jewish national feeling and consciousness. The fourth was preparatory steps toward obtaining the consent of governments, which was needed to reach the aim.[cite:rs_rd1_basel_wiki][cite:rs_rd1_basel_palquest] Read against the twenty years that followed, these four clauses can be read as a plan of construction: each can be matched to an institution or a line of action, set out below.

The founders said plainly what kind of project this was. Der Judenstaat had framed it as secular nationalism, modeled on the nation-building of nineteenth-century Europe, and Herzl himself was religiously non-observant.[cite:herzl_judenstaat] The First Congress's program, preserved in the Zionist Organization's own archives, deals with political organization, the mechanisms of land purchase and diplomatic outreach, not religious doctrine. Religious authority was contested at the Congress and largely absent from its decisions.[cite:wiki_first_zionist_congress] None of this shows that no delegate had religious motives, or that Zionism never took on religious meaning for its adherents later. It shows what the founding texts say about the founding project, and the texts are still there to be read.

The Congress's most durable product was an organization. The Zionist Organization was built as a standing, congressional-style body of elected delegates with a governing executive, and from 1899 it had its own bank, the Jewish Colonial Trust.[cite:wiki_wzo] Herzl was its first president; Chaim Weizmann would lead it in the period of the Balfour Declaration. It ran the Congresses, managed land purchase through the Jewish National Fund from 1901, and conducted the great-power diplomacy that produced the Declaration in 1917. In 1929 it created the expanded Jewish Agency for Palestine, which became the proto-government of the Jewish community under the British Mandate, running immigration, settlement, defense and diplomacy. When the state was declared in 1948, the Agency's executive effectively became Israel's provisional government. The organization continues today, and the Jewish Agency still administers immigration and diaspora relations with quasi-governmental status, formalized by an Israeli law of 1952.[cite:wiki_wzo] The line from Basel to the state is an institutional one: a durable, public bureaucracy that outlived everyone who built it, and whose working is traced in [scene:zorg_s01|the organization's machinery]. Herzl understood that the members would have to be won. At the Second Congress in 1898 his watchword was to "conquer the communities", meaning the established congregations.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_basel][cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_a]

Herzl's own verdict on the Congress has become part of its legend. After Basel he wrote in his diary: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State. Perhaps in five years, certainly in fifty, everyone will see it."[cite:herzl_diaries] He was wrong about the five years and almost exactly right about the fifty: the state was declared in May 1948, fifty-one years later. He did not live to see any of it. He died in 1904 at the age of forty-four, exhausted by the diplomacy of offering his project to the Sultan, the Kaiser and the British. The prediction should not be read as more than it was. Herzl foresaw an outcome, not the path to it; nothing in the entry anticipates the Balfour Declaration, the Mandate or the war of 1948 through which statehood actually came.

Opposition to the project came from several directions at once, and the objections were different in kind. The most popular was secular and socialist. The General Jewish Labour Bund was founded in Vilna in October 1897, in the same season as the Basel Congress, and became Zionism's great rival for the loyalty of the Jews of Eastern Europe.[cite:wiki_bund] Against emigration to Palestine it set the idea of doikayt, "hereness": Jews should fight for their rights and build their culture where they already lived, as a national minority with autonomy inside democratic, multi-ethnic states, in solidarity with the non-Jewish working class. It was socialist, Yiddishist and committed to the diaspora. From the 1900s to the 1930s it was arguably the largest and most dynamic Jewish political movement in Poland and the Russian Empire, and in interwar Poland it regularly outpolled the Zionist parties in Jewish community elections. The Bund did not lose its argument with Zionism; it was destroyed. The Bolsheviks dissolved it in 1921, its leader Mark Liber was later executed in the Stalinist purges, its mass base in Polish and Soviet Jewry was murdered in the Holocaust or suppressed under Stalin, and the Polish Bund was suppressed a second time after the war.[cite:wiki_bund] For half a century the most popular Jewish political movement in the heartland of Zionist recruitment was explicitly anti-Zionist.

A second objection was theological. Orthodox anti-Zionist rabbis held that the return of the Jewish people to the land and the restoration of Zion were redemptive events reserved for divine action in the messianic age, not a political program to be forced by secular nationalists ahead of God's time. To many Orthodox authorities, a secular state built in the Holy Land by non-observant socialists was closer to sacrilege than to fulfillment.[cite:neturei_karta] This was a different argument from the Bund's: the Bund wanted Jewish autonomy without a state, while these rabbis rejected human political action as the means of return. The position has been held continuously since 1897, through Satmar Hasidism and, most visibly, the small Neturei Karta group, whose members still appear at Palestinian-solidarity demonstrations with signs distinguishing Judaism from Zionism. It is a minority position within Orthodox Judaism; most Haredi Jews today are not Neturei Karta and have made various accommodations with the state.[cite:neturei_karta] Reform Judaism's opposition and the wider range of Jewish positions in these years are traced in [scene:rd1_currents|Jewish currents for and against].

A third objection came from inside the movement. Ahad Ha'am, "one of the people", was the pen name of Asher Ginsberg (1856–1927), the founder of what came to be called cultural Zionism and political Zionism's sharpest internal critic.[cite:wiki_ahad_haam] He had visited Ottoman Palestine, and in his essay of 1891, "Truth from the Land of Israel", he reported what much of the movement preferred not to see: the land was not empty. It had a substantial Arab population, farming and living on it, who would not simply accept displacement, and he warned that treating them with contempt would poison the whole enterprise. This is Zionist testimony, from 1891, that Palestine was inhabited. Against Herzl he argued that a hurried political and diplomatic state would produce "a state of Jews" but not "a Jewish state": a polity Jewish in population but hollow in spirit, having skipped the slow cultural and ethical renewal he believed must come first. He wanted a spiritual center in the land that would radiate a renewed Hebrew culture to the diaspora, not a nation-state won through great-power maneuvering.[cite:wiki_ahad_haam] Israelis across the political spectrum still invoke him. What the record establishes is narrower and firmer: the warning was made at the founding moment, not in hindsight. It cannot show that his alternative would have produced a better outcome had it been adopted.

The fourth opponent was the movement's greatest existing patron. Baron Edmond de Rothschild (1845–1934), of the Paris branch of the family, had privately funded the struggling agricultural colonies of the First Aliyah in Ottoman Palestine since the 1880s, among them Rishon LeZion and Zichron Ya'akov, which was named for his father. His money, sums that would run to tens of millions in today's terms, effectively kept early Jewish settlement alive when it might otherwise have collapsed. Yet Edmond personally opposed Herzl and political Zionism. He distrusted Herzl's public, diplomatic, state-seeking approach and favored quiet, paternalistic philanthropic colonization that would not provoke the Ottoman authorities or make grand political claims, and for years he refused to place his settlements under the Zionist Organization. The two men held incompatible theories of the project, as Simon Schama documents in Two Rothschilds and the Land of Israel.[cite:schama_two_rothschilds] Edmond must be kept distinct from Walter Rothschild of the London branch, who twenty years later was the ceremonial addressee of the Balfour letter and who neither funded the settlements nor wrote the policy. The phrase "the Rothschilds created Israel" fuses two men with opposite roles and opposite views into one imaginary actor, the move by which a documented banking family is turned into an antisemitic hidden hand. The record shows members of one family reaching their own, divergent judgments, not a coordinated plan; his colonies and his first refusal of Herzl are told in [scene:bc_s04|the baron who built the colonies].[cite:schama_two_rothschilds]

Two questions about Herzl himself remain open. The first is what moved him. The usual account, repeated in many references, holds that his catalyst was the Dreyfus Affair: covering for a Vienna newspaper the 1894 degradation of a Jewish French officer, and watching a Paris crowd chant against Jews in the most emancipated society in Europe, he concluded that assimilation had failed as a strategy for Jewish safety and that a national home under public law was the only political answer.[cite:zionism_founding_1897] The historian Steven Beller, reviewing Derek Penslar's 2020 biography of Herzl, points to a difficulty: Dreyfus "is not mentioned by name in the Zionist diary of 1895", and Beller uses this to question whether the Affair was the catalyst at all.[cite:rs_rd1_beller_basel] The strongest form of the traditional case is that Herzl was in Paris as a correspondent during the Affair and that its lesson about European antisemitism fits his program exactly. The strongest form of the revision is that the diary in which he worked out his Zionism in 1895 is silent about the case. The record here does not settle which reading is right.

The second question concerns words Herzl never wrote. Fabricated quotations cluster around him, and the best known is a line claiming a Jewish state from the Nile to the Euphrates. It is not in his writings; the attribution has been traced to a misattribution of 1994 and debunked. The Herzl of the record was a secular nation-builder who weighed Argentina and entertained Britain's East Africa offer, not a maximalist with a scriptural map. Keeping the real Herzl distinct from the invented one is part of reading the founding of the movement accurately.

What the record of 1897 does not settle should also be stated. It does not give a single agreed count of the delegates or their countries. It does not explain why rechtlich became öffentlich-rechtlich in the program's aim, only that it did. It does not measure how many Jews in 1897 supported Herzl, opposed him or were indifferent; it records the institutions and named individuals who took positions, and on that evidence the movement began as one current among several in Jewish life, contested from its first summer. It does show that the program asked for a "home", not a "state", in its text, while its convener wrote in private that he had founded a state.
