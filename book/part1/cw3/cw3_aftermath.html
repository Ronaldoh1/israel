The Architecture in Spring 1957

On 9 March 1957, the day after the last Israeli troops left the Gaza Strip, President Eisenhower signed into law a joint resolution of Congress on the Middle East. [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The two events, a day apart, mark the end of the Suez–Sinai war and the shape of what replaced the order it had broken. Britain and France had gone to war in October 1956 over the Canal; by the spring their forces were gone, the British Prime Minister had fallen, and the United States had written into its own law an offer of money and, if necessary, armed force to the states of the region. Israel had gone to war, by its own account, to destroy the forces that threatened it and to open the Straits of Tiran; it had withdrawn from everything it took, with a United Nations force in its place and an American statement of policy on the Straits. Egypt had lost Sinai and Gaza for four months and kept the Canal. This section takes stock of that architecture: who now held power in the region, through what instruments, and who was left outside them.

Eden falls, and the alliance is repaired. Eden resigned in January 1957. The State Department's Office of the Historian links his fall to the public criticism the United States directed at its allies during the crisis. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The rupture did not last. By March 1957, under Harold Macmillan, relations between Washington and London had recovered. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] The speed of the repair is itself part of the record. The United States had used the General Assembly and its financial weight against Britain in November, as told earlier in this chapter; four months later the alliance was intact. On one reading its terms had changed: in the Middle East, the senior partner was now in Washington.

The Eisenhower Doctrine: the request. The instrument of that change was the Doctrine that bears Eisenhower's name. On 1 January 1957 the President met congressional leaders at the White House for four hours; on 5 January he asked Congress for authority to give military and economic aid to Middle Eastern states and to use force to protect them. [cite:rs_cw3_lawfare_waxman] The request came while Israel was still in Sinai and Gaza, and the debate in Congress ran in parallel with the withdrawal fight told in the previous section.

The House of Representatives passed the measure, House Joint Resolution 117, on 30 January 1957 by 355 votes to 61. The Senate passed it, as amended, on 5 March 1957 by 72 to 19. [cite:rs_cw3_voteview_h3] [cite:rs_cw3_voteview_s10] The margins were wide in both chambers, but the Senate took five weeks longer than the House and changed the text. One exchange that Waxman records shows the hesitation. Senator Richard Russell warned against any appearance that only "a small war" might follow; Eisenhower replied that "should Russia move it could not possibly be a small war." [cite:rs_cw3_lawfare_waxman]

What the law said. Public Law 85-7, 71 Statutes at Large 5, approved 9 March 1957, is short, and each of its six sections defined a piece of the new structure. [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7]

Section 1 authorized the President "to cooperate with and assist any nation or group of nations in the general area of the Middle East" in developing economic strength to maintain their independence. [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] [cite:rs_cw3_cornell_22usc1961]

Section 2 was the heart of it. The United States "is prepared to use armed forces" if the President deemed it necessary to assist any nation that requested help "against armed aggression from any country controlled by international communism," acting "consonant with the treaty obligations of the United States and with the Constitution." [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] The clause named one threat only. On a plain reading of its wording it did not reach a war between Israel and an Arab state, or between Arab states, unless one of them could be described as controlled by international communism.

Section 3 provided the money, and its terms are precise. The President could use up to $200,000,000 "from any appropriation now available" under the Mutual Security Act of 1954, for the rest of fiscal year 1957, and only fifteen days after reporting to the congressional committees. The resolution itself authorized no new appropriation. [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] [cite:rs_cw3_cornell_22usc1961] The Doctrine did not add to the foreign-aid budget; it gave the President authority to redirect money Congress had already voted, under congressional notice.

Section 4 declared support for the United Nations Emergency Force, the body now stationed between Egypt and Israel. Section 5 required the President to report to Congress each January and July. Section 6 let the resolution expire on a presidential finding or a concurrent resolution of Congress. [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] [cite:rs_cw3_cornell_22usc1961]

Where the money went. According to the historian Peter Hahn, the United States under the Doctrine "immediately dispensed tens of millions of dollars in aid" to Turkey, Iran, Pakistan, Iraq, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon and Libya. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Israel is not on his list; the Doctrine's first beneficiaries were other states of the region, several of them Arab. How the money was divided among the recipients is not established in the sources used for this book; the allocations of the Richards mission remain an open question. The force clause was tested the next year. In 1958 Eisenhower sent about 14,000 troops to Lebanon, but, as Matthew Waxman notes, without citing the resolution. [cite:rs_cw3_lawfare_waxman] That episode belongs to Stage Four.

The dispute: communism or succession? What the Doctrine was for is read in two ways. By its text, it was about communism: the force clause applies only to aggression "from any country controlled by international communism." [cite:rs_cw3_pl85_7] The Office of the Historian frames it differently, as a response to American concern about European power in the region after Suez, one that "gave the administration increased power to aid countries in the region"; and Hahn records the aid flowing at once to regional allies. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] On this second reading, the Doctrine was the means by which the United States took up the role in the Middle East that Britain and France had lost at Suez, with anti-communism as its legal basis rather than its whole purpose. On this book's reading the two are compatible: the law's stated enemy was Moscow, and its practical effect was to put Washington where London and Paris had stood.

Nasser. Whether Egypt's army was beaten in Sinai or withdrew to defend the Canal is disputed, as the account of the war itself shows; politically, Nasser emerged stronger. In Hahn's words, Nasser gained prestige among Arab peoples as "a leader who had defied European empires and survived a military invasion by Israel." [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] He kept the Canal, whose nationalization had begun the crisis. He now hosted on Egyptian soil a United Nations force, which remained until he ordered it out in May 1967; his later wars are told in [scene:egi_src_s03|Egypt's wars, 1967–73].

The war's cost also fell on Egypt's Jews. The Jewish Agency's history records that many Egyptian Jews emigrated to Israel as "an indirect outcome of the war." [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen] That phrase leaves out what the documents show of arrests, expulsions and the proclamation of 23 November 1956, told in full in [scene:cw3_egypt_jews|the Jews of Egypt].

Israel's gains. For Israel the balance sheet was mixed, and both columns are documented. On the gains side, Ben-Gurion told the Knesset on 5 March 1957 that free passage through the Straits of Tiran had been secured, "vital to Israel, and is also of great importance to other countries," and that UN forces stood at Sharm el-Sheikh and in Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] The Israel Defense Forces describe the campaign as "an impressive military success" because it halted the attacks from the Egyptian border. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] UNEF remained on Egyptian soil as "a buffer against Egyptian-Israeli hostilities" until May 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn]

The French alliance outlasted the war. The reactor agreement Peres finalized at Sèvres, told with the conference itself, bore fruit in September 1957, when France delivered a reactor of twice the capacity first promised. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] That story belongs to Stage Four. So does a second consequence: between 1956 and 1958, meetings in Europe, Ankara and Tehran produced the "Trident" intelligence alliance of Israel, Iran and Turkey, driven in part by the Sinai campaign and concern over Nasser's Egypt, and told in [scene:ira_s00c|the periphery alliance]. [cite:foreignaffairs_trident_legacy]

Israel's costs. On the other side of the ledger, Israel had withdrawn from all it had taken without a peace treaty or a recognized border change; the assurances it relied on were an American statement of policy and its own stated assumptions in the General Assembly, and whether these amounted to guarantees is disputed, as the previous section showed. It had been condemned by an Assembly in which only Britain, France, Australia and New Zealand had voted with it on the cease-fire, and it had been publicly pressed by an American President. [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_997] [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57] And it had been seen to fight alongside Britain and France, the two powers whose regional role, in Hahn's account, the war ended. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Inside Israel, the war's first day had also been the day of the killings at Kafr Qasim, whose trial opened in March 1957. On 29 October 1957, the first anniversary of both, a grenade thrown into the Knesset chamber wounded Ben-Gurion and several ministers. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_knesset3]

The dispute: who won? The question has two well-argued answers. The first is that Israel won, militarily and strategically. Michael Oren, offering an interpretation, argues that Israel emerged "economically, diplomatically, and militarily stronger," and that the war partly fulfilled goals the 1949 armistices had left open: Tiran opened, the fedayeen stopped, the French alliance and its reactor secured. [cite:rs_cw3_oren_hnn] The IDF's own verdict, as above, rests on the end of the raids from the Egyptian border. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai]

The second answer is that Nasser won, politically. He kept the Canal and gained standing across the Arab world; Britain and France lost their regional role; and Israel was tied to the colonial powers and forced out without a treaty. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Oren himself reports this case from his opponents: Avi Shlaim and other New Historians argued that Israel paid a heavy political price for aligning with colonial powers, and Fromkin called the victory Pyrrhic. [cite:rs_cw3_oren_hnn] Those last two judgements reach this book through Oren's summary of them, not from their own texts.

The two answers are measuring different things. Oren and the IDF count what changed on Israel's borders and in its alliances in the decade that followed; Hahn, Shlaim and Fromkin count what changed in the region's politics and in Israel's standing within them. On the documents used here, both sets of changes happened. On this book's reading, Britain and France were the clear losers in either accounting, and the United States, which had fought no battle, emerged with the largest new role.

Ben-Gurion's own verdict. The man who had led Israel into the war gave the army a sober one. Addressing the army's senior command on 27 December 1956, in the debriefing in which he called the campaign "a war deluxe," a phrase discussed in the account of the war itself, he warned that the next war "could be a prolonged one" and that Israel might then be "the defenders" rather than the attackers. [cite:rs_cw3_jpost_dec56] There was, he said, "no basis to assume that in the next war, we will have helping forces from abroad." [cite:rs_cw3_idf_archive] [cite:rs_cw3_jpost_dec56]

What the record leaves open. How the Doctrine's money was divided among the states Hahn names, and what the Richards mission allocated to each, is not established in the sources used for this book. Whether the Doctrine was chiefly about communism or chiefly about succeeding Britain and France is a matter of interpretation; its text supports the first, its context and its first payments the second. And who won the war depends, as shown, on what is being counted. What the documents used here do show is the structure that stood in spring 1957: British and French forces out of Egypt and Eden out of office; the United States committed by statute, with up to $200 million of existing funds and an offer of force against communist aggression; Egypt in possession of the Canal and host to a United Nations force it could dismiss; and Israel back behind the armistice lines, with Tiran open on the strength of an American statement of policy, a French reactor on the way, and no treaty.
