Who Lived at Qumran?

The ruins of Qumran sit on a terrace a short walk from the caves. The settlement was built between 134 and 104 before Christ, or slightly later, under the Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus, and was occupied most of the time until 68 after Christ, when the Romans destroyed it during the Jewish revolt, possibly as late as 73. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran] The French Dominican archaeologist Roland de Vaux excavated it in the 1950s. He identified a room where inkwells were found as a "scriptorium", a hall with stacks of bowls nearby as a "refectory", and many stepped cisterns as Jewish ritual baths. He concluded that the site was home to an Essene community and that the manuscripts "were copied in the scriptorium of Qumran". [cite:ds_wiki_qumran]

The Essenes were a Jewish movement described by three ancient writers. The historian Josephus wrote that they "take great pains in studying the writings of the ancients", and de Vaux compared their practices with what he found. [cite:ds_ng_whowrote] The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History of 77, placed a community of Essenes by the Dead Sea, and the scrolls' emphasis on isolation, celibacy, determinism and a rigorous initiation fitted what the ancient writers said. [cite:ds_enc_dss] [cite:ds_wiki_qumran]

The fit is not perfect. Many observant Jews, not only Essenes, used ritual baths. The philosopher Philo wrote that the Essenes lived "in many cities of Judea and in many villages", not in one desert settlement, and Josephus mentions married Essenes, while the scrolls' references to the "sons of Zadok" have led some scholars to link the community with the priestly Sadducees. [cite:ds_ng_whowrote] [cite:ds_enc_dss] Other readings of the ruins have been proposed. Norman Golb argued that Qumran was a fortress, that "there were no sectarians at the site at all", and that the scrolls came from libraries in Jerusalem, hidden by Jews fleeing the Roman army. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran] [cite:ds_ng_whowrote] The Israeli archaeologists Yitzhak Magen and Yuval Peleg accepted that it began as a fort but argued it became a pottery works; Yizhar Hirschfeld proposed a fortified trading station. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran]

Science has narrowed the question without closing it. Since about 2000 there has been broad agreement that much of the pottery at Qumran was brought from elsewhere; one analysis suggests that as much as two-thirds was not made from local clay, and the Hebrew University's Jan Gunneweg found that only about half of the jars that held scrolls were local. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran] [cite:ds_ng_whowrote] A 2021 analysis of handwriting at the University of Groningen suggested that more than one scribe worked on the Great Isaiah Scroll. [cite:ds_ng_whowrote] Most scholars still favour some form of the Essene hypothesis, though "Essene" may cover several related groups, and the interpretation of the site remains debated, with no single model generally accepted. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran] National Geographic sums up the state of play: the debates continue, but no one disputes that the scrolls give "a rare glimpse into the first-century Jewish world, whoever their authors were." [cite:ds_ng_whowrote]

The record also shows how a first interpretation can set the frame for decades. De Vaux's names for the rooms, scriptorium and refectory, are words from the life of a monastery, and de Vaux was a Dominican friar; the names went into the literature as descriptions rather than as interpretations. [cite:ds_wiki_qumran] [cite:ds_wiki_deception] The Essene identification may well be right in some form. But it was a hypothesis before it was a consensus, and the evidence that has come in since, from the pottery and the handwriting, has made the picture more varied, not less.
