Did Money Buy the Politicians? What the Record Can Show, and Where It Goes Dark

The question of what money did to American policy on Palestine before and during 1948 is usually answered in one of two ways. In one version, Zionist money bought Congress and the White House. In the other, money played no role at all. Both answers share a flaw: they treat a bundle of separate claims as a single claim, and so they deliver one verdict where the evidence supports several different ones. Pulled apart, the question becomes four claims. They stand on very different footing, and one of them cannot be settled in either direction with the records that exist.

The first claim is that there was organized political pressure. This is documented in detail, much of it in the movement's own files. The American Zionist Emergency Council, the movement's national lobbying body in the 1940s, built local committees across the country and ran letter and telegram campaigns aimed at members of Congress. It compiled legislators' statements of support into a book, America and Palestine, and used it to press for more. The same files document the campaign around both parties' 1944 platforms and the Wagner–Taft resolution in Congress. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944] Nothing about this was hidden. It was lobbying, organized openly and on a national scale, and the way it worked is traced tie by tie in [scene:nw_s02|the network of the American build-up].

The second claim is that money changed a specific decision. This is documented once. The historian John Judis records that in mid-1948 Truman wanted Israel to stop taking territory beyond the United Nations partition lines and was disturbed by its refugee policy, but backed away from pressing either objection, in part because his nearly broke campaign needed $100,000 from Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann for a whistle-stop tour. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] That account, and the man behind it, are set out in [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg]. It is one historian's account of one decision. It is specific, dated and sourced, and it is also the only instance of its kind in the record.

The panel above makes the point by contrast. Among the dollar flows the record documents between 1921 and 1949, the Feinberg–Kaufmann money is the smallest, about a tenth of a million dollars beside a $100 million Export-Import Bank loan of 1949 and tens of millions raised for arms. [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:israelaffairs_italy] Yet it is the only sum tied in the historical literature to a particular policy decision. Most of the other money built institutions over decades; this sum arrived at the moment a decision was being made. The size of a payment and its documented effect are different measures, and here they point in opposite directions.

The third claim is that members of Congress were paid, systematically, before 1948. This cannot be established either way, and the reason is legal rather than historical. Until the Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971, the governing law was the Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1925. It required disclosure mainly from party committees operating in more than one state rather than from candidates. It carried few penalties. It left enforcement to Congress itself, and it was rarely enforced. It was riddled with loopholes, and the donors to individual candidates were largely undisclosed until 1971. [cite:wiki_fcpa_1925] [cite:sage_fec_history] [cite:crs_campaign_finance] The records that would answer the question were never required to exist. In the absence of disclosure, any claim that particular legislators were paid is unsupported. So is any claim that they were not. Silence of this kind is not evidence of innocence and not evidence of guilt; it is a gap produced by the law of the time, and it should be reported as a gap.

The fourth claim is the one most often left out by those who argue that money explains the outcome: other documented forces pushed in the same direction without money. The moral urgency of the Holocaust years was real and widely felt. Jewish voters were concentrated in electorally important states such as New York. Christian restorationist conviction, the belief that the Jews would be restored to their ancient land, was sincere and had a history going back to 1839. Henry Wallace held Zionist views of his own. And the 1922 Lodge–Fish resolution, by which Congress endorsed the Balfour Declaration, is credited to lobbying with no payment documented. Each of these points the same way as the money, and none of them needs it.

The opposing record belongs in the same account. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense opposed the policy in writing, and they were overruled. The documented forces that beat them were political: organization, votes, conviction and access. A financial force is documented only in the single case above.

Set out this way, the two familiar answers both fail. "Money bought Congress and the White House" stretches one documented episode, involving one decision and one campaign, into a general claim about legislators for whom no payment record exists. "Money played no role at all" contradicts the one case that is documented. The defensible position sits between them and is narrower than either: an organized, well-funded political movement with direct access to decision-makers, plus one documented case in which money shifted a decision.

The note above, from the dashboard that follows the money of these years, states the same limit in its own terms. The record documents private, organized finance and one specific episode of money bearing on policy in 1948. It does not show that any government secretly funded the movement, and it does not show that money decided every outcome. The money itself, how much there was and where it went, is the subject of [scene:gf_s01|the next section].

What remains open is exactly what the law of the time left open. Whether any member of Congress received money in connection with a vote on Palestine before 1948 cannot be known from the records that exist, and this book does not supply an answer the documents cannot. Nor does the Judis account, by itself, measure how much weight the campaign's need carried against the other pressures on Truman in the summer of 1948; it records the need as one reason among others, and that is how it is reported here.
