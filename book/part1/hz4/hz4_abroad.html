Attacks Abroad: Buenos Aires and Burgas

Hezbollah's war with Israel in these years was fought mostly in south Lebanon. Twice in the 1990s, and again in 2012, bombs went off far from it, against Israeli and Jewish targets in Argentina and against Israeli tourists in Bulgaria. Courts in both countries attributed the attacks to Hezbollah, and in Argentina to Iran as the state that ordered them. Hezbollah and Iran deny all three. This section sets out what the courts found, how they reached it and what remains unresolved.

The Israeli embassy, 17 March 1992. A van loaded with explosives was driven into Israel's embassy in Buenos Aires. The usual count is 29 dead and more than 200 injured; a 1999 act of Argentina's Supreme Court set its own count of the dead at 22. [cite:hz4_infobae_1992] Two days later a statement signed "Islamic Jihad Organization" was sent to the Beirut daily An-Nahar. [cite:hz4_infobae_1992] The claim said the bombing avenged Hussein, the five-year-old son of Abbas al-Musawi, killed with his father by Israeli helicopters a month earlier. [cite:hz4_wiki_musawi] [scene:hz4_zone|Musawi and Nasrallah] On 10 May 1999 Argentina's Supreme Court found that Islamic Jihad organised and carried out the attack; later rulings in the case named Imad Mughniyeh, a senior Hezbollah security commander, as responsible and an international warrant was issued for him. [cite:hz4_infobae_1992] Infobae's account treats "Islamic Jihad" as a cover name for Hezbollah; Hezbollah has never accepted responsibility.

AMIA, 18 July 1994. A car bomb destroyed the building of the Argentine Israelite Mutual Association (AMIA), the main Jewish community centre in Buenos Aires, killing 85 people and injuring about 300. It remains the deadliest attack in Argentina's history. [cite:hz4_bat_cassation] The investigation became a scandal of its own. The first investigating judge, Juan José Galeano, was later convicted for paying a suspect to give false testimony against provincial police officers, and in 2024 his sentence was cut from six years to four on appeal. Former president Carlos Menem, accused of covering up a "Syrian connection," was acquitted. [cite:hz4_bat_cassation] Thirty-two years on, no one has been arrested for the bombing itself. [cite:hz4_bat_cassation]

In October 2006 Argentine prosecutors formally accused Iran of ordering the attack and Hezbollah of carrying it out, and asked for the arrest of former president Akbar Hashemi Rafsanjani, six other senior Iranian officials and Imad Mughniyeh. Iran's foreign ministry called the charges "Zionist propaganda" and cited Galeano's corruption as proof they were baseless. [cite:hz4_aje_2006] Hezbollah rejected the claims too; when prosecutors named a Lebanese man as the suicide bomber, it said he had been killed fighting Israeli forces in south Lebanon. [cite:hz4_jpost_hzdeny] The lead prosecutor, Alberto Nisman, was found dead on 18 January 2015, hours before he was due to present evidence to Congress that President Cristina Fernández de Kirchner had covered up Iran's role through a deal with Tehran. In December 2017 a federal judge ruled that his death "could not have been a suicide." Kirchner called the accusation against her an "absurdity." [cite:hz4_toi_nisman] Who killed Nisman has not been established in court.

The 2024 cassation ruling. On 11 April 2024, ruling on appeals in the cover-up trial, Argentina's Federal Criminal Cassation Court (judges Carlos Mahiques, Ángela Ledesma and Diego Barroetaveña) found in a 711-page decision that Iran ordered both the 1992 embassy bombing and the 1994 AMIA bombing, and that Hezbollah carried them out under "the inspiration, organisation, planning and financing of state and parastatal bodies subordinate to the Ayatollahs' government." [cite:hz4_bat_cassation] It declared both attacks crimes against humanity, which places them beyond any statute of limitations, and called Iran "a terrorist state." [cite:hz4_bat_cassation] [cite:hz4_almon_cassation] It found the motive in Argentina's 1991–92 cancellation of three nuclear technology contracts with Iran. [cite:hz4_bat_cassation] Judge Mahiques said the attack was carried out "under the mandate of a government, of a State." [cite:hz4_almon_cassation] Iran has denied involvement throughout and refuses to hand over the accused. [cite:hz4_bat_absentia]

Status as of October 2026. Under a law that took effect in March 2025 allowing trials of defendants who evade justice, Federal Judge Daniel Rafecas ordered in June 2025 that ten Iranian and Lebanese suspects be tried in absentia, among them former intelligence minister Ali Fallahian, former foreign minister Ali Akbar Velayati, former Revolutionary Guard commander Mohsen Rezai, former Quds Force commander Ahmad Vahidi and three named Hezbollah members. [cite:hz4_bat_absentia] On 24 September 2026 the Cassation Court rejected a constitutional challenge to the in-absentia law, clearing the way for an oral trial; no trial date had been reported. [cite:hz4_dc_absentia] The court findings are the strongest attribution in this book's record for any attack abroad: a judgment of the country's highest criminal appeals court after three decades of investigation. They are also findings against defendants who have never appeared, in an investigation once corrupted by its own judge, and the denials of Iran and Hezbollah stand beside them.

Burgas, 18 July 2012. A bomb exploded on a bus carrying Israeli tourists at the airport in Burgas, on Bulgaria's Black Sea coast, killing five Israelis and the Bulgarian driver. The man carrying it, Mohamad Hassan El Husseini, a dual Lebanese-French citizen, also died. [cite:hz4_aje_burgas] Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu blamed Hezbollah and Iran within hours; Iran called the accusation "baseless." [cite:hz4_wiki_burgas] On 5 February 2013 Bulgaria's interior minister, Tsvetan Tsvetanov, said investigators had followed the suspects' financing and contacts in Australia and Canada and that "a reasonable assumption can be made that the two of them were members of the militant wing of Hezbollah." [cite:hz4_lwj_burgas13] After a change of government, the new foreign minister said in June 2013 that there was "no conclusive evidence"; in July the new interior minister said there were "clear signs" of Hezbollah's responsibility. [cite:hz4_wiki_burgas] In September 2020 Bulgaria's Specialised Criminal Court, trying them in absentia, sentenced Meliad Farah, an Australian of Lebanese origin, and Hassan El Hajj Hassan, a Canadian of Lebanese origin, to life without parole for giving the bomber logistical support. Hezbollah denies involvement. [cite:hz4_aje_burgas]

The EU listing. On 22 July 2013 EU foreign ministers agreed unanimously to put Hezbollah's "military wing" on the EU terrorism list, citing Burgas, a conviction of a Hezbollah member in Cyprus and its fighting in Syria. The EU's foreign policy chief, Catherine Ashton, said the step did not prevent "dialogue with all political parties in Lebanon." [cite:hz4_lwj_eu13] The split between wings was the EU's construction, not Hezbollah's: its deputy leader, Naim Qassem, had said in 2012, "We don't have a military wing and a political one." [cite:hz4_lwj_eu13] Israeli ministers welcomed the decision; one, Avigdor Lieberman, said the EU had gone only halfway. [cite:hz4_lwj_eu13]

These cases matter to this book's thesis in a specific way. When Israel or the United States names Hezbollah as a threat beyond Lebanon, part of the record behind the claim is court findings, not intelligence assessments alone: an Argentine appellate judgment and a Bulgarian conviction, each with its weaknesses stated above. The thesis that Hezbollah serves as a justification for war has to be weighed against that record, as Part Eleven does. [scene:hz11_pattern|The pattern tested]
