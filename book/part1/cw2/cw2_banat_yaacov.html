Banat Yaacov, 1953: The First Time Washington Held Back Aid

In the autumn of 1953, in the same weeks as Qibya, the United States withheld economic aid from Israel until Israel complied with a request from the United Nations. The dispute was not about a raid but about a canal, dug in a strip of land on the Syrian front whose ownership the 1949 armistice had left unresolved. It lasted eight weeks. It ended with the works suspended, the money released and the underlying quarrel handed to an American envoy. As a case study in how leverage worked in this period, it is unusually complete: the condition, the pressure and the compliance are all on the documentary record.

The zones and the quarrel over them. The armistice with Syria, signed in July 1949, left small demilitarized zones along the frontier whose status was disputed: strips without agreed sovereignty. Volume II tells how those zones were created, and Stage One tells the first Security Council crisis over them, the Huleh drainage of 1951, in [scene:cw1_huleh|the Huleh dispute]. By 1953 Israel's position was explicit. In a meeting recorded by the State Department in 1953–54, Moshe Dayan told American officials that Israel claimed sovereignty over Jewish-owned lands in the zones, that "someone must keep the area alive," and that Israel did not want Syria to have direct access to the Jordan River. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz]

The conduct on both sides of that line is on the record, and neither side's account alone describes it. Syria fired on Israeli farmers and fishermen from the heights above the zones. Israel sent armoured tractors to cultivate disputed land, and carried out reprisal raids. A common account holds that the clashes on this front before 1967 were caused entirely by Syrian shelling from the Golan. The record shows a running contest in which shelling and cultivation drove each other. Dayan himself, in an interview given in 1976 and published in 1997, said that at least 80 percent of the clashes had been provoked by Israel; that statement, and the dispute over it, are taken up in [scene:rd6_syria_1949|Syria in 1949]. [cite:w_dayan] The later struggle over the Jordan's headwaters, from 1964 to 1967, belongs to Stage Six.

The works, September 1953. On 2 September 1953 Israel began works at Banat Yaacov, inside the Israel–Syria demilitarized zone. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100] Israel's case was that this was a development project. Abba Eban later told Secretary of State John Foster Dulles that it was "an electric power project," that it had been begun with the knowledge of Colonel Tillotson of the UN Truce Supervision Organization, and that no water would actually be diverted until sometime in 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53]

The officer charged with supervising the armistice asked Israel to stop. On 23 September 1953 General Vagn Bennike, the UNTSO Chief of Staff, asked Israel to "cease its operations in the demilitarized zone" until an agreement was reached. Sharett, Israel's foreign minister, replied on 24 September with reservations. Syria complained to the United Nations on 12 October. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53]

The lever. Israel had not stopped, and Washington now attached a price to that. In a conversation of about 25 September 1953 with Eban and Shiloah, Dulles said he would "withhold release of MSA funds until the issue of the Jordan waters had been further clarified." MSA funds were economic aid under the Mutual Security programme. Dulles was acting on the advice of Assistant Secretary Henry Byroade. Eban asked him to keep American aid and the water dispute separate. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53] Dulles did not.

The suspension became public in October. At his press conference of 20 October 1953, two days after the State Department's statement on Qibya, Dulles said no allocation had yet gone to Israel, because "as long as the State of Israel was acting in defiance of that decision" it was questionable whether to make one. He was careful to separate the two crises: the deferral, he said, followed Bennike's decision, not "the more recent incidents." Asked how much was involved, he put the total "in the neighborhood of $100,000,000," after an aide had suggested $50–60 million. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_presser_20oct53] A week later, on 27 October, he added that no new economic-assistance allocations had been made from the fiscal-year fund "either to Israel or any Arab state," and declined to discuss the deferment while the Security Council was seized of the matter. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_presser_27oct53] Qibya and the deferment were thus running side by side in Washington, and Dulles insisted they were separate; the raid is told in the preceding section.

The Council and the suspension. The matter was resolved at the United Nations, not in Washington. On 27 October 1953, at its 631st meeting and on a French draft, the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 100. It deemed it desirable that the works begun on 2 September be suspended while the Council examined the question. It noted Eban's undertaking to suspend them "without prejudice" to the merits of the dispute, and it relied on the Chief of Staff to confirm compliance. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100] The phrase "without prejudice" was what made the undertaking possible: Israel stopped digging without conceding that it had been wrong to dig. Bennike later reported that the works had stopped at midnight on 28 October. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100]

The money followed at once. In Press Release No. 604, issued the day after the resolution, Dulles announced that "the impediment to the present grant of economic aid to Israel has been removed." He recommended about $26 million for the first six months of the fiscal year, the amount tentatively earmarked for Israel under the Mutual Security Act of 1953. He also gave the reason for the suspension in a single sentence: granting aid while Israel ignored the UN request "would tend to undermine the authority" of UNTSO. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_pr604] The Council's consideration of the wider Banat Yaacov question continued for three months more, from its 629th to its 656th meeting, between 27 October 1953 and 22 January 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100]

The mechanism. Set out in order, the episode shows how the structure of 1953 actually worked. The armistice gave the UN Chief of Staff authority to rule on what could be done in the zones, but no power to enforce his ruling; Bennike could ask, and Israel could reply with reservations. Syria could complain to the Council and did, but had no lever of its own. What turned a UN officer's request into a decision Israel had to meet was an American funding instrument. Dulles did not claim to be judging the water dispute; he said he was upholding UNTSO's authority, and he made the release of money conditional on Israel's compliance with it. The Council then supplied a face-saving form, suspension "without prejudice," and the money moved within a day of the works stopping. The cost fell on Israel, whose grant aid was held for about five weeks; the gain, by Dulles's own account, was to the authority of the armistice machinery.

How much was at stake. The amounts given in the record differ, and the differences are not contradictions so much as different measures. About $26 million was the sum Dulles released in Press Release 604, the tranche earmarked for the first half of the fiscal year. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_pr604] The "neighborhood of $100,000,000" that Dulles recalled at his 20 October press conference, and the $50–60 million his aide suggested, referred to the year's programme as a whole, and neither was offered as a precise statement. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_presser_20oct53] The figure that can be stated with confidence as the money actually held and then released is the $26 million.

The episode of 1953 is an earlier documented case of the same pattern, and a clearer one than most: the condition was stated in private on about 25 September and made public on 20 October; Israel gave its undertaking at the Council on 27 October, the release was announced the day after the resolution, and the works stopped at midnight on 28 October. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_dulles_sep53] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_presser_20oct53] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_res100] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_pr604] It does not show that every condition would be met, or that the lever worked on the larger question of who controlled the river. It shows that, on the narrow question of whether Israel would dig in the zone against a UN request while American money was pending, it did.

The Syrian front is one link in a longer chain, which the library's map of the Golan line traces from 1949 to the present.

What came next. The suspension settled nothing about the water itself. On 16 October 1953, while the aid was still being withheld, President Eisenhower named Eric Johnston as his personal representative to discuss the regional development of the Jordan's waters. [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_johnston] That effort, Washington's diplomatic answer to Banat Yaacov, is told in [scene:cw2_johnston|the Johnston mission].

What the record here does not settle should be stated. The sources fetched do not give the outcome of the Council's three months of debate after Resolution 100, or say whether and when Israel resumed any works at Banat Yaacov. They do not give Syria's own statements in its 12 October complaint, so Syria's case is known here mainly through what the UN officer did with it. Dayan's remark to American officials is dated only to 1953–54. And while Dulles's stated reason was the authority of UNTSO, the documents do not show how far other considerations, including the Qibya raid he insisted was separate, weighed in his mind when the money was held.
