Britain, France and the Wider World

Outside the region, most countries teach this history briefly or not at all, and the fights are less over whole curricula than over a single word, a single guidance letter or a single chapter. The cases below are the ones on record, and their pattern is consistent: the argument is settled by whoever controls the gate, whether that is a ministry, an exam board or a publisher.

Britain: the duty of balance

In England, pupils can study the conflict as one option in GCSE history: Pearson's Edexcel board listed "Conflict in the Middle East (1945–95)" among the new topics in its 2015 specification for the reformed qualification. [cite:nat_pearson_gcse2015] Teachers are bound by a legal duty of political impartiality, and in February 2022 the Department for Education issued guidance on meeting it that named the Israeli-Palestinian conflict as an example. The education secretary, Nadhim Zahawi, said "no subject is off-limits in the classroom" if handled with sensitivity. Balance, the guidance said, does not require equal time for every view. [cite:nat_dfe_impartiality2022] It told schools to avoid materials on the conflict that did not offer a balanced view and not to encourage pupils to adopt "a one-sided view expressed with a political purpose." The Board of Deputies of British Jews welcomed it. The campaign group Cage was meanwhile challenging an earlier ministerial directive that schools avoid organizations rejecting Israel's right to exist. [cite:nat_jc_impartiality2022]

France: two fights over one publisher

French schools follow a national programme, but private publishers write the textbooks. Two disputes over the same house show the pressure from organized groups and from the state. In the first, Hachette Éducation issued a history textbook for the première, the second-to-last year of upper secondary school, with a chapter on the United Nations and the Palestinian question in 1947–48 that used the word Nakba and defined it as the expulsion of the Palestinians. The Representative Council of Jewish Institutions of France (CRIF) and an anti-hate monitoring group objected; CRIF's president called the presentation scandalous and ideological. Hachette said some "contestable passages" would be changed when the book was printed. [cite:nat_livres_hachette] A French writer argued that the original wording followed the work of Israeli historians and that a publisher, not the state, had yielded to pressure. [cite:nat_reporterre_nakba] The sources give the year of this first dispute inconsistently.

In January 2026 the second dispute ran the other way in kind but not in outcome. A Hachette exam-revision guide described the people killed on 7 October 2023 as "more than 1,200 Jewish settlers" and did not call Hamas a terrorist organization. President Emmanuel Macron called the guide "intolerable" and said it falsified the facts; Hachette recalled three titles at once, apologized and opened an internal inquiry. [cite:nat_brut_hachette2026] The second case was a plain factual error, which the first was not shown to be. In both, the publisher changed course after public pressure.

Pakistan, South Africa and Latin America

In some places the topic has been removed from the syllabus altogether. In March 2023 Cambridge Assessment International Education, whose British exams are taken by many private-school pupils in Pakistan, removed the chapter "Israelis and Palestinians since 1945" and a chapter on the Gulf from its history syllabus, effective 2024. Pakistan's national curriculum council said Cambridge schools would need to follow national syllabuses; a teacher quoted by the Pakistani press saw the removal as serving Israel. Cambridge gave no public reason. [cite:nat_news_caie2023]

South Africa, whose government took Israel to the International Court of Justice in 2024 (see the survey that opens this part), may soon teach it even less. Its draft new history curriculum, published for comment in March 2026, devotes the senior years to Africa's history, and the South African Institute of Race Relations complained that global events of the twentieth century had been left out. [cite:nat_irr_2026] In Latin America, home to the largest Palestinian diaspora outside the Middle East, the record located for this book documents commitments on Holocaust education, such as Argentina's announcement in 2006 that it would become a required part of the curriculum, [cite:nat_jta_argentina2006] but no study of how the region's schools teach 1948 or the occupation. The same is true of East Asia. Those are gaps in the research available, not findings that nothing is taught.
