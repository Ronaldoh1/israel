The American state has turned its surveillance on its own lawful political life before, under presidents of both parties: the NSA copied international telegrams for three decades and kept a watch list of some 1,650 Americans that included two senators of opposite parties, the FBI's COINTELPRO ran from 1956 to April 1971, and the CIA's Operation CHAOS indexed about 300,000 names before a presidential commission found that it "unlawfully exceeded" the agency's authority. [cite:de29_schneier_shamrock] [cite:de29_nbc_minaret] [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro] [cite:de29_rock_chaos] Most of these programmes were ended or brought to account through exposure from outside, a burglary, the press or a congressional inquiry, and the record of the abuse is the government's own. [cite:de29_csm_media] Of the programmes exposed after 2013, bulk phone-record collection was ruled unlawful and ended, and section 215 expired in 2020; Section 702 collection continues under certifications to March 2027 although its statute lapsed in June 2026; collection under the president's own order and the purchase of commercial data, which the FBI confirmed in March 2026, grew. [cite:de29_eff_215] [cite:us10_bcj_702] [cite:de29_ap_ciabulk] [cite:de29_tc_patel] Those who disclosed the programmes were imprisoned or exiled, while the official who told the Senate "Not wittingly" was never charged; the asymmetry is documented, a deliberate double standard is not. [cite:de29_cbs_winner] [cite:de29_doj_snowden] [cite:de29_wex_clapper] The precedent is proven; its present repetition against political opponents is not documented.
