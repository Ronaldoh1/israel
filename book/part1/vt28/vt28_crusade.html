The Crusade: Béziers to Montségur, 1208–1244

Innocent III became pope in 1198 resolved to deal with the heresy in Languedoc. He tried persuasion first: Cistercian legates and preachers, among them Dominic, founder of the Dominican order, debated the Good Men in the towns, and by the account of the movement's historians converted few. [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] The legates also pressed the local lords to act, above all Raymond VI, count of Toulouse, whom they excommunicated in 1207 for failing to move against the heretics. [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade]

The trigger and the call

In January 1208 the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau met Raymond VI; the meeting ended in anger, and on the following morning, 14 January, Castelnau was murdered. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_albigenses] Innocent answered with a call to crusade against Languedoc, offering the lands of heretics and their protectors to those who took them. [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] It is often called the first crusade against Christians, which is loose, since Christian armies had fought each other for centuries. What was new was a crusade aimed at heretics inside Latin Christendom, with the same spiritual rewards as an expedition to the Holy Land. Six years later the Fourth Lateran Council wrote that equivalence into law: those who "take up the cross" against heretics were to receive "the indulgences and privileges granted to those who go in defense of the Holy Land". [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3]

Béziers, 22 July 1209

The crusading army, led by the papal legate Arnaud Amalric, abbot of Cîteaux, reached Béziers on 21 July 1209. The town's bishop, Renaud de Montpeyroux, had drawn up a list of 222 people, mostly Cathars and some Waldensians, probably their perfect and leaders; the list survives. Handing them over proved impossible, because the town protected them, and Catholic residents overwhelmingly refused the offer to leave and save themselves. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] On the morning of 22 July a sortie from the town ended in a brawl, the army's mercenaries (routiers) followed the retreating townspeople through an open gate, and the leaders sent the whole army in. People who had fled into the churches were killed inside them; the town was plundered and burned, and the cathedral with it. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers]

The numbers come from the attackers. Arnaud Amalric wrote to the pope in August that his men had put "almost 20,000" people to the sword, a report that admits no one was spared, while describing the attack as begun by low-ranking men without orders. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] The crusade's chronicler Peter of Les Vaux-de-Cernay put the dead in the church of Mary Magdalene alone at 7,000. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] Modern historians treat both figures as inflated: Christopher Tyerman writes that the true figure was "almost certainly far less", and Laurence Marvin concludes that most of the town's population and buildings survived. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] No count of the dead exists.

The most quoted sentence of the crusade, "Kill them all; God will know his own" (Caedite eos. Novit enim Dominus qui sunt eius), appears in only one source: the Dialogue on Miracles of the German Cistercian Caesarius of Heisterbach, written between 1219 and 1223, a decade after the event and far from it. Historians doubt that the abbot said it, and Marvin calls it apocryphal; the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 already stated that the words were "never pronounced"; it is better treated as a story told within the abbot's own order than as a record of his words. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_albigenses] Amalric's own letter, which boasts of the slaughter, is the firmer document.

The war of sieges

Carcassonne surrendered on 15 August 1209; its inhabitants were expelled, not killed. The northern French baron Simon de Montfort took command and was granted the conquered lands. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_albigenses] Minerve surrendered on 22 July 1210 after a summer siege. Catholics, soldiers and believers were allowed to leave; the perfect were offered their lives if they recanted, and very few did. The crusade's chronicler Peter of Les Vaux-de-Cernay gives the number burned as 140. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_minerve] [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] Lavaur fell on 3 May 1211 "amid dreadful carnage", in the Catholic Encyclopedia's words. Montfort had its lord, Aimery of Montréal, hanged with his knights, about eighty of them, and when the gallows gave way the rest were killed with the sword; modern historians put the Cathars burned there at up to 400, and here no offer to recant is recorded. [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_albigenses] [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_lavaur] The war then widened into a conflict for the lordship of the south. Peter II of Aragon came to the aid of Toulouse and was killed at Muret on 12 September 1213; Montfort was killed by a stone while besieging Toulouse in June 1218 (sources give the 25th or the 29th); the French crown took over the war in 1226 under Louis VIII, who died that November. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_albigenses] It ended with the Treaty of Paris, agreed at Meaux and sealed on 12 April 1229: Raymond VII kept Toulouse for his lifetime but ceded lands to the crown, promised his daughter in marriage to the king's brother Alphonse, undertook to hunt down heretics and pledged to pull down the walls of Toulouse; the same day he did public penance in Notre-Dame in Paris and was absolved. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_raymond7]

Montségur, 1243–1244

After 1229 the Good Men went underground, and the hilltop castle of Montségur in the Pyrenean foothills became the seat of their bishop and a refuge. Royal forces under the seneschal Hugues des Arcis besieged it from May 1243; its commander, Pierre-Roger de Mirepoix, surrendered in early March 1244. The terms let everyone leave, including men who had taken part in killing inquisitors, on condition that they renounce Catharism. A two-week truce followed. During it, some defenders asked for the consolamentum and joined the perfect. [cite:vt28_wiki_montsegur]

On 16 March 1244 about 210 to 215 people, about 190 of them perfect, the rest believers who would not recant or who had been consoled during the truce, were burned on a pyre at the foot of the mountain. Their bishop, Bertrand Marty, led them down. [cite:vt28_wiki_montsegur] The often repeated statement that they walked into the fire of their own accord is not established by a cited source; what the terms show is that they could have lived by recanting and did not. A claim that three or four perfect escaped with the community's money appears in later accounts and is unverified; the Holy Grail "treasure" of modern books is a twentieth-century invention. [cite:vt28_wiki_montsegur] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism]

How many died

Popular accounts, including earlier Sophia material, give the crusade's total dead as anything from 200,000 to 1,000,000. One reference work gives one million, citing a news-magazine article. [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] Neither end of that range rests on a contemporary count or on a published demographic study. The only figures from the time are a handful of battlefield and siege claims, made by the crusade's own leaders and chroniclers, and the best documented of them, Béziers, is the one historians most doubt. [cite:vt28_wiki_beziers] The honest statement is that the war killed many thousands, through massacre, siege, battle and burning, and that there is no reliable overall figure.

Whether the crusade should be called a genocide is also disputed. Mark Pegg argues that it "ushered genocide into the West by linking divine salvation to mass murder"; Robert Lerner disputes the classification. The record adds one fact both sides must weigh: at Minerve and Montségur people could save their lives by recanting, and some did, though at Lavaur no such offer is recorded. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_minerve] [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_lavaur] The war did not end the movement. That took a different instrument, built in the same years, and the next section turns to it. [scene:vt28_inquisition|The Inquisition]
