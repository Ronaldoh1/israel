Counting the Country

Every later argument about Palestine, about immigration, land and partition, rests on numbers, and the most important of them were gathered by the British administration in the years this chapter covers. Two slogans have grown up around those numbers, one on each side. The first says Palestine was "a land without a people." The second says Palestinian Arabs owned 94% of the land. Neither survives the Mandate's own statistics. This section sets out what the counts show, so that the arguments that follow can start from them.

Britain's first census of Palestine, taken in 1922, counted 757,182 people. Of these, 83,794 were Jews, about 11% of the population [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The second census, in 1931, found Jews at 17% of the population, Muslims at 73% and Christians at 9% [cite:jvl_census_1931]. In nine years the Jewish population had doubled. Yet the growth of the non-Jewish population over the same years was larger than the entire Jewish population [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The 1931 census also asked where people were born: 42% of Jews had been born in Palestine and 58% abroad [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The Yishuv of 1931 was, by a clear majority, a community of immigrants, though a substantial share was native-born.

The line above traces the Jewish share of the population across three counts: about 11% in 1922, 17% in 1931 and about 33% at the end of 1946 [cite:jvl_census_1931] [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. The first two points are British census results. The third is an estimate, made by the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP). UNSCOP put the population at the end of 1946 at about 1,846,000: roughly 1,203,000 Arabs (65%) and 608,000 Jews (33%) [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. Over almost the same span, British records count 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946 [cite:ssrn_mandate_immigration]. How those arrivals were distributed year by year, and why two published series for the same years disagree, is taken up in [scene:rd3_aliyot|the immigration section].

These three figures frame the period. The 757,182 people of 1922 are the country as Britain found it when the Mandate began; the 376,415 recorded Jewish immigrants of 1920–1946 are the arrivals that changed its composition; and the roughly 1.85 million of late 1946 are the country UNSCOP had in front of it when it made its recommendation [cite:jvl_census_1931] [cite:ssrn_mandate_immigration] [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. Even at the end of 1946, after a quarter-century of immigration, about two in three of the inhabitants were Arabs.

There is a second count from these years, and it does not match the census. In 1937 David Horowitz of the Jewish Agency's Economics Department prepared the Agency's economic case for the Peel Commission. According to that paper, the Jewish population in 1922 was 84,000, or 13 percent, against a non-Jewish population of 565,000 [cite:rs_rd3_horowitz_1937]. The Jewish figure is close to the census count of 83,794. The non-Jewish figure is far below the census, so the two together make a smaller total and a higher Jewish share. The Agency's paper does not explain its base in the sources used here. It is advocacy, and it is examined as such in [scene:us_s06|the economy section]; the census figure of about 11% is the one this book uses.

Land was counted too, and here the numbers have been bent in both directions. The Village Statistics of 1945 was compiled for the Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry and later used by UNSCOP. It classified land by ownership, by use and by tax category [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. In 1947, the United Nations partition plan allotted about 56% of the territory to the proposed Jewish state, at a time when Jews owned under 7% of the land; the proposed Jewish state would also have contained a large Arab minority [cite:historyskills_partition]. How the Jewish share of land was assembled in these years, through the purchases of the Jewish National Fund and others, is told in [scene:rd3_land|the land section]; the partition plan itself belongs to a later chapter.

With these figures, both slogans can be tested. "A land without a people" is refuted by every census the British took. The 1922 count found three-quarters of a million people, and the 1931 and 1946 figures found the Arab majority still growing [cite:jvl_census_1931] [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. The country was inhabited, and mostly by Arabs.

The counter-slogan fails in a different way. The claim that Palestinian Arabs privately owned 94% of the land counts large areas of state and public land as Arab-owned [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. The 1945 survey keeps those categories apart: land in private ownership is one column; land held by the state is another [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. Jewish ownership of under 7% does not mean Arab private ownership of the remainder. The honest comparison, for anyone making either case, uses the survey's own categories.

What do these numbers not settle? They do not tell us how the land not privately owned by Jews or Arabs was divided between state land and other public categories in any given year; the sources here give the distinction, not the totals. The 1946 population figure is an estimate, not a count, and the sources do not give its margin of error. And the gap between the census and the Jewish Agency's 1937 figures for 1922 is not explained in the material used here. The direction of the record is nonetheless clear. Palestine between 1922 and 1946 was a populated, majority-Arab country with a fast-growing Jewish minority, which owned a small share of the land, much of the rest of which was held by the state rather than by private Arab owners.
