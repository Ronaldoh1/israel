Long before anyone convened a Zionist congress, the structures that would fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state were already on the table. Crowns and empires held the territory. Houses of finance lent to those crowns and, in one case, to the papacy itself. Churches supplied both a political constituency and a body of prophecy. An Ottoman reform turned farmland into registered deeds that could one day be sold. And inside Judaism, rabbis argued over whether scripture permitted a return to the land at all.

This opening stage of the book surveys that board as it stood before the game began. It covers the City of London's unusual privileges and the London house of N M Rothschild & Sons, the 1831 banking arrangement between the Rothschilds and the Holy See, the Protestant restorationist lobby that pressed for a Jewish return to Palestine in 1839 and again in 1891, the great powers of the Napoleonic age, the Ottoman Land Code of 1858, and the religious debate over the Three Oaths.

The question running through every section is a simple one: what actually existed, on the documentary record, before political Zionism did? The answer requires separating what can be shown from the legends that have grown around these institutions, some of them deliberately manufactured. Where the record is firm, this book says so. Where it is thin or disputed, the book says that too.
