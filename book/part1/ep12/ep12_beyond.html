Beyond Europe and America, and When It Worked

Every precedent so far comes from Europe, North America or Australia. That reflects where inquiries and court records are easiest to read in English, not where abuse happens, and the book should not pretend otherwise. Two documented cases from elsewhere also serve a second purpose: they show what it looks like when the system acts.

In Muzaffarpur, in the Indian state of Bihar, a social audit by the Tata Institute of Social Sciences reported to the state government in May 2018 that girls in a state-funded shelter were being abused. Medical examinations confirmed abuse of 34 of the 42 girls. A police case was registered within days, the Supreme Court moved the investigation to the federal Central Bureau of Investigation and the trial to Delhi, and in January 2020 a court convicted 19 people, among them the shelter's owner, Brajesh Thakur, a former member of the state legislature. [cite:ep12_tnm_muzaffarpur2020]

In South Korea, a ring that blackmailed at least 74 women, including 16 teenagers, into making abusive videos sold through the Telegram app was uncovered in March 2020. Its leader, Cho Ju-bin, was sentenced in November 2020 to 40 years in prison for running a criminal organisation. [cite:ep12_aj_nthroom2020]

These are the control cases the pattern needs. In both, the abuse was real and organised, and in Muzaffarpur the man running it was a former legislator. What differed from the Epstein case was the speed: an independent report that could not be shelved, a court that took the case out of local hands, and prosecutors who charged the organisation, not only its leader. In both countries, conviction came within about two years of exposure. Epstein's case took eleven years to reach a ruling that the law had been broken, and the people around him were never charged.
