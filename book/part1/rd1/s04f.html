East Africa Rejected: Palestine Chosen by Elimination

The East Africa question did not end with the walkout of 1903. It ended two years later, by a second Congress vote, and it ended at a price: a permanent split in the movement and the founding of a rival organization that spent twenty years looking for a homeland anywhere but Palestine.

The man who led that rival organization had already led one side of the 1903 debate. Israel Zangwill, a British-Jewish writer and Zionist leader, led the "Territorialist Faction" at the Sixth Congress. His argument was not that East Africa should replace Palestine. It was that the British offer should be used as leverage in later negotiations with the Ottomans over Palestine.[cite:jewishaction_uganda_truth] He was not alone among the movement's prominent figures. Otto Warburg, a German botanist (1859–1938) from a wealthy Hamburg banking family, also supported the East Africa plan at the Sixth Congress.[cite:wiki_otto_warburg_botanist] [cite:encyclopedia_otto_warburg] He is a different man from the better-known twentieth-century biochemist of the same name, and his later career, traced below, shows that support for East Africa in 1903 did not rule out leading the movement's work in Palestine.

Between the two Congresses the movement lost its founder. Herzl died in 1904.[cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress]

The Seventh Zionist Congress met in Basel from 27 July to 2 August 1905. It did two things. It formally rejected the East Africa offer, closing the question the Sixth Congress had opened, and Britain withdrew the offer. And it elected David Wolffsohn, co-founder of the Jewish Colonial Trust and designer of the movement's flag, as president of the Zionist Organization in Herzl's place.[cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress] One reference work states the decision in broader terms: the Seventh Congress rejected a national home outside Palestine and its vicinity.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_c] The two decisions came at the same meeting, but the record does not link them as cause and effect. The succession followed from Herzl's death and would have come whatever the Congress decided about Africa.

For Zangwill the rejection was final. When the Congress turned the offer down outright, he led 28 delegates out of the hall and founded the Jewish Territorial Organization, the ITO, dedicated to finding land for Jewish settlement anywhere, no longer bound to Palestine.[cite:jewishaction_uganda_truth] He did not go alone or without means. The ITO had support at the time from two members of the Rothschild family, the brothers Leopold and Baron Nathan Mayer Rothschild, not to be confused with Baron Edmond de Rothschild.[cite:jewishaction_uganda_truth]

The ITO's search was wide. It sent exploratory expeditions to Canada, Australia, Mesopotamia (today's Iraq), Cyrenaica (in today's Libya) and Angola.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] It also ran the Galveston Movement, which directed Jewish immigrants to the United States through the port of Galveston, Texas, instead of New York.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] Zangwill himself, looking back, said that "there was not a land on earth that we did not think about." The search also went to court. Zangwill fought a case in the English courts against the Jewish Colonial Trust; as the sources describe it, the suit sought to restrain the Trust from colonizing anywhere outside Palestine, Syria, Asiatic Turkey, Sinai and Cyprus.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] The sources do not explain the reasoning behind the suit or record its outcome, so its purpose has to be left as they state it. What it shows is that the boundaries of the movement's territory were contested in law as well as in Congress.

None of the expeditions produced lasting settlement. The sources characterize the result in slightly different terms. One calls the Galveston effort a failed attempt to settle Jewish refugees in Texas and says the ITO's search met with little success; another speaks of a single modest, temporary success without naming it. They agree on the larger outcome: the ITO achieved little lasting settlement anywhere.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] [cite:wiki_jewish_territorial_organization] Zangwill rejoined the mainstream Zionist movement before his death in 1921. The ITO officially disbanded around 1925, four years later.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism]

Otto Warburg's path ran the other way. Having backed East Africa in 1903, he became the Zionist Organization's third president, serving from 1911 to 1921, and with Arthur Ruppin founded the Palestine Land Development Company, which he named and led until his death, bringing the Jewish National Fund in as a shareholder.[cite:wiki_otto_warburg_botanist] [cite:encyclopedia_otto_warburg] The company's work is told in [scene:s04g|the section on the Palestine Office]. His career shows how fluid positions on the territorial question could be.

A common view treats East Africa as a footnote and holds that no real alternative to Palestine ever existed. The record does not bear that out. The alternative was put to two Congresses. It cost the movement a walkout in 1903 and a permanent split in 1905. And after the split, an organized faction with prominent backers and a court case of its own spent two decades looking for other land and did not find it. That is a different finding from "no alternative existed." Alternatives existed, were pursued, and failed.

It is in that sense that Palestine was confirmed by elimination. Its place as the movement's target survived two organized internal challenges: the 1903 vote to examine East Africa, and the ITO's departure in 1905 to search elsewhere.[cite:wiki_jewish_territorial_organization] The phrase needs care, though, in both directions. It does not mean the 1903 Congress chose East Africa: it voted to examine the offer, under a sponsor who said plainly it was not Zion. Nor does it mean Palestine won only because everything else failed. The Seventh Congress rejected East Africa in 1905, while the ITO's search had barely begun; the decision came first, and the failures came after.

What "confirmed by elimination" cannot show is why. The record documents the votes, the walkouts, the split and the search. It does not record a single rationale shared by the delegates who chose Palestine. Some, like the Russian delegates of 1903, argued from the Basel Program, which named Palestine. Others, like Warburg, backed East Africa in 1903 and later led the organization's work in Palestine. A reading that ties the outcome to the Jewish masses' religious and historical attachment to the land is offered in [scene:s04fa|the section on the Uganda counterfactual], and it is a reading, not a documented finding.

The gaps are real. The sources here say nothing about what the East Africa commission reported, or when exactly Britain withdrew its offer. They do not give the vote at the Seventh Congress, only its result. They do not name the 28 delegates who left with Zangwill, explain his suit against the Trust, or identify the "modest success" one of them mentions.
