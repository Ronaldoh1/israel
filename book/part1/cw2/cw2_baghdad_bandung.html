Choosing Sides: Baghdad, Bandung and Amman, 1955–1956

In the twenty months between February 1955 and October 1956, the states of the Middle East were asked, in one forum after another, which side of the Cold War they stood on. Two answers took institutional form within two months of each other. In February 1955 Iraq and Turkey signed a defence pact open to Western membership. In April 1955 twenty-nine Asian and African states met at Bandung, without Israel. Between those poles stood Jordan, whose government tried to join the first camp in December 1955 and whose streets, army and voters pulled it toward the second over the following ten months. It is tempting to read this sequence as a single design. The documents show something less tidy: several governments, each pursuing its own interest, whose decisions converged and collided.

The Pact of Mutual Cooperation between Iraq and Turkey was signed at Baghdad on 24 February 1955. Its text is short and its commitments are general. Article 1 bound the two states to "co-operate for their security and defence". Article 5 opened accession to "any member of the Arab League" or to any other state actively concerned with the security of the region and recognized by both parties. The pact was to run for five years, renewable. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_baghdad] The accession clause carried the political weight. A treaty between Iraq and Turkey alone was a bilateral arrangement. A treaty open to every member of the Arab League invited each Arab government to decide whether to join, and made the decision a public test.

The membership grew through 1955. Britain adhered on 5 April 1955, Pakistan on 23 September 1955, and Iran on 23 October 1955, the dates as given in the footnote to the Avalon Project's text. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_baghdad] By the end of the year the pact joined one Arab state, Iraq, to Turkey, Iran, Pakistan and Britain. No other Arab government had acceded. Turkey's own path toward Israel ran on a separate track: it had recognized Israel in March 1949, [cite:w_isr_tur] and its secret intelligence arrangement with Israel belongs to Stage Four, told in [scene:tk_s03|Turkey and Israel].

The United States never signed. Its reasons are set out in a State Department position paper forwarded by Rountree to the Acting Secretary of State in November 1956. Joining, the paper concluded, "would not serve the U.S. national interest". It gave several reasons. Membership would revive Israeli demands for an American security guarantee, and that would complicate ratification in the Senate. It would entangle Washington in the rivalries among the Hashemite monarchies, Saudi Arabia and Egypt. And it would provoke the Soviet Union. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_baghdad_us] The first reason ties the pact directly to Israel's position. A treaty commitment to Iraq, on this logic, would have invited the question why Israel, too, should not have one, in the Senate that would have to ratify it.

Staying out did not mean staying away. Washington joined the pact's Economic and Counter-Subversion Committees in April 1956, set up a military liaison group in Baghdad on 1 November 1956, and paid a one-sixth share of the pact's administrative budget: $76,350 of $459,746. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_baghdad_us] The money was small. The United States took part in the pact's work and paid toward its upkeep while declining the treaty obligation that would have required the Senate's consent. One reading is that this gave Washington a place inside the pact without the commitments that membership would have created.

The alternative alignment assembled at Bandung on 18–24 April 1955. The Asian-African Conference brought together twenty-nine states. It was convened by the prime ministers of Pakistan, India, Burma, Ceylon and Indonesia. [cite:rs_cw2_enc_bandung] One of the convenors, Pakistan, would accede to the Baghdad Pact five months later; the two camps were not sealed off from each other. For the Middle East, one consequence, on Zachary Karabell's reading, was a person. In his words, Bandung "saw Nasser emerge as an international leader". [cite:rs_cw2_enc_bandung] Egypt, which stayed outside the pact, now had a leader with standing well beyond the Arab world.

Israel was not there. It was not invited, according to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, despite the insistence of Burma's U Nu. [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bandung] Middle East Eye adds that Nehru and U Nu both tried to have Israel invited, and that Indonesia "vehemently opposed" it. [cite:rs_cw2_mee_bandung] The two reports differ in emphasis rather than substance: one names a single advocate for Israel's inclusion, the other two, and names Indonesia as the opponent. Neither source explains the full reasoning of the governments involved.

Before the conference opened, the Arab delegations sought a resolution condemning Israel. Nehru was reported to be cool to the idea, and India's delegate, Arthur Lall, said it "remains to be seen" whether such a resolution would reach the floor. [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bandung] What emerged was not a condemnation. The final communiqué declared "support for the rights of the Arab people of Palestine" and called for the implementation of the United Nations resolutions on Palestine and for a peaceful settlement. [cite:rs_cw2_enc_bandung] [cite:rs_cw2_mee_bandung] The wording is worth reading closely. It named the Arab people of Palestine as holders of rights and tied the question to the United Nations resolutions; in the passages quoted in the sources here, it did not name Israel as an aggressor, and it asked for a peaceful settlement. Among the delegates was Haj Amin al-Husseini, who attended as part of Yemen's delegation. [cite:rs_cw2_enc_bandung] [cite:rs_cw2_mee_bandung]

The two gatherings offered two different answers to the same question. The pact's text presented it as cooperation for security and defence, open to any Arab League member that chose to join, and its membership included Britain. Bandung gathered twenty-nine Asian and African states, and, on Karabell's reading, gave Nasser his international stage. Neither text, as quoted in the sources here, states the other camp's objections, and this book does not supply them. Israel had no place in either: it was not invited to Bandung, and nothing in the sources here records any approach to it about the pact. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_baghdad] [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bandung]

The test came in Jordan. In December 1955, after a British military delegation visited Amman, Prime Minister Hazza al-Majali said he would take Jordan into the Baghdad Pact. Three days of rioting followed. The Arab Legion restored order, and Majali's government resigned within a week. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] The sequence matters. The announcement came from Jordan's own prime minister; the record shows that it followed the British visit, not that the visit dictated it. And the decision was reversed not by a foreign government but by the reaction in Jordan's own streets.

The reckoning with Britain's place inside Jordan came three months later. In March 1956 King Hussein dismissed Glubb as commander of the Arab Legion. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] A report from Amman dated 11 March 1956 records how it was done and why. Glubb, it says, was given about 19 hours and left on 2 March. Among the triggers it lists his "open endorsement of Hazza' al-Majali and the supporters of the Baghdad Pact", and a radio station that attacked Egypt while defending Iraq. [cite:rs_cw2_wilson_glubb] The report thus ties Glubb's removal to the pact crisis of December: the commander of Jordan's army had been seen to take one side in a dispute that had brought crowds into the streets.

The army itself was then remade. The Arab Legion was renamed the Jordan Arab Army, its British officers were phased out, and Ali Abu Nuwar succeeded Glubb in command. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] The force that had restored order in December after the pact riots was, by the spring, under Jordanian command and Jordanian name.

The voters completed the turn. In Jordan's elections of 21 October 1956, the National Socialist Party won a plurality, and Hussein named its leader, Sulayman Nabulsi, an admirer of Nasser, as prime minister. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] Within a year, a government that had announced its intention to join the Western pact had been replaced by one led by a man who looked to Cairo.

Read together, these events show convergence rather than conspiracy. Iraq's Hashemite government chose a Western alignment. Britain joined it, and sent a military delegation to Amman. The United States paid toward the pact and worked inside its committees but stayed out of the treaty, for reasons its own officials wrote down, Israel's guarantee demand among them. Egypt stood outside, and its leader emerged from Bandung, in Karabell's phrase, as an international leader. Jordan's prime minister moved toward Baghdad; Jordan's crowds, its king and finally its voters moved the other way. Each decision has a named author and a stated or documented reason. None of the sources used here shows a single hand directing them.

What the record does not settle matters as much as what it does. It gives Washington's reasons for staying out only as they were set down in November 1956, after the events of this chapter; whether the same reasoning governed American decisions in 1955 is not shown. It does not give Egypt's own statements against the pact, so Cairo's case appears here only through others' records: the American paper's mention of rivalry, the radio station that the Amman report says attacked Egypt, Nabulsi's admiration for Nasser. It does not show whether the British delegation that visited Amman in December 1955 asked Majali to join, or whether he acted on his own. And on Bandung, the two reports of who argued for Israel's invitation, and why Indonesia opposed it, leave the governments' full reasoning undocumented. The alignments are clear. The private calculations behind several of them are not.
