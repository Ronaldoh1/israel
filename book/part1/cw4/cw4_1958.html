1958: The Year the Region Turned

Ben-Gurion turned to the periphery in a year when the region seemed to come apart around Israel. Stage Three closed with the Eisenhower Doctrine, told in [scene:cw3_aftermath|the aftermath of Suez], as the frame of American policy in the region. In 1958 that frame was tested. Between February and October 1958 two Arab states merged, a rival union was formed and destroyed, a monarchy fell, and American Marines landed on a Mediterranean beach while British paratroopers flew into Amman. Israel took no part in any of these events, but each of them changed its position, and by the end of July its government had made its own choice about where it stood. Two explanations of the year have been argued over ever since: that the crisis was driven from Cairo, and that it grew from each country's own politics.

Two unions, February 1958. In February 1958 Egypt and Syria joined to form the United Arab Republic, with Nasser as its ruler. A rival union of two monarchies, the Arab Union of Jordan and Iraq, was formed in answer. It lasted from February to August 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco] The two unions drew the region's line in its plainest form that year: on one side the republic that Nasser led from Cairo and Damascus, on the other two kingdoms, one of which, Iraq, was the main pillar of the Baghdad Pact. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv]

Lebanon, from May. The first crisis came in Lebanon. Unrest began in May 1958 when President Camille Chamoun appeared to be seeking a second term. The American intelligence community's own assessment, Special National Intelligence Estimate 36.4–58 of 5 June 1958, put the cause plainly: "the present conflict was touched off by indications that Christian President Chamoun intended to amend the constitution." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] The historian Bruce Riedel describes what followed as a rebellion by Lebanon's Muslim population and some Christians against a term that his article calls unconstitutional. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] The magazine New Lines adds that Chamoun's forces killed several Nasserite protesters, and that a small civil war followed between pro-Chamoun Christian militias and Sunni and Shiite fighters inspired by Nasser. [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines]

Chamoun had another explanation. He blamed Nasser, charging that arms were being smuggled into Lebanon from the United Arab Republic. The United Nations tested the charge. In June 1958 the Security Council sent the UN Observation Group in Lebanon, known as UNOGIL, nearly 600 strong and staffing 49 observer posts. [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco] According to Riedel, the UN inspectors did not support Chamoun's allegations. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958]

The two readings of Lebanon's crisis should be set side by side, because both were held at the time by people with standing to hold them. The first is domestic: a president's bid for a second term, which Riedel's article calls unconstitutional, and a rebellion against it. This reading is supported by the American estimate of 5 June, which named Chamoun's intention to amend the constitution as the spark, and by Riedel and New Lines. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines] The second is regional: Nasser's new republic, with Syria now under its rule, arming the opposition to a pro-Western government. Its strongest evidence is that the fighters opposing Chamoun were, by New Lines's account, inspired by Nasser, and that Chamoun made the charge. Its weakness is that the observers the UN sent to find the smuggling did not support it, by Riedel's account. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] The sources here do not say that no arms crossed. They say that the evidence put to the United Nations did not establish the charge, and that Washington's own estimators located the cause in Beirut.

The same estimate looked ahead to an American intervention. It judged that Israel "would probably avoid direct involvement," and CIA Director Allen Dulles said that "Israel will probably stay out" if the United States intervened. It expected the Soviet reaction to be "confined to vigorous diplomatic and propaganda action" unless American forces fought on the territory of the United Arab Republic. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] Israel did stay out of the fighting, and Moscow's answer, when it came to Israel on 1 August, was a diplomatic note. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017]

Baghdad, 14 July. What changed the summer was not Lebanon but Iraq. On 14 July 1958 an army coup in Baghdad, which surprised American and British intelligence, killed King Faisal II, the crown prince and the prime minister, Nuri al-Sa'id. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] The White House record of that day shows how uncertain Washington's knowledge was: Dulles reported that "the Crown Prince has been killed, and perhaps Nuri also." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] Iraq's monarchy was overthrown. [cite:w_1958_iraq] The consequences reached well beyond Iraq. Liel writes that the Baghdad Pact thereby lost its main pillar, and that Turkey sought closer ties with Israel, a turn told in [scene:cw4_periphery|the periphery and the Trident]. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] The Arab Union of Jordan and Iraq ended the same summer. [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco]

Eisenhower convened the National Security Council the same day. In his memoirs he wrote: "we feared the worst…the complete elimination of Western influence in the Middle East." [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] The sentence suggests that it was not Lebanon's presidency alone that Eisenhower feared for, but the whole Western position in the region.

Blue Bat and Amman. On 15 July United States Marines landed near Beirut, in Operation Blue Bat. About 1,700 came ashore in the first contingent at Khalde, backed by three carrier groups. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] Britain moved in parallel. On 14 July Prime Minister Macmillan referred to a planned British contingent of "about 3,700," and British paratroopers flew to Amman to support King Hussein. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_docs] [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines] The route those British flights took, over Israel, would produce a crisis of its own in August.

How large the American force became is reported differently. Riedel gives a peak of "almost 15,000" Marines and soldiers; Byron Cannon gives about 14,000; New Lines says the force never exceeded 14,000. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco] [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines] The difference is about a thousand men and the sources do not resolve it. The other figures come from one source or another. One American died from sniper fire; Cannon adds three accidental deaths. The intervention cost about $200 million, according to New Lines. All American forces had left by 25 October 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco] [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines]

How Lebanon's crisis ended. The landing did not decide Lebanon's politics; a Lebanese compromise did. General Fuad Chehab, the commander of the Lebanese army, was chosen in 1958 as the compromise successor to Chamoun; Cannon dates the choice to October, and New Lines says that Nasser favoured the outcome. [cite:rs_cw4_cannon_ebsco] [cite:rs_cw4_boswall_newlines] Riedel records an act of improvisation on the ground: the American ambassador, he writes, disobeyed instructions to broker the Lebanese army's escort of the Marines. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] The force landed during Chamoun's presidency thus left behind his successor, an outcome that, by New Lines's account, the man Chamoun had blamed for the crisis favoured. That outcome can be read as support for the domestic reading: the crisis was settled by a Lebanese compromise that Nasser favoured, not by the removal of his influence.

The American dissent. Not everyone in Washington accepted the case for intervention. According to Riedel, Senators John F. Kennedy and J. William Fulbright opposed it, and Kennedy argued that Nasser was not a Soviet puppet. [cite:rs_cw4_riedel_1958] Their objection, on one reading, went to the premise. The Eisenhower Doctrine, as Secretary Dulles described it to Israel's envoys that August, promised support to Israel if it were attacked by a Communist power; if Nasser was an Arab nationalist acting for his own ends rather than Moscow's, then the doctrine's logic did not reach him, and sending troops against his influence was a different kind of act from the one the doctrine described. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d35]

Israel's position, July 1958. Israel stayed out, as the American estimators had predicted. But it did not stay silent. Its government's view of the summer was set out to Dulles in London on 27 July 1958 by its envoys, Abba Eban and Reuven Shiloah. They told him that Israel had no expansionist ideas, that it would not seek to take the West Bank if that could be avoided, but that it wished to avoid hostile forces on its border. Ben-Gurion believed, they said, that Jordan "could be preserved only if anti-Nasser forces in the area cooperated among themselves." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d31] The two statements fit together. Read together, they put Israel's interest in a Jordan that survived rather than one absorbed by the United Arab Republic, and its proposed means in cooperation among the forces opposed to Nasser, which matches the periphery pact that Ben-Gurion had put to Eisenhower three days earlier. The qualification "if that could be avoided" is also part of the record; the envoys did not rule out every circumstance.

Dulles, for his part, read the moment as a check to Nasser. He judged that Nasser's anger at the landings reflected the fact that they had checked his success. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d31] What Israel received from Washington that week was an assurance in general terms. Eisenhower's letter to Ben-Gurion of 25 July 1958 said that Israel could "be confident of United States interest in the integrity and independence of Israel." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d31] It was an expression of interest, not a guarantee. Within days Israel's government would close its airspace to the British and American flights to Jordan, and in the argument that followed, its complaint that it had never been given a written guarantee came into the open. That story is told in [scene:cw4_overflights|the overflight crisis].

What 1958 changed. A familiar account holds that the region's autocracies and wars grew only from its own culture and religion. The record of 1958 does not support that, and it does not support the opposite simplification either. Lebanon's crisis began, by the reckoning of Washington's own estimators, with a president's attempt to extend his term; Iraq's monarchy was ended by its own army; the United Arab Republic was a union of two governments. These were political events with political causes. At the same time, the United States landed an army in Lebanon at a cost of about $200 million, Britain flew troops into Jordan, and Eisenhower, by his own account, acted in fear of "the complete elimination of Western influence in the Middle East." The year's events were made by local actors and foreign powers together, and neither account alone explains them.

The record leaves several questions open. How many American troops were in Lebanon at the peak is given as about 14,000 and as almost 15,000. Whether arms crossed from Syria into Lebanon in any quantity is not settled by these sources; what is settled is that the UN observers did not support Chamoun's charge. The planning of the Baghdad coup, and why it surprised both American and British intelligence, is not described in the sources gathered here. And the meaning of the American ambassador's disobedience in Beirut, as Riedel reports it, is given in a single sentence that the sources do not expand. For Israel, the summer's lasting result was clear enough: the fall of the Iraqi monarchy brought Turkey towards it, and the Anglo-American response brought Israel's airspace into the Cold War.
