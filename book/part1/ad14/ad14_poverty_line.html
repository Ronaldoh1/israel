The Poverty Line: A 1963 Grocery Bill, Still Counting

The taught Dream draws a line between the poor and everyone else and promises that work carries a family across it. Where that line sits decides who counts as poor, who qualifies for help and whether the promise looks kept. This section traces the official line back to the grocery budget it was built on in 1963, sets it beside the Census Bureau's newer Supplemental Poverty Measure, adds the private ALICE measure that tries to price what a household actually needs, and gives the strongest case that the official line overstates poverty rather than understates it. Part Two set out how poverty is measured and where the taught version holds; this section tests the measuring stick itself. [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two: How to Measure]

How the line was drawn

The official poverty thresholds were worked out in 1963 and 1964 by Mollie Orshansky, an economist at the Social Security Administration, and published in the Social Security Bulletin in January 1965. She started from the Agriculture Department's "economy food plan", the cheapest of its model diets, which the department itself described as "designed for temporary or emergency use when funds are low". She then multiplied its cost by three for families of three or more, and by 3.7 for couples, because the 1955 Household Food Consumption Survey showed that families of three or more spent about one-third of their after-tax income on food. [cite:ad14_aspe_history] The logic was simple: if a family's whole income was less than three times the cost of an emergency diet, it could not afford that diet and everything else as well.

The Office of Economic Opportunity, which ran President Johnson's War on Poverty, adopted the thresholds as its working definition in May 1965. In August 1969 the Bureau of the Budget made them the federal government's official statistical definition of poverty and changed the yearly update from the cost of the food plan to the Consumer Price Index, the government's main measure of inflation. In 1981 the separate thresholds for farm families and for families headed by women were dropped, shrinking the table from 124 thresholds to 48. [cite:ad14_aspe_history] Since then the line has been raised each year by inflation and nothing else. The Census Bureau's own description of the measure in its report on 2025 still reads "three times the cost of a minimum food diet in 1963", and the bureau says the measure has "remained mostly unchanged since it was introduced in the mid-1960s". [cite:ad14_census_p60290pdf] [cite:ad14_census_compare]

That is the sense in which the line was never re-based. Indexing to inflation keeps the 1963 diet's purchasing power constant, but it never asks again what share of a budget food takes, or what a household now needs that it did not need in 1963: child care so that both parents can work, a car to reach a job, a phone and internet to apply for one, health insurance premiums. The official measure also counts only cash income before taxes, so it ignores food stamps, housing aid and tax credits on one side and medical bills, work costs and taxes on the other, and it uses one line for the whole country whether rent is cheap or expensive. [cite:ad14_census_compare] This is designed friction rather than a hidden flaw: the method is published, and its limits have been known since a National Academy of Sciences panel proposed a new approach in May 1995. [cite:ad14_aspe_history]

Two lines, two answers

In 2025 the official threshold was $16,749 for a single person under 65 and $32,649 for two adults with two children. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] The Department of Health and Human Services turns the thresholds into simpler "poverty guidelines" used to decide eligibility for programmes; for 2026 they are $15,960 for one person and $33,000 for a family of four in the 48 contiguous states. [cite:ad14_hhs_guide2026] A full-time worker at the federal minimum wage of $7.25 earns $7.25 × 40 hours × 52 weeks = $15,080 a year before tax, which is $880 below the 2026 guideline for one person and $1,669 below the 2025 Census threshold. Work at the legal floor does not, on its own, lift even a single adult over the line. [scene:ad5_wages_floors|Part Five: the frozen floors]

The Supplemental Poverty Measure, or SPM, was built from the 1995 panel's proposals after an interagency working group asked the Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics to develop it in 2010. Its threshold is based on what families actually spend on food, clothing, shelter, utilities, telephone and internet, set at 82 per cent of the median spending on that bundle, averaged over five years, and adjusted for local housing costs and for whether a family rents, pays a mortgage or owns outright. Its resources add benefits such as food stamps, housing aid and tax credits, and subtract taxes, work expenses, medical spending and child support paid to another household. [cite:ad14_census_compare] [cite:ad14_census_p60290pdf]

The two measures give different answers. In 2025 official poverty was 10.2 per cent, 34.5 million people, while the SPM rate was 13.1 per cent, 2.9 points higher. [cite:ad14_census_p60290pdf] Since 34.5 million is 10.2 per cent of the population the measure covers, that population is about 338 million, and 13.1 per cent of it is about 44 million people. The SPM also shows which programmes and costs move people across the line. Social Security lifted 28.8 million people out of SPM poverty in 2025, cutting the rate by 8.5 points; refundable tax credits lifted 6.1 million, including 3.3 million children. Medical expenses, including insurance premiums, pushed 7.7 million people into poverty and added 2.3 points to the rate. [cite:ad14_census_p60290pdf] The SPM has its own weakness: in July 2026 the Bureau of Labor Statistics found errors in how in-kind benefits were imputed in its 2019–2024 thresholds, and the corrected rates for 2019–2023 came out 0.1 to 0.3 points lower than first published, without changing the trend. [cite:ad14_census_spmrevise] [cite:ad14_census_compare]

ALICE: the gap above the line

United Way's United For ALICE project measures a group that both official lines miss. ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed: households that earn more than the federal poverty level but less than the cost of a bare-bones budget for housing, child care, food, transportation, health care and technology in their county. Using 2024 data, it counted 131.9 million households, of which 16.5 million (13 per cent) were below the poverty level and 38.0 million (29 per cent) were ALICE, so that 54.5 million households, 41 per cent, fell below what the project calls the ALICE Threshold. [cite:ad14_alice_national] The ALICE budget is a threshold chosen by an advocacy organisation, not an official statistic; its value is that it is priced county by county from current costs, which is exactly what the official line, frozen to a 1963 diet, does not do. It also counts households rather than people, which is why its poverty share differs from the Census rate.

Living below or just above any of these lines carries its own price list, from overdraft and late fees to check cashing and rent-to-own contracts. Those charges, and what happened to the federal rules on them in 2024 and 2025, are set out in Part Nine; the fines, fees and benefit cliffs that bear hardest on low incomes are in Part Eleven. The case that the line overstates poverty

The strongest critics of the official line argue the opposite way: that it overstates poverty because it leaves out most of what government now provides. The economists Richard Burkhauser, Kevin Corinth, James Elwell and Jeff Larrimore built a "full-income" poverty measure that counts taxes, tax credits, food and housing aid and the value of health insurance, set its line so that it matched the official rate of 19.5 per cent in 1963, and then raised it only by inflation, as the War on Poverty's planners intended. On that absolute standard poverty fell to 1.6 per cent by 2019, while the official rate stood at 10.5 per cent; real median income more than doubled over the period and transfers did much of the rest. On a relative version of their measure, which moves with typical incomes, poverty fell only from 19.5 to 16.0 per cent. [cite:ad14_nber_fpm] The study was published in the Journal of Political Economy in 2024. [cite:ad14_nber_fpm]

Both critiques can be true at once, because they answer different questions. Measured against what a very poor family could buy in 1963, almost no American is now that poor, which is a real achievement of growth and of the programmes launched in the 1960s. Measured against what it costs to live in a given county in 2025, with rent, child care and health insurance priced in, tens of millions of working households fall short. The official line sits between these two answers and is accurate to neither.

Where it works as promised

On its own terms the taught version holds for most full-time workers. Official poverty among people who worked full time all year was 1.6 per cent in 2025, and child poverty on the official measure was 13.4 per cent, a historic low. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] [cite:us18_census_p60290] The official rate has fallen from 22.4 per cent in 1959 to 10.2 per cent in 2025, and the SPM shows that public programmes do much of that work: Social Security and refundable tax credits together lifted almost 35 million people (28.8 + 6.1) above the supplemental line in 2025. [cite:us18_epi_povtrend] [cite:ad14_census_p60290pdf] The safety net, in other words, works where it is funded. The open question for 2026 is whether it stays funded: the number of children receiving food stamps fell by 1.2 million between July 2025 and July 2026, according to an analysis by the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, a left-leaning research group, and analysts expect the 2025 budget law's cuts to Medicaid and food aid to show up in later poverty figures. [cite:us18_njm_poverty]
