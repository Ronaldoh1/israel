Authentic and Narrow

Much of what circulates as proof of a "Greater Israel" plan falls into three classes, and this part takes them in turn. Some items are authentic: the text exists, the words were written or spoken, and the date and venue can be checked. Some are real documents that are misread, so that a forecast, a proposal or a coin design is made to carry a claim it does not contain. Some are myths: the object said to exist has never been shown to exist, or its origin is documented and is something else. This section deals with the first class. Each authentic item is real, and each is narrower than the use made of it. The finding has to be stated in both directions, because denying an authentic text in order to answer its misuse is the error that two earlier accounts in this library made about Herzl.

The verse

The phrase "from the Nile to the Euphrates" is built on Genesis 15:18, in which God makes a covenant with Abram: "Unto thy seed have I given this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates" (King James Version). The New International Version translates the same words "from the Wadi of Egypt". [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] The text is authentic in the plainest sense: it is in every edition of the Hebrew Bible. What it is not is a map. The verse uses nahar, "river", and one standard reference work, the Encyclopedia of The Bible, reads "the river of Egypt" as the Nile; the boundary passages in Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 use a different term, the "brook of Egypt", which the same work takes to be "highly probably" the Wadi el-Arish in northern Sinai. [cite:gi1_ebg_riveregypt] [cite:gi1_ebg_brookegypt] Those boundary passages describe a land much narrower than the Genesis promise, lying west of the Jordan. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] Daniel Pipes, writing in 1994, argued that the specific boundaries govern the general promise, and that a covenant made with all of Abram's offspring, who in the narrative include Ishmael, cannot be read as a deed for a modern state. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The readings, and the religious currents that take the broader verse literally, are set out in [scene:gi1_phrase|Part One] and [scene:gi11_religious|Part Eleven]. For this part the point is narrower. A verse is evidence of what a scripture says. It becomes evidence of a state's programme only when a named government, party or official adopts it as one, and that is the test Parts Five to Nine apply to each actor.

Herzl's note of 1898

Theodor Herzl's diary contains a note, from 1898, of a plan to ask the Ottoman sultan for a territory running from the frontier of Egypt to the Euphrates. The source for that statement is the scholar most often cited for having debunked it. Pipes wrote in the Middle East Quarterly in 1994: "In 1898, Herzl planned to ask the Ottoman sultan for a territory stretching from the Egyptian frontier to the Euphrates", citing the German critical edition of the diaries, volume 2, page 650. His verdict was confined to what followed: "Herzl did not in fact request the Nile-to-Euphrates region from the Ottoman king or anyone else." [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny]

That is the Pipes correction this volume makes. Book Four of the Israel Architecture series called the Herzl item a fabrication, "not authentic", and the Road to 1948 volume states that it "is not in his writings" and began as a 1994 misattribution ([scene:s04|the founding of the Zionist Organization]). Both rely, directly or indirectly, on the article that confirms the note. On the record, the note is authentic. What is false is the claim that Herzl put it to the sultan, or that it became the programme of the Zionist Organization. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The exact English wording most often quoted ("From the Brook of Egypt to the Euphrates") has not been checked against the printed English edition and is not quoted here as Herzl's words. The entry, the Hechler maps that preceded it and the requests Herzl actually made are examined in [scene:gi2_herzl|Part Two].

The map at Paris, 1919

The first boundary the Zionist movement formally put before the world's governments is also documented, and it is the clearest answer to the claim that the movement asked for the Nile and the Euphrates. The Zionist Organization's "Statement on Palestine" to the Paris Peace Conference, dated 3 February 1919, drew the northern line from "a point on the Mediterranean Sea in the vicinity south of Sidon", along the Lebanon foothills and the slopes of Mount Hermon; the eastern line ran "close to and west of the Hedjaz Railway terminating in the Gulf of Akaba"; in the south it asked for "a frontier to be agreed upon with the Egyptian Government." [cite:gi2_bu_zo1919] That line takes in the Golan, the slopes of Hermon, a strip of southern Lebanon and a band east of the Jordan as far as the railway. It does not reach the Nile, Sinai, Damascus or the Euphrates. Its stated grounds were water and access: it called Hermon "Palestine's real 'Father of Waters'" and asked for Litani water rights by international arrangement, not for the river. [cite:gi2_bu_zo1919] The map is authentic and it was a real claim to more land than any later border gave; it is also far narrower than the slogan. The powers rejected it, as [scene:gi2_map1919|Part Two] shows.

A rabbi's answer to UNSCOP, 1947

A fourth item is less often quoted and is also authentic. In July 1947 the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine heard Rabbi Judah L. Fishman, a leader of the religious Zionist Mizrachi movement, in Jerusalem. According to a Jewish Telegraphic Agency dispatch printed in the Detroit Jewish News on 18 July 1947, the Indian delegate Sir Abdur Rahman questioned him on theology and asked: "What were the boundaries of the land promised by God to the Jews?" Fishman replied: "From Egypt to the Euphrates." He added that "God promised it to Abraham 4,000 years ago", and that the promise ran to Isaac's descendants: "He gave Ishmael another land." [cite:gi10_djn_fishman] The exchange is sometimes cited, in Book Four among other places, as a "Rabbi Fischmann" testimony and marked unverified. The contemporary report confirms it. It also shows its scope. A rabbi answered a delegate's question about the extent of a biblical promise, and in the JTA's account he did not mention the Nile. That shows how one religious leader read Genesis in 1947. It is not evidence of what the Jewish Agency asked the committee to recommend, and the record of the borders the movement actually sought and accepted between 1937 and 1949 is in [scene:gi2_peel|Part Two] and [scene:gi3_borders|Part Three]. [cite:gi10_djn_fishman]

The pattern across the four is consistent. The broad formula appears in scripture, in a founder's private note and in a rabbi's answer about scripture. In the one formal territorial claim the movement made to the powers before 1948, it does not appear. The documents that carry territorial language into the policy of the State of Israel come later and are of a different kind: the 1977 Likud platform, the Golan Heights Law of 1981, the 2018 Nation-State Law and the 2022 coalition guidelines, examined in [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five] and [scene:gi6_plans|Part Six]. [cite:gi5_toi_nslaw] Those are better evidence than anything in this section, and they concern the land between the Jordan and the sea and the Golan, not the Nile.
