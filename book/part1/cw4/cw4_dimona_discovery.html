How Washington Found Dimona, 1958–1960

By the autumn of 1960 a French-built reactor complex had been rising in the Negev near Beersheba for more than two years. How Israel obtained it, through agreements with France signed in 1957 and a construction site run in secret, is told in [scene:cw4_french_deal|the French agreement]. This section follows the other side of the same years: what the government of the United States knew about the site, when it knew it, and why it took until December 1960 for the knowledge to reach the President's table as a formal judgment. It is an intelligence story, and the documents that tell it were written partly by the officials who missed the signs and partly by those who were later asked to explain why.

The first questions, 1958. Washington did ask. On 7 March 1958 a State Department instruction, A-128, asked the embassy in Tel Aviv for information on Israel's atomic energy program. On 15 April 1958 the embassy's second secretary, Lewis Townsend, met Ernst David Bergmann, the scientist who had approached the Atomic Energy Commission for a reactor in 1956. Bergmann told him that a claim about uranium made by a man named Benard was untrue. The embassy reported the conversation in Despatch 652 the next day, 16 April 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The exchange was routine in form: an instruction went out, a diplomat asked a question, an official gave an answer, and a despatch came back. Nothing in it obliged anyone in Washington to look further.

What else was said in April 1958 mattered more, though no one saw it at the time. Bergmann had also mentioned an "experimental reactor." That remark was read in Washington as a reference to Soreq, the small research reactor that the American firm AMF had contracted in March 1957 to build for Israel under the Atoms for Peace program. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

What the post-mortem found. The clearest account of the failure is the government's own. An intelligence post-mortem was delivered to President Kennedy's office on 31 January 1961. It found that information on the site had been available to some parts of the intelligence community as early as April 1958, and that site information which reached intelligence channels in mid-1959 "was discounted." It blamed reports that had been lost and dots that were "not properly connected," among them Bergmann's April 1958 "experimental reactor" remark, which analysts had wrongly tied to Soreq. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The post-mortem is a confession of a particular kind. It does not say the government was deceived about facts it had looked for; it says pieces of the record were in the government's possession and were set aside or misfiled.

What the U-2 saw. One question the documents do not settle is the role of aerial photography. Three positions are on record about what photographs taken by the U-2 reconnaissance aircraft showed. The Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library say that U-2 overflights captured Dimona's construction in 1958, but that the site was not identified as nuclear until 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] The Nuclear Weapon Archive goes further, saying that US intelligence identified a probable reactor complex before the end of 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] William Burr and Avner Cohen, who edited a published collection of the declassified documents, say it is unclear whether U-2 photographs were especially telling, and that the photographs of the site taken in late 1960 came from military attachés. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The first two positions imply that Washington had seen the site in 1958 and failed to understand it; the third implies that the images that counted came later. The 1961 post-mortem, as Burr and Cohen present it, points the same way as they do: information existed from April 1958 but was discounted or lost, so that the government as a whole did not know until the middle of 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] None of the sources fetched for this chapter reproduces a 1958 U-2 photograph of Dimona or a 1958 analytical report identifying it.

A businessman's report, July 1960. On Burr and Cohen's reading of the record, the United States first learned of Dimona in midsummer 1960, and from an American corporate official in Tel Aviv rather than from any intelligence service. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2015] In late July 1960 an employee of AMF, D. Anderson, reported a conversation with Daniel Kimhi of Naptha Petroleum. According to Anderson, Kimhi said that French personnel were building "a 60 megawatt atomic power reactor" near Beersheba. The embassy passed the report to Washington in Tel Aviv Despatch 75, dated 2 August 1960, the earliest reference to Dimona among the US documents declassified so far. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2015] The source of the first solid lead is worth noting. AMF was the firm that had contracted to build Soreq, the open American project; its employee heard about the French one in conversation. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

A "textile plant." American officials also saw the site for themselves, and were told what it was not. During a helicopter ride over the area, Addy Cohen, an official of Israel's Finance Ministry, described it as a "textile plant." The briefing book that published the documents dates this episode to September 1960 in some places and, in one document, to February 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The difference matters. If the ride took place in February, an American official had looked at the site and been given a cover story months before Anderson's report; if in September, the cover story was offered after the embassy had already been alerted. The documents as published do not resolve which date is right. Addy Cohen reappears in December, telling American diplomats a very different story.

Paris and London, November 1960. In November the question moved from the embassy to the capitals. On 22 November 1960 an official of France's Atomic Energy Commission, the CEA, told John Rouleau of the US Atomic Energy Commission that France was not collaborating with Israel on a power reactor. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] The denial was carefully bounded. What France had agreed to supply, according to the sources on the 1957 agreements, was a research reactor, not a power reactor, together with a reprocessing plant covered by unwritten protocols. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] Three days later, on 25 November, Secretary of State Herter told the British ambassador, Harold Caccia, that Israel might be building a plutonium-producing reactor with French aid. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] By then the suspicion had reached the level of the Secretary of State and had been shared with Washington's closest ally.

Gomberg's debriefing, 1 December 1960. The technical judgment that turned suspicion into alarm came from an academic. Henry Gomberg, a professor of nuclear engineering at the University of Michigan, had visited Israel, and he was debriefed in Washington on 1 December 1960. He judged the Beersheba site a "large classified project" with a reactor "far beyond any kind of a training reactor," one that would be "capable of producing weapons grade plutonium." He estimated its power at about 250 megawatts thermal and noted its resemblance to France's G-1 reactor at Marcoule. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal]

The estimates of the reactor's size are themselves part of the story. Anderson's source spoke of 60 megawatts; Gomberg put it at about 250 megawatts thermal. The agreement with France, according to the sources on the 1957 deal, was for a 24-megawatt reactor whose cooling and waste systems were designed for three times that power. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] No American observer had yet been inside, and each figure was an outsider's estimate.

The intelligence machinery responds, 2–8 December. Within a week the formal machinery produced two documents. On 2 December 1960 the Joint Atomic Energy Intelligence Committee issued a report titled "Israeli Plutonium Production." It referred to a "plutonium separation plant." [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2024] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] When the National Security Archive released the report in December 2024, its editors called it the first and only known US intelligence report to state that Dimona would include a reprocessing plant; later US intelligence treated that question as unsettled until the late 1960s. [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2024] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] The point is not small. A separation, or reprocessing, plant is the facility that extracts plutonium; it was the part of the French package that, by the French official Bertrand Goldschmidt's later recollection, had been "quite difficult" to agree. [cite:rs_cw4_goldschmidt] The committee had named that element in its first report, yet later US intelligence treated the question as open until the late 1960s.

On 8 December 1960 came Special National Intelligence Estimate 100-8-60. It judged that "plutonium production for weapons is at least one major purpose" of the project, and that Israel "will produce some weapons grade plutonium in 1963-64 and possibly as early as 1962." The same day, CIA Director Allen Dulles briefed the 470th meeting of the National Security Council, showing photographs taken by the US Army attaché, and Herter put the cost of the project at $40–80 million. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] The Federation of American Scientists also dates to 8 December 1960 a CIA report on Dimona's implications for the spread of nuclear weapons. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel]

How the machinery worked, and where it failed. The chain in the figure shows who could act on a report. An embassy officer could ask questions and send despatches, but could not compel an answer from the Israeli government or a site visit. The State Department could instruct the embassy and raise the matter with the French and British. The formal judgments in December came from the Joint Atomic Energy Intelligence Committee and from a special national intelligence estimate, and it was in that form that the matter reached the National Security Council, where the President and his senior advisers could decide what to do. Information had to travel the whole length of that chain to become policy. The post-mortem's finding is that, in 1958 and 1959, it did not: a remark was filed under the wrong reactor, and a report in mid-1959 was discounted before it reached the people who could weigh it. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] When the chain did work, in the last week of November and the first week of December 1960, it moved fast: from Herter's word to Caccia on 25 November to an estimate and a National Security Council briefing on 8 December took less than two weeks.

Failure or success? Burr and Cohen, the editors of that collection, give a hard judgment on the American side. They call the delay "a major blunder, what is called in our times an intelligence failure," and compare it to the failures that preceded India's nuclear tests of 1974 and 1998. Seen from the other side, they call it "a tremendous counterintelligence success for Israel." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2015] Their argument has a counterfactual edge: earlier pressure from Washington on France, which supplied the reactor, and on Norway, which sold the heavy water, might in their view have stopped the project. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] These are their judgments, not findings of the documents. The case for them rests on the post-mortem's own admission that information was available from April 1958. The case against the stronger version rests on the uncertainty about what the U-2 photographs showed and when, and on the fact that the one embassy report of 1958 described here records a direct denial from Bergmann. Whether Washington failed to see what was in front of it, or was successfully kept from seeing it, is a question of emphasis on which the record supports both readings in part.

Several things remain open. The documents published so far do not show what the mid-1959 information was, who discounted it, or why. They do not settle whether any U-2 photograph taken in 1958 was recognized at the time as showing a reactor. The date of the "textile plant" helicopter ride is given two ways in the same collection. And the 2 December 1960 report's reference to a separation plant raises a question that the sources here do not answer: why a finding made in the government's first formal report was treated by later American intelligence as unsettled for most of the decade. What is settled is the sequence. By 8 December 1960 the United States government had concluded, in a national estimate, that weapons were at least one major purpose of the Negev reactor, and the day before it had summoned Israel's ambassador for an explanation. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] What followed is told in [scene:cw4_dimona_december|the disclosure month].
