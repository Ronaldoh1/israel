The Courts and the Inspector General, as of October 2026

With no enforcement mechanism in the law, the fight moved to two places. On 23 April 2026 the Justice Department's own Inspector General announced a review of the department's processes for identifying, redacting and releasing the records under the Act. [cite:ep9_aj_ig2026] It has not reported. Four days later Katie Phang, a lawyer and journalist, sued the acting Attorney General in federal court in Washington, arguing that the department had withheld and redacted records in breach of the Act and had failed to publish the required log of its redactions. [cite:ep9_clearinghouse_phang]

Judge Emmet Sullivan granted a preliminary injunction on 25 June 2026, finding Phang likely to succeed, and ordered the department to produce specified records, begin reviewing foreign-language documents and publish the redaction log, or explain why not. He then reviewed documents privately, heard argument in August, and on 16 September granted Phang's motion to compel. The department appealed twice and asked for the orders to be paused. [cite:ep9_clearinghouse_phang] On 28 October 2026 a three-judge panel of the appeals court in Washington refused, finding that the government had "failed to show that it is irreparably harmed by merely initiating review" of the foreign-language documents; arguments over particular redactions can still be heard. [cite:ep9_yahoo_phang_dc] The appeal itself is pending.

So, as of the end of October 2026: about half the records remain unpublished; a federal court has ordered the department to do more; the department is contesting that order; the Inspector General's review is unfinished; and a second law to give the first one teeth has been introduced, not passed. [cite:ep9_rollcall_final] [cite:khanna2] These are the facts as of this writing, and they are still moving.
