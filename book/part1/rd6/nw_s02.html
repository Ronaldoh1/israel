The Network Map, 1897–1948

The American pro-Zionist effort is sometimes described as a loose collection of unconnected sympathizers. The documented record describes something more structured: a set of organizations linked to one another by founding, funding and coordination, and reaching outward to named legislators, White House aides and donors. This section maps those ties by degree of distance from the centre, and names the type of each tie. It maps documented organizational, political and financial relationships only. It does not assert any tie on the basis of identity.

At the centre stood the Jewish Agency and the World Zionist Organization, under Weizmann and Ben-Gurion. They set the movement's goals. A Justice Department order of 1962 alleged that the Jewish Agency funded the American Zionist Council, the postwar successor of the wartime lobbying body; the department later withdrew its registration demand. [cite:azc_fara_doj]

One step out were the bodies tied directly to that centre by organization. The American Zionist Emergency Council, co-chaired by Abba Hillel Silver and Stephen Wise, ran the national campaign. The Zionist Organization of America had reached 147,000 members by 1918. Hadassah completed this first circle. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]

A second step out were bodies created or coordinated by those organizations. The American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine were both organized by the Zionist executive Emanuel Neumann, and a historian has described them as "hardly autonomous." The United Jewish Appeal was run by Henry Montor. The Sonneborn Institute, with its front company Materials for Palestine, was set up at Ben-Gurion's meeting in 1945, linking fundraising to arms. [cite:hnn_clark_truman]

The third step reached political and financial endpoints. In Congress they included Senator Robert Wagner, chairman of the American Palestine Committee, and Senator Robert Taft, co-sponsor of the Palestine resolution; Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and Representative Hamilton Fish III, sponsors of the 1922 resolution; and Representative Sol Bloom, chairman of the Foreign Affairs Committee and a target of telegram campaigns. [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation] In the White House they included the aides David Niles, Clark Clifford and Max Lowenthal, and Truman's friend Eddie Jacobson; aides and friend alike carried the Zionist case to the President. [cite:wiki_david_niles] Among donors they included Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann, whose $100,000 for Truman's 1948 campaign is documented, and the tour of 1948 that raised about $50 million through Montor's network.

Two loops stand out in the structure, and they are the clearest finding of the map. The first might be called the commitment loop. AZEC collected legislators' statements of support, printed them in its book America and Palestine, and mailed the book to every member of Congress. It then used the book to press for votes and platform planks, which produced new statements, which fed the next round. Public statements became leverage for further statements.

The second is the Montor loop. Henry Montor gathered the donors for the 1945 Sonneborn meeting, from which the arms network formed. Montor then secured Golda Meyerson's platform for 1948, and her tour raised about $50 million, largely for arms. One fundraiser's network ran from the arms meeting of 1945 to the tour of 1948.

The map does not show a single command structure, and it should not be read as one. Some of the ties are organizational, some political, some financial, and the degree of control varied. What the map does show is that the effort was not a loose collection of sympathizers. It was a connected structure, built over decades, whose links can be named and sourced.
