The Refugees of 1948

The war of 1948 produced two outcomes that have shaped everything since: a Jewish state, and a Palestinian Arab population largely displaced from the territory that state came to hold. Palestinians call the second the Nakba, Arabic for "catastrophe." [cite:npr_hamashistory] Three questions about it have been argued over ever since, and they are best kept apart. How many people became refugees? Why did they leave? And why did they not return? The record answers the first within a range, the second only in part, and the third more clearly than is often supposed.

How many. The displacement began before the Arab armies entered. In its cablegram of 15 May 1948 the Arab League told the United Nations that "approximately over a quarter of a million of the Arab population have been compelled to leave their homes." [cite:rs_rd5_un_s745] That was the League's figure, given on the day of the invasion; an IDF intelligence estimate of June, discussed below, gave a higher one for 1 June.

The figure most often cited for the whole war comes from the United Nations itself. In a report dated at Lausanne on 19 August 1949 (document A/AC.25/Com.Tech/9), the Technical Committee of the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine adopted an estimate made by P. J. Loftus of the UN Statistical Office: about 711,000 refugees from the territory under Israeli control, a figure the committee described as "as accurate as circumstances permit." [cite:rs_rd5_unccp_1949] The same report explained why the number of people drawing relief was larger than the number of refugees: duplicate ration cards, people displaced from other areas, and destitute people who were not refugees at all. [cite:rs_rd5_unccp_1949] The warning matters, because UNRWA's later figure drew on relief rolls.

Rex Brynen's Palestinian Refugee ResearchNet at McGill University set out the range in 1996. It gives the Conciliation Commission's figure as 726,000; UNRWA's 1950 figure as 957,000; the Israeli government's past figure as "as low as 520,000"; and Palestinian researchers' upper estimate as 850,000. [cite:rs_rd5_prrn_1996] The two figures attributed to the Conciliation Commission, 711,000 in its own technical report and 726,000 in Brynen's summary, do not match, and this book gives both. A widely used round figure is approximately 700,000. [cite:npr_hamashistory]

The panel above sets that figure, about 700,000 with the UN estimate of about 711,000, beside the land and immigration numbers of the Mandate years. The range around it is wide, and each figure carries its method.

Where they went. According to Brynen, about a third of the refugees went to the West Bank, a third to Gaza, and the rest to Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and beyond. [cite:rs_rd5_prrn_1996] Gaza was then under Egyptian administration and the West Bank under Jordanian. [cite:npr_hamashistory] Lebanon received an estimated 100,000–110,000, the large majority from northern Palestine, including the Acre area, in a country of about 1.3 million people. The International Committee of the Red Cross, and from 1950 UNRWA, ran the camps that resulted. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy] The receiving states treated them differently. Jordan later gave citizenship to most Palestinians who had come after 1948, under its 1954 nationality law; Lebanon restricted their work and, much later, their right to own property; and the Arab League instructed its members not to grant citizenship, in its words "to avoid dissolution of their identity and protect their right of return." That divergence, and the arguments on both sides of it, are told in [scene:sq_s20|the section on the host countries]. [cite:jns_arab_citizenship] [cite:fmr_lebanon] The displacement became, in the words of one account, the foundational political fact around which every later Palestinian political movement organized. [cite:npr_hamashistory]

Why they left. This is the most disputed of the three questions, and the evidence has to be read with care for who produced it. The most cited single document is an intelligence report of the Israel Defense Forces from June 1948, known through the work of the Israeli historian Benny Morris and summarized by Zachary Lockman. It estimated that by 1 June 1948 about 240,000 Arabs had fled the areas of the Jewish state and about 150,000 had fled other areas. It attributed about 70% of the flight to direct Jewish military operations, 2% to psychological warfare and 2% to expulsion orders, a figure Morris considers an underestimate, and no more than 5% to evacuation orders from Arab leaders. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] Those numbers were compiled by the army that was doing the fighting, and its own share of responsibility is the largest it records.

The claim that Palestinians left on the orders of their own leaders, broadcast by Arab radio, corresponds to that last category, and the IDF's own estimate put it at 5% or less. The investigation of the radio claim itself is told in the section on independence and the Nakba.

From June 1948 the pattern changed. Morris concludes, according to Lockman, that deliberate expulsions helped create about 300,000 further refugees, but that there was no explicit, general Israeli decision to expel. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] The expulsion from Lydda and Ramla in July, after Ben-Gurion's reported words "Expel them" and Yitzhak Rabin's signed order, is the best-known case; its tolls are disputed and are set out in the section on the Arab states' war. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war]

Historians divide on what this evidence adds up to. Morris, as Avi Shlaim summarizes him, "rejected both the 'Arab order' and the 'Jewish robber-state' explanations" and treated the refugee problem as "a by-product of the war." Shlaim holds that the evidence "suggests a far higher degree of Israeli responsibility." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_ref] Walid Khalidi's reading goes further: that the flight was produced by design, under Plan Dalet, the plan discussed earlier in this chapter. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_ref] The strongest form of Morris's case is that the flight came in waves with different causes, that most of it followed fighting rather than orders, and that no general order to expel has been found. The strongest form of the opposing case is that the army's own estimate assigns the great majority of the flight to its operations, that deliberate expulsions grew from June, and that a policy need not be written as a general order to be carried out.

Why they did not return. On this point the record is plainer. "As early as June 1948," Lockman writes, "the cabinet had reached a consensus against allowing the refugees to return to their homes." [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] The decision came while the war was still being fought, and before the expulsions from Lydda and Ramla in July. Lockman also writes that a report by Yosef Weitz in October 1948 falsely blamed Arab leaders for organizing the flight. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] That is Lockman's judgment of Weitz's report; the point it carries is that the "Arab leaders' orders" explanation was being put forward inside Israel's own administration within months. One offer to settle part of the question came from an Arab government: in 1949 Syria's new military ruler, Husni al-Za'im, offered to resettle 250,000–300,000 refugees in Syria as part of a peace treaty, an offer Ben-Gurion declined and which ended with Za'im's overthrow and execution that August. [cite:tf_zaim] [cite:majalla_zaim] [cite:ecf_zaim] The United Nations' response, and the negotiations that followed, belong to the next stage: see [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194] and [scene:lau_s01|Lausanne].

The villages. How many Arab villages were emptied depends on what is counted. Morris, in 1987, counted 369. Walid Khalidi's All That Remains (1992) counted 418, excluding towns and 151 places found on other lists. Aref al-Aref counted 399; Nijm and Muammer 443; Saleh 472; and the Palestine Land Society 531 localities, counting tribal lands and towns. [cite:rs_rd5_plands_villages] The source attributes the spread to method: what counts as a village, whether towns and tribal lands are included, and which lists are merged.

What the record does not settle. No single count of the refugees commands agreement; the sources range from 520,000 to 957,000, and even the Conciliation Commission's figure is reported two ways. The share of the flight caused by deliberate expulsion, as against fighting, fear and the collapse of Palestinian society, is estimated from an army report that its own principal interpreter considers incomplete on expulsions. Whether the flight was the product of a plan or of the war is the central argument among historians, and it is not resolved here. The number of Arabs who remained inside Israel in 1948–49 has not been established in the sources gathered for this book. What is not in dispute is the outcome: by the summer of 1949 several hundred thousand Palestinian Arabs were outside the territory Israel held, and its government had decided, a year earlier, that they would not come back.
