Primaries and the Two-Party Trap

The Electoral College decides between the names on the November ballot. Two earlier filters decide whose names those are and which voters each candidate has to face. The first is the party primary, the election inside a party that picks its nominee. The second is the district map, which the next section takes up. Both filters run through the rules for counting votes that the United States uses almost everywhere: one winner per district, and the candidate with the most votes wins even without a majority. This section sets out what that counting rule does to the number of parties, who votes in the primaries that follow from it, and how often the primary, not the general election, is the contest that counts.

One winner per district, two parties per country

Political scientists call the pattern Duverger's law, after the French scholar Maurice Duverger. Where each district elects a single member by plurality, two parties tend to dominate. The pattern has two causes. The first is mechanical: a small party whose support is spread thinly across many districts wins few or no seats, however many votes it gathers nationally. The second is psychological: voters who prefer a small party often vote for the nearer of the two large ones, so as not to waste their vote or help the side they like least. Ross Perot won 19 per cent of the popular vote in 1992 and no electoral votes. Duverger himself treated the pattern as a strong tendency rather than an absolute rule; regional parties in Canada, Britain and India win seats under the same system. [cite:dt3_wiki_duverger]

American law adds a second wall. A "sore loser" law bars a candidate who sought a party's nomination and lost the primary from running in the general election for the same office as an independent or as another party's nominee. As of July 2026, 48 states had some form of such a law; Connecticut and New York were the exceptions. [cite:dt3_bp_soreloser] The effect is that the primary is usually a candidate's only route onto a competitive November ballot. A candidate who loses it cannot take the argument to the wider electorate.

The demand for something else is real but not overwhelming. In a Pew survey of 10,357 adults taken from 17 to 30 November 2025, 37 per cent said the statement "I often wish there were more political parties to choose from" described them extremely or very well. The share was 46 per cent among Democrats and Democratic leaners, 29 per cent among Republicans and Republican leaners, and 44 per cent among adults under 50 against 25 per cent among those 65 and over. [cite:dt3_pew_moreparties] Most Americans, on this measure, do not report a strong wish for more parties. A large and younger minority does, and the counting rule gives that minority very little room.

Who votes in primaries

Primaries draw far fewer voters than general elections. The Bipartisan Policy Center found that turnout of all eligible voters in the 2022 primaries was 21.3 per cent, higher than in the previous four cycles but still about one eligible adult in five. Its report found that states with more open primaries averaged 24.5 per cent turnout, semi-open states 21.5 per cent and closed states 20.7 per cent, and that top-two or top-four systems ran about three points higher. [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] The differences by system are real but small; the low level is common to all of them.

The rules for who may vote differ by state and by party. In a closed primary, state law requires a voter to be registered with a party to vote in its primary. In a semi-closed primary, voters registered with no party may choose one party's primary without joining it. In an open primary, any registered voter may take part. As of August 2026, Ballotpedia counted Republican primaries as closed in 23 states, open in 14 and semi-closed in 10, and Democratic primaries as closed in 17 states, open in 18 and semi-closed in 12; five states used top-two or top-four systems, in which all candidates appear on one primary ballot and the leading finishers advance whatever their party. [cite:dt3_bp_primarytypes] An independent voter in a state with closed primaries for both parties has no say in either nomination. In the many districts where one party's nomination settles the result, that voter has no meaningful say in who represents them.

When the primary is the election

A primary matters most where the general election is not close. The Cook Political Report measures each House district's lean with its Partisan Voting Index, which compares the district's presidential vote with the nation's. On the 2025 index, before the mid-decade redrawing described in the next section, 87 of the 435 districts fell between five points Democratic and five points Republican, the band Cook treats as swing territory, and 56 of those were held by Democrats. [cite:dt3_cook_pvi2025] In 1999 the same band held 164 districts. Cook's own analysis attributes 58 per cent of the decline since 1997 not to redistricting but to voters themselves: areas becoming steadily redder or bluer as Americans sorted by party. [cite:dt3_cook_decimated]

The redrawn 2026 maps narrowed the field further. Cook found that the median House seat moved two points toward the Republicans, to R+3, and described the new map as "less elastic", meaning fewer seats likely to change hands in response to a national swing. [cite:dt3_cook_pvi2026] In early May 2026, Bloomberg Government reported, Cook rated only 16 House seats as toss-ups and 16 more as leaning to one party; in more than 400 seats the outcome was all but settled before the campaign began, possibly the fewest competitive seats since Cook began its ratings in 1984. [cite:dt3_pw_tossups]

Put these facts together and, in most districts, the decisive vote is the dominant party's primary, held on a different day and attended by about one eligible voter in five, divided between two parties. Unite America, a group that campaigns for open primaries, estimated in October 2024 that 87 per cent of House seats had been "effectively decided" by 7 per cent of eligible voters, and that 169 members had run unopposed in the primary for a safe seat. [cite:dt3_ua_87pct] Those figures come from an advocate for change, and the page that reports them does not define "effectively decided" or show its method, so they should be read as an estimate. The direction is consistent with the neutral measures above: few competitive seats, and low primary turnout.

What is design and what is choice

Some of this is design. The plurality rule, single-member districts and sore loser laws are written into statute and state constitutions, and other democracies with proportional representation or two-round systems do not produce the same two-party squeeze. [cite:dt3_wiki_duverger] Each of these rules can be changed by the same route that made it: a state legislature, a ballot measure or, for congressional elections, an act of Congress. The reforms that would change them, from open and top-two primaries to ranked ballots and multi-member districts, are tested in [scene:dt6_how_it_counts|Part Six] and [scene:dt7_structure_fixes|Part Seven].

Some of it is not design. Cook's finding that most of the loss of swing seats comes from voters clustering by party means that no map-drawing reform could restore the competitive House of the 1990s on its own. [cite:dt3_cook_decimated] And the low turnout is partly a choice: in open-primary states, where anyone may vote, about three eligible voters in four still stayed home in 2022. [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] These facts limit how much any rule change can promise, and the book returns to them when it weighs the reforms. What the record does establish is narrower and firmer: under the rules now in force, a small share of the electorate settles most House seats, and the law in most states keeps the losers of that contest off the ballot that everyone else sees.
