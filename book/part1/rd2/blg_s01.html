By What Right: How a British Letter Became International Law

The peace conference that set the King–Crane report aside also wrote the rule under which Palestine would be governed. Any account of the Mandate for Palestine has to answer a prior question: by what authority did Britain act? The familiar answer treats the Balfour Declaration as a lawful grant, made by the rightful authority over the country. The documents describe a longer and less tidy process. A letter of policy issued by one government in wartime was written into an international instrument only some five years later, after the victors of the war had built the machinery to carry it, and the moment that instrument took legal effect is itself disputed. That instrument contained its own rule about the wishes of the people it governed, and whether the Palestine Mandate kept that rule is a question lawyers and historians still dispute.

Begin with the date. On 2 November 1917, when Arthur Balfour signed his letter, Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire. British forces had taken Beersheba on 31 October. They would take Jerusalem only on 9 December. [cite:w_sinai_palestine] Britain was promising a territory that it neither held nor governed. The declaration was a statement of policy by one government, and it had no legal effect over land that government did not rule. [cite:w_balfour_decl] Its wording began with a draft formula that Lord Walter Rothschild sent to Balfour on 18 July 1917, after months of drafting with Zionist leaders; the final text was negotiated and drafted by others. It was addressed to him for two reasons the sources give: his standing as the most prominent figure in British Jewry, and his presidency of the British Zionist Federation, to which the letter was to be passed. [cite:w_balfour_decl] [cite:balfour_letter] The drafting and the choice of addressee are told in [scene:s06|the section on the three wartime promises]; the occupation that followed in December 1917 belongs to the story of Allenby's entry into Jerusalem.

What turned the letter into law was a new piece of machinery: the mandate system of the League of Nations. Its charter was Article 22 of the League's Covenant. The League Commission's final report containing the article was approved on 28 April 1919, during the Paris Peace Conference, and the Covenant entered into force on 10 January 1920 together with the treaty of peace with Germany. [cite:rs_a22_frus]

Article 22 set out its purpose in the language of trusteeship. Its first paragraph declared that "the well-being and development of such peoples form a sacred trust of civilisation." Its second provided that "the tutelage of such peoples should be entrusted to advanced nations." [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] The phrase "sacred trust" came from here. In the Covenant's own terms, the well-being of such peoples was a trust, and the Mandatory administered under supervision. Paragraph 7 required each Mandatory to render "an annual report" to the League Council, and paragraph 9 created "a permanent Commission" to examine those reports. [cite:rs_a22_lon1945]

The fourth paragraph dealt with the Arab provinces of the defeated empire, and it is the one on which the whole argument turns. Communities formerly belonging to the Turkish Empire, it said, had reached a stage where "their existence as independent nations can be provisionally recognised subject to the rendering of administrative advice and assistance by a Mandatory." It added: "The wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration in the selection of the Mandatory." [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] [cite:rs_a22_frus] [cite:avalon_covenant] These became known as the "A" mandates: communities whose existence as independent nations could, in the Covenant's words, be provisionally recognised, subject to a Mandatory's advice and assistance.

The "A" mandates were allocated by the Allied powers at San Remo on 25 April 1920. They covered Palestine, Trans-Jordan, and Syria and Lebanon; Iraq was also designated an "A" mandate and was admitted to the League in 1932. In all, 15 mandates were created over 3 former Ottoman and 7 former German territories. [cite:rs_a22_frus] Britannica lists Iraq, Syria, Lebanon and Palestine as Class A mandates and states that they all "achieved full independence by 1949." [cite:rs_a22_britannica] The League Council then approved the text of the Mandate for Palestine, with Balfour's language set into its preamble, conditionally on 24 July 1922, pending a France–Italy agreement on Syria. The League recorded the Mandate as in force on 29 September 1923, a date John Quigley disputes. [cite:rs_md_lon1923] [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] The articles of that text, and the dispute over its date, are examined in the section on the Mandate itself. Here the point is the sequence: legal force came from the victors, through San Remo and through the League, and it came after the letter, not with it.

The mechanism matters because it shows who could act and who could not. The Allied powers chose the Mandatory. The League Council approved the terms and received the reports. The Mandatory governed. The communities whose wishes Article 22 called "a principal consideration" were not among the actors in these steps; according to the UN study discussed below, Palestinians were never consulted on the choice of Mandatory. [cite:rs_a22_un_origins]

The man whose name the policy carried was candid about this. In a memorandum of 1919 Balfour wrote that "in Palestine we do not propose even to go through the form of consulting the wishes of the present inhabitants." [cite:w_balfour_decl] Speaking to the League in 1922, he described a mandate as "a self-imposed limitation by the conquerors on the sovereignty which they exercised over the conquered territory." [cite:un_league_journal_1922] On Balfour's account, then, a mandate was a limitation the conquerors placed on themselves, and in Palestine consultation was not intended.

It was not for want of evidence about what the inhabitants wanted. The objections were on the record from the start. Palestinian Arab congresses opposed the policy from 1919 onward; an American commission sent by President Wilson recorded hostility to the Zionist program in the summer of 1919, and its report was shelved, as told in [scene:pc_s01|the section on Paris in 1919]; and Britain's own upper house objected. [cite:stausaus_1922_lords] On 21 February 1922 the Palestine Arab Delegation in London invoked Article 22 directly, demanding a "national independent Government." [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700] The movement behind that delegation, and Britain's answers to it, are traced in the section on the Palestinian national movement.

The objection inside Parliament came in June 1922, on a date the sources give differently. The House of Lords voted 60 to 29 for Lord Islington's motion that the Mandate in its present form was unacceptable. [cite:stausaus_1922_lords] [cite:spectator_1922_lords] The Spectator called it "a significant defeat of the Government." [cite:rs_mca_spectator_1922] A PalQuest biography of Musa Kazim al-Husseini dates the vote to 20 June 1922; The Spectator of 24 June places it on the Wednesday of that week, which was 21 June. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:rs_mca_spectator_1922] The government did not change course. On 4 July 1922 the House of Commons voted to support its policy, by a margin reported as 292 to 35. [cite:rs_mca_langworth] [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] Across the Atlantic, Congress took up the Balfour policy after hearings that included anti-Zionist Jewish and Palestinian Arab witnesses. [cite:jps_1922_congress] The House passed a resolution endorsing it on 30 June 1922, the Senate passed it by unanimous consent on 13 July, and President Harding signed it on 21 September. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] What that resolution did and did not commit the United States to belongs to the American side of this story.

Was the Palestine Mandate consistent with the Covenant that created it? Three positions are on the record, and each rests on real evidence.

The first holds that it was not. On this reading Palestine was an "A" community like its neighbours, whose provisional independence and whose wishes Article 22 protected, and the Mandate's national-home provisions, together with the absence of any consultation, departed from the article. The United Nations study The Origins and Evolution of the Palestine Problem states that Palestine "was in no manner excluded" from the class "A" provisions and that Palestinians were never consulted on the choice of Mandatory. [cite:rs_a22_un_origins] John Quigley, in Britain and Its Mandate over Palestine (Anthem Press, 2022), goes further: he argues that Article 22 gave the League Council no power to distribute territory at all, and that the Mandate, drafted by Zionists, ignored the population's wishes in a manifest violation of the article. [cite:rs_a22_quigley_review] The Palestine Arab Delegation made the same argument in 1922, from the text of the Covenant itself. [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700]

The second holds that the Palestine Mandate was meant to be different, and was lawfully so. Those who argue this cite the League itself. The League of Nations' own study of 1945 described the Palestine Mandate as "of a very special character": it followed the "A" framework but added provisions to carry out the Balfour Declaration. [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] The legal scholar Matthijs de Blois, writing in the Israel Law Review in 2016, argues that the Mandate was unique in naming the Jewish people, wherever they lived, as its beneficiaries, and that its specific obligations to them are its defining legal feature. He also records that Palestinian Arab representatives rejected it as incompatible with self-determination. [cite:rs_a22_deblois] On this view the Mandate was not a misreading of Article 22 but a deliberate special case, approved by the same Council that administered the article.

A third, middle position is attributed to Ralph Wilde: Palestine remained within the mandates system, and those provisions of the Mandate that purported to vary Article 22 had no legal effect. [cite:rs_a22_quigley_review] On this reading the Covenant's protections for the inhabitants survived, whatever the Mandate's own text said.

Some things in this debate are not in dispute. The text of Article 22 is fixed, and it does say that the wishes of the former Ottoman communities "must be a principal consideration in the selection of the Mandatory." [cite:avalon_covenant] Balfour's own words show that the British government did not intend to consult the inhabitants of Palestine. [cite:w_balfour_decl] And both sides agree that the Palestine Mandate differed from the other "A" mandates: the League itself said it added provisions to carry out the Balfour Declaration, and de Blois calls it unique in naming the Jewish people, wherever they lived, as beneficiaries. [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] [cite:rs_a22_deblois] One side calls that difference a violation; the other calls it the Mandate's defining purpose.

What the record does not settle is the legal question itself. The sources gathered for this book record no ruling, by a court or by the League Council, on whether the Palestine Mandate conformed to Article 22. The answer depends on a prior judgment about where authority lay: in the Covenant's general rule, or in the Council's power to approve a special text. The first reading makes the Mandate a breach of the trust it claimed to carry out. The second makes it the lawful fulfilment of a promise the victors had chosen to make. What can be said with certainty is how the letter acquired legal force. It did so through the decisions of the victorious powers and the League, over the recorded objections of Palestinian Arab congresses, of an American commission and of Britain's own House of Lords.
