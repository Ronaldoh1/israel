As Taught: The Promise in Standards, Textbooks and Tests

Most Americans never read The Epic of America. They met the Dream in school, in a form nobody would call a dream: as a set of plain statements about how the economy works. Taken together, those statements make a chain. Work and education raise what a person can earn; earnings, saved and borrowed against, buy a home; the home and the savings carry a family through illness and old age; and each generation, starting a little higher, does better than the last. No single document states the whole chain. Each link is taught somewhere, and this section sets out where, in the documents' own words.

The economics standards

The best-known national statement of what American schoolchildren should learn about the economy is the Council for Economic Education's Voluntary National Content Standards in Economics, whose second edition appeared in 2010. Its Standard 13, on income, reads: "Income for most people is determined by the market value of the productive resources they sell. What workers earn primarily depends on the market value of what they produce." [cite:ad1_cee2010] In plain terms, your pay reflects what your work is worth to buyers. Standard 15 adds the second link: investment in machinery and technology, "and in the health, education, and training of people," stimulates economic growth "and can raise future standards of living." [cite:ad1_cee2010] Economists call skills and education "human capital," because, like a machine, they cost something to acquire and raise what a person can produce afterwards.

The standards are voluntary, but the same ideas appear in the framework published in 2013 to guide states writing their own standards, the National Council for the Social Studies' College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework. Its high-school economics indicator D2.Eco.13 asks students to explain why "investments in capital goods and human capital increase economic growth and standards of living." [cite:ad1_c3] A widely used college textbook, N. Gregory Mankiw's Principles of Economics, opens with ten principles, the eighth of which is that a country's standard of living depends on its production: the more goods and services a country produces, the higher its standard of living. [cite:ad1_mankiw]

Read carefully, these statements are narrower than the promise built from them. Standard 13 says "most people" and "primarily," not everyone and always. Mankiw's principle is about a country's average, not about any particular family. None of these documents says that productivity gains will be shared evenly, that a given worker's pay will rise with the economy's output, or that hard work guarantees a home. Those are the inferences a student, or a politician, draws from them. That distinction runs through the whole book: where a mechanism behaves as these standards describe, the taught version holds; where the record departs from them, the question is whether the standards were wrong or whether they were stretched to claim more than they said.

One state's version: Texas

State standards make the same points with more emphasis. In Texas, the required high-school economics course is titled "Economics with Emphasis on the Free Enterprise System and Its Benefits." Under the standards adopted in 2010, students must understand "the basic characteristics and benefits of a free enterprise system" and explain "the benefits of the U.S. free enterprise system, including individual freedom of consumers and producers." [cite:ad1_tx_teks] The title was not invented by the State Board of Education: it dates from 1997 and comes from wording the legislature wrote into the Texas Education Code. When the board approved the revised economics standards in May 2010, the vote was 14 to 0, the only part of that year's contested social studies revision to win support from both parties. [cite:ad1_edweek_tx] The same Texas course asks students to "analyze the costs and benefits of personal financial decisions," a strand that has grown nationally. [cite:ad1_tx_teks]

That growth is one of the clearest recent changes in how the Dream is taught. As of 22 October 2026, 30 states guarantee that every student takes a personal finance course before graduating from high school, 11 with the requirement fully in force and 19 still phasing it in; in 2022 the figure was 17. [cite:ad1_ngpf] Budgeting, saving and avoiding high-interest debt are useful skills, and later parts show how much an individual decision about a loan or a credit card can cost. But the shift also moves the lesson from how the system works to how an individual should navigate it, which is the emphasis of Lincoln's 1859 sentence about fault. [scene:ad1_the_phrase|Lincoln's ladder]

History class: the postwar boom as the model

Students who take Advanced Placement United States History meet the material version of the Dream at its peak. The College Board's course framework, in its unit on 1945 to 1980, asks students to "Explain the causes of economic growth in the years after World War II," and gives the answer as a key concept: "A burgeoning private sector, federal spending, the baby boom, and technological developments helped spur economic growth." A companion concept states that increasing social mobility encouraged the migration of the middle class to the suburbs. [cite:ad1_apush_ced] That is an accurate summary of what happened, and it matters for how the Dream is understood. The generation born around 1940 is the one for whom the taught chain worked most completely, and their experience became the benchmark against which later generations measure their own. [scene:ad2_what_holds|Where the Dream holds]

The civics test and Saturday-morning civics

The most compact official statement of the American economic creed is the one immigrants learn. The United States Citizenship and Immigration Services civics test, in its 2025 version of 128 questions, asks: "What is the economic system of the United States?" The accepted answers are "Capitalism" and "Free market economy." Another question asks in which founding document the words "Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness" appear, and a third asks why the colonists came to America, with "Economic opportunity" among the accepted answers. [cite:ad1_uscis2025] The phrase "American Dream" itself does not appear in the test; its parts do.

For several generations of children, civics also came set to music. Schoolhouse Rock! began running between ABC's Saturday-morning cartoons in January 1973; its America Rock series of 1975–76 produced "I'm Just a Bill," and a Money Rock series on personal finance was added when the programme was revived in 1993. The songs were added to the Library of Congress's National Recording Registry in 2018. [cite:ad1_loc_shr] Whatever else they taught, they taught that the system was knowable, orderly and fair to those who learned its rules.

What Americans now believe

The taught version is no longer the believed version for most adults. In a Wall Street Journal and NORC poll of 1,527 adults taken from 10 to 23 July 2025, 31 per cent said the American Dream still holds true, 46 per cent said it once did but no longer does, and 23 per cent said it never did. Only 25 per cent said they had a good chance of improving their standard of living, the lowest in that question's history since 1987, and 78 per cent were not confident their children's generation would be better off than their own. [cite:ad1_wsjnorc25] An AP-NORC poll of 2,596 adults taken from 16 to 20 April 2026, defining the Dream as the belief that if you work hard you will get ahead, found almost the same: a third said it still holds, half that it once did, and 15 per cent that it never had. [cite:ad1_apnorc26]

The same poll shows how much belief depends on who is asked. Republicans were more than three times as likely as Democrats to say the Dream still holds (57 per cent against 17), adults over 60 twice as likely as adults under 30 (46 against 22), and white adults about twice as likely as Black adults (40 against 19), with Black adults more likely to say it had never been true. Americans born abroad were about as likely as those born in the United States to say it still holds. [cite:ad1_apnorc26] The partisan split was already visible a year earlier: in July 2025, about 55 per cent of Republicans and 90 per cent of Democrats gave a negative outlook for themselves and their children. Gaps of this kind can move with which party holds the White House. [cite:ad1_wsjnorc25] Why belief moves with party, age and race, and why it survives at all where the data are discouraging, is the subject of [scene:ad16_belief_vs_data|Part Sixteen].

The taught version, in short, is not a caricature. It rests on real documents that say defensible things, and on a postwar generation for whom it was close to literal fact.
