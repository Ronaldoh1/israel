The Case That Iran Has Avoided War

Ron's thesis is that Iran does not want a war with the United States, and that the United States has nonetheless been drawn into one. This part tests the first half of that claim; Part Fourteen tests the second. The question is about intent, and intent cannot be read directly from any document. What can be examined is conduct and words: whether Iran's government has negotiated with Washington, how it has answered American attacks, what its leaders have said on the record, and how analysts who study its military doctrine read the pattern. This section sets out the evidence for the thesis. The next sets out the evidence against it, and then weighs the two. Where a reading of the evidence is an argument rather than a fact, it is labelled as one.

Negotiation

The first offer is the most disputed. In May 2003, weeks after the American invasion of Iraq, the Swiss ambassador in Tehran, Tim Guldimann, faxed the State Department a one-page outline with a cover memo dated 4 May. According to documents later released and reported by Gareth Porter of Inter Press Service, it was drafted by Guldimann with the Iranian diplomat Sadeq Kharrazi and set out a possible agenda for comprehensive talks in which Iran would consider compromises on its nuclear programme, on its support for Hezbollah and Hamas, and on "acceptance of the two-states-approach" for Israel and the Palestinians. Kharrazi reportedly said that the Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, President Mohammad Khatami and the foreign minister had agreed to "85%-90%" of the text. Flynt Leverett, a former National Security Council official, said it reached the administration; Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice later testified that she never saw it. [cite:ir12_ips_bargain] The document's authority is contested. State Department officials argued that Guldimann had acted on his own, and the analyst Michael Rubin, a former Pentagon official writing in National Review, calls the "grand bargain" story false, citing the deputy secretary of state Richard Armitage's reported view that the paper was "perhaps more the product of Guldimann" and an email in which, Rubin says, the Iranian diplomat Mohammad Javad Zarif denied that it originated in Tehran. [cite:ir12_nr_rubin] The public record shows that the paper existed and that Washington did not answer it; it does not settle whether Iran's leadership stood behind every line.

The second negotiation is not in dispute. Talks between Iran and six powers, which began in 2013, produced the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action in July 2015, endorsed unanimously by the UN Security Council in Resolution 2231. [cite:ir7_usip_2231] On 16 January 2016 the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) reported that Iran "has carried out all measures required" to begin implementation. [cite:ir7_iaea_impday] Part Seven tells how the deal was made and how the United States left it in 2018 ([scene:ir7_deal|the deal]; [scene:ir7_withdrawal|the withdrawal]). After the American withdrawal Iran negotiated again: talks in Vienna to revive the agreement began in April 2021 and ran until the European Union tabled a "final" text on 8 August 2022, which Iran answered with demands for guarantees on sanctions and the IAEA's open investigations. [cite:ir7_afp_vienna] That round failed, and both governments blamed the other.

The third round of talks was direct in substance if not in form. On 12 April 2025, in Muscat, an Iranian delegation under Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and an American one under the envoy Steve Witkoff sat in separate rooms while Oman's foreign minister, Badr Albusaidi, carried messages between them; at the end the two men spoke for several minutes, and Iran called the meeting "constructive". [cite:ir12_aj_muscat2025] A second round followed in Rome on 19 April, with Oman stating the aim as a deal that would keep Iran free of nuclear weapons and of sanctions. [cite:ir12_om_rome2025] After a fifth round in Rome on 23 May, which Albusaidi said made "some but not conclusive progress", Oman announced on 12 June that a sixth would be held in Muscat on Sunday 15 June. [cite:ir12_too_sixth] Israel began striking Iran on 13 June, and the sixth round never took place. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] [cite:ir12_euronews_feb26]

The pattern repeated in 2026. Iran and the United States reopened talks in Muscat on 6 February, moving to Geneva the following week. [cite:ir12_olj_feb2026] After a round in Geneva on 26 February, the Omani mediators reported "significant progress" and Araghchi said the talks had "entered into the elements of an agreement very seriously", with another round expected in "perhaps less than a week". The gap remained wide: Washington wanted an end to enrichment, limits on long-range missiles and an end to support for armed groups, while Iran said it would discuss only the nuclear file, and President Trump had given Tehran 15 days. [cite:ir12_euronews_feb26] Two days later, on 28 February, the United States and Israel opened their joint campaign against Iran. [cite:ow7_npr_epicfury] [cite:hz8_wiki_iranwar] Iran negotiated during that war too: a ceasefire from 8 April led to the Islamabad Memorandum of 17 June, which collapsed on 8 July [cite:ow7_wiki_ceasefire2026]; as of mid-October 2026 an Iranian ceasefire proposal of late September had been publicly rejected by Trump while contacts continued through mediators. [cite:hz12a_cbs_oct]

Calibrated retaliation

Twice Iran has struck American forces directly in answer to an American attack, and both times the strike was announced in advance. On 3 January 2020 a US drone killed Major General Qassem Soleimani, commander of the Revolutionary Guards' Quds Force, at Baghdad airport; the Pentagon called it "decisive defensive action" and Khamenei promised "harsh retaliation". [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] [cite:hz10_fox_pentagon2020] Shortly after midnight on 8 January, the office of Iraq's prime minister, Adil Abdul-Mahdi, received what it called a "verbal message" from Iran that its response had begun or was about to begin and would hit only places where American forces were present. Iraq's military counted 22 missiles, 17 at Ain al-Asad air base and five at Erbil. [cite:ir12_aj_iraqwarn] Within hours Zarif, by then foreign minister, wrote that Iran had "took & concluded proportionate measures in self-defense" and that "We do not seek escalation or war, but will defend ourselves against any aggression." [cite:ir12_spg_zarif] No one was killed. The attack was not harmless: President Trump first said that no Americans had been harmed, but by 21 February 2020 the Pentagon had diagnosed 110 service members with traumatic brain injuries, all classed as mild. [cite:ir12_rferl_tbi]

The second case followed the American bombing of Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan on 21–22 June 2025. On 23 June Iran fired at the American base at Al Udeid in Qatar, after notifying the United States. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] Trump said 14 missiles were fired and 13 shot down, and added: "I want to thank Iran for giving us early notice, which made it possible for no lives to be lost, and nobody to be injured." [cite:ir12_ahram_udeid] Ali Vaez, Iran project director at the International Crisis Group, said that day that Iran "opted for a symbolic response to a US attack because they don't want further US involvement". [cite:ir12_c4_vaez]

What Iran's leaders said

Iran's leaders have repeatedly said, in public, that they do not seek a war with the United States, while refusing the talks Washington wanted. On 14 May 2019, at the height of the "maximum pressure" campaign, Khamenei told officials in Tehran: "There will be no war," and "Neither us, nor them are after a war"; in the same speech he called negotiation with Washington a mistake because "the Americans will not stand by their promises." [cite:ir12_rf_nowar] Zarif's statement of 8 January 2020, quoted above, said the same in the moment of retaliation. These are statements of position. They show what the government chose to say; they do not by themselves prove what it intended.

How analysts read the doctrine

The argument that Iran avoids direct war rests on a reading of its military doctrine. The Iranian scholar Kayhan Barzegar describes what Iranian officials call "forward defence" as "preempting the penetration of symmetric and asymmetric threats inside Iran's borders": fighting through allied forces abroad so as not to fight at home. A New America study of the Revolutionary Guards found Iran's military strategy still "cautious". The same study records the opposite reading: the analyst Patrick Clawson described Iran as both "the fireman and the arsonist" in Iraq and Syria, and Iran's regional rivals see the doctrine as ideological expansion rather than defence. [cite:ir12_na_forward] Vaez, who read the 2025 strike as symbolic, warned in February 2026 that Iran's leaders had concluded that only inflicting casualties would deter American and Israeli strikes, that the government was "cornered and could act in a reckless way", and that it regarded conflict as less dangerous than accepting Washington's terms. [cite:ir12_npr_vaez2026] That is the strongest form of the thesis: by the analysts' account, Iran's government has tried to avoid war with the United States, but not at any price.
