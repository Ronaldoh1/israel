Jubilees, Meqabyan, 4 Baruch and the Books of Order

Enoch is the most famous of Ethiopia's own books, but it is not alone. Three more works in the narrower canon appear in no other major church's Bible: Jubilees, a retelling of Genesis and Exodus given to Moses by an angel; the three books of Meqabyan, which share a name with the Maccabees but tell different stories; and 4 Baruch, a tale of the Babylonian exile. [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The broader canon adds books of church order. This section summarises each, with the same questions as the rest of Book Two: what the text is, what it teaches, who set it aside elsewhere and what scholars dispute.

Jubilees: what it is

Jubilees calls itself "the history of the division of the days of the law and of the testimony", and it presents everything that follows as revealed on Mount Sinai during Moses's forty days on the mountain. [cite:vt27_ccel_jub1] [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] God tells "the angel of the presence" to "Write for Moses from the beginning of creation", and the angel dictates a history from the first day to the giving of the law. [cite:vt27_ccel_jub1] It was written in Hebrew, probably around 160–150 BCE. About fifteen copies were found in five caves at Qumran, which shows how widely the community there read it. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] No complete Hebrew, Greek or Latin text survives; a Latin translation covers about a quarter of the book, and only the Ge'ez version is complete, a translation that agrees closely with the Hebrew fragments from the Dead Sea. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] It has 50 chapters and is canonical for the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches and for the Beta Israel. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees]

Jubilees: the outline

Jubilees: what it teaches

Time is the book's first subject. History runs in jubilees of 49 years, fifty of them from creation to Sinai, 2,410 years in all, so that Israel's entry into its land completes a perfect cycle. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] The year has 364 days in four quarters of thirteen weeks, the same calendar as Enoch's Astronomical Book, and the author rejects the lunar calendar "in the strongest terms", because a moon-based year lets feasts drift and so profanes them. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] [cite:vt27_je_jubilees]

The second subject is a law older than Moses. Jubilees teaches that every event and statute is "written down on the tablets of heaven", and that the patriarchs already kept laws that Exodus and Leviticus later give: the Feast of Weeks was celebrated in heaven from creation and first kept on earth by Noah, then by Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. [cite:vt27_je_jubilees] The highest angels were created circumcised and keep the Sabbath in heaven, and Israel shares both with them. [cite:vt27_je_jubilees] The third subject is evil. The Watchers' story is told again, and after the Flood a tenth of the giants' spirits are allowed to remain under their chief, Mastema, to test humankind; in the Passover account Mastema stands where Exodus has God himself, as the power behind the killing of Egypt's firstborn. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] [cite:vt27_je_jubilees] The book also insists on separation from the nations, forbidding intermarriage and eating with gentiles, and condemns public nakedness. [cite:vt27_je_jubilees]

Elsewhere Jubilees fell away. The Gelasian Decree, the sixth-century Latin list of books not to be received, named "Little Genesis", one of Jubilees' ancient titles, among the apocrypha. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt23_wiki_jubilees] Eastern Orthodox, Catholic and Protestant churches classify it as pseudepigrapha; no Jewish community except the Beta Israel treats it as scripture. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] Scholars dispute its exact date (estimates have ranged from the 160s BCE to the reign of John Hyrcanus later in the century), its relationship to the group at Qumran, and whether its calendar was ever actually used in the Temple. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] [cite:vt27_je_jubilees]

Meqabyan: not the Maccabees

The three books of Meqabyan are the clearest case of a book that is easy to mislabel. The Greek books of the Maccabees, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles contain, tell of the Hasmonean revolt against the Seleucid king Antiochus IV in the 160s BCE. The Ethiopian canon does not include them. Its Meqabyan are "entirely different in their scope, content and subject": they do not tell the Hasmonean story, the martyrdom of the seven brothers and their mother, or the rededication of the Temple. [cite:vt27_wiki_meqabyan] Cowley noted that they "are not the same as any of the 4 LXX books of Maccabees". [cite:vt27_cowley1974] They were written in Ge'ez, probably in the high medieval period, since the Ethiopian church seems not to have known the Greek Maccabees until well into the fourteenth century. [cite:vt27_wiki_meqabyan] Little scholarly work has been done on them; a full English translation of all three appeared only in 2008 (Feqade Selassie), and an annotated translation of the first two by D. P. Curtin and Tesfaye in 2018 and 2023. [cite:vt27_wiki_meqabyan] One popular version describes them as the story of "three Ethiopian brothers" martyred in Ethiopia; they are not set in Ethiopia. [cite:vt27_wiki_meqabyan]

Scholars have suggested that the king's name, Tseerutsaydan, may preserve a folk memory of Antiochus IV, whose coins bore the names of Tyre and Sidon, and that Akrandis may echo Alexander Balas, a later Seleucid ruler; others note that the second and third books may draw on the Josippon. These are proposals, not findings. [cite:vt27_wiki_meqabyan] Meqabyan was never in any Western list to be excluded: it was written in Ethiopia, centuries after the Western canon lists were made.

4 Baruch: the sleeper and the eagle

4 Baruch is known in Greek manuscripts as the Paralipomena of Jeremiah, "things left out of Jeremiah", and in Ge'ez as the Rest of the Words of Baruch. [cite:vt27_wiki_4baruch] God tells Jeremiah that Jerusalem will fall; Jeremiah and Baruch see angels open the gates to the Babylonians; Jeremiah hides the Temple vessels in the earth and goes into exile with the people, while Baruch stays behind. Abimelech, the Ethiopian servant who rescued Jeremiah in the Book of Jeremiah (Ebed-melech), falls asleep under a tree and sleeps for 66 years, waking to find the figs in his basket still fresh. An eagle carries Baruch's letter and some of the figs to Jeremiah in Babylon, lands on a corpse at a funeral and brings it back to life, a sign that the exile is ending. The people return, but only those without foreign spouses may cross the Jordan. [cite:vt27_wiki_4baruch]

The book teaches that God keeps what is his through catastrophe, that the righteous are preserved as the figs were, and that the return from exile is a kind of resurrection. Its 66 years point scholars to about 136 CE, sixty-six years after the destruction of the Second Temple, which makes it a Jewish response to that loss; its last chapter, with Christian content, appears to be an addition made in the Christian era. [cite:vt27_wiki_4baruch] Only the Ethiopian church counts it as scripture. [cite:vt27_wiki_4baruch]

The books of order in the broader canon

The eight New Testament additions of the broader canon are not narratives or prophecies but rule books for the church. The Sinodos is a collection of canons attributed to the apostles and to early councils, and it is the source the Fetha Negest cites for its own canon of scripture. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The Didascalia, or Didesqelya, is a church order in 43 chapters, close to the first seven books of the Greek Apostolic Constitutions, a fourth-century compilation of church law; the Ethiopic Clement is a letter presented as Peter's teaching sent to Clement of Rome; and the Book of the Covenant is a church-order work in two parts. [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] [cite:vt27_eotc_books] These books govern fasting, ordination, worship and discipline. Cowley found that in practice they function more as authoritative commentary than as scripture read alongside the gospels. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] Their presence in the canon shows something the Western division between "Bible" and "church law" hides: in Ethiopia the rules of the community were counted among its holy books.
