The Mystics: The Kingdom Within, on Trial

The thread that runs through this platform's reading of the Gnostic texts, from the Gospel of Thomas to the Gospel of Mary, is the "kingdom within": the claim that God can be known directly, inside the person, without a priest standing in between. [scene:vt25_sayings_gospels|Part Twenty-Five] and [scene:vt26_gospel_of_mary|Part Twenty-Six] showed where that idea appears in the ancient texts. This section follows it inside the Latin Church, where it returned through laywomen, friars and priests. For each, the record shows what they taught, what the institution condemned, and in which document. [cite:vt29_wiki_porete] [cite:vt29_mckay_inagro] [cite:vt29_wiki_alumbrados] [cite:vt29_wiki_molinos]

Beguines and Marguerite Porete

Beguines were laywomen who lived, alone or in groups, a life of prayer, work and charity without taking the vows of a nun. That freedom was the problem in the eyes of their critics. The Council of Vienne's decree on them, later known by its opening words Cum de quibusdam, complained that they "promise obedience to no one", renounce no property and follow no approved rule, and that some of them "argue and preach on the holy Trinity and the divine essence". It forbade their way of life under automatic excommunication, while exempting devout women living penitent lives in hospices. [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne]

Marguerite Porete, born around 1258 probably in Hainaut, was a Beguine who wrote in Old French, probably in the 1290s, The Mirror of Simple Souls. It is a dialogue between Love, Reason, the Virtues and the Soul in the language of courtly romance. Its subject is the "annihilated soul": a soul that has given up everything except God, including its own will, through love. The book has Love say "I am God, says Love, for Love is God and God is Love". [cite:vt29_wiki_porete]

The bishop of Cambrai, Gui de Collemedio, condemned the book and had it burned in her presence at Valenciennes sometime between 1296 and 1306. She kept circulating it. Arrested around 1308, she was handed to the Dominican inquisitor William of Paris and refused to take the oath or answer any of his questions. In 1310 a commission of twenty-one theologians of Paris judged fifteen propositions drawn from the book heretical. As a relapsed heretic, one who had returned to an error already condemned, she was handed to the secular authorities and burned on the Place de Grève in Paris on 1 June 1310. The chronicler Guillaume de Nangis, no friend of hers, recorded that the crowd was moved to tears by her calm. [cite:vt29_wiki_porete]

The book outlived her. Extracts from it appear among the errors condemned in the Council of Vienne's decree Ad nostrum, aimed at the so-called Free Spirit. It went on circulating without her name, in Latin, Italian and Middle English, and was at one time thought to be the work of the Flemish mystic John of Ruusbroec. Only in 1946 did the Italian scholar Romana Guarnieri identify Porete as the author. [cite:vt29_wiki_porete]

Meister Eckhart: the ground of the soul

Eckhart, born around 1260, was not a marginal figure. He was a Dominican friar, a master of theology at Paris, and held senior posts in his order before teaching in Strasbourg and Cologne. In his sermons he taught that there is a "ground" in the soul where God and the soul are one; that God the Father gives birth to the Son, the Word, in that ground, in the person who has let go of everything; that this letting go, "detachment", is the way; and that beyond God as named and described lies the Godhead, which no name reaches. His best-known line runs: "The Eye with which I see God is the same Eye with which God sees me". [cite:vt29_wiki_eckhart]

In 1326 Archbishop Henry of Virneburg of Cologne opened an inquisitorial trial against him. Eckhart protested his orthodoxy, said he would retract anything shown to be in error, and appealed to Pope John XXII at Avignon. He died there, probably on 28 January 1328, before the case ended. On 27 March 1329 the pope issued the bull In agro dominico. It lists twenty-eight articles from his works: seventeen judged heretical and eleven judged "evil-sounding" and "very rash" but capable of a Catholic sense with explanation. The bull records that before his death Eckhart "absolutely and totally revoked" them and submitted his writings to the pope's judgment. [cite:vt29_mckay_inagro] [cite:vt29_wiki_eckhart]

Eckhart's readers Johannes Tauler and Henry Suso carried his teaching on within the Church, and in the 1990s the Dominican order sought his rehabilitation. A Vatican letter of 1992, made public in 2010, stated that he had never been condemned by name, only certain propositions attributed to him. [cite:vt29_wiki_eckhart]

The alumbrados: yielding to God

In Castile in the 1510s and 1520s, circles around Isabel de la Cruz, a woman from Guadalajara, and Pedro Ruiz de Alcaraz practised what they called dejamiento: yielding oneself to God's love, making no effort at methodical prayer, letting thoughts pass and doing whatever God moves one to do. Alcaraz put it in one sentence: "El amor de Dios en un hombre es Dios", the love of God in a man is God. It differed from the recogimiento, the disciplined, step-by-step prayer of recollection taught by the Franciscan Francisco de Osuna, which the Church accepted. [cite:vt29_hughes_cazalla] Critics called the dejados alumbrados, "the enlightened". In the Inquisition's summary they held that the soul could reach a perfection in which it saw God in this life, that outward worship and sacraments were superfluous, and that sin was impossible for a soul in union with God. [cite:vt29_wiki_alumbrados]

In 1525 the Inquisitor General, Alonso Manrique, issued an edict listing forty-eight propositions drawn from the trials of Isabel de la Cruz and Alcaraz. [cite:vt29_wiki_alumbrados] One of them, number eleven, condemned the view that once a person has left everything to God, nothing more is needed to save the soul, and that fasting and works of mercy are unnecessary. [cite:vt29_hughes_cazalla] Convicted alumbrados were generally not executed; in 1529 a group at Toledo was flogged and imprisoned. The suspicion spread wide. Ignatius of Loyola, the future founder of the Jesuits, was examined at Salamanca in 1527 for alumbrado sympathies and let off with a warning. [cite:vt29_wiki_alumbrados] María de Cazalla, a follower, was arrested on 22 April 1532 and after two years of questioning and torture abjured lightly suspected heresy and was fined 100 ducats; one study compares her teaching directly with Porete's Mirror. [cite:vt29_hughes_cazalla]

Spain's great mystics of the next generation wrote under the same shadow. The Augustinian Luis de León was held in the Inquisition's prison at Valladolid from March 1572 to December 1576, chiefly for translating the Song of Songs into Spanish, and was cleared; in 1588 he published the first collected edition of the works of Teresa of Ávila. [cite:vt29_wiki_luisdeleon]

Molinos and Quietism

Miguel de Molinos, a Spanish priest in Rome, published his Spiritual Guide in Italian in 1675 with the approval of several church censors. It went through about twenty editions in twelve years. It taught that after a time of meditation the soul could be drawn into a simple "prayer of quiet", resting in God, and that the way to perfection was "interior annihilation": people must, in Britannica's summary, "banish their individual wills" so that God's will could act in them. [cite:vt29_cathen_molinos] [cite:vt29_brit_molinos] [cite:vt29_wiki_molinos] Jesuits such as Paolo Segneri answered that this would lead people to abandon meditation, the sacraments and moral effort. [cite:vt29_wiki_molinos]

He was arrested on 18 July 1685. Some twelve thousand letters from people who had sought his direction were reportedly seized. On 3 September 1687, in the Dominican church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva, he was declared a "dogmatic heretic", abjured and was sentenced to life imprisonment; on 20 November 1687 Innocent XI's bull Coelestis Pastor condemned sixty-eight propositions attributed to him, among them the annihilation of the soul's powers, the ceasing of acts of devotion and indifference to one's own salvation. He died in the prison of the Holy Office in December 1696. [cite:vt29_cathen_molinos] [cite:vt29_wiki_molinos] [cite:vt29_audit_denz_molinos] The trial also turned on charges of sexual misconduct with penitents, which, by the Catholic Encyclopedia's account, Molinos excused as acts in which his free will had no part; Britannica notes that the condemned doctrines do not appear in the Guide itself. [cite:vt29_cathen_molinos] [cite:vt29_brit_molinos]

What the four cases show

The record supports part of the thesis plainly. Each of these teachers said that God could be found in the depth of the person, and each was condemned in documents that object, among other things, to the idea that the soul in union with God no longer needs outward works, set prayers or the ordinary disciplines of the Church. [cite:vt29_wiki_porete] [cite:vt29_hughes_cazalla] [cite:vt29_wiki_molinos] The record also limits the thesis. The same Church kept reading and printing mystics who described union with God in comparable terms, and the line it drew ran at least as much through obedience as through the inner experience itself: Eckhart submitted and was not condemned by name; Porete refused to answer and was burned; the alumbrado-suspect Ignatius was warned and went on to found the Jesuits; Teresa of Ávila's works were printed by a friar the Inquisition had itself cleared. [cite:vt29_mckay_inagro] [cite:vt29_wiki_luisdeleon]

Interpretation, not finding: the kingdom within as a threat to mediation

In the platform's reading, carried in the Sophia platform's Suppressed Lineages material, these trials are one pattern repeated: whenever someone taught that the kingdom of God is within and needs no intermediary, the institution answered with a condemnation, because unmediated access makes the mediator unnecessary. Read through this lens, Porete's annihilated soul, Eckhart's birth of God in the ground, the alumbrados' yielding and Molinos's quiet are the Gospel of Thomas's saying about the kingdom spread out inside you, surfacing again in Latin Christendom. The counter-reading, set out in the cases above, is that the institution tolerated the inner way under obedience and condemned it when it was joined to claims that sin was impossible, that works and sacraments were superfluous, or that the teacher answered to no one. Both readings fit the documents; the documents alone do not decide between them. The fuller argument is weighed in [scene:vt31_two_economies|Part Thirty-One].
