"Bless Israel and Be Blessed": Genesis 12:3 and the Christian Dispute

The name Israel carries religious weight within Christianity as well as Judaism, and for a large American constituency that weight bears directly on politics. One position holds that the Bible requires Christians to support the modern State of Israel. The opposite holds that the Bible has nothing to say on the question. The record shows something else: a single verse that Christians read in sharply different ways, with the disagreement running through Christianity itself.

The verse is Genesis 12:3. In it God speaks to Abram, later called Abraham:

"I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse; and all peoples on earth will be blessed through you."

The promise of land follows in Genesis 12:7, 15:18 and 17:8. The words were spoken to Abraham. The question is whom they address today.

Christian Zionists read the verse as a standing mandate. Groups such as the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem describe it as the biblical basis for standing with Israel.[cite:icej_cz] [cite:rmc_cz] Many dispensationalist believers go further, viewing Israel's restoration as a precondition for the return of Christ. On this reading, the verse applies to the modern state, and supporting that state is a religious obligation.

Other Christian traditions read the verse differently. Covenant theology, and the theology known as amillennialism, hold that the promise to Abraham is fulfilled in Christ and the church, citing Galatians 3:16 and 3:29, and that it does not pass to a modern government.[cite:studylight_gen12] Some scholars also note that the passage stresses blessing to all nations more than it stresses land.[cite:pctii_gen12] On these readings, the Israel of Genesis and the secular state founded in 1948 are not the same thing.

In June 2025 the dispute broke into public view in an exchange between Senator Ted Cruz and Tucker Carlson. Cruz cited the verse. Carlson asked whether the political state led by Benjamin Netanyahu is the same "Israel" as the one in Scripture.[cite:think_gen12] The exchange settled nothing, but it put a theological disagreement in open political view.

Judaism, too, holds differing views on how scripture relates to the modern state. Early Zionism was largely secular, and some Haredi, or ultra-Orthodox, Jews oppose the existence of a Jewish state before the coming of the Messiah. The belief that a religious text mandates support for a particular political state is therefore not held uniformly by Christians or by Jews.

Palestinian Christians, about 1 percent of the population today, are also part of this story; this book treats their history separately.

Whether Genesis 12:3 applies to a modern state is a theological question, argued inside Christianity itself, and this book does not resolve it. What it establishes is narrower. The verse is cited for opposite conclusions, the disagreement is ongoing, and the claim that "the Bible requires it" is one tradition's reading, not a settled fact.
