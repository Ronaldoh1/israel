Enoch's Ascent and the Chariot

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The Book of Enoch, which survives complete only in Ge'ez, the language of the Ethiopian church, contains one of the oldest accounts of a human being carried up to the throne of God. In chapter 14, Enoch is lifted into heaven and comes to a great house whose floor is of crystal; beyond it is a second, greater house, whose floor "was of fire". [cite:et_cojs_enoch14] Inside he sees "a lofty throne", whose "appearance was like crystal", with "Streams of flaming fire" issuing from beneath it, and "the Great Glory was sitting upon it". [cite:et_cojs_enoch14] In R. H. Charles's older translation the same line reads: "And the Great Glory sat thereon". [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] The historian Lawrence Schiffman calls this part of the book "Enoch's hekhalot vision", a mystical vision of God's Temple "based on Ezekiel and heavily influenced by Daniel, which enriched later Jewish mystical literature." [cite:et_cojs_enoch14]

That later literature is the tradition known as Merkabah, from the Hebrew for "chariot", after the throne-chariot that the prophet Ezekiel saw in the first chapter of his book. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] Its main texts, the Hekhalot or "palaces" literature, were composed mostly between about 200 and 700, and they describe an ascent through seven heavens and seven palaces to the throne. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] At each gate stand fierce angelic guards, and the mystic needs purification and the right names and seals to pass them. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] The practitioners were called, strangely, the yordei merkavah, "those who descend to the chariot", a name that may describe a journey inward. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] And the teaching itself had an outer gate: the Mishnah ruled that the account of the chariot was not to be expounded even before one student, unless he was wise and understood it of himself. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah]

The inner reading takes the houses and palaces as layers of consciousness, each further in than the last, and the throne as the centre of the self. That is why the ascent can be called a descent: the journey up through the heavens is the journey down into the person. The guards at the gates are the same rulers the Gnostic texts set at theirs, and the names that pass them are forms of recognition. Read this way, Enoch is the first in a long line of travellers who found the holiest place by going inward, and the Ethiopian church kept his account when the West stopped copying it.

The same pattern this shelf has followed appears here in a new form. The palaces have gatekeepers; the teaching about the palaces had a gatekeeper too, in the Mishnah's rule that only the wise could hear it. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] The difference from the gates of The Canon is the test. The Mishnah's gate opened to understanding, not to office: the student had to understand it himself.

The record limits the reading. Enoch's vision is a vision of the heavenly temple, and its authors meant a real heaven; the inner reading of the houses as states of mind is the reader's. The Merkabah texts are Jewish and later than Enoch, and how directly Enoch fed them is a question for specialists. What the record does show is the line Schiffman draws: a throne vision in a book that only Ethiopia kept whole stands near the start of a mystical tradition of ascent. [cite:et_cojs_enoch14]
