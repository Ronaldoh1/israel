From Recognition to the Airlift, 1948–1975

The relationship this volume examines is built of paper: statutes, appropriations, memoranda, executive orders and letters exchanged between governments. Before the agencies are taken one by one in the parts that follow, this part sets out that paper in the order it was written, administration by administration. The timeline below is the spine of the whole volume; each later part returns to one of its points and follows it inside a single agency. This first section covers the years in which the United States moved from recognizing Israel to arming it, and in which Congress wrote the first law devoted to Israel's emergency military needs.

Recognition, 1948. On 14 May 1948 the Truman administration announced that "the United States recognizes the provisional government as the de facto authority of the new State of Israel." Full, de jure recognition followed on 31 January 1949. [cite:ow1_nara_recog] Recognition created a diplomatic relationship; it did not create a military one. Through the 1950s American aid to Israel was economic, and the United States declined to be its principal arms supplier, as earlier volumes of this library describe.

The Hawk, 1962. The first break came under President Kennedy. On 7 August 1962 Secretary of State Dean Rusk recommended offering Israel the Hawk anti-aircraft missile if no arms-limitation arrangement with Egypt looked likely within two months. Later that month the President's deputy special counsel, Myer Feldman, carried the decision to David Ben-Gurion and Golda Meir, and on 7 September a State Department circular instructed American posts to describe the Hawk as purely defensive. The sale was made on American credit, and the first batteries arrived by early 1965. [cite:ow1_kramer_hawk] Even inside the administration the decision was framed cautiously: Rusk told the British on 18 August that Feldman was "in no sense attempting to close a Hawk deal" and that a sale was open in principle "subject to the possibility of arms control arrangements in the area." [cite:ow1_frus_hood1962] The full story is told in [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision], and the first direct tank sale of 1965 in [scene:cw6_tanks|from Bonn to Washington].

The Phantoms, 1968. The Hawk was defensive; the next major sale was not. In July 1968 the Senate adopted a sense-of-Congress resolution calling on President Johnson to sell Israel supersonic aircraft, and the House later agreed. On 9 October 1968, signing the Foreign Assistance Act of 1968, Johnson said he had "taken note of the section concerning the sale of planes to Israel" and asked the Secretary of State to open negotiations. The sale of 50 F-4 Phantoms for $285 million was announced on 27 December 1968; 16 aircraft arrived in late 1969 and 34 in 1970. [cite:ow1_jvl_phantom] The negotiation carried a nuclear condition. In a letter of 22 November 1968, Ambassador Yitzhak Rabin reaffirmed that Israel "will not be the first power in the Middle East to introduce nuclear weapons" and agreed not to use American aircraft as nuclear carriers; the American reply reserved the right to cancel in "unusual and compelling circumstances." [cite:ow1_jvl_phantomconds] Part Eleven returns to how that formula became settled policy.

The airlift and the emergency law, 1973. The October 1973 war produced the first American law written specifically for Israel's military needs. From 14 October to 14 November 1973 the Air Force flew Operation Nickel Grass, delivering about 22,300 tons of equipment in 32 days. [cite:ow1_afhist_nickel] On 19 October President Nixon asked Congress for $2.2 billion in emergency security assistance for Israel. His stated reason was strategic: the United States was replacing Israeli losses to maintain "a balance of military capabilities" against "a large-scale resupply of Syria and Egypt by the Soviet Union." [cite:ow1_nixon_msg1973]

Congress passed the Emergency Security Assistance Act, Public Law 93-199, and it was signed on 26 December 1973. It authorized up to $2.2 billion for Israel as grants, credits, or both, as the President determined; amounts above $1.5 billion required a presidential finding of national interest and twenty days' notice to Congress, a condition waived if fighting resumed. Section 4 allowed the President to release Israel from paying for arms bought between 6 October 1973 and 30 June 1974. [cite:ow1_pl93_199] In practice the $2.2 billion was allocated as $1.5 billion in grants and $700 million in loans. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

The Senate vote of 20 December 1973 shows both the margin and the dissent. The bill passed 66 to 9. Before passage, Senator J. William Fulbright of Arkansas, chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, offered an amendment that would have made aid above $1.2 billion contingent on steps toward compliance with Security Council Resolution 242; the Senate tabled it 62 to 12. [cite:ow1_vv_rs587] [cite:ow1_vv_rs583] It was an early instance of a pattern Part Two follows: a proposal to attach a condition to aid for Israel, raised on the floor and set aside by a large majority.

The 1975 memorandum. The first written American commitments to Israel beyond arms sales came with the second Sinai disengagement agreement in September 1975. An accompanying US–Israel memorandum promised that, for five years, the United States would help Israel obtain oil if normal supply failed; that Washington would "view Israel's requests sympathetically, including its request for advanced and sophisticated weapons"; and that the United States would not recognize or negotiate with the Palestine Liberation Organization "so long as" it did not recognize Israel's right to exist and accept Resolutions 242 and 338. [cite:ow1_jvl_moa1975] These were executive commitments, not statutes, but the last of them bound American diplomacy for thirteen years.

By 1975, then, three features of the later relationship were in place. Arms flowed under presidential authority with congressional encouragement; Congress had written an Israel-specific emergency law that let the President forgive payment; and the executive had begun to make written political commitments to Israel outside the treaty process. [cite:ow1_pl93_199] [cite:ow1_jvl_moa1975] What did not yet exist was a standing structure. The next section follows how the Reagan years built one.
