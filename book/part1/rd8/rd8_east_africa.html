East Africa: An Offer the Movement Refused, 1903–1905

The best-known alternative was the only one a great power put formally on the table, and the one that came closest to splitting the movement.

After the Kishinev pogrom, Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain offered land for Jewish settlement in British East Africa: about 16,300 square kilometres of the Uasin Gishu plateau, in what is now Kenya, between Lake Nakuru, Kisumu and Mount Elgon. That is smaller than Mandatory Palestine (about 26,000 square kilometres). [cite:a_ea_kenya] [cite:a_wp_mandpal]

26 Aug 1903: the Sixth Zionist Congress voted 295 to 178 only to send a commission to look at it. Herzl presented it as a temporary refuge for Jews in danger in Russia, not a replacement for the Land of Israel. [cite:a_jvl_uganda] [cite:a_ea_kenya]

The three-man commission split two to one against it. [cite:a_ea_kenya]

British settlers opposed the plan; the East African Standard denounced "pauper alien Jews" turning the highlands into "Jewganda". [cite:a_ea_kenya]

The plateau was Maasai grazing land. Under agreements of 1904 and 1911 the British moved the Maasai off much of the Rift Valley and the Uasin Gishu to make room for European settlers; over a million acres had been leased or sold by the end of 1905. [cite:a_unpo_maasai] [cite:a_ea_kenya]

The Seventh Zionist Congress rejected the offer in 1905. Israel Zangwill led the minority out and founded the Jewish Territorial Organization, which searched for a territory elsewhere (Angola, Galveston and others), found none, and dissolved in 1925; in 1944 Australia vetoed the last such plan, in the Kimberley. [cite:a_wp_ito]

The likely path. At most a small self-governing settlement under British rule, opposed by the British settlers, on land taken from the Maasai. On the record the movement itself refused it, and those who did go looking for a territory found no ruler willing to give one.

The cost. Not a peaceful alternative: the land was already the Maasai's, and its settlers fought for it.
