The Arms Network in Operation, 1945–1948

Money was only half the problem: weapons still had to be found, bought and moved. The network founded at Rudolf Sonneborn's apartment in 1945, described in [scene:s07d|the Sonneborn meeting], was not a fund or a statement of intent. For three years it bought real weapons, aircraft and machinery in the United States and moved them, through a set of named front operations and transit countries, to the Haganah in Palestine. Two claims about it are common, and the record contradicts both. One is that American law enforcement knew nothing of an organized effort to arm the Haganah before 1948. The other is that the new state's founding war was armed only by its own government. The documents show federal agencies watching parts of the operation while it ran, and a large share of the material reaching Palestine from private American buyers through third countries.

The routes. The network's first problem was the law. Both the United States and Britain maintained embargoes on arms for Palestine, and the network's purchases were designed to defeat them. Academic research on the operation documents purchases of trucks, radios, aircraft parts and weapons components, funnelled through countries that were neutral in the conflict: Czechoslovakia, Panama, Mexico, Canada and France. [cite:mexico_arms] Mexico was one of the named and documented transit points, not an incidental stop. [cite:mexico_arms] The method was simple to describe and hard to police. Goods left the United States for a country that was not at war, and were re-shipped from there to Haganah operatives. The research documents the strategy and the countries; it does not give a shipment-by-shipment ledger of what went by which route. [cite:mexico_arms]

The scale. By late January 1948 the operation had already moved thousands of tons of disguised military material. Over its whole life it sent some 800 disguised shipments, roughly 2,400 tons, and most of them reached their destination before the war ended. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The volume matters for the second common claim. The weapons that the new state carried into 1948 were bought in part by American citizens, with American donations, and moved through other countries' ports; the Czechoslovak supply that began in January 1948 is the other half of that story, told in [scene:cz_s01|the Czechoslovak pipeline].

Al Schwimmer's airline. The most elaborate single operation was in the air. Al Schwimmer, a veteran of the US Army's Air Transport Command, bought roughly 30 surplus American warplanes and moved them toward Palestine via Panama, Brazil and Casablanca, evading the FBI and the State Department for months. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] The two accounts of his operation name its cover differently. One describes a fictitious Panamanian airline, Lineas Aereas de Panama, created to carry the aircraft. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] The other describes a near-bankrupt charter airline, Service Airways Inc., used as commercial cover to fly aircraft and parts out of the United States. [cite:sdjw_schwimmer] The accounts do not say whether both companies were used at different stages, and this book does not resolve the point.

The crate on the New Jersey dock. By the end of January 1948, a crate marked "used industrial machinery" broke apart while it was being loaded onto a freighter in New Jersey, spilling explosives onto the dock. It was one of 76 identical crates bound for Palestine. [cite:schwimmer_sdjw] The two accounts weigh the incident differently. In one, the accidental spill, not investigative work, gave US authorities their first concrete evidence that the network existed. [cite:schwimmer_sdjw] In the other, the FBI, the Customs Service and other federal authorities had already been uncovering parts of the network while it operated, and the spill accelerated their work into open prosecution. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The two can be read together: the first account itself claims only that the crate supplied the first concrete evidence, not that the authorities had no suspicion before it.

What the government knew. The fuller record supports the second account. Later scholarship and reporting from the era of declassification confirm that the FBI, the Customs Service and the intelligence units descended from the wartime OSS each uncovered parts of the smuggling operation while it was running, not after the fact. A National Security Council memorandum of the period referred to the operation directly. [cite:ampalestine_haganah] Enforcement was real but partial. Some participants were charged and most were not, and the network's core organizers were never prosecuted. [cite:ampalestine_haganah] Most of the material, as noted, arrived. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The pattern is not one of a government blind to the network. It is one of agencies that saw parts of it and a government that prosecuted a few of the people at its edges.

A separate investigation. One thing the FBI was doing in the same months is easily confused with this case, and the record keeps them apart. Records obtained decades later under the Freedom of Information Act by the Cato Institute show that the FBI maintained an investigative focus on the Zionist Organization of America, founded in 1897, in 1947–48. [cite:cato_fbi_wiretap] Its reason was not the arms network. The Bureau believed, incorrectly on the evidence of what it overheard, that the organization had been infiltrated or influenced by Communists. The activity it monitored was constitutionally protected and showed no violation of federal law by the organization. [cite:cato_fbi_wiretap] Between September 1947 and May 1948 the FBI also wiretapped Sol Rotenberg, a Jewish political activist tied to the International Workers Order, on the theory that the Order was a Communist Party front. [cite:cato_fbi_wiretap] These were two distinct concerns running at the same time: a Neutrality Act investigation of the arms network, and a counter-subversion inquiry into suspected Communist influence. Reading the wiretaps as part of the arms case, or the arms case as an outgrowth of the wiretaps, merges two files the record keeps separate.

The trial and the pardons. Nine men involved in the network were tried in a federal courthouse in Los Angeles in 1950. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] Schwimmer was convicted under the Neutrality Act and lost his American citizenship. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] One account adds that he was fined $10,000. [cite:sdjw_schwimmer] He later moved to Israel and led its aircraft industry; one account says he founded Israel Aerospace Industries, the other that he became head of Israel Aircraft Industries. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] [cite:sdjw_schwimmer] Charlie Winters was charged with conspiracy to export military aircraft in violation of the Neutrality Act, pleaded guilty and served 18 months in prison. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] [cite:sdjw_winters] One account describes him as the only participant in the network with a documented felony conviction for this activity. [cite:sdjw_winters]

The sources disagree on several details of the legal record, and the disagreements are set out below rather than resolved. Each account is internally specific; neither is obviously the stronger, and the primary court and pardon records are not among the sources used here.

On every version, both men were eventually pardoned by an American president, decades after the trial. [cite:schwimmer_wiki] [cite:sdjw_schwimmer] [cite:sdjw_winters] A man convicted of breaking the Neutrality Act went on to lead the new state's aircraft industry, and the United States later formally forgave both him and Winters. The arc runs from a federal prohibition, through a selective prosecution, to official absolution.

What the record does not settle. The documents establish the routes, the scale of some 800 shipments and roughly 2,400 tons, the Los Angeles trial and the pardons. They do not provide a full ledger of what moved by which route, or a total cost; the library's funding ledger records Schwimmer's roughly 30 aircraft without a price. They name only two of the nine men tried in 1950 and do not give the charges and sentences of the other seven. They do not explain why the network's core organizers were never prosecuted while the agencies knew of its activity, and they disagree on the details shown above. The question of who decided to prosecute so few, and why, remains open.
