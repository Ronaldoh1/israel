Accountability: Cardinals, Founders, Bishops and the Bill

National reports count what happened in dioceses. The question for this volume is narrower: what the Holy See knew about particular men, what it decided, and what it later said about its own decisions. Five cases are documented well enough to answer that from Rome's own records or statements: Cardinal Bernard Law of Boston, Marcial Maciel of the Legionaries of Christ, Theodore McCarrick, the Chilean bishops in 2018, and Joseph Ratzinger's years in Munich. The section then turns to the money paid in the United States, where most of the bill has fallen, and to the limit set by the Holy See's sovereign immunity. Each person named here is an individual; none of them stands for the Church as a whole.

Boston: Cardinal Law, 2002

The Boston Globe's reporting from January 2002 showed that the Archdiocese of Boston had kept abusive priests in ministry for years. The best-known was John Geoghan, whom Law reassigned to a parish in 1984 despite a warning from an auxiliary bishop; Geoghan was removed in 1993, dismissed from the priesthood in 1998, and found to have abused more than 130 children in six parishes. [cite:vt12_wiki_law] John Paul II accepted Law's resignation as archbishop on 13 December 2002. Law wrote: "To all those who have suffered from my shortcomings and mistakes I both apologize and from them beg forgiveness." [cite:vt12_wiki_law] In May 2004 John Paul II appointed him archpriest of the Basilica of St Mary Major in Rome, a largely ceremonial post he held until November 2011; he died in Rome on 20 December 2017. [cite:vt12_wiki_law] The appointment in Rome is the fact critics cite; the claim that he "fled" to Rome, found in earlier material, is an interpretation of a move that followed his resignation by more than a year.

The Legionaries: Marcial Maciel, 2006–2010

The CDF had received accusations against Maciel, the Mexican founder of the Legionaries of Christ, since 1998. On 19 May 2006 it announced that, in view of his age and health, it would not open a canonical trial and instead invited him to "a reserved life of prayer and penance," renouncing all public ministry; Benedict XVI approved the decision. The Legion said Maciel maintained his innocence and accepted the decision "with faith." [cite:vt12_zenit_maciel2006] Maciel died in 2008, so the decision came during his life, not after it, contrary to a claim in earlier material. [cite:vt12_wiki_maciel] The Legion admitted his "reprehensible actions" in March 2010, and a Vatican statement of 1 May 2010 called his acts "true crimes" and described a "mechanism of defense" built around him. In 2019 the Legion acknowledged 175 victims of 33 of its priests, 60 of them abused by Maciel. [cite:vt12_wiki_maciel]

McCarrick: the Holy See's own report, 2020

Theodore McCarrick, formerly cardinal archbishop of Washington, was dismissed from the clerical state in 2019 and continued to deny misconduct. [cite:vt12_ru_mccarrick2020] On 10 November 2020 the Holy See published a 449-page report on what it had known, drawing on more than 90 interviews. It is the most detailed account any part of the Holy See has published of its own handling of one abuser. It found that John Paul II named McCarrick to Washington in 2000 despite allegations made the year before, relying on "inaccurate and incomplete information" from three New Jersey bishops and on his own experience of false accusations used by Poland's communist government; that Benedict XVI chose to believe McCarrick and did not sanction him; and that Francis had not covered up reports but continued his predecessors' approach. On Archbishop Carlo Maria Viganò's claim that Benedict had imposed sanctions that Francis lifted, it said: "No records support Vigano's account." [cite:vt12_ru_mccarrick2020] The report is a self-examination; no external body tested it.

Chile, 2018: a pope's own error

Francis appointed Juan Barros bishop of Osorno in 2015 over objections from Chilean bishops and his own advisory commission. Victims of the priest Fernando Karadima said Barros had witnessed their abuse. During a visit to Chile in January 2018 Francis called their accusations "calumny" and asked for proof. [cite:vt12_cc_chile2018] He then sent Archbishop Charles Scicluna, who returned with a 2,300-page dossier from 64 witnesses, and in April 2018 Francis wrote that he had made "grave errors" of judgment and invited the victims he had discredited to Rome. [cite:vt12_cc_chile2018] All 34 Chilean bishops offered their resignations in May 2018; on 11 June Francis accepted those of Barros, Cristián Caro of Puerto Montt and Gonzalo Duarte of Valparaíso. [cite:vt12_aje_chile2018] It is the clearest case in which a sitting pope publicly retracted his own defence of a bishop.

Munich: Benedict XVI, 2022

On 20 January 2022 the law firm Westpfahl Spilker Wastl, commissioned by the Archdiocese of Munich and Freising, reported on abuse there from 1945 to 2019 and found four cases in which Joseph Ratzinger, archbishop from 1977 to 1982, could be accused of misconduct. Ratzinger later acknowledged that he had attended a 1980 meeting about an abusive priest. [cite:vt12_ap_benedict2022] In a letter published on 8 February 2022 the retired pope asked forgiveness for any "grievous faults," without admitting specific wrongdoing; his lawyers issued a separate technical response to the report. [cite:vt12_ap_benedict2022]

The bill: settlements and bankruptcies in the United States

In the United States the cost has been carried by dioceses and religious orders, which are separate legal persons, not by the Holy See. By early 2004 dioceses had paid $472.5 million; by 2006 the total had reached $1.5 billion. [cite:vt12_am_johnjay2004] The bishops' 2025 annual audit report, published in June 2026, recorded about $390 million in diocesan abuse-related costs in fiscal 2025 alone (about $484 million with religious communities), up 61 per cent on the year before, and OSV News estimated that dioceses had paid roughly $5 billion from 2004 to June 2025, and likely more than $6 billion including recent settlements. The same report counted 1,070 new allegations from 973 survivors in the audit year, 24 of them involving people who were minors at the time. [cite:vt12_ev_usccb2026]

Bankruptcy has become the main legal route. The Archdiocese of Portland in Oregon filed first, on 6 July 2004. [cite:vt12_ncr_bankruptcy2018] Filings accelerated after states opened "lookback windows" that revived old claims: Minnesota from 2013 to 2016, New York from August 2019, California from 2020 to 2022. [cite:vt12_ncreg_bankruptcy2023] As of March 2026, according to a tracker kept at Penn State Dickinson Law, 44 Catholic organisations in the United States had sought chapter 11 protection, 15 cases pending and 29 concluded. [cite:vt12_dick_bankruptcy2026]

Where the Holy See stands: immunity

Survivors have also sued the Holy See itself. Those suits, described in Part Five, have failed. In O'Bryan v. Holy See a federal appeals court held in 2008 that the Holy See is a foreign state under US law, and the plaintiffs withdrew in 2010 citing the high bar of immunity; in Doe v. Holy See a federal judge held in 2012 that the priest concerned was not the Holy See's employee; and in 2021 the European Court of Human Rights accepted Belgium's grant of immunity to the Holy See. [cite:vt5_ncr_obryan2010] [cite:vt5_jurist_doe2012] [cite:vt5_eapil_jc2021] [scene:vt5_recognition|Part Five: sovereignty and immunity] The Holy See's counsel, Jeffrey Lena, said the outcome of O'Bryan confirmed "there has never been a Holy See policy requiring concealment of child sexual abuse." [cite:vt5_ncr_obryan2010] The narrower finding is that no court has held the Holy See itself liable for an abuser's acts; claims that its bank, the IOR, "shields" abuse money have no source, since the IOR has not been a defendant in any case found. [scene:vt9_ior|Part Nine: the IOR]

The record thus divides along the line the Holy See itself draws. Rome has disciplined individuals, sometimes late (Maciel in 2006, McCarrick in 2019), has published one detailed self-examination (McCarrick, 2020), and has seen a pope retract his own judgment (Chile, 2018). [cite:vt12_zenit_maciel2006] [cite:vt12_ru_mccarrick2020] [cite:vt12_cc_chile2018] Compensation, by contrast, has been paid locally, by dioceses and orders under national law, while the Holy See has defended its immunity from the same claims. [cite:vt12_dick_bankruptcy2026] [cite:vt5_eapil_jc2021]
