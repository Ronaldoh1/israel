The Suppression Theory and the Record

A delay that long invited explanations, and the most famous one was religious. In 1991 Michael Baigent and Richard Leigh published The Dead Sea Scrolls Deception. Following mainly the American scholar Robert Eisenman, they argued that the scrolls were written by Jewish zealots who may have been the first followers of Jesus, led by his brother James, and that the scrolls had been kept under wraps by a team dominated by Catholic scholars and led by de Vaux, a Dominican friar, whose preconceptions led them to overlook evidence that many scrolls dated from the first century. [cite:ds_wiki_deception] An earlier member of the team, John Allegro, had made a similar charge in a 1984 essay, blaming the delays on scholarly possessiveness and on "deliberate suppression of potentially controversial religious information". [cite:ds_wiki_allegro]

Some of the premises are documented. The original team was led by a Dominican friar, de Vaux, and its later chief editor, Strugnell, was Roman Catholic; and the delay was real, as the team's own members later admitted. [cite:ds_wiki_deception] [cite:ds_jta_strugnell] Some members did keep texts to themselves for decades. Hershel Shanks, the editor who did most to open the scrolls, campaigned for exactly that reason. [cite:ds_vq_pubcontro]

The conclusion did not follow. Shanks himself severely criticised Baigent and Leigh's book, and the scholar Joseph Fitzmyer found in it a "pattern of errors and misinformed statements". [cite:ds_wiki_deception] Once the texts were open to everyone, from 1991, any scholar could look for the hidden Christian material. The scholarly view since is that early Christianity differs from the groups the scrolls describe. [cite:ds_wiki_deception] The Teacher of Righteousness cannot be Jesus or James, because the texts that mention him are older than Jesus by nearly a century, and there is no direct link between Qumran and the first Christians, though the scrolls share with early Christian texts ideas such as communal life and the opposition of light and darkness. [cite:ds_enc_dss]

Allegro's own story shows how the two kinds of claim can come apart in one person. He published the first translation of the Copper Scroll and the first official volume of Cave 4 texts, and his complaint about delay was borne out. [cite:ds_wiki_allegro] But in 1970 he published The Sacred Mushroom and the Cross, arguing that Christianity began as a cult built around a hallucinogenic mushroom; fourteen British scholars publicly repudiated it, his publisher later apologised for issuing it, and the scholar Sidnie White Crawford wrote that, rightly or wrongly, "Allegro would never be taken seriously as a scholar again." [cite:ds_wiki_allegro]

The record therefore supports a narrower finding than the theory. The scrolls were held by a small group that kept access to itself, and that is a documented instance of the pattern this shelf follows: knowledge controlled by an office. What the record does not support is the claim that the office was hiding a secret about Jesus. The gate was about possession, prestige and slowness, not about a buried gospel, and the proof is that when the gate opened, the buried gospel was not there.
