The Rules on Use: Leahy, the Forum and NSM-20

American law does not leave the use of American weapons entirely to the buyer. Two statutes named after former Senator Patrick Leahy bar aid to foreign security units that have committed gross violations of human rights, and in February 2024 a presidential memorandum required recipients to promise in writing to follow international law. This section sets out how those rules were applied to Israel: through a vetting process found, by the official who ran the standard one, to be unlike that used for any other country; through one unit's case; and through a memorandum that lasted just over a year.

The Leahy laws. The State Department's version (22 U.S.C. 2378d) prohibits assistance to any foreign security-force unit when the Secretary of State has credible information that it committed a gross violation of human rights; the Defense Department's version (10 U.S.C. 362) bars Pentagon-funded training, equipment or other assistance on the same basis. Vetting looks above all for torture, extrajudicial killing, enforced disappearance and rape under colour of law. "Credible information" is a deliberately low bar: in the 2024 appropriations act, information that "supports a reasonable belief that a violation has occurred". Both laws allow aid to resume if the government concerned takes effective steps to bring those responsible to justice. [cite:ow8_crs_leahy2025] The laws apply to Israel as they do to every recipient; the Congressional Research Service noted reports from former officials that the process for Israel differs. [cite:ow8_crs_leahy2025]

The Israel Leahy Vetting Forum. The most detailed account of the difference comes from Charles O. ("Cob") Blaha, who directed the State Department office responsible for Leahy vetting for seven and a half years before retiring in 2023, and who has since become a senior adviser to DAWN (Democracy for the Arab World Now), an advocacy group. [cite:ow8_js_blaha_ilvf] According to Blaha, the department set up a special "Israel Leahy Vetting Forum", which first met in 2020 under unpublished procedures. For Israel alone, he wrote, a decision that a unit is ineligible rests with the Deputy Secretary of State, which is "true for no other country in the world"; before a case reaches that level the department must send a formal written request for information to the Israeli government, which can take up to three months to reply. His conclusion, as of June 2024, was that "there are no ineligible Israeli units". [cite:ow8_js_blaha_ilvf] The State Department has not published the forum's procedures, and its own account of the process is not on the public record; Blaha's is that of a named former official with first-hand knowledge, now working with an advocacy group.

Netzah Yehuda. The forum's one public outcome concerned five Israeli units. On 29 April 2024 the State Department announced that Secretary Blinken had found that four had committed gross violations but had taken sufficient corrective steps, and that a fifth, the Netzah Yehuda battalion, had committed a gross violation and would remain eligible while the department worked with Israel on remediation. [cite:ow8_js_blaha_ilvf] On 9 August 2024 the State Department announced that Israel had "effectively remediated" the battalion's violations, making it fully eligible. Blaha, by his own account, replied that the only known measures were administrative, the discharge of two soldiers and a reprimand for a commander, that no member of the battalion had been punished or charged over the 2022 death of Omar Assad, and that the decision was "directly contrary to the Leahy law". The State Department's own description differs: it said two soldiers were removed from combat duty, vetting was changed and human-rights training required, as Part Eleven sets out. [scene:ow11_exceptions|Part Eleven: the Leahy Law] [cite:ow8_js_blaha_nz]

NSM-20. A broader rule came from the White House. On 8 February 2024 President Biden issued National Security Memorandum 20, which grew out of an amendment proposed by Senator Chris Van Hollen in December 2023 and backed by eighteen Democratic senators. It required countries receiving American weapons to give "credible and reliable written assurances" that they would use them in line with international law and would "facilitate and not arbitrarily deny, restrict, or otherwise impede" American-supported humanitarian aid; countries at war had 45 days, and the administration had to report to Congress within 90 days. [cite:ow8_aca_nsm20] Israel's defence minister gave the assurances on 14 March 2024, and Blinken accepted them on 25 March. [cite:ow8_js_nsm20report]

The May 2024 report. The report, released on 10 May 2024, said it was "reasonable to assess" that American weapons had been used by Israeli forces "in instances inconsistent with its IHL obligations". It made no finding about any specific incident, explaining that Israel "has not shared complete information" to verify whether American weapons were used in alleged violations. It judged Israel's assurances "credible and reliable", allowing deliveries to continue, and found no breach of the law that bars military aid to a government restricting American humanitarian aid, while describing aid reaching Gaza as "insufficient". [cite:ow8_js_nsm20report] The same week, the administration had paused the 2,000-pound bombs over Rafah. [cite:ow8_axios_pause]

Revocation. On 21 February 2025 President Trump revoked NSM-20, and on 14 March the White House rescinded the Biden administration's wider conventional arms transfer policy, which had aimed to prevent transfers risking "facilitating or otherwise contributing to violations of human rights"; it offered no explanation and issued no replacement. [cite:ow8_aca_rescind] Secretary Rubio described the memorandum as having imposed "baseless and politicized conditions on military assistance to Israel". [cite:ow8_toi_rubio2025] The Leahy laws, being statutes, remain in force as of October 2026; the memorandum's assurance-and-report system does not.

The asymmetry, stated plainly. The two governments' positions are on the record. The Biden administration's was that Israel's assurances were credible and that the law was being applied; the Trump administration's was that the conditions were themselves illegitimate. [cite:ow8_js_nsm20report] [cite:ow8_toi_rubio2025] What both positions share is the limit the May 2024 report admitted: the United States tracks its weapons up to the point of delivery, but depends on Israel for information about how they are used, and Israel did not supply it in full. [cite:ow8_js_nsm20report] That is the "one-way mirror" in its narrowest and best-documented form, and Part Thirteen returns to it.
