Three Thousand Years in Brief: Judah, Rome, Palaestina, Islam, Crusaders, Ottomans

Both peoples in this history reach back to a deep past, and each is often handed a version of it that erases the other. One erases the Jews from the land for two thousand years; the other has the Palestinians appear only in the twentieth century. The record supports neither.

The story opens with Jewish kingdoms of Israel and Judah in the first millennium BCE. Rome conquered the region in 63 BCE, and in 6 CE it created a predominantly Jewish province, Judaea. Two great revolts against Roman rule failed. The first ended with the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. The second, the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132 to 135 or 136, ended in catastrophe for Judea's Jews. Rome killed, enslaved or expelled much of the Jewish population. It rebuilt Jerusalem as a pagan colony called Aelia Capitolina and barred Jews from entering it. It also renamed the province Syria Palaestina.[cite:wiki_syria_palaestina] Most scholars read that renaming as an attempt to sever the Jewish connection to the land.[cite:wiki_palestine_region]

The attempt did not fully succeed. A smaller Jewish presence continued without a break, centered religiously in Galilee, where much classical Jewish literature was produced.[cite:encyclo_israel_history] The land itself passed through a long line of rulers: Byzantine Christian rule; Muslim rule from the seventh century; the Crusader kingdoms; the Mamluks in the thirteenth century; and from 1516 the Ottoman Empire, which governed for four centuries.[cite:wiki_palestine_region]

In the sixteenth century, Jewish communities took root in four cities regarded as holy: Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed. They formed what is called the "Old Yishuv," the Jewish population of the land before the Zionist movement. It remained a minority, and its numbers rose and fell over time.[cite:encyclo_israel_history]

Over the same centuries, the majority population became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] They are the ancestors of today's Palestinians. Their presence is documented across those centuries; they did not appear in the twentieth.

Put together, the two histories do not cancel each other. The record documents an ancient Jewish connection to the land, a continuous if reduced Jewish presence after Rome crushed the revolts, and centuries of residence by the Arabic-speaking majority. Each popular simplification works by pulling out one of these strands. The Jewish tie to the land is in the record. So is the long residence of the people who became the Palestinians.

That is why the modern conflict cannot be read as a contest between a true claim and a false one. It is about how two documented claims to the same ground came to be handled after 1897, and the chapters that follow trace that handling. This section sets only the starting point. By the end of Ottoman rule, the land held a majority of Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians and a Jewish minority whose ties to it reached back to antiquity.
