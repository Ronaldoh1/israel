Promised to Others: 1915–1923

The war that ended Ottoman rule brought Palestine's Arabs two British promises, and only one of them was made to them. In letters of 1915 and 1916 Sir Henry McMahon, the High Commissioner in Egypt, pledged to Sharif Hussein of Mecca that Britain would support Arab independence if the Arabs rose against the Ottomans, and they did, in the Arab Revolt of 1916–18. [cite:w_mcmahon] [cite:wiki_hussein_mcmahon] The Arab side read the pledge as including Palestine; McMahon later said it had not, and the wording allowed Britain to argue either way. [cite:wiki_hussein_mcmahon] [cite:hussein_mcmahon]

The second promise was the Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917, made to the Zionist movement. It named the people of the country only by what they were not. It promised a national home "for the Jewish people," on the condition that nothing be done to prejudice "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities in Palestine." Those communities were, by one estimate, about 700,000 Muslim and Christian Arabs, some 90 percent of the population. The letter gave them civil and religious rights. It said nothing of political or national rights. [cite:balfour_letter]

When they were asked, they answered plainly. In June and July 1919 the American King–Crane Commission toured the region to learn what its people wanted. It received about 1,875 petitions, 72 percent of them hostile to the Zionist plan, and it reported that the non-Jewish population, nearly nine-tenths of the whole, was "emphatically against the entire Zionist program." It recommended the program's "serious modification." [cite:britannica_king_crane] [cite:jewishmag_king_crane] The report was set aside. [scene:ri_s01|Britain's own inquiry] into the violence of April 1920 in Jerusalem, which put the political weight on Zionist impatience and the Balfour Declaration, was not published either. [cite:rs_hg_palin]

The violence itself was real, and the people of the land were not only its victims. At the Nebi Musa festival in April 1920, speeches by Arab religious leaders were followed by attacks on Jerusalem's Jews; the military court counted 5 Jews killed and 211 wounded, and 4 Muslims killed. [cite:rs_hg_palin] [cite:jvl_arab_riots_1920s] In Jaffa in May 1921 the British inquiry found "the Arab majority, who were generally the aggressors," and found the cause in Arab "discontent with, and hostility to, the Jews, due to political and economic causes, and connected with Jewish immigration." [cite:jvl_haycraft_report] Both halves belong to the record.

Alongside the crowds there was an organized national movement, and its demands were in writing. From 1919 Muslims and Christians organized together in Muslim–Christian Associations. Seven national congresses met between 1919 and 1928, called for "a national government responsible to a representative assembly," and rejected the Balfour Declaration and mass Jewish immigration. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive] The third congress, in Haifa in December 1920, elected an Arab Executive under Musa Kazim al-Husseini, a former mayor of Jerusalem whom the British had dismissed after the April riots. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] He led delegations to Winston Churchill in 1921 and then to London. On 21 February 1922 the Palestine Arab Delegation rejected Britain's draft constitution, demanded a "national independent Government," invoked the League of Nations' own Covenant, and said the Balfour Declaration could not be a basis for discussion. [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] [cite:rs_a22_cmd1700] They came home with nothing. Musa Kazim wrote that the cause would not find justice "at the hands of the British government which is under Zionist influence." [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]

Britain then offered them a seat at a table already set. The constitution of 1922 created a Legislative Council of ten British officials and twelve elected members under the High Commissioner, inside a Mandate whose terms, including the Balfour Declaration, it could not change. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] The Fifth Palestine Arab Congress resolved to boycott it, and in February 1923 Muslims filled about a sixth of their places and Christians about a third, too few for a council to be formed. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] [cite:rs_lc_albright] Britain next offered an "Arab Agency" to parallel the Jewish Agency that the Mandate recognized, but with its members chosen by the High Commissioner. Musa Kazim refused it in October 1923: the Arabs' "sole object is independence." [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_arabagency] From the end of 1923 the High Commissioner governed with officials alone for the rest of the Mandate. The Zionist Organization kept the standing the Mandate gave it as the recognized Jewish agency; the Arab Executive was never recognized at all. [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] To the people of the country, the arrangement looked like what it was: a government in which one side had an official voice and the majority had none.
