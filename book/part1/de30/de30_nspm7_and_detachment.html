NSPM-7 and Detachment 201

Two documents of 2025 complete the legal architecture this book has followed: a presidential memorandum of September on domestic political violence, which names the ideas it calls warning signs, and the swearing-in of four technology executives as Army officers in June. Neither is a surveillance tool. One sets who the tools are pointed at; the other places the builders of the tools inside the force that buys them. This section sets out each, then tests the book's worst-case reading against Book Five as a whole.

What NSPM-7 says

A National Security Presidential Memorandum (NSPM) is a directive from the president to the agencies, published but not passed by Congress. NSPM-7, "Countering Domestic Terrorism and Organized Political Violence," was signed on 25 September 2025. It cites the assassination of Charlie Kirk that month, alongside the killing of a health-insurance executive in 2024, the 2022 attempt on Justice Kavanaugh and attacks on ICE officers. Section 1 names the "common threads animating this violent conduct": "anti-Americanism, anti-capitalism, and anti-Christianity," "support for the overthrow of the United States Government," and "extremism on migration, race, and gender." [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] Section 2 directs the FBI's Joint Terrorism Task Forces to investigate "institutional and individual funders, and officers and employees of organizations"; the Treasury to "identify and disrupt financial networks" and guide banks on filing Suspicious Activity Reports, the confidential reports banks send to the Treasury; and the IRS to ensure that no tax-exempt body is "directly or indirectly financing political violence or domestic terrorism." Section 3 lets the Attorney General recommend groups for designation as a "domestic terrorist organization," citing the definition of domestic terrorism in federal criminal law, 18 U.S.C. 2331(5). The memorandum does not mention the First Amendment; it says it "shall be implemented consistent with applicable law." [cite:de30_wh_nspm7]

No federal statute provides for designating a domestic group as terrorist. [cite:de30_dla_nspm7] The criminal code defines domestic terrorism but, unlike the State Department's foreign list, attaches no list or penalties to it. A law firm's analysis for its clients concluded that NSPM-7 "creates no new legal authority" and that a domestic label carries reputational and practical, not formal legal, consequences. [cite:de30_dla_nspm7] Three days before the memorandum, on 22 September, the president had signed an order stating, "I hereby designate Antifa as a 'domestic terrorist organization,'" calling it "a militarist, anarchist enterprise" and directing agencies to "investigate, disrupt, and dismantle" its operations and their funders; the order cites no statute. [cite:de30_govinfo_antifa] On 13 November the State Department used the foreign-list power it does have against four European groups. [cite:de30_state_fto]

The Attorney General's implementing memorandum of 4 December 2025, addressed to all federal prosecutors, made the program operational. It ordered the FBI to "compile a list of groups or entities engaged in acts that may constitute domestic terrorism," updated every 30 days, set up cash rewards for informants, reached back five years and told prosecutors to consider tax crimes. Its list of indicators includes "opposition to law and immigration enforcement," "radical gender ideology," and "anti-Christianity." A footnote states: "No investigation may be opened based solely on activities protected by the First Amendment." [cite:de30_bondi_memo]

What has been done under it

The documented prosecutions concern violence. On 4 July 2025 a police officer was shot, and survived, outside the Prairieland ICE detention centre in Alvarado, Texas. By April 2026 a federal jury in Fort Worth had convicted eight of nine defendants, whom prosecutors called the "North Texas Antifa Cell," of providing material support to terrorists, built on attempted murder of a federal officer; the prosecutor told jurors they were on trial for their conduct, not their beliefs. [cite:de30_kera_prairieland]

The use against organisations is reported, not adjudicated. On 2 September 2026 Senators Ron Wyden and Raphael Warnock wrote to the Treasury citing press reports that officials were reviewing the tax-exempt status of the Open Society Foundations, the Southern Poverty Law Center and the Council on American-Islamic Relations under a "blueprint," and that IRS criminal investigators were working with the FBI on nonprofits; the letter asked whether any group had been recommended for examination or revocation and reports none completed. [cite:de30_wyden_letter] An amicus brief filed on 25 August 2026 for FIRE, the Society for the Rule of Law and the libertarian Cato Institute argues that DHS has used NSPM-7 to infiltrate meetings, monitor encrypted chats and seize years of financial records from unions and nonprofits, and that the memorandum "treats left-leaning political viewpoints as indicia of domestic terrorism." These are allegations; no court has ruled on them. [cite:de30_pd_amicus] Ideological deportation cases and the unmasking subpoenas are in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Thirty]; the best-known earlier federal programme aimed at domestic political groups, the FBI's COINTELPRO, which ended in April 1971, is in [scene:de29_the_precedent|Part Thirty-One]. [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro]

Detachment 201

On 13 June 2025 the Army swore in four executives as lieutenant colonels in the Army Reserve: Shyam Sankar, chief technology officer of Palantir; Andrew Bosworth, chief technology officer of Meta; Kevin Weil, chief product officer of OpenAI; and Bob McGrew, an adviser at Thinking Machines Lab and formerly OpenAI's chief research officer. As "Detachment 201" they serve part-time as senior advisers on technology for the Army's reorganisation. [cite:de30_army_det201] An Army spokesman said in June 2025 that "They're not making acquisition decisions" and that the conflict rules were those for any reservist. [cite:de30_d1_det201] When three more executives were commissioned on 10 June 2026, the Army described stricter terms: "mandatory" recusal under the Joint Ethics Regulation, confidential financial disclosure, legal review of each task and a bar from vendor selection. [cite:de30_ds_det201]

Sankar's company has been an Army software contractor since 2019 ([scene:de27_money_and_war|Part Twenty-Nine]). [cite:de10_defnews] That is a documented conflict of position, which the 2026 rules exist to manage; no document shows any member steering a contract.

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis is the Sophia platform's, not a finding: immigration enforcement as the occasion, a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination. Book Five has read the toolkit through one lens, that the mechanisms may not be connected now but exist, so the test asks three things: whether the parts exist, which joins are on the record, and what has been done to critics.

The parts exist; that is documented in all nine parts. Brokers sell location trails and profiles, and "anonymous" data can be traced to the person ([scene:de24_the_myth_of_anonymous_data|Part Twenty-Four]); the state holds benefit, school, vetting and credit records ([scene:de24b_benefits|Part Twenty-Five]); phones can be followed through data an internal ICE analysis said needs no warrant ([scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Six]); TSA and CBP record who travels ([scene:de25b_who_knows_you_travel|Part Twenty-Seven]); ICE matches faces in the street, and every regulated stablecoin must carry a freeze switch ([scene:de26_faces_drones_satellites|Part Twenty-Eight]). Palantir sells the platform that joins records ([scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Nine]), and ICE buys from every layer ([scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty]). The precedent of such tools turned on lawful political life is proven ([scene:de29_the_precedent|Part Thirty-One]), and a federal directive now names ideas, including opposition to immigration enforcement, as indicators of domestic terrorism. [cite:de25_404_webloc] [cite:de26_404_fortify] [cite:de26_crec_genius] [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:de30_bondi_memo]

Documented joins: a Medicaid dataset went from CMS to ICE and on to Palantir over a Teams chat in January 2026, beyond a court's limits [cite:de27_npr_medicaid]; TSA passed records on more than 31,000 travellers to ICE between January 2025 and February 2026, with more than 800 arrests by ICE's own count [cite:de25b_reuters_tsaice]; records from 2014 to 2022 show student-visa and air-passenger data searched, and phone extractions imported, through ICE's Palantir case system [cite:de28_guardian_icm]; and Social Security data was linked to SAVE and run against citizens' voter rolls. [cite:de19_npr_save] Probable: LexisNexis's people-search data flowing into "the Palantir platform," which its 2026 work statement requires. [cite:de28_bu_lexis] Possible, with no document: the commercial vendors' location, face and social-media data, the newly opened welfare files and DHS's planned biometric system joined to the same platform; each would need an interface or an agreement, not a new law. Not supported: Thomson Reuters's CLEAR feeding Palantir, or one standing system that carries a person from a camera to a cell outside immigration enforcement. [cite:de28_lawsites_tr]

The targeting of critics has to be graded by group. For non-citizens it is documented: after testimony that a DHS team had written more than a hundred reports drawing on a private list of campus activists, a federal court found on 30 September 2025 that a policy of targeting non-citizen academics for their speech violated the First Amendment, and on 22 January 2026 it vacated the policy; the government's appeal was pending as of October 2026. [cite:de28_crimson_hatch] [cite:de28_ch_aaup] [cite:de30_audit_aaup] For citizens the record holds specific cases, not a programme. DHS used administrative subpoenas, without a judge, to try to unmask people who criticised or tracked its agents, and in one case agents came to a critic's home; it withdrew the demands when challenged. [cite:de28_aclu_doe] [cite:de28_int_meta] A Minnesota citizen who had been watching agents lost her Global Entry three days after an agent named her using face recognition, a link alleged in her pending suit and denied by CBP. [cite:de25b_bu_cleland] The Director of National Intelligence revoked the clearances of at least 37 current and former officials, in a memo reported to cite no specific evidence. [cite:de24b_kvia_gabbard] Use of the surveillance stack as a programme against citizens for their speech is not documented: no court has found it, no nonprofit has lost its status, and the terrorism convictions on record follow a shooting.

Probable: that the indicator lists steer investigative attention toward critics of immigration policy and the left, because the texts name them and the reported reviews of nonprofits follow them; this rests on text and reports, not on a finding about any person. [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] [cite:de30_wyden_letter] Not supported: that any of it came from the Dark Enlightenment's texts; the line from RAGE to the new civil-service schedule is rhyme, not provenance ([scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|the first section]).

Against the destination, the vetoes worked in many places: courts held 42,695 tax disclosures unlawful, limited Medicaid sharing to five fields and paused it after the January breach, vacated the speech-targeting policy and handed the Justice Department thirteen straight losses on voter rolls; a camera company paused federal searches after a state audit; and much of the toolkit was built or bought under presidents of both parties ([scene:de26_counterweights|Part Twenty-Eight]). [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de30_bl_medicaid] [cite:de30_bgov_voterrolls] [cite:de24_ap_flock] [cite:de30_tracker_orr] They lost others: the Supreme Court stayed the SAVE ruling, no court has blocked the new civil-service schedule, and Trump v. Slaughter widened the removal power. [cite:de19_npr_save] [cite:de30_afge_lit] [cite:de0_slaughter] The parts exist, several joins are documented and more are designed; the executive without a veto is not shown.
