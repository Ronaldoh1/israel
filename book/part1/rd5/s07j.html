The Warnings: State, the Pentagon and Oil

A familiar explanation holds that the strategic costs of supporting partition were not understood until much later, a blind spot produced by limited information or the slow communications of the era. The documents do not support that explanation. In the weeks before the United States backed partition, three separate parts of the American government, the State Department's regional experts, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense, each warned in writing that partition would damage American strategic interests, citing oil access and Soviet opportunism. The oil industry lobbied against it directly. The warnings were heard, and they were set aside.

The first came from Loy Henderson, director of the State Department's Office of Near Eastern and African Affairs. Henderson had served as American ambassador to Iraq in the mid-1940s and had seen at first hand how Arab governments were reacting to the Palestine question. On September 22, 1947, he sent Secretary of State George Marshall a memo warning that partition was "not workable" and would lead to "untold troubles" in the future. He stated that his views were shared by "nearly every member of the Foreign Service or of the department who has worked to any appreciable extent on Near Eastern problems." [cite:wrmea_henderson_warning_1994] The memo was not buried. Under Secretary Robert Lovett was impressed enough to read it aloud to President Truman himself. [cite:wrmea_henderson_warning_1994]

The military reached the same conclusion through its own channels. On October 10, 1947, the Joint Chiefs of Staff filed a memorandum titled "The Problem of Palestine." It warned that dividing Palestine into Jewish and Arab states would allow the Soviet Union to displace American and British influence in the region and would endanger American access to Middle East oil. The next day, October 11, 1947, the United States announced its support for partition. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] The gap between a formal military warning and a decision that ran against it could hardly have been narrower. The surviving record documents both the warning and the decision, but not any stated internal reasoning that connects them.

The Secretary of Defense said much the same thing in public. James Forrestal, who had earlier been Secretary of the Navy and who oversaw the new Department of Defense created under the National Security Act of 1947, told a House committee that oil was "the life blood of a war machine." He warned that the Arab world's response to partition would threaten the pipelines carrying oil out of the Persian Gulf. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] His public testimony and the Joint Chiefs' internal memo made the same argument through two different channels.

Private industry added its own pressure. A 1947 memorandum submitted to President Truman, published under the title "Oil and the State Department policy on Palestine: the documentary evidence of how ARAMCO attempts to destroy the partition resolution of the United Nations," documents a direct lobbying campaign against partition by the Arabian American Oil Company. [cite:loc_aramco_oil_pamphlet] ARAMCO held the core American oil concession in Saudi Arabia, the relationship that also lay behind Roosevelt's meeting with Ibn Saud in 1945, and a region inflamed against the United States threatened its value. The campaign and the government warnings ran in parallel; the sources treat them as separate efforts, not one coordinated one, and do not reproduce ARAMCO's internal communications.

None of this changed the outcome. Truman had heard Henderson's memo directly. According to multiple historical accounts, he was worried about the 1948 election and was urged by Clark Clifford to endorse partition in order to secure Jewish-American votes in key swing states. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] The United States backed partition. In the months that followed, Palestine descended into the violence the warnings had predicted, and on February 24, 1948, George Kennan, the State Department's Director of Policy Planning, warned that only foreign troops could now stop it. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman]

Two cautions apply. Historians still debate how much of the later violence was inevitable and how much depended on other factors, so Henderson's predictions should not be treated as correct in every detail. And no one can show that a different decision would have produced a better result by every measure. What the record does establish is that this was not a failure of information. It was an informed decision that weighed documented strategic risk against domestic political considerations and chose the latter.
