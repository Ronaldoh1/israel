Does Scripture Forbid a Jewish State? Jeremiah, the Three Oaths, and a Debate Older Than Zionism

Two confident answers circulate about what Jewish tradition says on a Jewish state. One holds that Judaism commands it. The other holds that Judaism forbids it. Each speaks as if the tradition had one voice. The texts tell a different story: the question was argued inside Judaism long before anyone founded a political movement, and it is still argued now. This section sets out the argument as the sources give it. It does not rule between the sides, because the tradition itself holds both readings.

The oldest text in the debate comes from the book of Jeremiah. The prophet instructed the exiles carried off to Babylon to build lives where they had been taken, and to "seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it" (Jeremiah 29:7). [cite:sefaria_jer29] The verse does not speak of a state at all. Its weight in the later argument lies in what it asks of exiles: to build lives where they are, and to seek the welfare of the place that holds them.

The second text is legal and far more specific. The Babylonian Talmud, in tractate Ketubot (110b–111a), derives from the Song of Songs three oaths. Israel was not to go up to the land "as a wall", meaning en masse and by force. Israel was not to rebel against the nations. And the nations, in turn, were not to oppress Israel excessively. [cite:w_three_oaths] Two of the three oaths bind Israel; the third binds the nations. That structure matters, because each side of the later debate reads the three clauses together and draws a different conclusion from them.

For many Orthodox authorities, the oaths settle the matter. On this reading, a sovereign Jewish state established before the coming of the Messiah violates them: a return to the land could come only through divine redemption, not human political action, and to force it is a transgression. [cite:w_vayoel][cite:neturei_karta] The fullest statement of this view came well after the period of this chapter. Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, the rebbe of Satmar, built his work Vayoel Moshe, published in 1959, on the three oaths, and Neturei Karta holds the same view today. [cite:w_vayoel] Earlier, in 1912, Orthodox opposition to secular Zionism took organized form with the founding of Agudat Israel, whose story belongs to a later part of this library [scene:dh_s01|the first political assassination].

The opposite case rests on a different set of authorities. Religious Zionists cite Nachmanides, who counted settling the land among the commandments. On that view, living in and building up the land is not a forbidden forcing of the end but a religious duty in its own right. They cite as well Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook, who would become the first Ashkenazi chief rabbi of Mandate Palestine and who saw even secular Zionism as part of the process of redemption. [cite:w_religious_zionism][cite:w_kook] Rav Kook's office, like Agudat Israel, lies beyond 1896; he enters this account as the clearest named voice of the reading that would answer the oaths. Against the oaths themselves, religious Zionists have made two further arguments. Some argue that the oaths lapsed after centuries of persecution. Others argue that the Balfour Declaration and the League of Nations supplied the nations' consent. Both arguments are recorded as positions some religious Zionists hold, not as the view of every authority on that side.

The strongest form of each case deserves to be stated plainly. The anti-Zionist reading can point to a Talmudic text that speaks directly to a mass return by force, and to the many Orthodox authorities who read it as barring a state before the Messiah. The religious-Zionist reading can point to Nachmanides, a classical authority who treated settlement of the land as a commandment, and to the argument that the conditions the oaths assumed no longer held. Neither side invented its texts. Both are arguing from inside the same tradition, over the same pages.

A third position stood outside both religious readings and falls squarely inside this chapter's period. In 1885 American Reform Judaism, in its Pittsburgh Platform, rejected a return to Palestine altogether, on the ground that its members were "no longer a nation, but a religious community". [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] Reform did not argue from the oaths; it set the whole expectation of national restoration aside. It later reversed that position. The platform and its place among the responses of the 1880s are told in full in [scene:s02|the idea appears, 1862–1885].

Why did the argument become so sharp at the end of the nineteenth century? Part of the answer lies in who led the new movement. Early Zionism was mostly secular, and the record gives that as one reason so many rabbis opposed it at first. For readers of the oaths, a movement of this kind raised the objection the oaths name: a return pursued by human political action rather than awaited. The shift from a return awaited in prayer to a return pursued by human action, and the rabbis who first argued for that shift, are traced in [scene:ideo_s01|from prayer to program].

Where does the debate stand today? Most Orthodox Jews in Israel accept or support the state. Anti-Zionist Haredi groups, Neturei Karta among them, remain a minority. The minority's existence is the point. A religious tradition that still contains, in organized and named form, both a reading that commands settlement and a reading that forbids a state before the Messiah cannot be summed up as saying either thing alone. Zionism is a political movement; Judaism is a religion whose authorities have argued over that movement from its beginning.

Several things the record available here does not settle. It does not give the text of Nachmanides' ruling or its date, so the strength of his statement can be reported only as religious Zionists cite it. It does not measure how many rabbis or communities held each reading between the late eighteenth century and 1896; "many Orthodox authorities" is as precise as the sources allow.
