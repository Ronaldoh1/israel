Gold and the Crown

On 13 January 1404 King Henry IV of England signed the Act against Multipliers into law. It forbade "the transmutation of base metals into gold or silver". [cite:hm_loc_multipliers] The Library of Congress's legal historians explain the reason: Henry was "wary of anything that could weaken the crown", and gold or silver made outside the Mint could be used to make counterfeit coins and debase the currency. [cite:hm_loc_multipliers] About four decades later, Henry VI began issuing special licences to alchemists to get around the statute, in an effort to pay for costly wars. [cite:hm_loc_multipliers] The same crown that outlawed making gold licensed it when it needed gold.

On the Continent an emperor made alchemy a court enterprise. Under Rudolf II, Prague became Europe's leading centre of alchemy, "accommodating as many as 200 alchemists", so many that they spilled out of the palace into the gardens; Rudolf practised himself, once singeing his beard. [cite:hm_quil_rudolf] Among those drawn to his court were the English scholar John Dee and his medium, Edward Kelley, who had "already had his ears cropped for the crime of counterfeiting" before he reached Prague, and who eventually died in prison there. [cite:hm_quil_rudolf]

The English ban lasted almost three centuries. The chemist Robert Boyle successfully lobbied for its repeal, which came with the Royal Mines Act of 1688; other accounts date the lifting to 1689. [cite:hm_loc_multipliers] [cite:hm_neh_newton] By then the most important alchemist in England was Isaac Newton. He wrote "over one million words" on alchemy. [cite:hm_neh_newton] He kept them hidden, and the National Endowment for the Humanities gives several reasons: alchemy had been illegal in England since 1404, counterfeiting remained a capital crime, the crown feared alchemy could destabilise the economy, and Newton himself was guarded and obsessed with credit for his discoveries. [cite:hm_neh_newton]

Late in life Newton took charge of the Royal Mint, and he had one counterfeiter hanged and publicly disembowelled. [cite:hm_neh_newton] The man who had spent years in private trying to make the precious metal became the official responsible for protecting the coin against those who faked it. His alchemical papers stayed out of sight until 13 July 1936, when a chest of his private papers was auctioned at Sotheby's in London and split among about three dozen buyers for about £9,000. [cite:hm_neh_newton] [cite:hm_ox_sotheby] The economist John Maynard Keynes bought many of them, concentrating on the alchemical papers, and reassembled about half the collection. His verdict on what he read was that Newton "was the last of the magicians." [cite:hm_ox_sotheby] [cite:hm_neh_newton]

The money trail shows the same pattern as every book on this shelf, in its most literal form. The secret that alchemy claimed to hold was the secret of making the most valuable thing in the economy, and the crown's response followed its interest: prohibition when unlicensed gold threatened the coin, licences when the crown needed funds, patronage when an emperor sought prestige, and the death penalty for those who faked the result. [cite:hm_loc_multipliers] [cite:hm_neh_newton] No one in this record is shown to have made gold; what the record does show is that the state always meant to control whoever might.
