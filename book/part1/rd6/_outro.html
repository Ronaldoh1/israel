By the early 1950s the new state's position had been consolidated on several fronts at once. In Washington, it had gained de jure recognition in January 1949 and a $100 million Export-Import Bank loan, and it had rejected a presidential warning that was never enforced. At home, the Absentees' Property Law of 1950 had vested refugee property in a state Custodian, reaching even citizens classed as "present absentees," estimated by 2001 at about 200,000, while military rule kept the displaced from returning. In the immigrant camps, the treatment of Jews from Arab and Muslim countries had opened a wound the state would acknowledge with "regret" only in 2021.

Abroad, the refugee question had hardened. Lausanne had failed on the order of peace and return. Resolution 194 had been adopted, reaffirmed, and read two ways. The new human-rights order counted, relieved and debated the displacement of about 711,000 people, but placed them outside the main refugee convention and offered no court. In the West, the story that they had left on orders would stand until the archives opened.

And the books on the pre-state period could be closed. The record showed mass private giving, documented budgets and defense channels, a connected American network, one documented case of money changing a decision, and an apparatus built over fifty years before the state was declared.
