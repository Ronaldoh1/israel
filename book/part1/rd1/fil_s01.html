A Newspaper Called Palestine: The Arab Public Sphere Before 1914

It is often said that "Palestinian" was an identity invented after 1948, or even after 1967. The newspapers, parliamentary records and political programs of the years before the First World War answer that claim directly. By 1914 the country had Arabic newspapers arguing about Zionism in print, elected deputies who had raised it in the Ottoman parliament, a governor who had tried to block a land sale, and a paper in Jaffa whose masthead carried the name Filastin, "Palestine." What these sources show, and what they do not, needs to be stated precisely, because the record is richer than the claim allows and more complicated than a simple reversal of it.

The argument began outside Palestine and in French. In 1905 Negib Azouri published Le Réveil de la nation arabe, and in it he set the Arab national awakening beside the Jewish one and wrote of the two movements: "Both these movements are destined to fight each other continually until one of them wins."[cite:rs_rd1_azouri] It was a prediction, not a program, written eight years after the Basel Congress and before any large Zionist purchase in the Jezreel Valley; but it framed the encounter as a contest between two national movements six years after Herzl had answered a Jerusalem notable's warning by writing that the existing population would be enriched by Jewish settlement (that exchange is told in [scene:rd1_arabquestion|Herzl and the Arab question]).

The same year Najib Nassar began the most sustained campaign. From 1905 he wrote against Zionism in two Arabic papers, al-Muqattam in Cairo and Lisan al-Hal in Beirut. In 1908 he founded his own paper, al-Karmil, in Haifa, appearing twice a week.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_nassar] It was the year of the Young Turk revolution. The authorities' tolerance of the new paper had limits: they closed al-Karmil for two months in June 1909. In 1910 the Ministry of Justice ordered Nassar tried on a complaint from the Jewish community alleging incitement, and the court acquitted him.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_nassar] The episode cuts in more than one direction. It shows an Arab journalist prosecuted for his writing on Zionism; it shows a Jewish community in Palestine using the Ottoman courts against him; and it shows those courts declining to convict.

Nassar did not stop. In October 1911 he collected his articles as a book, Zionism: Its History, Objectives and Importance. In 1913 he called for a non-Zionist national society based in Nablus. In the summer of 1914 he urged Palestinian youth to press Arab landowners not to sell land to Jews.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_nassar] That last appeal is the point at which the press campaign met the mechanism described in [scene:s04j|the land purchases]: the sellers were Arab landowners, many of them absentee, and the papers were trying to reach them through public opinion, since the law gave tenants no say.

The 1908 revolution also gave Palestine elected deputies in the Ottoman parliament. Ruhi al-Khalidi was one of three deputies elected for Jerusalem that year. In 1911, with his fellow Jerusalem deputy Sa'id al-Husayni, he raised Zionism in the Ottoman parliament, and he stressed that what he opposed was Jewish immigration, not the residence of Palestine's own Jews.[cite:rs_rd1_ruhi_wiki] The distinction is worth noticing. It drew the line where the Ottoman government's own restrictions had drawn it since the 1880s: between Jews who were already subjects of the empire and organized settlement from abroad. In the same parliament Shukri al-Asali, the former governor of Nazareth who had tried to stop the al-Fula sale and had resigned to win a seat for Damascus, argued, during a term that ran until April 1912, that Jewish settlers held Ottoman citizenship alongside foreign citizenship and were taking control of rural villages.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] Ruhi al-Khalidi went further than speeches. He left an unpublished manuscript, "Zionism and the Zionist Question," and died of typhoid in Istanbul in 1913, while serving as deputy to the head of parliament.[cite:rs_rd1_ruhi_wiki] The texts of the 1911 speeches, and the month in which they were given, are not established in the sources used here.

Into this setting came Filastin. It was founded in Jaffa on 15 January 1911 by Issa El-Issa and his cousin Yousef El-Issa, Arab Orthodox Christians educated in Beirut.[cite:wiki_falastin] [cite:nli_filastin_archive] It began as a twice-weekly paper, and its first cause was not Zionism at all but the dispute between the Arab Orthodox community and the Greek-led hierarchy of its church.[cite:wiki_falastin] Between 1911 and 1914 its coverage of Zionism shifted. A scholarly study of the paper documents its reporting on land purchases, immigration, violent incidents and the Zionist Congresses moving from cautious ambivalence to open hostility before the war.[cite:academia_filastin_zionism] The editors sent a copy of each issue to every village in the Jaffa district, so that the paper's argument reached the countryside where land was actually changing hands, not only readers in the town.[cite:academia_filastin_zionism] [cite:wiki_falastin]

The record also contains an event that complicates any account in which Arab politics before 1914 revolved around Zionism. The Arab Congress met in Paris on 18–23 June 1913 with 25 official delegates. It adopted ten resolutions, on Arab participation in the central government of the empire, on Arabic as an official language, and on administrative decentralization. Its resolutions did not address Zionism. Two Zionist representatives, Victor Jacobson and Sami Hochberg, attended unofficially.[cite:rs_rd1_arabcongress_wiki] The Congress's agenda was reform within the Ottoman Empire, framed in Arab rather than specifically Palestinian terms, and its silence on Zionism shows that the subject that dominated al-Karmil and, by 1914, Filastin was not yet the first item for the wider Arab reform movement. Why the Congress left it out, and what Jacobson and Hochberg sought there, the sources used here do not say.

Filastin outlived the empire in which it was founded. Under the British Mandate it became one of the most influential Arabic dailies, known for its network of correspondents, and in 1927 Issa El-Issa helped found the Palestine Free Party. The paper appeared in Jaffa until 7 April 1948, shortly before the city fell, and later moved to Jerusalem.[cite:wiki_falastin] [cite:palquest_issa_el_issa] The National Library of Israel's Jrayed archive holds 6,692 issues, from 1911 to 1948.[cite:nli_filastin_archive]

Set against the claim that the identity was invented later, the record is plain on its central point. A paper named "Palestine," carrying a public argument about land and immigration, was printed in Jaffa from 1911, sent to the villages of its district, and preserved in Israel's own national library.[cite:nli_filastin_archive] That documents a Palestinian name in print and a Palestinian public sphere decades before 1948. The fair statement of the other side is that this public sphere operated inside larger frames: its deputies sat in the Ottoman parliament and drew the Ottoman government's own line between subjects and immigrants, and its most ambitious political gathering, in Paris, spoke as Arabs seeking reform within the empire. What the record does not settle is how widely, outside the press and the elected deputies, a distinctly Palestinian political identity was held before the war, how many people read these papers, and what weight the villagers who received Filastin gave it. The sources used here give no circulation figures. They establish the name, the argument and the people making it; they do not measure its reach.
