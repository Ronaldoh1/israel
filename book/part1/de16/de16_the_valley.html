The Valley Turns

For most of the 2010s the technology industry's founders and investors were, in public, either apolitical or Democratic. By 2024 a visible part of it had moved right. Marc Andreessen, co-founder of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, known as a16z, endorsed Donald Trump; [cite:de16_techcrunch] Elon Musk, whose turn is told in [scene:de11_twitter_x_xai|Part Eleven], spent heavily for him; and a group of investors and engineers supplied the new administration with officials, recruiters and ideas. This section looks at the part of that turn that touches the texts at the centre of this book. It follows two men, Andreessen and the engineer and investor Balaji Srinivasan, through what they wrote and said in public, and places each on the ladder of support from [scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One], from author down to inference only, using only their own documented words and transactions.

Andreessen: from Democrat to "techno-optimist"

Andreessen co-founded a16z with Ben Horowitz and is one of Silicon Valley's best-known investors. By his own account he was a Democrat for most of his life: he said in July 2024 that he had endorsed or voted for Bill Clinton, Al Gore, John Kerry, Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton. [cite:de16_techcrunch] His break came in two public steps.

The first was a text. On 16 October 2023 he posted "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto" on the a16z website, about 5,200 words in short numbered declarations. [cite:de16_wiki_tom] [cite:de16_scanalyst] Its core claim is that technology and markets are the source of human progress and that slowing either costs lives. "We believe in accelerationism," it says, "the conscious and deliberate propulsion of technological development," to ensure "the techno-capital upward spiral continues forever." [cite:de16_lyons_p2p] [cite:de16_grosser] On artificial intelligence it goes further: "We believe any deceleration of AI will cost lives. Deaths that were preventable by the AI that was prevented from existing is a form of murder." [cite:de16_lyons_p2p] Its section on enemies names "stagnation," "statism, authoritarianism, collectivism, central planning, socialism," "bureaucracy, vetocracy, gerontocracy," and "corruption, regulatory capture, monopolies, cartels," along with "existential risk" and "tech ethics" as ideas. [cite:de16_scanalyst] [cite:de16_sfstandard] It closes with a list of 56 "patron saints of techno-optimism," beginning with three pseudonymous online accounts credited with founding "effective accelerationism" and including Friedrich Nietzsche, Milton Friedman and Nick Land. [cite:de16_sfstandard] [cite:de1_a16z_wiki]

Three things about the manifesto matter here. It names Land, the philosopher who coined "dark enlightenment," among its saints; that is a citation, documented, and it puts Andreessen on the rung of on-record engagement with Land. [cite:de1_a16z_wiki] Its "accelerationism" is not Land's. Land's, explained in [scene:de4_ccru_acceleration|Part Four], describes capital as an inhuman process that uses people and will outrun them; Andreessen's is a humanist promise of abundance, and one critic, Thomas Murphy, called the current it draws on "Nick Land diluted." [cite:de16_wiki_eacc] And its enemies list runs against its neighbours in this book: it names "authoritarianism" and "monopolies" as enemies, where Yarvin proposes a single executive and Thiel praised the creative monopoly. [cite:de16_scanalyst] [scene:de10_education_of_a_libertarian|Part Ten] The manifesto contains no proposal about how the American state should be governed.

The second step was an endorsement. On 16 July 2024, on their podcast, Andreessen and Horowitz explained why they would back Trump. Horowitz said "the future of our business, the future of technology, and the future of America is at stake"; Andreessen called President Biden's proposed tax on unrealised capital gains "the final straw," and praised Trump's plans for cryptocurrency and artificial intelligence. [cite:de16_techcrunch] Their stated reasons were policy toward their industry, and they gave them in public.

After the election Andreessen moved from donor to recruiter. In December 2024 the Washington Post and the Financial Times reported that, without any formal title, he was identifying and interviewing candidates for the Department of Government Efficiency, including people from his own circle. [cite:de16_fortune_doge] That work is part of the story of DOGE's staffing in [scene:de18_the_team|Part Eighteen]; the officials his firm supplied to the administration are in [scene:de16_elite_pipeline|the next section]. The Washington Post has reported that he is a member of the Rockbridge Network, a private donor group discussed in [scene:de14_foundations_and_opacity|Part Fourteen]. [cite:de14_wapo_rockbridge]

His ties to Yarvin are of two kinds. His firm invested in Yarvin's company, Tlon, alongside Thiel's Founders Fund, and was again sought as an investor in 2025, as [scene:de5_tlon_exit_and_return|Part Five] records; that is the patron rung, a venture investment with no documented link to Yarvin's political writing. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] And Andreessen has called Yarvin a "good friend," which is a statement of friendship, not of agreement. [cite:de5_sfstandard] No published text by Andreessen endorses Yarvin's program of a sovereign executive.

Srinivasan: exit, then the network state

Balaji Srinivasan is an engineer and investor who became a general partner at a16z in December 2013 and was chief technology officer of the cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase from 2018 to 2019; the first Trump administration considered him for commissioner of the Food and Drug Administration in 2017. [cite:de16_wiki_balaji] His political writing is about exit, the same word Land and Thiel used, which comes from the economist Albert Hirschman's distinction between leaving an organisation and complaining within it. In 2013, at the start-up school run by the accelerator Y Combinator, he gave a talk titled "Silicon Valley's Ultimate Exit," urging the industry to leave the United States, digitally and physically, rather than reform it. [cite:de16_wiki_balaji]

His 2022 book The Network State turned the idea into a design. A network state, he wrote, is "a social network with a moral innovation, a sense of national consciousness, a recognised founder," along with in-person communities, a built-in cryptocurrency and a social contract written in software, which would crowdfund territory and eventually seek diplomatic recognition. [cite:de16_maverick] [cite:de16_wiki_balaji] It is a theory of founding new polities by consent and purchase, not of taking over existing ones. In September 2024 he opened a "Network School" on an island development in Forest City, Malaysia; in July 2026 Malaysian authorities ordered it to shut for alleged administrative violations, and it moved to Kazakhstan under a five-year memorandum of understanding with the government. [cite:de16_wiki_balaji] Where these projects sit among the exit zones is in [scene:de22_seas_and_zones|Part Twenty-Two].

Srinivasan has one documented contact with Yarvin. In February 2021 the New York Times reported a 2013 email in which Srinivasan wrote to Yarvin that, if things got heated, it might be interesting to "sic the Dark Enlightenment audience on a single vulnerable hostile reporter to dox them," that is, to publish the reporter's private details; he did not respond to a request for comment on the report. [cite:de16_bi_balaji] That email is his own written words, and it shows both acquaintance and a willingness, in private, to think of Yarvin's readers as an audience that could be aimed at a journalist. It is not a statement of agreement with Yarvin's program, and his published theory differs from it: the network state is founded by people who choose to join, while Yarvin's sovereign corporation rules people who happen to live in its territory.

The turn is documented, and it was made in public, for stated reasons that were mostly about taxes, cryptocurrency and artificial intelligence. Its texts share a vocabulary with the Dark Enlightenment, exit and acceleration above all, and one of them names Land as a saint. What they do not share is the destination. Neither Andreessen's manifesto nor Srinivasan's book proposes a sovereign executive over an existing country; the first names authoritarianism as an enemy, and the second asks people to leave and found something new. The shared words are real, and so is the difference in what they are used to argue for. [cite:de16_scanalyst] [cite:de16_maverick] [cite:de16_techcrunch]
