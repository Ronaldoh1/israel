From Prayer to Program: The Thinkers Before Herzl

A common shorthand holds that Zionism sprang fully formed from the mind of Theodor Herzl in 1896. The record describes something slower. The idea moved through stages, and each stage was carried by different people for different reasons. Herzl arrived at the end of that sequence, not at its beginning.

For centuries, the return to Zion existed as a hope rather than a plan. It was prayed for daily and awaited as a messianic event, an act of God rather than a matter for political organization. Nothing in that liturgical longing amounted to a program. It named a destination and a redemption, but it left the timing and the means to heaven.

The first break with that waiting came from within religious tradition itself. Yehuda Alkalai, a Sephardi rabbi in Semlin, responded to the Damascus blood-libel affair of 1840 by urging practical settlement in the land as a step toward redemption. [cite:w_alkalai] Zvi Hirsch Kalischer of Thorn went further in a book published in 1862, arguing that redemption could begin by natural means, through people settling and farming the land. [cite:w_kalischer] These two rabbis were the first to argue that human settlement could begin what had been left to divine action. [cite:w_alkalai][cite:w_kalischer] Their argument was still theological, but it changed who was expected to act.

The same year as Kalischer's book, the idea crossed into secular politics. Moses Hess, a socialist, published Rome and Jerusalem in 1862. Twenty years later, after the pogroms of 1881, an Odessa doctor named Leon Pinsker published Auto-Emancipation (1882), which argued that Jews needed a territory of their own. That territory was not necessarily Palestine. With Hess and Pinsker, a socialist and a frightened physician turned a religious hope into something closer to a national program; their work is taken up in more detail in [scene:s02|the section on the idea's first appearance].

Organization followed the writing. Local societies calling themselves Hovevei Zion, the "Lovers of Zion," spread across the Russian Empire, and in 1884 they met at Kattowitz under Pinsker's leadership. [cite:w_hovevei] A student group known as BILU sent pioneers to the land in 1882. [cite:w_bilu] These were small efforts, involving a few hundred pioneers rather than a mass movement. Yet they gave the idea members, meetings and people on the ground. In 1890 the Viennese writer Nathan Birnbaum supplied the name: he coined the word "Zionism." [cite:w_birnbaum]

The motives recorded across these writers were several, and they did not all point in the same direction. There was persecution, most sharply the pogroms. There was disappointment that emancipation had not ended antisemitism. There was the wider age of European nationalism. And there was religious longing, the oldest strand, now reinterpreted as a call to act. To these internal motives an external one was joined: Christian restorationism in Britain and America, a Protestant current that favored a Jewish return for its own reasons and that is examined in [scene:bc_s05|Restoration Before Zionism].

Seen in sequence, the path runs from prayer, to rabbis who argued for human action, to political writers, to societies and pioneers, and finally to a word. What Herzl added in 1897 was not the idea. He gave a scattered movement an organization, a diplomacy and a bank. That distinction matters for everything that follows in this book. A movement that had to be assembled from separate thinkers and small groups over half a century was a human construction, shaped by particular pressures at particular moments. Its later unity was an achievement of organization, not evidence that the outcome had been settled from the start.
