The Transfer Agreement, 1933–1939

On 25 August 1933 Zionist bodies signed an agreement with the government of Nazi Germany. It let German Jews who were emigrating to Palestine take part of their assets with them, not as money but as German goods. Over the following six years it helped roughly 60,000 people reach Palestine. It also undercut the worldwide boycott of Nazi Germany that had begun in 1933, and it divided the Jewish world more sharply than any other financial arrangement of the pre-state period. It was called the Haavara, Hebrew for "transfer."

A common assumption holds that the Zionist movement and Nazi Germany were simply adversaries from 1933 onward. The documents show something harder to summarize. The two were adversaries, and they also made a commercial agreement, each for its own reasons. Two popular versions of the story each keep only half of that record. One calls Haavara a straightforward rescue; the other calls it collaboration. The documents support both halves at once: a mechanism that moved some 60,000 people out of Nazi reach, and a commercial accommodation that the Nazi state wanted for reasons of its own, chosen by the Zionist movement's own Congress over the objections of some of its own leaders [cite:jvl_haavara].

The agreement had three parties: the Zionist Federation of Germany; the Anglo-Palestine Bank, acting for the Jewish Agency; and the Reich Economics Ministry [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. Its mechanism was straightforward in outline. An emigrating German Jew deposited assets in an account in Germany. Those funds were used to buy German export goods, which were shipped to Palestine. When the goods were sold there, the emigrant was repaid in Palestine from the proceeds [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara].

The design explains why each party signed. For the emigrant, it was a way to move part of a lifetime's assets out of Germany. For the Zionist bodies, it brought immigrants and goods to Palestine. For the Nazi state, according to the record, it served two aims. The regime wanted Germany Judenrein, emptied of Jews, and it wanted to break the anti-Nazi boycott of 1933, which had mass Jewish support and which German officials regarded as an economic threat [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. Haavara did both. Jews left, and German goods found a market abroad at the very moment a boycott was trying to close markets to them. The rise of that regime and the destruction of the Weimar Republic are told in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse].

The scale was large for its time. The agreement was a major factor in the emigration of approximately 60,000 German Jews to Palestine between 1933 and 1939 [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. By the end of 1937, goods worth 77.8 million Reichsmarks had moved; by the outbreak of war, about 105 million [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. The Jewish Virtual Library records the total transfer as £8.1 million in Palestine pounds, roughly $40.4 million at the time, and states that this included £2.6 million provided by the Reichsbank [cite:jvl_haavara]. The war ended the program. How these German arrivals fit into the immigration of the 1930s as a whole, including the peak year of 1935, is set out in [scene:rd3_aliyot|the immigration section].

One figure in the sources does not agree with the others. The Center for Israel Education's account of the period gives the total moved through Haavara as $100 million [cite:rs_rd3_cie_arlosoroff]. The Jewish Virtual Library's figure, built up from the £8.1 million total, is about $40.4 million [cite:jvl_haavara]. The sources gathered here do not explain the difference, and this book uses the lower, itemized figure while noting the higher one. The same account ties the German trip that Chaim Arlosoroff, head of the Jewish Agency's political department, made shortly before his murder in June 1933 to the Haavara agreement; that claim is weighed in [scene:rd3_arlosoroff|the Arlosoroff section].

Two charts compiled for this library set Haavara beside the other documented money of the pre-state years. The first, of US-dollar flows in the record from 1921 to 1949, shows Haavara at about $40 million for 1933–39, one of the largest bars on the chart. The chart's own note warns that its figures are nominal dollars of the year reported, not adjusted for inflation, and that Haavara has been converted at the period estimate [cite:jvl_haavara]. The smallest bars matter too: the reported $0.15 million that sustained the American Christian committees founded by Emanuel Neumann, discussed in the section on the American Palestine Committee, appears near the foot of the same chart [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby].

The second chart, of documented fundraising and transfers in US dollars before independence, again places Haavara at about $40 million, converted from the 105 million Reichsmarks. Its note is a necessary caution: the bars record separate events, in different years and different currencies, and should not be added together as one budget [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. The other bars on both charts belong to the 1920s and to 1948–49 and are discussed in their own chapters.

The opposition was real and came from inside the movement as well as outside it. The Revisionist leader Ze'ev Jabotinsky, Rabbi Stephen Wise of the American Jewish Congress, and Abba Hillel Silver all opposed the agreement, on the ground that it sabotaged the boycott [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. Their case at its strongest was that the boycott had mass Jewish support and was regarded by German officials themselves as an economic threat, so that a Zionist agreement moving German goods abroad weakened pressure the regime actually felt. The defenders' case at its strongest was the one the numbers make: people were leaving Germany, they could take something with them only by this route, and tens of thousands reached Palestine through it.

The question went to the movement's highest body. The Nineteenth Zionist Congress, meeting at Lucerne in August 1935, voted by a large majority to continue the agreement and placed Haavara under the supervision of the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] [cite:jvl_haavara]. The opponents failed to persuade it. The decision was taken knowingly, two years into the agreement's operation and a month before Germany enacted the Nuremberg Laws. The historian Edwin Black wrote that the agreement "tore the Jewish world apart" [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement]. Haavara also served as the model for a smaller arrangement with Czechoslovakia on the eve of the war [cite:jvl_haavara].

The Haavara transfer appears as one stream among many on the map of how the pre-state build was financed, between the American donors of the 1920s and the fundraising and arms purchases of 1948.

What the record gathered here does not settle is considerable. It does not say how much of each emigrant's assets were lost in the transfer, or on what terms the goods were priced, so the cost to the emigrants themselves cannot be stated. It does not measure how much damage Haavara did to the boycott, only that it undercut it. It does not resolve the gap between the $40.4 million and $100 million totals. And it cannot answer the counterfactual on which the moral argument turns: how many of the 60,000 would have reached safety without it.
