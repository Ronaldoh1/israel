What the Gap Means in a Life

A score of 6.4 against 4.6 is easy to state and hard to feel. This section turns the gap into the terms a household lives in: how far each system takes a family towards the goals the scorecard sets, where the distance between them is greatest, what it adds up to over a lifetime, and what it does not mean. Every figure is one already scored or reported in this part; the lifetime totals are this book's arithmetic from annual figures held at today's values, and they illustrate scale rather than forecast anyone's life. Reading the distance

Each score measures how far a system gets from a floor, worth 0, to a goal, worth 10. On the nine areas a household feels, Israel's system takes its citizens about 64 per cent of the way and America's about 46 per cent. The gap of about 1.9 points means that, against the same goalposts, Israel's system carries a household about 1.4 times as far. On the first scorecard's 0–5 scale the gap is about 0.9, a little narrower than the 1.21 the simulation reported, and pointing the same way.

The gap is not spread evenly, and that is the most useful thing it says about the two systems. It is widest in housing, health, household debt, the family budget, and family support and the safety net, the areas where Israeli law guarantees what Americans are left to arrange and pay for themselves: coverage, paid leave, sick pay, a pension, an unemployment benefit that replaces more of a lost paycheck, and child support the state pays when a parent does not. It runs the other way in schools, where American fifteen-year-olds read better, and in work and wages, where Americans earn about half as much again on average and work fewer hours. In short, the American system pays its people more and asks them to buy their own security; the Israeli system pays less, charges more for food and housing, and builds more security into law.

Over a lifetime

Annual differences add up over the length of a life. The panel below sets out what the gap amounts to for one person or one family, from birth to old age, if each year looked like the latest one.

Some of these totals favour Israel and one does not. An Israeli lives about 4.3 years longer and about 7.7 more years in good health, is guaranteed about 480 days of paid vacation across a forty-year working life where American federal law guarantees none, and, on 2023 figures, pays about $642 a year less out of pocket for health care, which for a family of four over forty years comes to about $100,000. An Israeli also works about 2,800 more hours over the same forty years, and pays more for food and housing every year of it. When things go wrong

The gap is largest at the moments a family does not choose: a birth, an illness, a layoff, a parent who stops paying, old age. Those are the moments the household score weighs most heavily, and the moments people who have lived in both countries most often describe.

Take a worker on two-thirds of the average wage who is laid off. In Israel the first months' benefit replaces about 65 per cent of what he took home; in the United States about 55 per cent, and for an average earner 59 against 38 per cent. If his child falls ill, Israeli law gives him paid sick days that build up at a day and a half a month; American federal law gives none, though 81 per cent of private-sector workers get them from their employers. If the child's other parent stops paying support, Israel's National Insurance Institute pays the award and pursues the parent; an American parent waits on enforcement that collected 65 per cent of current support due in 2024. And at 66 and over, 22.9 per cent of Americans were poor on the OECD's measure against 15.3 per cent of Israelis: about one in four and a half against one in six and a half. What the gap does not mean

Four limits keep the number honest. First, a system score is not a verdict on every life inside it. An American on a high salary with good employer coverage buys much of what Israeli law guarantees, and the American average wage, about half as much again as Israel's in purchasing-power terms, pays for a great deal; the systems differ most for households without that cover, which is where the household score weighs. Second, Israel's average hides the Arab and Haredi households whose poverty runs well above it, and leaves out the Palestinians it governs without citizenship, for whom the guarantees in this section largely do not exist. Third, some of Israel's guarantees, the pension and the daycare subsidy among them, follow employment, so they reach working families more fully than others. Fourth, the gap is not paid for by the United States: one of the final section's tests takes the full value of American aid out of Israel's family benefits, and the household score moves only from 6.43 to 6.39.
