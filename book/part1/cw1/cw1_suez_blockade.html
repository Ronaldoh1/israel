The Canal Closed to Israel: Egypt's Restrictions and Resolution 95, 1951

The armistice between Egypt and Israel stopped the fighting. It did not, in Egypt's view, end the war. The clearest test of that view came not on the armistice line but at the Suez Canal, where Egypt stopped and searched ships and seized goods bound for Israel. In the summer of 1951 the practice came before the Security Council, and on 1 September 1951 the Council ruled on it. The resolution it adopted, Resolution 95, is short. The argument behind it, over whether a state could remain at war with a neighbour it had signed an armistice with, ran on for years.

Egypt's legal case deserves to be stated first and at full strength, because it was a legal case and not merely a refusal. Leo Gross, writing in the American Journal of International Law in 1957, reconstructed it from the record. [cite:rs_cw1_gross_ajil_1957] Its foundation was that a state of belligerency still existed between Egypt and Israel. In the words Gross quotes from the record, the measures were taken "in the state of belligerency which in our opinion still exists between Egypt and Israel." An armistice, on this reading, suspended hostilities; it was not peace. A belligerent at war keeps the rights of a belligerent, among them the right of visit, search and seizure of contraband, and Egypt's regulations of 1950 rested on that right. [cite:rs_cw1_gross_ajil_1957]

Three further arguments stood on that foundation. The first concerned the Constantinople Convention of 1888. Israel was not a signatory, and Egypt argued that the Convention did not bind it toward a state that had not signed. The second concerned the Security Council's competence: Egypt maintained that the Council lacked authority over the matter. The third was practical. The restrictions, Egypt said, guarded against sabotage in the Canal. [cite:rs_cw1_gross_ajil_1957] Taken together, the case was coherent. If the war continued, the searches were lawful acts of war; if Israel had no rights under the Convention, it could not complain of a breach of it; and if the Council had no authority, its view did not bind.

The case against Egypt was made in the Council debate. On 16 August 1951, at the 553rd meeting, the representative of Ecuador answered Egypt's foundation directly. "There are at present no real hostilities," he said. Self-defence could not be invoked without an actual armed attack, and the interference with shipping could not be reconciled with the Constantinople Convention. [cite:rs_cw1_spv553] Ecuador's argument turned Egypt's own terms around. Egypt claimed a war; Ecuador pointed to the absence of any fighting. Egypt claimed self-defence; Ecuador asked where the attack was.

The Council adopted its resolution on 1 September 1951, at its 558th meeting, by eight votes to none. The United States voted in favour. China, India and the Soviet Union abstained. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] [cite:rs_cw1_spv558] The text proceeded in steps. It noted that the armistice regime had now lasted nearly two and a half years and was "of a permanent character," and that Egypt had not heeded the plea of the UN Chief of Staff to stop "interfering with the passage through the Suez Canal of goods destined for Israel." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] It then gave its findings. Egypt's practice was "an abuse of the exercise of the right of visit, search and seizure." It could not be justified as self-defence. It was an "unjustified interference with the rights of nations to navigate the seas and to trade freely with one another." And, the Council held, neither party could reasonably claim to be "actively a belligerent." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95]

Its demand followed from those findings. The Council called on Egypt to terminate the restrictions on "the passage of international commercial shipping and goods" through the Canal, except for what was needed for the safety of shipping in the Canal itself. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] The exception is worth noticing. The Council did not deny Egypt all control over the Canal; it allowed whatever measures the safety of the waterway required, and ruled out only those aimed at Israel-bound trade.

The resolution answered Egypt's foundation without using Egypt's terms. Egypt had said that belligerency continued; the Council said the armistice was of a permanent character and that no party could be "actively a belligerent." It did not declare the war over, and it did not say the armistice was peace. It said, in effect, that whatever the formal state of relations, a party to an armistice of two and a half years' standing could not exercise wartime rights against the other's trade. The abstentions are part of the record of what the Council decided. Three of its members declined to vote for that conclusion.

The final exchange at the 558th meeting shows how far apart the two sides remained once the vote was taken. Egypt's representative, Mahmoud Fawzi, told the Council that "Peace is acts and facts." He spoke of people driven from their homes, likened the Council's demand to an unconditional surrender, and reserved his government's rights. [cite:rs_cw1_spv558] The record cited here does not say further whom he meant; twelve days later the Conciliation Commission opened its conference in Paris on the future of the Palestinian refugees, as told in [scene:cw1_refugees|the refugee question after Lausanne]. Eban, for Israel, read the vote the other way. The Council, he said, had rejected "one-sided belligerency or unilateral blockade" and had affirmed the armistice as a path to permanent peace. [cite:rs_cw1_spv558] For Eban the armistice was a step toward peace that Egypt was refusing to take; for Fawzi a demand to behave as if at peace, while the conditions of peace were absent, was a demand for surrender.

The mechanism behind the dispute was simple, and that is why it was hard to move. Egypt held the Canal. Its regulations of 1950 gave its officials the instrument: the right to stop, search and seize. The only body that could pronounce on the practice was the Security Council, whose competence Egypt disputed, and the Council's resolution called on Egypt to act. It did not provide any means to make it act. Israel, whose trade bore the cost, could complain but could not open the Canal. The United States, which voted for the resolution, could press Egypt; in the same autumn it was also seeking, with Britain, Turkey and France, Egypt's partnership in a regional defence command, as told in [scene:cw1_mec|the Middle East Command]. The vote of 1 September and the invitation of 13 October were addressed to the same government six weeks apart.

The dispute itself should be left where the sources leave it. Egypt's position, that a state of belligerency survived the armistice, that the Constantinople Convention did not run in favour of a non-signatory, and that the Council lacked authority, was argued on legal grounds and is documented as Egypt's position. [cite:rs_cw1_gross_ajil_1957] The opposite position, that the armistice was permanent in character, that no party could claim to be actively a belligerent, and that the searches were an abuse of visit and search, was the position of the resolution and of Israel and Ecuador in the debate. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] [cite:rs_cw1_spv558] [cite:rs_cw1_spv553] What the Council decided is a matter of record: by eight votes to none it adopted the second view. Whether that decision settled the legal question is a separate matter, and three abstaining members, along with Egypt's own reservation of its rights, show that it was not settled by agreement.

Several things the sources used here do not record. They do not give the number of ships stopped or the value of the cargoes seized under the 1950 regulations. They do not explain why China, India and the Soviet Union abstained. And they do not show what Egypt did after 1 September 1951 in response to the resolution beyond Fawzi's reservation of rights. The Canal's story after 1951, through the crisis of 1956, belongs to Stage Three.
