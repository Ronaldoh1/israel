The King David Hotel Bombing, 22 July 1946

The bombing of the King David Hotel is sometimes passed over as an incident in a British office building, one act of violence among many in the last years of the Mandate. The numbers alone tell against that view. On 22 July 1946 an explosion in the hotel's southern wing in Jerusalem killed 91 people, British, Arab and Jewish. It was the deadliest single attack against the British in the whole Mandate period. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] It was carried out by an organization whose commander would later lead the government of Israel, it drew a public condemnation from the Jewish Agency, and it hardened British opinion toward leaving Palestine. Each of these points is on the record, and each is taken in turn below.

From Agatha to the hotel. The attack came twenty-three days after Operation Agatha. On 29 June 1946 British forces had arrested more than 2,700 people, including senior Jewish Agency officials, and seized documents from the Agency's Jerusalem headquarters; the documents were believed to link the Agency's leadership to Haganah operations, and they were taken to the Secretariat wing of the King David Hotel to be processed and stored (see [scene:s07e3|Operation Agatha]). [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] The hotel's southern wing housed the Mandate Secretariat, the central administrative offices of the Mandate government, and the British military command; hotel business carried on in the rest of the building. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The Irgun, assessing that the seized papers were held there, planned the attack in direct response to the raid, with the documented aims of destroying that evidence and striking the administration that had carried out the arrests. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

The attack. The Irgun was commanded at the time by Menachem Begin. [cite:wiki_menachem_begin] Its fighters placed explosives, reportedly concealed in milk churns, in the basement of the southern wing, beneath the Secretariat and the military command, and detonated them. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The explosion killed 91 people. They were a mix of British, Arab and Jewish civilians and administrative personnel. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The sources give the total and the fact that the dead came from all three communities; they do not itemize who each person was or what work each did. Many had no connection to the administrative functions the Irgun meant to strike. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

The warning calls. Whether those inside were warned is one of the oldest disputes about the attack, and it has not been resolved. The Irgun's own account holds that warning calls were placed about 25 minutes before the detonation to three places: the hotel switchboard, the French consulate and the office of the Palestine Post newspaper, each urging evacuation. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] British investigators, and later historians, have disputed whether any warning actually reached the hotel's management in time to act on it, or whether a call that did arrive was dismissed as a hoax. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

Each side has a case. The Irgun's account is specific: it names the time, the three recipients and the purpose of the calls, and, taken on its own terms, it presents an intention to empty the building rather than to kill those in it. The British case rests on the outcome and on the investigation: 91 people did not leave, and the investigators doubted that any usable warning had reached the people who could have ordered an evacuation. Even on the Irgun's own account, the question of whether a warning of about 25 minutes, sent partly to outside offices, was adequate remains open. The record does not settle which account is right, and this book does not pretend that it does.

The Jewish Agency's condemnation. The Jewish Agency publicly condemned the bombing. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The condemnation carried an edge that is easy to miss. It was the Agency's own documents, seized in Agatha, that the attack was meant to destroy. And the Haganah, whose ties to the Agency's leadership the seized documents were believed to show, had itself been part of the united Jewish Resistance Movement with the Irgun and Lehi since September 1945, carrying out the Night of the Bridges only five weeks earlier through its Palmach. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] The condemnation is therefore a real instance, in the open, of the tension between the mainstream political leadership and the paramilitary groups, played out around the most consequential single attack of the period. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The sources record the public condemnation; they do not record the internal debate behind its wording or the private views of individual Agency officials.

Did it end the united resistance? A reader will want to know whether the bombing broke the alliance. The sequence is suggestive: about two weeks later, in early August 1946, the Jewish Agency's leadership, meeting in Paris, decided to halt armed action until the Zionist Congress met, and the Congress confirmed the halt in December. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] But the only source for those decisions, the Lehi heritage association, does not say that the bombing caused them, and no other source verified for this book does either (see [scene:rd5_revolt|the Jewish Resistance Movement]). The order of events is clear; the link between them is not established.

What it changed in London. The bombing is documented as having significantly hardened British public and parliamentary opinion toward disengaging from Palestine altogether. It contributed directly to Britain's decision, the next year, to hand the whole question to the United Nations. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] It was one factor among several. The same record that ties the bombing to Britain's change of mind does not claim that the attack alone caused the referral of 1947; other pressures were at work, and how much weight the armed campaign carried in Britain's withdrawal is itself disputed, as the section on [scene:rd5_britain_exits|Britain's exit] sets out.

Begin, then and later. Menachem Begin personally commanded the Irgun at the time of the bombing. He later served as Israel's sixth Prime Minister, from 1977 to 1983. [cite:wiki_menachem_begin] The line from the command of 1946 to the premiership three decades later is direct and documented. What the sources used here do not provide is Begin's own account: his justification for the attack and what he said about it in later years are not part of this material, and they are not supplied here.

The claim and the record. The view of the bombing as a minor, passing incident does not survive the facts. Ninety-one people from three communities were killed in a single attack, the largest such toll against the British in the Mandate. Its commander went on to lead the government of Israel. It drew a public condemnation from the Jewish Agency, and it moved British opinion toward leaving. On its own documented merits it was among the most consequential single events of the Mandate period. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

What the record does not settle. Three questions stay open. The first is the warning: whether the calls the Irgun describes were made as it says, whether any reached someone able to order an evacuation, and whether they were treated as a hoax. The second is the full list of the dead, by name, nationality and role, which the sources here do not give. The third is cause and effect after the attack: how far the bombing drove the Jewish Agency's August decision to halt armed action, and how far it, rather than the other pressures on Britain, drove the decision to go to the United Nations. On all three, the documents available here establish the sequence and leave the weighing to further evidence.
