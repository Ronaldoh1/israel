By What Right? How a Letter Became International Law

When Balfour signed his letter on November 2, 1917, Palestine was still part of the Ottoman Empire. British forces had taken Beersheba on October 31. They would not enter Jerusalem until December 9. [cite:w_sinai_palestine] The familiar picture of a lawful grant, made by the rightful authority over the land, does not survive the calendar. The declaration was a statement of policy by one government about territory it did not yet hold or govern. On its own, it carried no legal force there.

Its wording had a history of its own. The text grew out of a draft formula that Lord Walter Rothschild sent to Balfour on July 18, 1917, after months of drafting with Zionist leaders. The final letter was addressed to him as the most prominent figure in British Jewry, to be passed on to the Zionist Federation. [cite:w_balfour_decl] His name on the envelope reflected his standing and the formal route the letter traveled. Accounts of his part differ. This one has him sending the draft formula; the sources behind the previous section treat him as a figurehead addressee, and credit the drafting to Weizmann, Sokolow and Sykes.

Legal force came later, and it came from the war's victors. At San Remo in 1920 the Allied powers assigned Britain the mandate, a form of governing trusteeship, for Palestine. On July 24, 1922, the Council of the League of Nations approved the text of the Mandate, with Balfour's language in its preamble. Yet the League's own founding Covenant had set a standard for territories in Palestine's position. For such communities, it stated, "the wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration in the selection of the Mandatory." [cite:avalon_covenant] The Allies and the League turned the letter into law even as their own charter required that the inhabitants' wishes weigh heavily in choosing who would govern them.

Balfour himself was candid about how little those wishes counted. In a memorandum of 1919 he wrote: [cite:w_balfour_decl]

"in Palestine we do not propose even to go through the form of consulting the wishes of the present inhabitants"

Speaking to the League in 1922, he described a mandate as "a self-imposed limitation by the conquerors on the sovereignty which they exercised over the conquered territory." [cite:un_league_journal_1922] By his own account, then, the authority rested on conquest, tempered only by limits the conquerors chose to accept. That is a different claim from the one often made on the declaration's behalf, and it came from the man whose name it bears.

Nor did the policy pass without objection. The objections were on the record at the time. Palestinian Arab congresses opposed it, as [scene:pp_s01|the section on the Palestinian national movement] sets out. In 1919 an American commission sent by President Wilson, known as the King–Crane Commission, reported that the population was against it. And in London, on June 21, 1922, the House of Lords voted 60 to 29 for a motion by Lord Islington declaring the Mandate unacceptable in its present form. [cite:stausaus_1922_lords] [cite:spectator_1922_lords] The vote did not bind the government, which held to its policy.

Across the Atlantic, the reception was different. In September 1922 the United States Congress endorsed the Balfour Declaration unanimously. It did so after hearings at which anti-Zionist Jewish witnesses and Palestinian Arab witnesses had also testified. [cite:jps_1922_congress] The opposition was heard. The endorsement was unanimous all the same.

Taken together, these records give a specific answer to the question of legitimacy. The authority behind the national home did not come from any act of the people living in Palestine. Nor did it come from Ottoman sovereignty, which still held the land when the letter was written. It came from the war's winners, acting first through their own conference and then through the League they had created. Palestinians, an American commission and a majority of Britain's upper house all objected on the record. The policy went ahead. [cite:stausaus_1922_lords]
