Schools: Streams, Money and Scores

Both countries promise free public schooling, but they organise it in very different ways. Israel runs one national ministry that funds several separate school streams, each with its own curriculum, language and religious character. The United States has no national school system at all: public schools are run by local districts under state law and paid for mostly by states and local property taxes. This section sets out how each is built, what each spends, who is left behind and how pupils have scored; the next turns to universities and debt. [scene:us17_higher|Universities, cost and debt]

Israel: one ministry, separate streams

Israel's Education Ministry funds four main streams: Hebrew-language state schools, state-religious schools, Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) schools, and Arabic-language schools for Arab citizens. The balance among them is shifting fast. In 2021–22 about 373,000 pupils were in Haredi education, 25.5 per cent of Hebrew-language pupils and 19.5 per cent of all pupils in the country. [cite:us17_idi_haredi2022] An analysis of Education Ministry data by Eli Morgenstern, a former senior Finance Ministry official, found that first-grade enrolment in secular Hebrew state schools fell by about 12 per cent between 2023 and 2026 while Haredi first-grade enrolment rose by about 11 per cent. In 2025 Arab schools had about 23 per cent of first graders and state-religious schools about 16 per cent; on his projection of current trends, Haredi schools will be the largest stream for first graders by 2030. [cite:us17_ynet_firstgrade]

The streams are funded differently, and the Haredi stream is funded on terms that no other stream receives. Only about 7 per cent of Haredi pupils attended the state's own Haredi schools in 2024–25. The rest attend schools that are privately run but state-funded in part or in full. "Exempt" schools receive about 55 per cent of the per-pupil funding of a state school in return for teaching 55 per cent of the core curriculum of language, mathematics, science and English; "recognised but unofficial" schools receive 75 per cent for teaching 75 per cent; and the two large networks tied to the Shas and United Torah Judaism parties are fully funded and expected to teach all of it. [cite:us17_toi_haredi_core] A study by Ariel Finkelstein for the Israel Democracy Institute, published in July 2025, found that the ministry did not enforce the curriculum in exempt and unrecognised schools; only 6 per cent of network schools had faced a budget cut, averaging NIS 225,000 in 2023–24; no school had ever been closed; and at least half of Haredi schools, by his conservative estimate, did not comply. In an earlier survey, 76 per cent of Haredi parents of boys said their sons learned no English at all. The Education Ministry replied that supervision of core studies "is carried out regularly and according to the procedures" and that "when violations are identified, clear enforcement measures are implemented." [cite:us17_toi_haredi_core] The rules have swung with coalitions. A 2013 law pressed by Yesh Atid made core subjects a condition of funding from 2018; on 24 July 2016, under a coalition deal with United Torah Judaism, ministers voted to repeal that clause and give the education minister discretion to fund schools that do not teach them. [cite:us17_toi_core2016] How this fits the wider bargain over Haredi exemptions is set out in Part Seven. [scene:us7_haredi|Part Seven: the Haredi exemptions]

The gap for Arab schools runs the other way: they teach the full curriculum but have long received less money per pupil. The Taub Center's study of official primary schools from 2014 to 2023 found that the per-pupil gap between Hebrew state-religious and Arab state schools narrowed from about 15 per cent to about 12 per cent, and concluded that "Arab State education continues to receive less funding than does Hebrew education." [cite:us17_taub_primary2025] The outcomes differ sharply by stream. In 2011, 48.1 per cent of Israeli pupils completed a matriculation certificate (the bagrut): 65 per cent of Jewish pupils outside the Haredi system, 38.2 per cent of non-Jewish pupils and 8.1 per cent of Haredi pupils. [cite:us17_toi_bagrut2011] In 2018–19, 14 per cent of Haredi pupils earned the certificate against 83 per cent in the state and state-religious streams; Haredi girls sitting at least one matriculation exam rose from 31 to 59 per cent between 2008–09 and 2019–20, while for Haredi boys the share stayed at about 15 per cent. [cite:us17_idi_haredi2022] These figures measure what each stream's schools are funded, required and permitted to teach, not the ability of the children in them.

The United States: fifty systems and the property tax

American public schools spent $17,619 per pupil in the 2024 fiscal year, up 6.6 per cent from $16,526 the year before, in figures the Census Bureau does not adjust for inflation. Of $994.9 billion in revenue, states provided 45.2 per cent, local governments 43.2 per cent and the federal government 11.6 per cent; property taxes made up 63.3 per cent of local revenue. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] Because so much money is raised locally, spending depends on where a child lives: it ranged from $31,918 per pupil in New York to $11,060 in Idaho. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] The federal role is small and shrinking. The Education Department had just over 4,100 employees when President Trump took office in January 2025; after the Supreme Court allowed mass layoffs to proceed in July 2025, the department was effectively cut in half, adult and career education were moved to the Labor Department, and, as reported in July 2025, moves of student aid to the Treasury and special education to Health and Human Services were proposed. Abolishing the department outright requires an act of Congress. [cite:us17_74_ed] The 2025 reconciliation law also created a federal Education Freedom Tax Credit, effective from 1 January 2027 in states that opt in; 31 states had done so or said they would by 28 July 2026. [cite:us7_k12dive_taxcredit] Part Seven sets out the church-and-state questions it raises. [scene:us7_us|Part Seven: religion and state in the United States]

What each spends, on one definition

The OECD's Education at a Glance puts both countries on one basis. In its 2023 edition Israel spent $10,279 per full-time student across all levels, in purchasing-power dollars, against $19,973 in the United States and an OECD average of $12,647. As a share of GDP the two were close: 6.4 per cent in Israel and 6 per cent in the United States, against about 5 per cent across the OECD. [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_isr] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_usa] The two facts fit together because Israel has far more children: 43.1 per cent of its population was under 25 in 2022, against an OECD average of 29.7 per cent. [cite:us17_toi_eag2024] Israel's money is spread across more pupils in larger classes, 27 per primary class against an OECD average of 21 in the 2026 edition, and its teachers are paid less: a teacher with fifteen years' experience in upper-secondary school earned a statutory $39,321 in Israel in 2022, against $69,641 in the United States. [cite:us17_ynet_eag2026] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_isr] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_usa] No part of the American military aid to Israel goes into its education budget; the memorandum money is military financing. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [scene:gi16_aid|The aid]

Scores over time: PISA, 2000–2025

The OECD's PISA tests 15-year-olds every three years or so and is the only long series covering both countries on one scale. Israel sat the first round in 2002, two years late, and has taken part in every round since 2006. [cite:us17_oecd_pisa2018_isr] [cite:us17_wiki_pisa] In mathematics, Israel's average rose from 433 in that first round and 442 in 2006 to 466 in 2012, then slipped to 458 by 2022; the American average fell from 493 in 2000 to 465 in 2022. In reading, Israel rose from 439 in 2006 to 486 in 2012 and stood at 474 in 2022, while the United States stayed near 500 throughout, at 504 in 2022. In science the United States scored 499 in 2022 and Israel 465. [cite:us17_wiki_pisa] The OECD's own reading of the Israeli series was that most of the gains came before 2012 and that the gap between advantaged and disadvantaged pupils was wide and growing: 121 points in reading in 2018, against an OECD average of 89, up from 103 in 2009. [cite:us17_oecd_pisa2018_isr]

The 2025 round, sat in March and April 2025 and published on 8 September 2026, recorded a sharp fall in Israel. Reading dropped from 474 to 436, the lowest Israel has recorded, mathematics from 458 to 433, and science from 465 to 442; Israel ranked between 31st and 33rd of 38 OECD countries, against OECD averages of 461, 463 and 482. [cite:us17_ih_pisa2025] The test was taken eighteen months into the war that began in October 2023; the results do not by themselves show how much of the fall the war explains. The American reading score fell 14 points to 490, the same fall as the OECD average, while mathematics, at 463, and science were not significantly changed. [cite:us17_nw_pisa2025] [cite:us17_edweek_pisa2025] The United States ranked 13th in reading and science and 27th in mathematics, and had the widest gap between its highest- and lowest-scoring pupils in reading of any country tested. [cite:us17_gma_pisa2025] [cite:us17_edweek_pisa2025] America's national assessment shows the same pattern: in 2024 American twelfth-graders' average mathematics score was the lowest since 2005, their reading score the lowest on record, and the gap between high and low performers in mathematics the widest ever measured. [cite:us17_chalkbeat_naep]

Each system's weak point follows its structure. In the United States, spending gaps follow district lines drawn by local wealth, and its reading-score gap was the widest of any country tested in 2025. In Israel, the stream a child enters sets what the school is required and funded to teach, and the state has chosen, under successive coalitions, to fund schools that do not teach the full core curriculum; many Haredi boys' schools stop mathematics and English at sixth grade or earlier.
