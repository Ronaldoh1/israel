A Murder on the Beach and a Peace That Failed, 1933–1935

The Haavara agreement was signed inside a movement already at war with itself. On the night of 16 June 1933, Chaim Arlosoroff was walking on the beach at Tel Aviv with his wife, Sima, when he was shot. He died early the next morning, 17 June [cite:rs_rd3_cie_arlosoroff] [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst]. Arlosoroff was the head of the Jewish Agency's political department. His killing opened two years of open conflict between that leadership and the Revisionist movement led by Ze'ev Jabotinsky, a conflict that ran through a murder trial, a Zionist Congress, an attempt at reconciliation and a vote of the labor federation's members, and ended with the Revisionists leaving the Zionist Organization altogether.

Arlosoroff had come back from Germany about two days before he was shot. The Center for Israel Education links that trip to the Haavara transfer agreement with Nazi Germany, which was formally signed on 25 August 1933, some ten weeks after his death [cite:rs_rd3_cie_arlosoroff]. That link is reported by one source and is given here as its account; the agreement itself is examined in [scene:hv_s01|the Transfer Agreement section].

Three Revisionists were charged. Abba Ahimeir was accused of plotting the killing, and Avraham Stavsky and Zvi Rosenblatt of carrying it out as the gunmen. Sima Arlosoroff identified Stavsky and Rosenblatt [cite:rs_rd3_cie_arlosoroff]. The case did not hold. Only Stavsky was convicted, and in 1934 he was cleared on appeal for lack of corroborating evidence. No one else was ever charged with the murder [cite:rs_rd3_cie_arlosoroff] [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst].

The trial was fought in politics as much as in court. Jabotinsky, who believed the accused innocent, campaigned on their behalf for more than a year [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst]. The labor movement's leaders accused Revisionist extremists of the killing. At the Eighteenth Zionist Congress, which met in Prague from 21 August to 3 September 1933, the Revisionists were accused of the murder in the movement's own parliament [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst]. The Revisionists, for their part, charged that the accusation against them was a "blood libel" [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1985_panel].

The two camps' accounts can be set side by side, as far as the sources record them. Labour's account was that Revisionist extremists, the three accused, had killed Arlosoroff, and it could point to his widow's identification of two of the men. The Revisionists' account was that the accused were innocent and that the accusation was a defamation of their movement; the acquittal on appeal, for want of corroborating evidence, supported them. The sources gathered here record the accusation, the identification, the acquittal and Jabotinsky's campaign. They do not record the speeches or articles on either side.

Half a century later the state took the question up again. In March 1982 the government of Menachem Begin appointed a three-member commission chaired by David Bechor, a former Supreme Court justice. It reported in June 1985. It cleared Ahimeir, Stavsky and Rosenblatt. It could not determine who had killed Arlosoroff, or whether the killing was a political act at all. And it cleared the labor movement of the Revisionist charge that the accusation had been a "blood libel" [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1985_panel]. Each side was thus partly vindicated: the Revisionists in the innocence of the accused, labor in being cleared of the charge of a "blood libel." The murder itself remains unsolved.

The conflict did not wait for the courts. In 1934 Ben-Gurion and Jabotinsky, the leaders of the two camps, signed three agreements in London [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists_j] [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst]. One of them was a labor agreement. The sources gathered here do not give the exact dates on which the agreements were signed or set out their full terms. The labor agreement was put to the members of the Histadrut, the labor federation, who rejected it in a referendum [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists_j] [cite:rs_rd3_jabotinsky_inst].

The referendum was held on 25 March 1935. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency's report the next day gave early returns: about 12,000 votes against the agreement and about 9,000 for it. In Tel Aviv the count was 3,983 against and 3,296 for; in Haifa, 3,785 against and 1,555 for [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_referendum]. The newspaper Haaretz called the result "a grave mishap for the entire Zionist movement" [cite:rs_rd3_jta_1935_referendum]. The final totals were not in that report, and this book has not found them; the figures above are partial tallies only. Even so, they show a difference between the two cities. In Tel Aviv the vote was close; in Haifa it was more than two to one against.

With the agreement dead, the break followed. Later in 1935 the Zionist General Council adopted a "discipline clause" barring independent political action, the Revisionists voted to secede, and in September 1935 Jabotinsky's New Zionist Organization held its constituent assembly in Vienna [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. That story, and the growth of the Revisionist movement that preceded it, is told in [scene:s07a3|the Revisionist line].

The episode mattered beyond the fate of one man. On the reading offered here, the murder, and the accusation that followed it, hardened a division between the labor leadership and the Revisionists that the London agreements tried and failed to close. The secession that followed left the Zionist movement, by the end of 1935, with two rival organizations and two rival armed lines.

The record leaves the central question open. No court and no commission has established who killed Arlosoroff or why. The Bechor commission, half a century later, could not say whether the killing was political. The link between his German trip and Haavara rests on one source. The exact dates and full terms of the London agreements, and the final count of the 1935 referendum, are not in the sources gathered here.
