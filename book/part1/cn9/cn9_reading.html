Reading the Architecture: A Symbolic Reading

Everything before this section rests on documents. This section does not. It sets out how the pattern described in the previous section looks when read through the inner traditions whose books the canon left out, and through one modern esoteric idea. It is offered as a reading, a way of seeing the documented record, and it makes no claim that the record itself proves.

The first reading comes from the excluded books themselves. The Apocryphon of John, one of the texts Irenaeus attacked and the jar at Nag Hammadi preserved in four copies, tells a story of how the world came to be governed. Sophia, Wisdom, brings forth without the consent of the highest God an imperfect being named Yaldabaoth, who creates powers called archons to rule the lower world with him. He declares, "I am a jealous God, and there is no other God beside me", and the text remarks that by saying so he showed that another God exists. [cite:vt25_wiki_apjohn] [cite:vt25_ecw_apjohn] Adam is brought to life by a power stolen from above, the rulers send a counterfeit spirit to lead humanity astray, and a saviour comes as the remembrance of the source to awaken those who listen. [cite:vt25_wiki_apjohn] The text ends with a curse on anyone who will "exchange these things for a gift or for food or for drink or for clothing". [cite:vt24_apjohn]

Read as myth, the story is about the cosmos. Read as a lens on the history in this book, it describes an architecture that the documents have traced in another key: a power that stands between the person and the source, presents itself as the only way through, and keeps the person unaware that any other way exists. The Gospel of Thomas, another excluded text, states the alternative in a single saying: "the Kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you". [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] A kingdom inside needs no gatekeeper. A canon closed by an office, read only in Latin by its clergy and guarded by a list of forbidden books, is in this reading the outward form of the archons' gate, and the curse on selling the teaching reads, after 1517, like an indictment written thirteen centuries early. This is an interpretation. The documents do not show that anyone involved saw it in these terms.

The second reading uses a picture from engineering. Imagine a system in which every message, whatever its sender and whatever its content, must pass through a single switchboard before it reaches its destination. The switchboard's operator does not need to write the messages or forbid them all. It is enough to decide which ones are delivered, in what language and at what price. In plain terms, that is what the documents show the canon's guardians became: the single point through which a believer's access to scripture, sacrament and forgiveness was routed. Each step in the previous section, the list, the authorised reader, the enforcement, the revenue, the restriction of language, strengthened the switchboard. Each failure point, the printing press, the rival patron, the jar in the desert, was a line that bypassed it.

The third reading is the most speculative, and it is marked as such. In his book Far Journeys, published by Doubleday in 1985, the American out-of-body researcher Robert Monroe coined the word "loosh" for a kind of energy that, he said, human beings produce, especially in states of strong emotion, and that non-human beings who control reality harvest and feed on. [cite:cn_wiki_monroe] [cite:cn_kirkus_monroe] Monroe presented this as the report of his own experiences, not as a demonstrated fact, and nothing in the historical record bears on whether it is true. Some readers of the inner traditions set it beside the Gnostic archons, the rulers of the lower world in the Apocryphon of John who hold humanity in place with a counterfeit spirit. [cite:vt25_wiki_apjohn]

Applied to the indulgence trade, the idea yields a reading worth stating carefully. The documented mechanism of 1517 took a family's grief for its dead and fear for their suffering after death, and converted it into coin that repaid a bank loan and rebuilt a basilica. [cite:cn_nwe_indulgence] [cite:cn_brit_albert] One reading holds that such a mechanism produces two outputs at once from the same act: money that rises up the hierarchy, and fear that spreads outward through the people who pay. On that reading the features of the system that seem needlessly cruel, the preacher's rhyme about the coin and the soul, the dead parent held out as the reason to pay, are not accidents but the part of the design that generates the second output. The first output is documented in the bank's arrangements and the pope's accounts. The second is a reading, offered for readers who find the inner traditions illuminating, and the book leaves it there.

What the readings share is the conviction, preserved in the excluded books, that knowledge of the divine cannot be owned. The documented history shows how persistently it was owned anyway: listed, licensed, translated or forbidden, taxed and sold. The books on the rest of this shelf follow the knowledge that the list left out, and the people who kept it alive outside the gate.
