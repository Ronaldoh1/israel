How This Scorecard Grades the Two Systems

Part Twenty-One set every measure in one ledger and added no total. The question it left open is the one readers put first: taken together, which system serves the people under it better, and by how much? This part answers with a number, and grades both systems across everything this volume measured, publishing every score and every weight so that a reader who disagrees with a choice can change it and recompute. [scene:us21_ledger|Part Twenty-One: the ledger]

Any such number has to meet some plain tests. Each score must be checkable from a named figure, not from an unstated judgment. A score must not run against the figures beside it. A contested estimate must not carry a score on its own. Two figures that measure different things, such as a gun-homicide rate and a gun-death rate that also counts suicides and accidents, must not be set against each other. And a comparison of how two states treat the people under them must include rights and coercion, not only services, and must say whether it counts everyone a state governs or only its citizens.

The rules of the scorecard

The scorecard keeps separate totals for separate questions and can be checked line by line. It follows seven rules.

  • Every score is a measure. Each of the 43 rows in the next section is one named figure from one named source, taken from the part of this volume that measured it, or from the sources added for this part. No row is a judgment. Where the two countries define a figure differently, the row uses one definition for both, or is left out; firearm deaths, for example, are counted from all causes in both countries. Where only one country has a figure, the row is shown and left out of every total.
  • Every measure is scored against printed goalposts. Each measure has a floor worth 0 and a goal worth 10, round numbers chosen around the range of rich democracies; a country's score is its position between the two, capped at 0 and 10. This is the method the United Nations uses for its Human Development Index. The goalposts are a choice, so they are printed beside every row, so that a reader can see, for example, that the homelessness row runs from 300 per 100,000 (0) to none (10), and the final section tests how much they matter.
  • Results are scored, spending is reported. A system is graded on what its money delivers, so spending on health, schools and research is reported beside the results but not scored. The one exception is public family benefits, because there the spending is itself the benefit households receive.
  • Areas, not rows, are weighted equally. The rows fall into twelve areas: eight citizen services, a ninth on the family budget, which asks of both countries how much a family has left once it has paid what the system leaves it to pay, then liberty and coercion, the economy and public finances, and equity inside each system. Each area's score is the plain average of its rows, so an area with many rows does not outweigh an area with one.
  • Three totals answer three questions. A household score averages the nine household areas; a liberty score is the liberty-and-coercion area; an economy score is the economy area. The overall score averages all twelve areas. No total is offered as more than what it is: this book's disclosed synthesis of published measures, which a reader who weighs the areas differently will weigh differently.
  • Israel is scored twice where it matters. Its citizens view uses its national figures, which cover its citizens and residents. Its everyone-governed view adds the Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza wherever a measure concerns how the state treats the people under its control: the Freedom House score weighted by population, detention without charge, and the share of people governed who cannot vote for the government that rules them. The service areas are scored for citizens in both views, because under the Oslo accords the Palestinian Authority, not Israel, runs health, schools and welfare for most of the West Bank; a reader who holds Israel responsible for those services as the occupying power would score its services lower still.
  • Every total is tested. The final section recomputes the result under other choices: every row weighted equally, liberty counted twice, three broad pillars only, every goalpost range widened by a quarter, no goalposts at all, and each area or row left out in turn. A conclusion that holds under all of them is reported as a finding; one that flips is reported as depending on the choice that flips it.

Some systems are discussed in this volume but not scored here, because the volume found no figure measured the same way in both countries: emergency services, transport, construction, energy and water, where Part Fifteen compares how each is organised rather than how well it performs; the price of a home against income, where the two countries' affordability measures cannot be set against each other; police killings, for which Israel publishes no series; and student debt, medical debt and the top one per cent's share of income, which Israel does not compile in a comparable form.
