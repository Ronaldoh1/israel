Where the Phone Says You Are

Book Five asks the same five questions of every surveillance tool: what it collects or does, who uses it and under what contract, where it has been misused or got things wrong, what the law and the courts allow, and what its worst use would look like. The last question is the book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, in which immigration enforcement is the occasion for building a machine that could later be pointed at anyone. A tool's ability is a documented fact; its use against critics needs a document of its own. [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four] described the data brokers, licence-plate cameras and fusion platforms that join records into a picture of a person, and [scene:de24_the_myth_of_anonymous_data|the section on anonymous data] showed why a trail stripped of a name rarely stays nameless. This section follows the most revealing single kind of record those systems hold: where a phone, and so its owner, has been.

Three ways a phone reports its location

A mobile phone gives away its position by three separate routes, each held by a different company and each reached by the state in a different way. The first is the carrier. To deliver calls and data, a phone keeps connecting to nearby cell towers, and the carrier logs which tower and which side of it the phone used. This is called cell-site location information. In a city, where towers are close together, it can place a phone within a block or two; in the countryside, within a few kilometres. A "tower dump" is a request for every phone that connected to one tower in a stretch of time, which in practice means thousands of people who had nothing to do with the matter under investigation. The second route is the phone's own operating system. Phones fix their position from satellites (GPS) and nearby Wi-Fi networks, often to within a few metres, and companies such as Google kept that history on their servers; a "geofence" request asks such a company for every account that was inside a drawn area at a given time. [cite:de25_crs_chatrie] [cite:de25_wlox_tower]

The third route runs through the apps on the phone, and it is the one that grew fastest. Many free apps carry pieces of code supplied by other companies, called software development kits or SDKs, which can report the phone's position back to the supplier. Others show advertisements. Every phone has an advertising identifier, a string of letters and numbers (on Apple devices the IDFA, on Android the AAID) that advertisers use to recognise the same device across apps without knowing its owner's name. When an app with an empty advertising slot opens, an auction is held in the milliseconds before the advertisement appears. The Federal Trade Commission's technologists described it in December 2024: the app's publisher offers the slot on an exchange, advertisers bid, and to help them decide, the exchange often sends details "like location or personal characteristics" to dozens of bidders at once, of whom only one wins. [cite:de25_ftc_rtb] That stream of auction data is called the bidstream. A company that takes part in enough auctions can collect location points from millions of phones without ever showing an advertisement. The FTC's staff put its view in five words: "location data is sensitive data, full stop." [cite:de25_ftc_rtb]

The sellers and what regulators found

The FTC's cases against location brokers, brought in 2024 and 2025, set out how the trade worked. In January 2024 it reached its first settlement with a location broker, X-Mode Social, later renamed Outlogic. The commission said X-Mode sold precise locations tied to advertising IDs that were not anonymised and could be matched to individuals' visits; until May 2023 it had no way of removing visits to clinics, places of worship or shelters; and its hundreds of clients included "private government contractors." [cite:de25_ftc_xmode] In December 2024 the FTC alleged that Mobilewalla had kept data from advertising auctions it lost, against the exchanges' rules. [cite:de25_wh_mobilewalla] [cite:de25_ftc_rtb] On 14 January 2025, by five votes to none, it finalised an order against Gravy Analytics and its subsidiary Venntel, which it said had collected location data without consent "for commercial and government uses." The order bars them from selling or using sensitive location data, with an exception for "limited circumstances involving national security or law enforcement." [cite:de25_ftc_gravy] Each of these orders regulates how a seller treats consumers. None stops a government agency from buying.

The buyers, under both parties

Federal purchases began in Donald Trump's first term and continued under Joe Biden and in Trump's second. The Wall Street Journal reported in February 2020 that Venntel sold phone-movement data to government agencies, which had used it to find border crossings and make arrests. On 5 August 2020 Customs and Border Protection paid just under $476,000 for "Venntel Software"; a CBP spokesperson said the agency had "acquired limited access to commercial telemetry data," the government's term for app-derived location. Senator Ron Wyden's office found that the criminal investigators of the Internal Revenue Service had used Venntel data under a year-long contract and failed to locate any targets with it. [cite:de25_vice_venntel] Local police bought the same kind of data more cheaply. An investigation by the Electronic Frontier Foundation and the Associated Press, published on 1 September 2022, found that Fog Data Science sold access, for under $10,000 a year, to what it described as billions of location points from more than 250 million devices, with at least 18 local, state and federal clients, often searched without a warrant. [cite:de25_eff_fog]

In September 2023 the inspector general of the Department of Homeland Security reported that CBP, ICE and the Secret Service had bought and used commercial telemetry data without following the department's privacy policies or completing the privacy impact assessments the E-Government Act of 2002 requires before such data is acquired. Auditors found shared accounts and passwords, ad hoc records and no supervisory review, and one CBP employee had used the data to track coworkers; the inspector general recommended that ICE stop using it until the assessments were approved. [cite:de25_oig_ctd] [cite:de25_hst_ctd] In 2025 ICE returned to the market through Penlink, a Nebraska company controlled since 2022 by the private-equity firm Spire Capital, which in 2023 bought the Israeli-founded Cobwebs Technologies and with it two products: Tangles, which gathers open-web and social-media material, and Webloc, which searches phone movements by place and time. In the autumn of 2025 ICE agreed to pay Penlink $2.3 million for licences to both; it bought the software, not the company. Penlink says its tools "exclusively use publicly or commercially available data." [cite:de25_ffp_penlink] [cite:de25_db_webloc] In January 2026 404 Media described its Webloc product as able to watch a neighbourhood for phones and follow them over time, using data from "hundreds of millions of phones"; an internal ICE legal analysis said it could be searched without a warrant. [cite:de25_db_webloc] [cite:de25_404_webloc] On 3 March 2026 seventy members of Congress asked the department's inspector general to investigate the no-bid Penlink contract. [cite:de25_keyt_letter] The full roster of ICE's data contracts is in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty].

The FBI's position changed twice. Its director Christopher Wray told senators in 2023 that the bureau had bought location data in the past but was not doing so. On 18 March 2026 his successor Kash Patel, asked by Wyden to commit to not buying Americans' location data, said: "We do purchase commercially available information that is consistent with the Constitution and the laws." He named no broker and gave no number. [cite:de25_tc_patel]

The carriers

The telephone companies sold location too. The Federal Communications Commission found that AT&T, Verizon, T-Mobile and Sprint had shared access to their customers' locations with "aggregators," which resold it to other brokers, without customers' consent and without "reasonable measures" to protect it. In April 2024 it fined AT&T about $57 million, Verizon about $47 million, T-Mobile $80 million and Sprint about $12 million, nearly $200 million in all. [cite:de25_rec_fcc] [cite:de25_wcc_fcc] The companies paid and sued, arguing that a penalty imposed without a jury trial was unconstitutional; the Fifth Circuit agreed with AT&T and the Second Circuit sided with the FCC against Verizon. [cite:de25_aa_fcc] On 4 June 2026 the Supreme Court upheld the fines by eight votes to one, in an opinion by Chief Justice John Roberts with Justice Clarence Thomas dissenting. [cite:de25_rec_fcc]

A second carrier arrangement served the police directly. In early September 2013 the New York Times revealed Hemisphere, a programme launched in 2007 in which AT&T employees worked alongside drug agents searching the company's call records; one slide told officials to "never refer to Hemisphere in any official document." [cite:de25_reg_hemisphere] The White House drug-policy office paid for it. Wyden's letter to Attorney General Merrick Garland of 20 November 2023 set out what had followed. Renamed Data Analytical Services, the programme searched records going back to 1987, to which about four billion were added each day, "trillions" in all; any federal, state, local or tribal officer could send a request to an AT&T analyst, often with an administrative subpoena rather than a warrant, and the searches did not have to concern drugs. Federal funding was paused in 2013 under Barack Obama, resumed in 2017 under Trump, paused in 2021 under Biden and restarted in 2022 with $270,000. [cite:de25_wyden_hemisphere] Its status after 2023 has not been reported.

What the courts have said

The Supreme Court has narrowed the old rule that information handed to a company loses its privacy. In Carpenter v. United States (2018) it required a warrant for a carrier's historical cell-site records. [cite:ow9_cornell_tpd] On 29 June 2026, in Chatrie v. United States, the Court ruled 6–3: Justice Elena Kagan, writing for five justices, with Justice Neil Gorsuch concurring in the judgment, held that obtaining Google Location History through a geofence warrant is a search under the Fourth Amendment; the case concerned a bank robbery, a 150-metre circle and an hour around the crime. The Court did not declare geofence warrants unconstitutional; it sent the case back to decide whether this warrant was adequate at each stage. Google had already said in 2023 that it would keep Location History on users' devices. [cite:de25_crs_chatrie] In August 2026 Judge Carlton Reeves of the Southern District of Mississippi went further for tower dumps, upholding a magistrate's refusal of requests covering towers near nine crime scenes and holding such warrants unconstitutional in every case: "The government cannot have access to an entire haystack because it may contain a needle." The ruling binds only that district. [cite:de25_wlox_tower]

Neither Carpenter nor Chatrie concerned data the government buys, and the FTC's orders bind sellers, not buyers; the doctrine that has grown in that gap, and the bills to close it, are the subject of [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Thirty]. The errors are documented alongside the purchases: a year of IRS searches that found no one, a CBP employee tracking colleagues, and, in the Fog records, a babysitter in a Missouri murder case tracked although she was never a suspect. [cite:de25_vice_venntel] [cite:de25_oig_ctd] [cite:de25_eff_fog]

The worst-case extension follows from the table, not from any document of intent. Location bought from brokers needs no judge, already reaches hundreds of millions of phones, and can be searched by place: a protest, a clinic, a mosque or a meeting hall can be drawn on a map and the devices present listed. That is a capability the record establishes. That any agency has used it to list people at a protest or to find a critic is not documented in the record as of October 2026.

If it were connected

A location trail on its own names a device, not a person. Its power depends on what it is joined to, and the joins are graded here as documented (it exists, with a source), probable (a contract, interface or authority already points to it), possible (technically feasible, no document) or not supported (the record runs against it). The full map of these systems is drawn in [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty].

Joined to identity records: documented. LexisNexis's Accurint service, which ICE uses, includes device-geolocation analysis alongside names, addresses, Social Security numbers and plates, and an ICE notice of 10 August 2026 required LexisNexis to connect by programming interfaces, the standard plugs by which one system queries another, to ICE applications "such as but not limited to" the Palantir platform and PenLink. [cite:de25_bu_lexis] Joined to the case-management and targeting systems that Palantir runs for ICE: possible for Penlink's location data. The same notice names PenLink and the Palantir platform as separate destinations for LexisNexis's identity data, which is not a feed from one to the other, and no document shows Webloc's output flowing into Palantir. [cite:de25_bu_lexis] [cite:de25_404_webloc] Joined to the government's own records of benefits, schooling and work, set out in [scene:de24b_benefits|Part Twenty-Five], so that a phone seen at a protest resolves to a name, an address and an eligibility file: possible; each piece exists, and the join would need only a query across systems an agency already licenses, but no record shows it. Joined to travel and border records ([scene:de25b_who_knows_you_travel|Part Twenty-Seven]), so that a location trail ends at a flagged boarding pass: possible, with no document. What stands in the way is thin: the FTC's orders bind sellers, the courts have not ruled on purchase, and the brake that remains is the agencies' own privacy rules, which their inspector general found they did not follow. [cite:de25_ftc_gravy] [cite:de25_oig_ctd] In April 2026 thirty-three members of Congress asked the Homeland Security department which commercial datasets sit inside its Palantir-built systems; no reply had been reported as of October 2026. [cite:de25_goldman_letter]
