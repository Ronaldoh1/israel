Keren Hayesod: Fundraising Made Permanent

In the same December of 1920 in which the new Histadrut took on matters of defense, the Zionist movement built the financial institution it needed to use the opening the Balfour Declaration had given it three years before. On 24 December 1920, at a Zionist conference in London, the World Zionist Organization founded Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund. It was to be the organization's central and permanent fundraising arm. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

Its originators were Hillel Zlatopolsky and Isaac Naiditch. The fund was registered in Britain in 1921. Its stated ambition was large: the originators contemplated raising £25,000,000 within five years. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] Two features of its design set it apart. First, it was open to non-Zionists as well as Zionists, so a donor did not need to join the movement in order to give. Second, it asked supporters for a self-imposed giving pledge modeled on the tithe, a regular share of income rather than a single gift. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] In 1926 the fund moved its headquarters from London to Jerusalem. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

The fund's first public backers suggest a broad intended appeal. Among the named early figures associated with its fundraising were Chaim Weizmann, the president of the Zionist Organization, and Albert Einstein, the most famous scientist of the day. Jabotinsky, who would lead the more militant Revisionist wing, was also among them. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] These three men disagreed on a great deal. Their names on the same fund tell the public that it was meant to reach across the movement's divisions, not to serve one faction. The association does not show that they worked closely together or agreed on strategy. Elsewhere this book records sharp disputes among Zionist factions in these same years. One of the first of those disputes was over this fund. In the spring of 1921 Weizmann and Einstein toured the United States to raise money for it. The tour opened a fight with Louis Brandeis's American faction over who would control the proceeds. That story is told in [scene:us_s01|the first American campaigns].

The money went to more than land. Keren Hayesod financed the absorption of immigrants, settlement, defense and water development. It also financed major enterprises, among them the Palestine Electric Company and the Palestine Potash Company. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] Histories of this period usually give most of their attention to land purchase. That subject is treated in other parts of this book. Keren Hayesod's record shows a second line of investment running beside it. A modern economy needed electricity, water systems and industry as well as land. The fund put the movement's money into all of these.

The mechanism mattered as much as the money. Keren Hayesod made fundraising a standing institution. It had a legal registration, a headquarters, a multi-year target and a regular form of giving. It was built so that donors in many countries would keep paying into it year after year, and so that the money would arrive at one central body, the Zionist Organization. The fund's openness to non-Zionists widened its base. Its move to Jerusalem in 1926 put the money's management in the country where it was spent. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

The next step came in 1929. That year Weizmann and Louis Marshall launched the enlarged Jewish Agency for Palestine. It gave non-Zionists equal representation with Zionists, and Keren Hayesod became its financial instrument. [cite:yivo_weizmann_encyclopedia] [cite:israeled_jewish_agency_expands] [cite:britannica_jewish_agency] The fund was now folded into the body that functioned, in the years before the state, as a government in waiting. Marshall died in September 1929, within weeks of the Agency's launch. His successor as leader of the Agency's non-Zionist wing in the United States was Felix Warburg. Warburg became chairman of its Administrative Committee and Weizmann's senior non-Zionist counterpart. [cite:yivo_weizmann_encyclopedia] [cite:israeled_jewish_agency_expands] [cite:britannica_jewish_agency]

Warburg's path tells its own story. In the years before 1929 he had been a prominent, on-record opponent of Zionism. [cite:yivo_weizmann_encyclopedia] [cite:israeled_jewish_agency_expands] [cite:britannica_jewish_agency] A decade later he was one of the senior figures in the pre-state body whose fund this was. His role does not show that he had abandoned his earlier non-Zionist position. The Agency's non-Zionist seats were designed for people who supported the development of Palestine without endorsing political Zionism in full. Warburg's role there was consistent with his earlier position. The principle on which Keren Hayesod had been opened to non-Zionists in 1920 was, by 1929, built into the Agency itself.

This record corrects a familiar story. It is sometimes said that Zionist fundraising was informal and improvised until American donors organized it in the late 1930s. The documents show otherwise. When the United Jewish Appeal was formed in 1939, Keren Hayesod had already been running as a permanent, structured, international fundraising institution for nearly two decades. It had named founders, a stated target, famous early supporters such as Weizmann and Einstein, and a headquarters in Jerusalem from 1926. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

Several things remain undocumented here. The sources give the founding target of £25,000,000 in five years, but they do not show whether it was ever reached. They give the categories the fund paid for, from immigration to electricity, but no itemized budget showing how much went to each. The first American tour's takings are recorded in [scene:us_s01|the first American campaigns], but there is no accounting here of the fund's total income in its first decade. Keren Hayesod made giving permanent and gave the movement an institution that outlasted any single campaign. How much money moved through it in these years is not settled by this material.
