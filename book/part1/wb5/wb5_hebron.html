Hebron, and the Outposts

The massacre. In February 1994, during the first months of the Oslo process, a settler from Kiryat Arba, Baruch Goldstein, opened fire on Muslims at prayer in the Cave of the Patriarchs in Hebron, killing 29. [cite:wb_wiki_hebron] [cite:wb_wiki_shuhada] After the riots that followed, the army did not close the settlement; it closed Shuhada Street, the main market street of the old city, to Palestinians, and shut 304 Palestinian shops and warehouses. [cite:wb_wiki_shuhada]

The divided city. In the Hebron Protocol of 1997 the city was divided into H1, under the Palestinian Authority, and H2, the centre around the settler enclaves, under Israeli control, and Israel agreed to reopen Shuhada Street. [cite:wb_wiki_hebron] [cite:wb_wiki_shuhada] It did not. The street remains closed to Palestinian shops and vehicles, and in parts to Palestinians on foot; Israel's stated reason is the protection of the settlers. By 2007 the Israeli human rights group B'Tselem found that 42 percent of the Palestinians of the H2 centre, 1,014 families, had left their homes. [cite:wb_wiki_shuhada]

The outposts. Through the Oslo years, and after them, small settlements were set up without government authorization, the "outposts." In March 2005 a government report written by Talia Sasson counted 105 of them, and found that the government itself funded at least part of them: the Housing Ministry supplied hundreds of mobile homes and millions of dollars, other officials connected them to electricity, and military administrators allowed 54 to go up on private Palestinian land. Sharon's spokesman blamed the West Bank's overlapping legal codes; the government said its policy was not to establish new settlements. [cite:wb_csm_sasson]
