Paris, 1919: A Conditional Signature and a Shelved Report

A common account of the peace settlement holds that the Arabs agreed to a Jewish homeland at the Paris conference, and that the great powers acted on what the inhabitants of the region wanted. Two records from 1919 complicate that account. One was edited before it reached the conference; the other reached the conference and was set aside.

The first is the agreement signed in London on 3 January 1919 by Emir Faisal and Chaim Weizmann. In it, Faisal accepted Jewish immigration into Palestine and the Balfour Declaration. That much is often cited. Less often cited is what Faisal wrote beside his signature. In Arabic, he added a condition, recorded in the text of the agreement held by the United Nations: he would concur only if the Arabs obtained their independence as he had demanded in his memorandum to the British Foreign Office, and if there were any departure from that, he would not be bound. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text]

The Zionist Organization submitted the agreement to the peace conference without the caveat. [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] [cite:israeled_faisal_weizmann] The condition, in any case, soon failed. When the mandates were divided along the lines of Sykes–Picot, the Arab independence Faisal had made his precondition did not materialize, and an Arab congress in Damascus repudiated his authority to have signed the agreement at all. The consent that would be cited for decades as Arab acceptance of the national home had been conditional from the start, and the condition had been removed from the copy the conference saw.

The second record came from Washington. President Wilson sent an American commission, known as the King–Crane Commission, to find out what the people of the former Ottoman territories actually wanted. The commission toured from June to July 1919. It received about 1,875 petitions, 72 percent of them hostile to the Zionist plan. Its report found that the non-Jewish population, nearly nine-tenths of the whole, was "emphatically against the entire Zionist program," and it recommended the program's "serious modification." [cite:britannica_king_crane] [cite:jewishmag_king_crane]

The report was delivered to the conference in August 1919 and shelved. Wilson may never have seen it. It was first published in December 1922, in Editor & Publisher, by which time Britain and France had already established their mandates. [cite:wiki_king_crane] [cite:palquest_king_crane] The record does not settle why it stayed unpublished for more than three years. Some members of the commission believed the State Department and Zionists were responsible for suppressing it. That attribution is theirs; the sources record it as their belief, not as an established finding about who decided to bury the report. [cite:palquest_king_crane]

What is not in dispute is the sequence. The opinion of the population had been measured, by a commission sent by an American president, in the summer of 1919. Its findings were in the conference's hands from August. The decisions about who would rule Palestine and on what terms were then made without the report being made public, and the report surfaced only after those decisions were in place. [cite:wiki_king_crane]

The two documents point in the same direction. The Arab consent so often invoked was qualified in Faisal's own hand, and the qualification was dropped. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text] [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] The wishes of the inhabitants were surveyed and written down, and the survey was put aside until it could no longer affect the outcome. Whether the governments at Paris knew what the people of Palestine wanted is not a question that must be answered by inference. There is a documented answer, dated 1919, and it was available to them.
