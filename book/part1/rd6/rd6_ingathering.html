The Ingathering: Mass Immigration, 1948–1952

In its first three and a half years the new state took in more people than it had when it began. According to the Jewish Agency, 203,000 Jews from 42 countries arrived in Israel's first year alone, and 684,201 immigrants arrived between May 1948 and the end of 1951, "more than the entire Jewish population when independence was declared". [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] No other fact of the period shaped the state's economy, its housing, its politics and its social order so directly. This section sets out who came, in what order, how they were housed and fed, and when the flow slowed.

The order of arrival followed the places where Jews were already waiting. The first to come were the former illegal immigrants whom the British had held in detention camps on Cyprus, and Holocaust survivors from the displaced-persons camps in Germany, Austria and Italy. Then whole communities followed. The Jewish Agency records 121,512 of Iraq's 130,000 Jews, more than 37,000 of Bulgaria's 45,000, 30,500 of Libya's 35,000, and nearly all of Yemen's 45,000. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] Why the communities of the Arab world left when they did is told in [scene:rd6_exodus|the Jews of the Arab world after 1948].

A table drawn from the Israel State Archives gives a fuller count, through the end of 1952. It records 738,891 immigrants from 1948 to the end of that year. Of these, 377,251 came from Muslim countries and 307,082 from Communist states. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] The largest single sources were Iraq, with 124,226, and Romania, with 121,537, followed by Poland, with 106,751. From the Muslim countries the table lists Iraq; Yemen and Aden, 48,375; Tunisia, Morocco and Algeria together, 52,584; Turkey, 35,025; Syria and Lebanon, 34,608; Libya, 32,129; Iran, 25,593; and Egypt, 17,114. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] The two blocs together account for 684,333 of the total; the remaining 54,558, by subtraction, came from elsewhere, and the figures given here do not break that remainder down.

The counts do not agree across sources, and the gaps are not trivial. For Iraq, the Jewish Agency's 121,512 sits beside the State Archives' 124,226, and Israeli government sources describe the community as reduced from about 135,000 to roughly 6,000 by 1952. For Yemen, the Jewish Agency's "nearly all of Yemen's 45,000" sits beside the archive figure of 48,375 for Yemen and Aden together, and the commonly cited count of about 49,000 flown out in Operation Magic Carpet. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] [cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:w_magic_carpet] Part of the difference is in the categories (Yemen alone, or Yemen with Aden; arrivals counted by year of immigration, or by community before and after), and part cannot be explained from the sources given here.

Housing could not keep pace. By May 1950, of the 393,197 people who had arrived since May 1948, 112,015 were still living in immigrant camps and temporary housing. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] The camps were holding points, where newcomers waited to be placed. In 1950 the state introduced a second kind of settlement, the ma'abara, or transitional camp, where newcomers could work while they waited for permanent homes. The ma'abarot multiplied fast. By May 1952 there were 113 of them, housing 250,000 people. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] Jews arriving from Arab and Muslim countries were often placed in these camps and, according to the Times of Israel, largely sidelined by the European-born leadership of the ruling Labor party. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] The gravest charge to come out of the camps is the subject of [scene:mz_s02|the Yemenite Children Affair], which sets out what the families allege and what the inquiries found.

Feeding the population took a regime of its own. Under the austerity programme known as tzena, food was rationed by coupon: oil, sugar, margarine and rice, with meat about once a week and fish about twice a month. Furniture and clothing were rationed too. [cite:rs_rd6_nli_tzena] The system bred its own resistance. A black market grew, and merchants went on strike in the summer of 1950. In the Knesset election of 1951, the General Zionists, who campaigned against rationing, won 20 seats. A New Economic Policy began to ease the controls in 1952, and rationing was officially abolished in 1959. [cite:rs_rd6_nli_tzena]

Then the flow fell away. Immigration peaked in 1951; in the three years from 1952 to 1954 only 51,463 immigrants arrived. [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] Who paid for the absorption and the state around it, through American aid, the sale of bonds and German reparations, is told in [scene:rd6_financing|how the new state was paid for].

The same numbers correct two easy assumptions. One is that the immigration of these years came mainly from Europe. Survivors from the displaced-persons camps came first, but by the end of 1952 more immigrants had come from Muslim countries, 377,251, than from the Communist states, 307,082. The other is that the newcomers were absorbed smoothly into a prepared country. In May 1952 a quarter of a million people were living in transitional camps, under a rationing regime that began to ease only in 1952 and was not abolished until 1959. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_immigrants_4852] [cite:rs_rd6_ja_absorption] [cite:rs_rd6_nli_tzena]

What these sources do not settle is how the burdens of absorption were shared among the newcomers: who was sent to the ma'abarot and who to permanent housing, for how long, and on what criteria. The figures show the scale of the camps; they do not show who lived in them by origin. That question, and whether selection followed origin, is where the record of discrimination begins, and it is not answered by the numbers given here.
