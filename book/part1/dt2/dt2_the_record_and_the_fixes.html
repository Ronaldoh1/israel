The Record and the Fixes

This section sets out what the Electoral College has produced, the elections won with fewer votes and the concentration of campaigns, and the reforms proposed to change either, each with its case, its critics, its legal route and its status as of October 2026.

When the electoral and popular votes split

In five presidential elections the candidate who received the most popular votes did not become president. In 1824 Andrew Jackson led John Quincy Adams by 44,804 votes and led in electors, 99 to 84, but had no majority, and the House chose Adams. In 1876 Samuel Tilden won 51.1 per cent of the popular vote and lost 185 to 184 after an Electoral Commission ruled on disputed returns from three Southern states. In 1888 Grover Cleveland led Benjamin Harrison by 100,456 votes and lost the electoral count 233 to 168. In 2000 Al Gore led George W. Bush by 537,179 votes and lost 271 to 266 after the Supreme Court's decision in Bush v. Gore ended the Florida recount. In 2016 Hillary Clinton led Donald Trump by 2,868,686 votes, 48.2 per cent to 46.1, and lost 304 to 227. [cite:dt2_brit_splits] The Congressional Research Service counts four such elections rather than five, leaving out 1824, when several states' electors were still chosen by legislatures and no candidate won an electoral majority. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] [cite:dt2_fairvote_wta]

The split is not the usual outcome. In most elections, including 2024, the popular-vote winner also wins the Electoral College: Trump took 77,303,568 votes, 49.8 per cent, to Kamala Harris's 75,019,230, 48.3 per cent, and 312 electoral votes to 226. [cite:dt2_bp_2024] But two of the seven elections held since 2000 went to the candidate with fewer votes, both times to a Republican. [cite:dt2_brit_splits]

Where the campaigns go

Winner-take-all also decides where candidates spend their time. From the end of the Republican convention on 28 August 2020 to Election Day, the two major-party tickets held 212 general-election events; 204 of them, 96 per cent, were in 12 states, and 33 states and the District of Columbia had none. [cite:dt2_npv_events2020] In 2024, 94 per cent of 261 events were in seven states, Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, North Carolina, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, and 32 states received no visit from a candidate. [cite:dt2_fv_events2024] Both counts were compiled by FairVote and published by it and by National Popular Vote, organisations that campaign to change the system; the event logs themselves are public and can be checked.

Fix one: a constitutional amendment

The direct route is to amend the Constitution, which requires two-thirds of both houses of Congress and ratification by three-quarters of the states. It came closest half a century ago. On 18 September 1969 the House passed an amendment sponsored by Representative Emanuel Celler and Senator Birch Bayh, both Democrats, to elect the president by national popular vote, with a runoff if no ticket won 40 per cent; the vote was 339 to 70, with support from both parties, and President Richard Nixon endorsed it. In the Senate, mainly Southern and small-state senators of both parties, who argued their states would lose influence, filibustered it. Cloture votes on 17 and 29 September 1970 failed, 54 to 36 and 53 to 34, short of two-thirds, and the measure died. [cite:dt2_wiki_bayh] Proposals have been introduced in recent Congresses, among them H.J.Res. 102 in the current one, but according to the Congressional Research Service none has "substantially advanced beyond introduction." [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026]

Fix two: the National Popular Vote compact

An interstate compact is a binding agreement among states. The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact uses the states' power over their own electors: each member state agrees to give all its electors to the candidate who wins the most votes nationwide, but only once states holding a majority of electoral votes, 270, have joined. [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026] On 13 April 2026 Governor Abigail Spanberger signed Virginia into the compact, bringing it to 18 states and the District of Columbia with 222 electoral votes, 48 short of the threshold. Virginia's bill passed after Democrats gained the governorship and full control of the legislature. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia]

The compact's critics argue that it changes the Constitution without amending it. Patrick Valencia, now Iowa's deputy solicitor general, has described it as an effort to "usurp the constitutionally required electoral procedures," keeping the Electoral College on paper while changing what electors do; other scholars note that the Framers rejected a popular presidential vote and that comparable changes, such as the extension of suffrage, were made by amendment. Supporters answer that Article II gives each state full power over how it appoints its electors, and they expect the compact to be challenged in court once it reaches 270. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia]

Fix three: the 2022 counting law

The one change that has passed addresses a different weakness: how Congress counts electoral votes. The Electoral Count Act of 1887 let a single member of each chamber force a vote on objections to a state's electors. The Electoral Count Reform Act raised that threshold to one-fifth of the members of each house, made the vice-president's role at the count ministerial, named the governor as the official who certifies a state's electors unless state law says otherwise, and provided expedited court review of disputes over those certificates. It was introduced by Senator Susan Collins, a Republican, with Senator Joe Manchin, a Democrat, attached to the year-end spending bill, passed the Senate 68 to 29 on 22 December 2022 and the House 225 to 201 the next day, and was signed by President Joe Biden on 23 December. [cite:dt2_bp_ecra] It makes the count harder to disrupt. It does nothing about whether the winner of the count is the winner of the vote.

What the public wants, and why it moves

Majorities favour a national popular vote, but the size of the majority moves with party advantage. Gallup found 61 per cent support for an amendment in 2020, with Democrats at 89 per cent, independents at 68 and Republicans at 23; the only time support fell below half was in November 2016, at 49 per cent to 47, just after Trump won with fewer votes. [cite:dt2_gallup_ec2020] In September 2024 Gallup found 58 per cent in favour, with Democrats at 82, independents at 60 and Republicans at 32. [cite:dt2_ktvu_gallup2024] Pew, asking a similar question in the same weeks, found 63 per cent for a popular-vote system, about 80 per cent of Democrats and 46 per cent of Republicans, up from 27 per cent of Republicans in November 2016. [cite:dt2_pew_ec2024]

The test

The test asks whether the claim that democracy has failed survives this part's facts. Documented: the method of choosing a president is not majority rule. The national popular vote is neither necessary nor sufficient to win, the candidate with fewer votes has won five times, twice since 2000, and campaigns concentrate on a handful of states. [cite:dt2_brit_splits] [cite:dt2_fv_events2024] Documented: the deliberative screen the Framers defended has not operated since the 1790s and, after Chiafalo, exists only where state law permits it. [cite:dt2_crs_chiafalo] Not supported: that the outcomes of split elections show democracy failing, since in each case the mechanism that decided was the part of the system that departs from a popular vote. Not supported either: that the country's condition since 2016 can be laid at the Electoral College's door; the measures each side cites began falling earlier and are read differently by each party. [cite:dt1_eiu2016] [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026]

The record cuts against this book's thesis as well. In most elections, including the most recent, the Electoral College and the popular vote agree, so it cannot explain the symptoms Part One described in most years. [cite:dt2_bp_2024] The feature most often blamed for 2016, Hamilton's screen, was itself a check on the voters, and finishing democracy would abolish it rather than restore it. Support for reform rises and falls with partisan advantage, which suggests that much of the public judges the system by who it elects. [cite:dt2_gallup_ec2020] [cite:dt2_pew_ec2024] And the one reform enacted, the 2022 counting law, passed as an attachment to a year-end spending bill and left the allocation of electors untouched, a modest record for a system this book argues can correct itself. [cite:dt2_bp_ecra] [cite:dt2_wiki_bayh] The Electoral College shows that the presidency is not chosen by a national majority. It does not, alone, show what a fully democratic system would do better; that requires looking at how candidates reach the ballot in the first place, the subject of [scene:dt3_primaries_and_the_two_party_trap|Part Three].
