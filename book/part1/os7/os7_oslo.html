Oslo, 1993–1995

Secret talks in Norway produced the Declaration of Principles, signed on the White House lawn on 13 September 1993, with Rabin and Arafat shaking hands before President Clinton. Israel and the PLO recognised each other; a Palestinian Authority would govern parts of the West Bank and Gaza for an interim period of five years, while the hardest questions, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, borders and security, were left to final-status talks. [cite:oslo_1_text] [cite:wiki_oslo_accords] The interim agreement of 28 September 1995, Oslo II, divided the West Bank into Areas A, B and C: Area C, about 60 percent of the land, remained under full Israeli control. [cite:oslo_2_text] [cite:wiki_areas_abc] Jordan signed a peace treaty with Israel in October 1994. [cite:os_wiki_jordanpeace]

The process had violent enemies on both sides. In February 1994 a settler, Baruch Goldstein, killed 29 Palestinians at prayer in Hebron; Hamas and Islamic Jihad carried out suicide bombings in Israel; and on 4 November 1995 Yigal Amir, an Israeli religious-nationalist law student, assassinated Rabin. [cite:gi11_wiki_kach] [cite:wiki_yigal_amir] [cite:wiki_yitzhak_rabin] The Netanyahu Architecture follows the campaign against Rabin that preceded the killing.

The ground beneath the process

The accords did not freeze settlement. The settler population of the West Bank outside East Jerusalem grew from about 110,000 at Oslo to more than 500,000 by 2025, in the years of the "peace process" and after it. [cite:peacenow_settlement_population] [cite:ny_wiki_settlerpop] Critics, including the Israeli group Peace Now, argue that this growth made a viable Palestinian state ever harder to draw; Israeli governments have argued that settlements are not the obstacle to peace.
