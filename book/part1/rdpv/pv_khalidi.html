A Hundred Years' War: Rashid Khalidi's Account

One book has done more than any other in recent years to set the road to 1948 inside a longer story told from the land. Rashid Khalidi, for many years Edward Said Professor of Modern Arab Studies at Columbia, published The Hundred Years' War on Palestine in 2020. [cite:hyw_wiki] It is a history and a family memoir at once: the author's great-great-uncle wrote to Herzl in 1899, his uncle was mayor of Jerusalem and was exiled by the British, and his father carried a message to King Abdullah on the night the United Nations voted for partition. [cite:hyw_intro] [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:hyw_ch2] This section sets out what the book argues, what it adds to the record already assembled in this part, where its critics push back, and the few places where its facts need correcting.

The argument. Khalidi's thesis is in his subtitle. He reads the conflict not as two peoples with equal claims meeting by accident, but as a colonial project of settlement backed by the great powers of each period, met by the resistance of the people already there. [cite:hyw_intro] He divides a century into six "declarations of war" on the Palestinians, each one a moment when an outside power, or Israel with outside backing, changed the terms by force or by decree. [cite:hyw_intro] [cite:hyw_wiki]

The warning of 1899. The book opens where this part opened. On 1 March 1899 Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi, former mayor of Jerusalem and a deputy in the Ottoman parliament, sent a seven-page letter to the Chief Rabbi of France, Zadoc Kahn, meant for Herzl. He granted the justice of the Jewish case in principle and asked that Palestine be left alone, because it was already inhabited. Herzl replied on 19 March, promising that the inhabitants would gain from Jewish wealth, and asked: "who would think of sending them away?" [cite:hyw_intro] [scene:pv_warnings|The warning of 1899] Khalidi sets beside that reply two documents Yusuf Diya never saw: Herzl's diary entry of 1895 about trying to "spirit the penniless population across the border," and the 1901 charter Herzl drafted for a Jewish-Ottoman Land Company, which provided for moving the inhabitants to other Ottoman provinces. [cite:hyw_intro] The point is not that Herzl planned 1948. It is that removal was thinkable at the movement's start, and was written down.

A promise without consultation. On the first declaration, Khalidi adds three things to the record already laid out. First, numbers: when the Balfour Declaration was issued, Arabs were about 94 percent of the country's people, and the Declaration named them only as "existing non-Jewish communities." [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:wb_bl_addms41178] Second, a private document. In a memorandum of 1919 Balfour wrote that in Palestine Britain did "not propose even to go through the form of consulting the wishes of the present inhabitants." [cite:hyw_ch1] Third, a private conversation. At a dinner at Balfour's home in 1922, Lloyd George, Balfour and Churchill told Weizmann that by a national home they had "always meant an eventual Jewish state." [cite:hyw_ch1] The Mandate's text then made the promise operational: article 2 charged Britain with conditions for the national home, article 4 recognized a Jewish Agency as a public body, article 6 told it to facilitate immigration and "close settlement," and article 7 called for a nationality law. The words "Arab" and "Palestinian" appear nowhere in it. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki]

How the Mandate worked against a majority. Khalidi's most useful contribution to the money and power questions this book keeps asking is his account of the Mandate's machinery. The posts of grand mufti of all Palestine and head of a Supreme Muslim Council did not exist before the British; High Commissioner Herbert Samuel created them and gave them to Hajj Amin al-Husayni, a young man who had promised to keep order. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] The yishuv, meanwhile, built a state within the state with outside capital. Citing the Israeli historian Zeev Sternhell, Khalidi writes that capital flowing into the Jewish sector in the 1920s averaged 41.5 percent more each year than that sector's own domestic product. [cite:hyw_ch1] When Nazi Germany came to power, the Transfer Agreement allowed German Jews to bring about $100 million into Palestine in the form of German goods. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:wiki_haavara_agreement] Immigration passed 60,000 in 1935 alone. Ben-Gurion's remark that sixty thousand a year "means a Jewish state" is quoted to show that the leaders on both sides could do the same arithmetic. [cite:hyw_ch1] By 1939 Jews were more than 30 percent of the population, up from about 6 percent at the end of the war. [cite:hyw_ch1]

The revolt and its price. On 1936 to 1939 Khalidi's account agrees with the record in the earlier section on the revolt and adds its cost. [scene:pv_revolt|The revolt] The general strike lasted six months. The Peel Commission proposed a Jewish state on about 17 percent of the country and the "transfer" of more than 200,000 Arabs out of it. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:wiki_peel_commission] After the district commissioner Lewis Andrews was assassinated in October 1937, Britain dissolved the Arab Higher Committee and deported five of its leaders to the Seychelles; one of them was the mayor of Jerusalem, Husayn al-Khalidi, the author's uncle. Isa al-Isa, the founder of the newspaper Filastin, was driven into exile the next year. [cite:hyw_ch1] Britain brought its forces up to about 100,000 men. By Khalidi's count about 10 percent of the adult male Arab population was killed, wounded, imprisoned or exiled, and well over a hundred death sentences were handed down by military courts, with many more men shot on the spot. Drawing on the Israeli historian Tom Segev, he notes that during the same years Britain armed and trained Jewish auxiliaries, experience the Haganah carried into 1948. [cite:hyw_ch1] When the 1939 talks at St James's Palace failed, Britain issued the White Paper limiting immigration and land sales and promising independence in ten years. Husayn al-Khalidi, newly back from exile, wanted to accept it; the mufti refused. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:white_paper_1939]

A people without the means to fight. Khalidi's chapter on 1947–1948 rests on a comparison of means. The Arab National Fund, founded in 1944 to stop land from being sold, raised about 176,000 Palestine pounds by mid-1947; the Jewish National Fund was collecting about $3.5 million a year in the United States alone by the mid-1930s. [cite:hyw_ch2] The Arab Office that Musa al-'Alami founded in 1945 to put the Palestinian case in London and Washington was paid for mainly by Iraq under Nuri al-Sa'id, a close British ally. Its staff were able, among them the Oxford historian Albert Hourani, who argued for a single democratic state before the Anglo-American Committee in March 1946. [cite:hyw_ch2] The Jewish Brigade, formed inside the British Army in 1944 with Churchill's support, gave thousands of Jewish soldiers experience of modern war. [cite:hyw_ch2] The Palestinian forces of 1948 had nothing comparable, and Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni, their best-known commander, went to Damascus to beg the Arab League's military committee for arms shortly before he was killed at al-Qastal on 8 April 1948. [cite:hyw_ch2]

The neighbours' own plans. Khalidi does not spare the Arab states. King Abdullah of Transjordan had been in secret contact with the Jewish Agency for years, and met Golda Meir before and after the partition vote. [cite:hyw_ch2] In February 1948 Britain's foreign secretary, Ernest Bevin, agreed that Abdullah's British-officered Arab Legion would take the Arab part of Palestine when the Mandate ended; the Israeli historian Avi Shlaim, whom Khalidi follows, says Bevin "colluded directly with the Transjordanians and indirectly with the Jews." [cite:hyw_ch2] On the night of 29 November 1947, Khalidi's father, Ismail, carried the Arab Higher Committee's refusal of Abdullah's offer of "protection" to the king. Abdullah's answer, as the family remembers it, was that the Palestinians deserved what would happen to them. [cite:hyw_ch2] Of the regular Arab armies, Khalidi writes, only four entered Mandate Palestine, and two of those, Jordan's Arab Legion and Iraq's forces, were forbidden by their British allies to cross into the area partition had given the Jewish state.

The emptying of the cities. The core of the book's 1948 chapter is the timing of the expulsions. Under Plan Dalet, the Haganah's operational plan of March 1948, the mixed and Arab cities fell before any Arab army crossed the border: Tiberias on 18 April, Haifa on 23 April, Jaffa's 60,000 people emptied in early May, Safad on 10 May, Beisan on 11 May. [cite:hyw_ch2] [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] About 300,000 Palestinians had been driven out or had fled before 15 May, and another 400,000 or more followed. At Dayr Yasin on 9 April, about a hundred people were killed, many of them women, children and the old. [cite:hyw_ch2] [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] By the summer of 1949 at least 720,000 of about 1.3 million Palestinians were refugees, and Israel held 78 percent of Mandate Palestine rather than the 55 percent the plan had assigned. [cite:hyw_ch2] [scene:pv_nakba|Partition and the catastrophe]

Washington's turn. Khalidi uses the American record to show how quickly policy followed politics. President Roosevelt wrote to Ibn Saud on 5 April 1945 that he would take no action "hostile to the Arab people." [cite:hyw_ch2] [cite:fdr_letter_ibnsaud] Months later President Truman told American diplomats from the region that he had to answer to "hundreds of thousands" anxious for Zionism's success and not to Arab voters, as the diplomat William Eddy recorded. [cite:hyw_ch2] The State Department and the military, Khalidi argues following Irene Gendzier, opposed partition until Israel's victories in 1948, then adapted within months. [cite:hyw_ch2]

After 1948. The book carries the same reading through the following decades. It records the raid on Qibya in October 1953, when Unit 101 under Ariel Sharon killed 69 villagers, and the killings at Khan Yunis and Rafah during the Suez war of November 1956, "more than 450" men, adding together the three lists in the special report of UNRWA's director: 275 names for Khan Yunis on 3 November, 111 for Rafah on 12 November and 66 more between 1 and 21 November. The Cold War volume reads that report closely and finds it more cautious than Khalidi's summary: for Khan Yunis it gave a list of names, not a confirmed count of the dead. [scene:cw3_gaza_killings|The Gaza killings, 1956] [cite:hyw_ch2] It treats the 1967 war as a war of choice, since the American government's own assessment was that an Egyptian attack was not imminent, and Resolution 242 as a settlement that named the Palestinians only as a refugee problem. [cite:hyw_ch3] It treats Oslo as a bargain in which Israel kept control of land, borders, water and movement while an unelected security role passed to the new Palestinian Authority. [cite:hyw_ch5] And it closes with advice on strategy: that the Palestinians should work on public opinion in the United States, Europe and beyond, and inside Israel itself, as the Algerians and Vietnamese did in the countries that ruled them, rather than expect help from Arab governments he calls subservient to Washington. [cite:hyw_concl]

How the book has been received. The reviews divide as the subject does. Lisa Anderson in Foreign Affairs called it the most cogent, comprehensive and compelling account yet written from the Palestinian vantage point. [cite:hyw_wiki] Benny Morris, the Israeli historian whose own archival work Khalidi cites on 1948, called it "simply poor history" in the Jewish Review of Books, arguing that it minimizes Palestinian violence, distorts the part the Western powers played, and treats Zionism as colonialism rather than as a national movement. [cite:hyw_wiki] Both readings are recorded here because both are serious. The documents in this part support Khalidi on the British promise, the Mandate's design, the cost of the revolt and the order of events in 1948. Morris's objection is about framing: whether a movement of refugees from European persecution, with no mother country, should be called colonial. That is a question of interpretation, and the reader can weigh it against the record.

Where the book needs correcting. A careful reader will find a few slips, none of which changes the argument. Khalidi calls Edmond de Rothschild "the British peer and financier"; Edmond was a French baron, and the land company PICA was a separate body he founded in 1924, not a renaming of Hirsch's association. [cite:hyw_ch1] [scene:pv_land_sold|The land sold] He refers to a "Hayward Commission" of 1920; the inquiry into the Jaffa riots was the Haycraft Commission of 1921. The Balfour memorandum is dated September 1919 in one chapter, 11 August 1919 in the notes and 1917 in the conclusion; the notes' date is the one given in the published British documents. [cite:hyw_ch1] [cite:hyw_concl] And he gives the 2016 Security Council vote on settlements, Resolution 2334, as 14 to 1; it passed 14 to 0, with the United States abstaining. [cite:hyw_ch6] These are logged with the library's other source conflicts.

On the map. The people and bodies in this account have been added to the network map of the road to 1948, each tie tagged to this book: the Khalidi uncle and father, Isa al-Isa, Musa al-'Alami and the Arab Office, Albert Hourani, Yusif Sayigh and the Arab National Fund, Nuri al-Sa'id, the Jewish Brigade, Balfour's 1919 memorandum, the St James's Palace talks, the Seychelles exile, the 1948 Legion understanding and the All-Palestine Government in Gaza. Ties that rest on this book alone are marked as reported, not verified. Most of the new ties sit on the Palestinian and Arab side of the map, and so, by the map's rules, they describe the road without counting as support for the state. The few that do count, Britain's arming of Jewish forces during the revolt, the Jewish Brigade, and Bevin's understanding with Abdullah, move the map's tests only slightly. The reason is itself a finding: the British contribution to the force of 1948 was already well documented, and Khalidi's account confirms it rather than enlarging it.
