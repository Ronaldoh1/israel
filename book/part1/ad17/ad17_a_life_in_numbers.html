A Life in Numbers: From Childcare to Social Security

The taught version of the Dream is a life script: a good school, college, a steady job with benefits, a first home, a retirement account and Social Security at the end. This section walks that script stage by stage using the documented typical figure at each step, most often the median, the middle value with half above and half below. It follows no real person and invents none. Each stage names the mechanism behind its number and points to the part of this book that explains it. Dollar figures are in the dollars of the year stated unless a source publishes them already adjusted for inflation, in which case its year is given.

Before school: childcare

The first large bill of a working family's life arrives before the child can walk. The Labor Department's National Database of Childcare Prices found that full-day care for one child cost between $6,552 and $15,600 a year across 2,512 counties in 2022, 8.9 to 16 per cent of the county's median family income. [cite:us18_dol_childcare] Set against the middle family of the previous section, the low end of that range is 8.8 per cent of its $74,474 income and the high end 20.9 per cent; five years of care before kindergarten would cost $32,760 to $78,000 in 2022 prices. [cite:us22_bls_ce24_q] Two incomes have become the norm that pays for it: both parents worked in 66.3 per cent of married-couple families with children in 2025. [cite:ad5_bls_famee2025] Federal childcare subsidies cap what a family pays only by rule, and the rule has moved: a 2024 rule limited subsidised families' copayments to 7 per cent of income, and a rule effective 13 July 2026 removed that federal cap, as of October 2026. [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2024] [cite:ad5_acf_ccdf2026] [scene:ad5_hours_two_jobs|Part Five]

School: the address decides the budget

Public schools spent an average of $17,619 per pupil in fiscal 2024, but the average hides the spread: $31,918 in New York against $11,060 in Idaho. Local sources supplied 43.2 per cent of school revenue, and property taxes made up 63.3 per cent of that local money, so the value of the homes around a school helps set its budget. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] The Supreme Court declined to treat this as a constitutional problem in San Antonio v. Rodriguez on 21 March 1973, holding by five votes to four that education is not a fundamental right under the federal Constitution. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez] The Education Trust found in 2018 that the highest-poverty districts received about $1,000 less per pupil in state and local money than the lowest-poverty ones, $2,000 less after allowing for the extra cost of teaching poorer children. [cite:ad7_edtrust_2018] The counter-evidence is real: counting federal money, the Urban Institute found that districts attended by poor students received about 2.5 per cent more per pupil than those attended by others in 2013–14, because states and Washington fill part of the local gap. [cite:ad7_chingos_ednext] The money matters either way: a 10 per cent rise in per-pupil spending throughout school was linked to 7.25 per cent higher adult wages, with larger effects for children from low-income families. [cite:ad7_jjp_nber] [scene:ad7_k12_property_tax|Part Seven]

College and the loan

A year at a public four-year college, counting in-state tuition, fees, housing and food, cost $25,850 in 2025–26. Published in-state tuition alone, in 2025 dollars, roughly doubled from $5,940 in 1995–96 to $11,950, but after grants the average net tuition fell to an estimated $2,300, below its 2012–13 peak of $4,450. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The maximum Pell grant of $7,395 covered 27.2 per cent of the average cost of attendance in 2024–25; in 1975–76 it covered more than three-quarters. [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell] Of 2023–24 bachelor's graduates, 47 per cent borrowed, with an average debt of $29,560, down from 56 per cent and $35,310 (in 2024 dollars) five years earlier. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] The degree still pays: median lifetime earnings were about $2.8 million for bachelor's holders against $1.6 million for high school graduates. [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] The risk sits with borrowers who do not finish or do not gain. Student debt totalled $1.65 trillion in mid-2026, about 7.7 million borrowers were in default at the start of the year, and the government planned to resume taking up to 15 per cent of a defaulted borrower's disposable pay without a court order in the autumn of 2026, with no start date announced as of October 2026. [cite:ad7_nyfed_pr] [cite:ad7_ci_garnish] [scene:ad7_college_cost_pell|Part Seven] [scene:ad7_student_debt_law|student debt law]

The first job

In the third quarter of 2026 the median full-time wage and salary worker aged 20 to 24 earned $802 a week, about $41,700 a year, and one aged 25 to 34 earned $1,173, about $61,000. Pay peaks in the next decade, at $1,414 a week for ages 35 to 44. [cite:ad17_bls_wkyeng_q3] From the first paycheck the machinery starts. Social Security and Medicare take 7.65 per cent, about $4,670 a year at $61,000; that money buys a future benefit. [cite:ad11_ssa_2026] If the employer offers health cover, as 67 per cent of firms with ten or more workers do, the average single policy cost $9,804 in 2026, of which the worker paid about 18 per cent, roughly $1,760, before an average deductible of $1,955. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] Whether a retirement account starts depends heavily on design: participation was 94 per cent in plans that enrol workers automatically against 64 per cent where workers must sign up, and 72 per cent of private workers had access to any retirement plan in 2026, 52 per cent took part. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] [scene:ad5_wages_floors|Part Five]

Rent or own

The median asking rent for a vacant rental unit was $1,531 a month in the second quarter of 2026; to keep it at 30 per cent of gross income, the usual affordability line, a renter needs $61,240 a year, almost exactly the 25-to-34 median. Nearly half of all renter households, 22.7 million, paid more than 30 per cent in 2024, a record, although national rents fell 0.5 per cent in the year to early 2026. [cite:ad17_census_hvs_q226] [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] Buying became harder over the same years. The national price of a home rose from about 3.2 times median household income in the 1990s to 5.0 times in 2024; the median new home sold for $410,700 in mid-2026, against $165,300 in early 2000. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:ad17_fred_mspus] At the 30-year mortgage rate of 7.40 per cent recorded in Freddie Mac's weekly survey on 15 October 2026, unchanged from the week before and up from 6.27 per cent a year earlier, a buyer putting 10 per cent down on that median new home would borrow $369,630 and pay about $2,559 a month in principal and interest, $30,711 a year, before property tax and insurance: 35.1 per cent of the 2025 median household income of $87,460. On the median asking price of homes listed for sale, $343,800, the same terms give about $2,142 a month. [cite:ad6_pmms] [cite:ad17_audit_pmms] [cite:ad17_census_hvs_q226] [cite:us14_census_p60] Homeownership among householders under 35 was 35.2 per cent in mid-2026, the only age group whose rate fell significantly from a year earlier; the realtors' trade association, from a survey with a 3.5 per cent response rate, puts the median first-time buyer's age at a record 40. [cite:ad17_census_hvs_q226] [cite:ad6_nar_2025] [scene:ad6_price_and_rent|Part Six]

Health cover through working life

Most working Americans are insured through a job: 53.5 per cent of residents had employment-based cover in 2025 and 7.9 per cent, 26.7 million, were uninsured all year. [cite:us16_census_2025] The average family premium reached $28,303 in 2026, of which workers paid $7,471, and over five years premiums rose 28 per cent against wages up 26.9 per cent. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] Insurance does not end the bills: in 2024, 23 per cent of working-age adults were insured all year but underinsured, meaning out-of-pocket costs high relative to income. [cite:ad8_cmwf_underins24] Those who buy their own cover lost the enhanced federal subsidies at the end of 2025, after the House passed an extension that stalled in the Senate. [cite:ad8_astho_ptc] [scene:ad8_costs_and_coverage|Part Eight]

Mid-life: debt and wealth

Debt runs through the middle decades. Three in four families, 77.0 per cent, held some debt in 2025, and the median family with debt spent 15.4 per cent of its income on payments. [cite:ad17_scf25_inc] Wealth climbs with age, though slowly at first: median net worth was $33,000 for families headed by someone under 35, $155,600 at 35 to 44, $253,700 at 45 to 54 and $411,900 at 55 to 64, in 2025 dollars; for the under-35s it fell from $42,700 in 2022. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] Much of that wealth is home equity, which is why the housing stage above shapes the retirement stage below. [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|Part Twelve] [scene:ad9_credit_and_scores|Part Nine]

The 401(k) and Social Security

The private pension that paid a set income for life has largely given way to the 401(k), an account whose value depends on what a worker puts in and how markets perform. Just over half of families, 54.9 per cent, held a retirement account in 2025, with a median balance among holders of $106,000; in Vanguard's plans, the median balance was $44,115 against an average of $167,970, because a minority of large accounts pull the average up. [cite:ad10_scf26] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] [scene:ad10_pension_shift|Part Ten] Social Security is the part of the script that holds for almost everyone. The full retirement age reaches 67 for people born in 1960 and later; the average monthly benefit was about $2,088 in 2026, about $25,000 a year, and rises 3.6 per cent to about $2,163 in January 2027. [cite:ad10_ssa_1983] [cite:ad10_napa_cola27] The combined trust funds are projected to run out in the third quarter of 2034, after which incoming taxes would pay about 83 per cent of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts. [cite:ad10_crs_tr26] Old-age poverty on the official measure fell from 35.2 per cent in 1959 to 9.9 per cent in 2024, but on the supplemental measure, which subtracts medical and other necessary costs, 15.4 per cent of people 65 and over were poor in 2025. [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:ad10_aarp_spm65] [scene:ad10_social_security|Part Ten]

Where the script works as promised

Several stages hold. Real median household income reached $87,460 in 2025, the highest since the series began in 1967. [cite:us14_census_p60] A bachelor's degree still raises lifetime earnings by more than a million dollars on average, and fewer graduates borrow than five years ago. [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] Social Security turned old age from the poorest stage of American life in 1959 into one with an official poverty rate below 10 per cent. [cite:ad10_census_a3] And for the fourth fifth of the income ladder, as the previous section showed, the script largely runs as taught. What has weakened is the guarantee that following it at the median brings each stage on time: about half of children born in the 1980s earn more than their parents did at the same age, against about 90 per cent of those born in 1940. [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] [scene:ad3_absolute_mobility|Part Three]

Interpretation, not finding: the life course as a series of tollgates

The Sophia platform reads this sequence as a series of tollgates: at each stage of the script a payment comes due, for care, credentials, shelter, cover and a retirement stake, to an industry that sets its terms, so that a life spent following the script is, in this reading, a life spent paying for the right to continue it. Read through this lens, the Dream functions less as a destination than as the instruction that keeps the payments flowing.

The strongest counter-readings answer that most of these payments buy something real: payroll taxes return as Social Security, which moved 28.8 million people out of poverty in 2025 [cite:us18_census_p60290]; tuition buys a documented earnings gain; a mortgage builds equity that rent does not. Prices at several stages are set in competitive or publicly regulated markets rather than by any single gatekeeper, and some, such as net tuition at public universities, have fallen. The tollgate reading also cannot by itself distinguish a cost that rose by design from one that rose with demand, land scarcity or medical technology; those distinctions are made mechanism by mechanism in the parts linked above, not by the metaphor.

What the record shows and does not. It shows a documented figure at each stage of the taught life script, from national surveys and administrative data, with the arithmetic shown where this book combined them. It does not show one person's life: the median at one stage is not the same person as the median at the next, and the figures come from different years, between 2022 and 2026. The mortgage example leaves out property tax, insurance and closing costs, and the rent line uses asking rents for vacant units, which can differ from what sitting tenants pay.
