The First Ties with American Intelligence, 1951 and After

While the aid argument was being fought in public, a second relationship with Washington was beginning in private. It is one of the longest-lasting intelligence partnerships the United States has had, and it has been publicly acknowledged since the 1970s. Its beginning, however, is less well documented than its later history, and the accounts of how and when it started disagree. A common view holds that the CIA and Israel's services operated from the outset as a single, seamless apparatus. The record of the early 1950s shows something more hesitant: an American agency that first counted Israel on the wrong side of the Cold War, a prime minister who persuaded its director to try cooperation, and a formal arrangement whose date, terms and American manager are all in dispute. This section tells that beginning only. What the partnership became belongs to later stages.

The starting point was suspicion. According to the journalists Yossi Melman and Dan Raviv, the CIA in 1948 initially viewed the new state as part of the Soviet sphere of influence. [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] How the agency's analysts had opposed recognition in 1948, and been overruled, is told in [scene:cia_s02|the CIA in 1948]. Israel's own services were then only months old. Their founding, from the dissolution of the Haganah's intelligence service in June 1948 to the creation of the coordinating institute under Reuven Shiloah that became the Mossad, is told in [scene:cw1_intelligence|the founding of Israel's intelligence community].

The turn, in Melman and Raviv's account, came in 1951. Ben-Gurion, touring the United States, met General Walter Bedell Smith, Truman's Director of Central Intelligence, and "convinced U.S. intelligence to give Israel a try." [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] Their account describes no formal agreement and no terms. After the visit, they write, James Jesus Angleton "was put in charge of the U.S. side", with Amos Manor and Teddy Kollek as his Israeli counterparts. [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] Manor later described the difficulty: "It wasn't easy to persuade the anti-communist Angleton that we could be friends." [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] The remark fits the starting point. The obstacle on the American side was not a lack of interest in the region; it was a doubt about which side of the East–West line Israel stood on.

Shiloah, the head of the new coordinating institute, had his own Western channels. The Encyclopaedia Judaica says he developed contacts with the CIA and with other Western intelligence services. It gives no date. [cite:rs_cw1_ej_shiloah] Whether those contacts came before Ben-Gurion's meeting, prepared it or followed it, the record cited here does not say.

Another widely repeated account gives the beginning a firmer shape. In it, formal liaison between the CIA and the Mossad began in 1951, when the two agencies agreed not to spy on each other, a "friends don't spy on friends" arrangement, and the CIA's counterintelligence chief, Angleton, personally managed the relationship from 1951 onward. [cite:wrmea_cia_mossad_tradeoffs] [cite:michaelcronin_cia_mossad_relationship] The same account credits Angleton with greater influence on American relations with Israel in his time than any Secretary of State except possibly Henry Kissinger, and describes the exchange that followed: American intelligence on the Arab states, in return for high-value material from Israel. [cite:wrmea_cia_mossad_tradeoffs] [cite:michaelcronin_cia_mossad_relationship] On two points, its date for Angleton's role and its date for the no-spying understanding, this account is contradicted by other sources, one of them the CIA's own historian.

The first correction comes from the CIA's own historian. David Robarge writes that Angleton became head of the new Counterintelligence Staff in 1954, not 1951. [cite:rs_cw1_robarge_angleton] He calls Angleton's control of Israel's account at the CIA "an administrative anomaly", and he counts it among the foundations of Angleton's influence inside the agency. Angleton, he writes, saw Israel as a bastion against the Soviet Union. [cite:rs_cw1_robarge_angleton] On this reading, Angleton could not have run the relationship as counterintelligence chief in 1951, because by Robarge's dating he did not head the Counterintelligence Staff, which Robarge describes as new, until 1954. And the account was his by an arrangement that was itself irregular, not by the ordinary working of the agency's structure.

The second correction concerns the understanding not to spy. Jeff McConnell, writing in 1986, said that Angleton had exercised formal authority over CIA liaison with Israel "since the early 1950s (how early is a matter of dispute)", and that the Israel desk sat under his counterintelligence staff. [cite:rs_cw1_merip_mcconnell] He placed the deliberate effort to end covert operations against each other in the mid-1950s, and cited the journalist Wolf Blitzer for a "discreet arrangement since the 1950s, banning covert operations against each other." [cite:rs_cw1_merip_mcconnell] That is a looser and later date than the 1951 agreement of the other account.

Each version has its strongest form. The case for 1951 rests on Ben-Gurion's meeting with Bedell Smith, which Melman and Raviv date to that year, and on the agreement of several accounts that something began then. [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] The case for a later and more gradual start rests on the CIA's own historian, who dates Angleton's post to 1954, and on McConnell, who says plainly that the date is disputed and dates the no-spying effort to the mid-1950s. [cite:rs_cw1_robarge_angleton] [cite:rs_cw1_merip_mcconnell] The two can be partly reconciled. A first contact in 1951, at the level of a prime minister and a director, may well have preceded both a formal liaison channel and a mutual understanding by several years. Robarge's date and McConnell's caution weigh against the specific claim that Angleton ran the relationship as counterintelligence chief in 1951, and against a no-spying agreement in that year. They do not weigh against a beginning in 1951.

The mechanism, as far as the sources show it, was personal before it was institutional. On the American side the decision ran from the Director of Central Intelligence, persuaded by the Israeli prime minister in person, to a single officer who held the account by what the agency's own historian calls an anomaly. On the Israeli side the counterparts named are Manor and Kollek, with Shiloah's separate contacts in the background. The Israeli services themselves answered to the prime minister, as told in the section on their founding. Who is absent is as telling: the sources cited here do not show the State Department or Congress involved at all. Nor do they describe any money moving between the services.

A further piece of the structure was laid in 1952. According to the reference account cited here, Israel's signals intelligence corps, Unit 8200, was founded in 1952, subordinate to Aman, the Military Intelligence Directorate. [cite:unit8200_wiki] Military publications call it the Israeli SIGINT National Unit, ISNU, the designation later used in American documents, and it is widely described as Israel's rough equivalent of the American National Security Agency. [cite:unit8200_wiki] This book has not confirmed the founding date from a primary document, and it should be read as reported. The same source's estimate of 5,000–10,000 personnel describes the unit in recent years, not in 1952. [cite:unit8200_wiki] Its later cooperation with the NSA, and the friction recorded in an NSA officer's account of the 2006 war in Lebanon, belong to a much later chapter: [scene:nsa_s01|Unit 8200 and the NSA].

The relationship's later course can be marked here only in outline. Melman and Raviv call the handover to the CIA in 1956, through Israeli intelligence in Warsaw, of Khrushchev's secret speech "the great turning point" of the relationship, an episode that belongs to Stage Two. [cite:rs_cw1_tablet_spies] The partnership's own record also documents friction alongside cooperation, including the Jonathan Pollard espionage case, which belongs to a later volume. [cite:wiki_israeli_espionage_us]

One discipline from the account that dates the liaison to 1951 deserves to be carried forward, because it applies from the first year to the last. Two real relationships that share one partner do not, by themselves, prove a third relationship between the other two. That account applies the rule to a twenty-first-century case: the CIA's documented tie to Israel's services, and its separate venture investment, from around 2005, in the data company Palantir, do not establish any tie between Israel's services and Palantir, and none is documented. [cite:wiki_palantir_technologies] A shared hub is not a pipeline. The same rule governs the early 1950s. That the CIA dealt with Israeli intelligence, and also with other Western services, and that Shiloah had contacts with several of them, does not make them one network, and the record of these years shows no such thing.

What the record does not settle should be stated without hedging. No document cited here records the terms of any agreement made in 1951, and the fullest account of that year says plainly that it describes none. The date of Shiloah's Western contacts is not given. When Angleton first took responsibility for the Israeli account, and on whose authority, is disputed by the sources themselves, one of which is the CIA's own historian. The no-spying understanding has three dates in circulation: 1951, "since the 1950s", and the mid-1950s. And the founding date of Unit 8200 rests on a reference account rather than a primary record. The beginning of the partnership is real. Its precise shape in 1951 is not yet documented.
