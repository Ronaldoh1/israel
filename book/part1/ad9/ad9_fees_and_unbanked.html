Fees, the Unbanked and the Rules That Did Not Survive

The first practical lesson of the Dream as taught is to open a bank account. A paycheck goes in, bills come out, savings grow, and the account becomes the base for every later step, from a credit card to a mortgage. For most Americans it works that way. This section looks at the people for whom it costs more: account holders who pay overdraft and late fees, and households who stay outside the banking system and pay to cash their own wages. It also follows two federal rules written in 2024 to cut those fees, and what happened to each of them in 2025.

Overdraft: a loan that was not called one

An overdraft happens when a bank pays a card swipe, check or withdrawal that the account does not have the money to cover, and charges a fee for doing so. A non-sufficient-funds (NSF) fee is charged when the bank refuses the payment instead. When Congress passed the Truth in Lending Act in 1968 to make lenders disclose the cost of credit, the Federal Reserve Board in 1969 exempted overdrafts, then treated as an occasional courtesy for a check that had not yet cleared. [cite:ad9_cfpb_od_final] The exemption meant an overdraft fee was not disclosed as a finance charge or an APR, even though, treated as a loan, a $35 fee on a $50 overdraft repaid within a week works out to an APR of about 3,650 per cent (35 ÷ 50 × 365 ÷ 7; book arithmetic).

The fees were large and concentrated. Banks with more than $1 billion in assets collected $11.96 billion in overdraft and NSF fees in 2019. Earlier CFPB research found that nearly 80 per cent of those fees were paid by fewer than 9 per cent of account holders, those who overdrew more than ten times a year. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] Then the largest banks changed their own policies, and the CFPB reported that the vast majority of NSF fees had been eliminated. Revenue at the same group of banks fell to $7.61 billion in 2022 and $5.83 billion in 2023, a drop of 51 per cent from 2019. The CFPB put the saving at about $6.1 billion a year, or about $185 for the average household that overdraws. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] That figure covers only banks above $1 billion. The National Consumer Law Center, an advocacy group, estimated that all banks and credit unions together collected about $12.4 billion in overdraft and NSF fees in 2025, extrapolated from the largest banks' reports; the two numbers measure different sets of institutions and cannot be joined into one trend. [cite:us22_nclc_od26] The same brief reported that the top 20 banks' overdraft revenue rose 6.2 per cent between 2023 and 2025, and that BMO Bank raised its fee from $15 to $20 in 2026. [cite:us22_nclc_od26]

The overdraft rule, 2024–2025

In December 2024 the CFPB issued a rule ending the 1969 exemption for banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets. Each could choose to charge no more than $5 per overdraft, to charge a fee no higher than its own costs and losses, or to treat overdraft as ordinary credit, with the disclosures and protections of a credit card. The bureau estimated savings of up to $5 billion a year and set an effective date of 1 October 2025. [cite:ad9_cfpb_od_final] The American Bankers Association called the rule "flawed and unlawful from the start", said most Americans value overdraft coverage, and argued that government price limits would make it harder for banks to offer it. [cite:ad9_aba_od]

The rule never took effect. Congress used the Congressional Review Act, which lets it overturn a recent agency rule by simple majority. The Senate passed the resolution 52 to 48 on 27 March 2025 and the House 217 to 211 on 9 April, largely along party lines, and President Trump signed it on 9 May 2025. [cite:ad9_ballotpedia_cra] [cite:ad9_plaw_119_10] As of October 2026 there is no federal limit on the size of an overdraft fee. The agency that wrote the rule is itself in dispute: in September 2026 a federal court in Oregon ruled that its acting director had unlawfully requested no funding from the Federal Reserve for the 2026 fiscal year, related cases were on appeal, and a House committee had advanced a bill to move the bureau's funding to annual appropriations. [cite:ad9_goodwin_cfpb]

The late-fee rule, 2024–2025

The CARD Act of 2009 requires credit-card penalty fees to be "reasonable and proportional" to the violation. The rules that carry it out include a safe harbor, a fee level an issuer may charge without proving its costs. In March 2024 the CFPB cut the safe harbor for the largest issuers from about $32 to $8, letting issuers charge more only if they showed their costs justified it; the National Consumer Law Center estimated that the change would save cardholders about $10 billion a year. [cite:ad9_hk_late] [cite:ad9_nclc_late] Bank and business groups sued in Texas before it took effect, and it never did. On 14 April 2025 the CFPB, under new leadership, joined the plaintiffs in asking the court to vacate it, agreeing that it violated the CARD Act, and on 15 April Judge Mark Pittman of the Northern District of Texas did so. [cite:ad9_hk_late] As of October 2026 the earlier safe harbor applies again. Cardholders paid $31.3 billion in card fees of all kinds in 2024. [cite:us22_cfpb_ccmr25]

Outside the bank

The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation surveys about 30,000 households every two years. In June 2023, 4.2 per cent of American households, about 5.6 million, had no bank account, down from 8.2 per cent in 2011; another 14.2 per cent, about 19 million, had an account but also used non-bank services such as check cashers or money orders. The most common main reason given for having no account was not having enough money to meet minimum-balance requirements (23.3 per cent), followed by distrust of banks (15.7 per cent); reasons tied to fees or minimum balances together accounted for a third. [cite:ad9_fdic2023] The Federal Reserve's own 2025 survey of adults found 94 per cent had an account, but only 77 per cent of those with incomes under $25,000. [cite:ad9_shed2025]

The rates differ sharply by circumstance. Households with lower or more volatile incomes, less education or a single parent are more often unbanked; so are working-age households with a disability, at 11.2 per cent against 3.7 per cent for those without. Black (10.6 per cent), Hispanic (9.5 per cent) and American Indian or Alaska Native (12.2 per cent) households had rates several times that of white households, and the FDIC found the gaps persisted at every income level, which means income alone does not explain them; the survey reports the gaps without assigning a single cause. The FDIC attributed about two-thirds of the decline since 2011 to rising incomes and education rather than to changes in banking. [cite:ad9_fdic2023] Only 10 per cent of unbanked households held a credit card and 78.4 per cent had no mainstream credit of any kind, which ties this section back to the credit-invisible population in the last. [cite:ad9_fdic2023] [cite:ad9_cfpb_invis2025]

Life outside a bank has its own prices. A check casher charges a percentage of each check; New York, one of the states that caps the fee, allows the greater of $1 or 2.2 per cent for a paycheck and 1.5 per cent for a government benefit check. [cite:ad9_nydfs_cc] A worker in New York cashing a $1,000 paycheck every two weeks at the cap pays $22 a check, or $572 a year (2.2 per cent × $1,000 × 26; book arithmetic); states without caps allow more. About one unbanked household in five used a prepaid card in 2023, and two in three were cash only. [cite:ad9_fdic2023] Prepaid cards carry their own monthly, reload and ATM fees; since 1 April 2019 a federal rule has required a short fee chart on the package and fraud and error protection once the card is registered. [cite:ad9_nclc_prepaid]

Where it works as promised

The record here contains real progress that owed nothing to the two rules. Overdraft and NSF revenue at large banks halved between 2019 and 2023 through the banks' own policy changes, NSF fees largely disappeared at the biggest institutions, and the share of unbanked households halved between 2011 and 2023. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] [cite:ad9_fdic2023] The banking industry's position is that customers value overdraft coverage and that price limits would make it harder to offer; the bureau's figures show that most of the cost fell on the small group who overdraw often, so the dispute was over price and over that group rather than over the service itself. [cite:ad9_aba_od] [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] What the record establishes is narrower than either side's slogan: the cost of an ordinary account falls mostly on a small group with thin balances, the two federal limits on that cost were removed in 2025 by a court and by Congress, and as of October 2026 the price of overdraft is set by each bank.
