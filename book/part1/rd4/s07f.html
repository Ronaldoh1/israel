The American Zionist Emergency Council, 1943–1945

In August 1943, five years before there was a state to lobby for, the leading American Zionist organizations placed their political work under a single body: the American Zionist Emergency Council. A familiar account holds that American political support for Israel grew up after 1948, a natural response to the founding of a new democracy. The council's own papers point to an earlier and more deliberate origin. By the time independence was declared, a functioning domestic political organization had already been built, staffed and run for five years. It worked in the open and within the law. Its purpose was to turn the movement's goals into congressional votes and party platforms.

Before 1943 the major American Zionist organizations had operated under separate leaderships and sometimes pursued competing priorities. The council pooled their resources for one purpose, domestic political influence, and ran them as one coordinated operation [cite:azec_powerbase]. Rabbi Abba Hillel Silver and Rabbi Stephen Wise were its first co-chairs [cite:azec_case]. Its importance outlasted its own years: through a documented line of succession, the council became the direct institutional predecessor of AIPAC [cite:azec_powerbase]. That later history belongs to other volumes of this library. What matters here is that the organization existed, and worked, during the war.

It came into being a little more than a year after the Biltmore conference of May 1942 had committed the movement to a "Jewish Commonwealth" in Palestine, and as another, rival campaign was already filling American newspapers with full-page advertisements: the Bergson group's committees, whose story is told in [scene:bg_s01|the Bergson group's campaign]. The council was the mainstream's answer, and its method was different. Where the Bergson committees bought space and staged pageants, the council built a standing national network that could be pointed at Congress on a given week.

Silver's break with quiet diplomacy. The two co-chairs did not share a view of how the work should be done. Silver argued for energetic public-opinion pressure and firm political mobilization. Earlier Zionist leaders had preferred a more cautious diplomatic approach, and Silver's course was an explicit and named departure from it, not a continuation of established practice [cite:azec_case]. Not everyone in the council or the wider movement agreed with the shift. The same period saw a comparable contest over method between Chaim Weizmann and David Ben-Gurion, told in the section on [scene:s07b|the Biltmore programme]. Inside the council the disagreement was serious enough that Silver resigned as co-chairman during a controversy in 1944–45 over how hard the organization should confront the American government; his own archived papers document the episode. He was later reinstated and led the council through 1949 [cite:azec_powerbase]. The internal conflict is part of the record, not a footnote to it.

The method, from the council's own files. The fullest account of how the council worked comes from its internal correspondence, preserved among the Abba Hillel Silver Papers at the American Jewish Archives. These are the organizers' own memos, and they describe a national method run from a New York headquarters at 342 Madison Avenue. Instructions went out to "Chairmen of Local Emergency Committees" in cities across the country [cite:aja_azec_local_1944].

The first test came quickly. When Senators Robert Wagner and Robert Taft introduced their Palestine resolution in January 1944, a memo told the local chairmen that they would be sent sample letters, so that within a concentrated period letters from all over the country would "pour into Washington." The same instructions told local committees to interview their senators and representatives in person and to place letters to the editor in their local newspapers [cite:aja_azec_local_1944] [cite:aja_azec_local_1945]. The sequence below follows those instructions step by step.

Telegram campaigns were aimed at particular members by name. Among them was Representative Sol Bloom, chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]. The council also published a book, America and Palestine, and sent it to every member of Congress. It printed the pro-Zionist statements of nearly 400 members of the 78th Congress, so that each legislator could see in print the public commitments his own colleagues had already made [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation]. The book turned scattered statements into a visible majority, and it put each member on notice that his own words were on record.

The same machinery carried on after the war ended in Europe. In 1945, with Harry Truman now president, local councils mobilized thousands of telegrams to the White House. In Detroit alone, 150 Zionist and community leaders organized the appeal [cite:aja_azec_local_1945]. The council did not reserve its pressure for opponents. In 1948, after Bloom remarked that Truman was "on the right track" during the administration's retreat toward a trusteeship for Palestine, an AZEC staff memo called the remark "horrible" and urged that people in Bloom's district take it up with him [cite:aja_bloom_1948]. That episode lies beyond this chapter, but it shows the method fully formed: an ally who drifted was handled through his own constituents.

One further claim comes from a critic's history rather than from the council's papers, and it should be read as such. According to that account, mail addressed to Senator Wagner in his capacity as chairman of the American Palestine Committee was opened and kept at Zionist headquarters, and Wagner was sent a copy [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. The council's own files, as cited here, do not confirm or refute it. It remains a reported claim from a single critical source.

What the campaign achieved, and when. The results came fast. Silver's biographical record credits his lobbying in 1944 with securing a Palestine resolution in Congress, and in the same year both major parties wrote Palestine into their platforms [cite:azec_case]. The congressional record, set out in the next section, is more exact about what "passage" meant. The House Foreign Affairs Committee approved the resolution on 30 November 1944; the full Congress deferred it that December, and a version was adopted only in December 1945 [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:frus1945_concres]. Both accounts agree on the substance: within roughly a year of its founding, the council had moved Palestine into the language of both parties and through a congressional committee, four years before there was a state. How those votes and platforms came about is told in [scene:us_s05|Palestine in Congress and the party platforms].

The money behind the movement. The council's files document a method, not a budget. They show organization: memos, sample letters, a book mailed to Congress, telegrams. They do not show money changing hands with legislators, and that is the finding the record supports. The money in the American movement during these years flowed through a different and far larger channel: philanthropic giving through the United Jewish Appeal.

The scale of that giving is set out in the library's funding ledger, drawing on Marc Lee Raphael's history of the United Jewish Appeal. In its first year, 1939, the appeal raised $14 million, about $324 million in 2025 dollars. Across the war years, 1939 to 1945, it raised more than $124 million, about $2.4 billion today. The ledger converts each sum using the federal consumer price index, and it carries one caution that matters for any reading of these figures. The campaign split its money three ways: relief for Jews in Europe, the Palestine programme, and the resettlement of refugees in the United States. Counting the whole sum as "funding Israel" would overstate what went to Palestine. The ledger's precise split is documented for 1948, when about half of the year's $150 million went to the Palestine arm, about 43% to relief in Europe and the rest to resettlement in the United States; for the war years the record here gives the three destinations but not the shares.

The second panel places the wartime giving on the longer curve. The war years averaged roughly $0.35 billion a year in today's dollars. After 1945 the totals rose steeply, to about $1.65 billion in 1946 and about $2.0 billion in 1948. The rise belongs to the next stage of the story. What the wartime figures show is a giving network that existed alongside, not inside, the political campaign the council ran. The appeal's money went to relief, the Palestine programme and resettlement; the council's work in Congress ran on letters, visits and telegrams.

Organized and sincere. The council's papers settle one question and leave another open. They settle that American political support for a Jewish commonwealth did not simply arise of its own accord. It was organized, timed and aimed, from a headquarters with a mailing list, against named members on named dates. They do not show that the sympathy behind the letters was manufactured. The letters were sincere, and the sympathy was real; the council's achievement was to organize both. That method, not money, is what the movement's own files document most fully. Both statements hold together, and neither cancels the other.

Two maps place the council within the larger structure. The first shows the two American machines of the 1940s, the Bergson committees and the council, side by side and moving toward Congress, the platforms and the White House. The second shows where the council fits in the half-century from the Basel Congress to the State of Israel: the "US political machine" between the Jewish Agency and the United Nations.

What the record does not settle. The council's files prove a sustained, organized campaign. They do not prove that the council alone set American policy toward Palestine. Truman's eventual decision to recognize Israel in 1948 had several causes, among them domestic electoral calculation and the competing objections of the State Department, and the council's five years of pressure were one factor among them, not the sole or sufficient explanation [cite:azec_powerbase]. Nor does the record here measure how many letters were drafted from the council's samples and how many were written unprompted, or how far any member's vote moved because of them. The papers show what the organizers did; they cannot show what each legislator would have done without them.
