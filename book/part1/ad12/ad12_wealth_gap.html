Income Is a Flow, Wealth Is a Stock: The Gap the Paycheck Does Not Show

The American Dream is usually taught as a story about income: work hard, earn more, and the rest follows. The record of the last three decades shows that the rest does not follow automatically, because the things the Dream promises most concretely, a paid-off home, a secure retirement and a head start for one's children, are bought with wealth, not with a single year's pay. Income is a flow: what a household takes in over a year from wages, interest, rents or government benefits. Wealth, or net worth, is a stock: what a household owns at a moment in time (a house, savings, retirement accounts, shares, a business) minus what it owes (mortgage, student loans, credit cards). Part Two sets out the two measures and why the median, the household in the exact middle, tells more than the average when a few households hold very large amounts. Two gaps of different size

The clearest way to see why the distinction matters is to set the two measures side by side for the same families in the same survey. The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), a detailed interview of several thousand households conducted every three years, found that the median American family had an income of $82,200 in 2024 and a net worth of $215,900 in 2025, both in 2025 dollars. [cite:ad2_scf_t1] [cite:us8_fed_scf_pr] Split by race, median income for white non-Hispanic families was $95,300 and for Black non-Hispanic families $49,400: a ratio of a little under two to one ($95,300 ÷ $49,400 = 1.9). Median net worth was $332,100 and $36,800: a ratio of about nine to one ($332,100 ÷ $36,800 = 9.0). [cite:ad2_scf_t1] [cite:us8_scf2025] The income gap is real and large; the wealth gap is almost five times wider. A family can close much of an income gap in one career. A wealth gap of that size is mostly inherited from the past, through housing, inheritance and the timing of when each family could first buy assets, and it compounds forward.

What the 2025 survey found, and how sure it is

The 2025 survey, released on 9 October 2026, reversed a story that had become common after 2022. Between 2022 and 2025, in 2025 dollars, the median net worth of white non-Hispanic families rose from $311,900 to $332,100 (up 6 per cent), Hispanic families from $67,400 to $79,600 (up 18 per cent), Asian families from $586,600 to $677,600, and Black families fell from $49,100 to $36,800 (down 25 per cent). [cite:us8_scf2025] A widely repeated figure from the 2022 survey held that the white–Black ratio had narrowed to about 6.3 to 1; on the same survey's 2022 figures the ratio was 6.4 ($311,900 ÷ $49,100), and in 2025 it was back to 9.0. [cite:us8_scf2025]

The Black decline needs a caution before it is treated as a trend. The Board's report says that statistical significance "generally is not addressed" in its summary tables, and it does not print standard errors for groups by race. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] The nearest guide is the Board's 2019 analysis, which gave the Black median as $24,100 with a range of about $19,800 to $28,400, roughly 18 per cent either side ($4,300 ÷ $24,100). [cite:ad12_fed_2019race] A fall of 25 per cent between two waves, each estimated with that kind of imprecision, is larger than either wave's own margin but not by a wide distance. What the survey does establish firmly is the level: in no wave since 1989 has the Black median been more than about a sixth of the white median. [cite:ad12_epi_race]

Age and education matter as much as race. Median net worth for families headed by someone under 35 fell from $42,700 to $33,000 between 2022 and 2025, while families headed by someone 75 or older rose from $367,300 to $504,900. Families headed by a college graduate had a median of $505,400, against $106,700 for a high-school graduate and $18,600 for a head without a diploma. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] Taken across all families, the Board concluded there was "little change in wealth inequality between surveys." [cite:ad12_scf25_t2]

The view from the top: the Distributional Financial Accounts

The SCF is precise about the middle but samples the very richest households only lightly. To see concentration at the top, the Federal Reserve also publishes the Distributional Financial Accounts (DFA), which spread the national balance sheet across wealth groups every quarter from the third quarter of 1989: the top 0.1 per cent, the rest of the top 1 per cent, the next 9 per cent, the next 40 per cent and the bottom half. [cite:ad12_dfa_overview] In the release of 18 September 2026, the top 1 per cent held a record 32.5 per cent of all household net worth in the second quarter of 2026, up from a low of 22.4 per cent in 1990; the bottom half of households held 2.3 per cent, against 3.5 per cent in 1989. [cite:ad12_ttt_top1] [cite:ad12_ad_bottom50] Put another way, the top 1 per cent of households held about 14 times as much wealth as the bottom 50 per cent combined (32.5 ÷ 2.3). The Fed revises the whole history at each release, so these shares describe that vintage. [cite:ad12_ttt_top1]

Financial assets, the part of wealth that pays dividends and interest, are more concentrated still and have been concentrating steadily. The top 1 per cent's share of all household financial assets (bank deposits, bonds, shares, funds, pension entitlements) rose from 25.8 per cent in 1989 to 31.2 per cent in 2007 and 36.5 per cent in the second quarter of 2026. Over the same years the bottom half's share of all assets, including homes and cars, fell from 7.2 per cent to 5.0 per cent. [cite:ad12_fred_t1fin] [cite:ad12_fred_b50assets]

How wealth compounds

The mechanism that turns a gap in stock into a widening gap is ordinary compounding, and it works the same way for every family that owns assets. A return is earned as a percentage of what is already owned. At a 5 per cent annual return, a family with $10,000 of savings gains $500 in a year; a family with $1 million gains $50,000, without either working an extra hour. Left to grow for 30 years at 7 per cent, $100,000 becomes about $761,000 ($100,000 × 1.0730). A wage, by contrast, is earned one hour at a time and stops when work stops. This is arithmetic, not a finding about anyone's conduct, but it explains why the shares above move in one direction during long periods of rising asset prices: families who already own more of the assets that rise collect more of the rise.

Which assets a family holds decides which rise it catches. Shares are concentrated at the top: the 2025 SCF found that 56 per cent of families owned stock directly or indirectly, mostly through retirement accounts, down from 58 per cent in 2022, with the decline among families outside the top tenth of income; only 19 per cent owned shares directly. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] Reporting on the Fed's distributional data in April 2026 put the top tenth's share of corporate equities and mutual-fund shares above 87 per cent. [cite:ad12_thestreet_eq] For the middle, the main asset is the house. In 2005, home equity (the value of the home minus the mortgage) made up 44 per cent of the net worth of white households, 59 per cent for Black households and nearly two-thirds for Hispanic households. [cite:ad12_pew2011] A household whose wealth is mostly one house is exposed to one local market and carries a large debt against it; a household whose wealth is mostly a diversified share portfolio is not. That difference is what made 2008 so unequal, as the third section of this part shows. [scene:ad12_2008_foreclosures|The 2008 foreclosures]

Where it works as promised

The record is not one of uniform loss. The bottom half's combined net worth collapsed after 2007 but then recovered strongly: from $331 billion in the third quarter of 2010 to $1.81 trillion in 2019 and $4.28 trillion in the second quarter of 2026, in current dollars. [cite:ad12_fred_b50nw] Between 2016 and 2019 the median net worth of Black families rose 32.6 per cent and of Hispanic families 64.9 per cent, faster than white families' 3.5 per cent, and Hispanic families gained again between 2022 and 2025. [cite:ad12_fed_2019race] [cite:us8_scf2025] Homeownership, the middle-class route into wealth, still reached 65.6 per cent of families in 2025. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] The Boston Fed's analysis of the white–Black gap found that lifetime earnings, pensions and education explain about 80 per cent of it, which means that work, and the pensions tied to it, remain the main road to wealth for most families. [cite:ad12_bostonfed_inherit] The ownership route the Dream describes is open; what the record shows is that it starts from very different places and that its returns compound for those already on it.

Interpretation, not finding: the Dream as a story that keeps people on the wage side

The Sophia platform reads the income–wealth split as the centre of its "illusion" thesis: in this reading, the Dream is taught as an income story so that households keep working for wages while the returns that actually build security flow to those who own capital, and the gap between the two is the extraction. Read through this lens, the steady rise of the top 1 per cent's share of financial assets is the system doing what it is built to do. The strongest counter-readings are documented. The Federal Reserve's own conclusion for 2022–2025 was "little change" in overall inequality, not a sharp rise. [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] Much of the concentration of shares reflects age and life-cycle saving as well as class: the 75-and-over median is fifteen times the under-35 median ($504,900 ÷ $33,000). [cite:ad12_scf25_t2] And compounding is a property of all saving, including the retirement accounts held by a majority of families; no document shows that the civic teaching of the Dream was designed to keep anyone off the ownership side. The lens is an interpretation of the pattern, not a finding about intent.
