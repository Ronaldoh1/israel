Palestinian Politics, 1928–1935: From Congress to Parties

A familiar claim holds that the Palestinians had no leaders, no national organization and no political demands before 1948. By 1928 they had already held seven national congresses, and their central demand to Britain was an elected government. From 1919, Muslims and Christians organized together through Muslim–Christian Associations; the congresses chose an Arab Executive under Musa Kazim al-Husseini, called for "a national government responsible to a representative assembly", and rejected the Balfour Declaration and mass Jewish immigration [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive]. That earlier history, from the first congress of 1919 to the delegations of 1921–22, is told in [scene:pp_s01|the Palestinian national movement]. This section takes up the story at the last of those congresses and follows it to the party system that stood on the eve of 1936. It is a story of choices made by Palestinians, among themselves and against one another, under a mandatory power that never recognized their Executive [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim].

The Seventh Palestinian Arab Congress met in June 1928. Its importance lay in who attended. Raghib al-Nashashibi's opposition bloc took part, and Nashashibi himself joined the Arab Executive that emerged from it [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi]. Rivalry between the factions aligned with the Husseini family and those aligned with the Nashashibis had divided Palestinian politics since the British dismissed Musa Kazim al-Husseini as mayor of Jerusalem in 1920 and appointed Raghib al-Nashashibi in his place [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]. The 1928 congress briefly brought the two camps into one front [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress]. Nashashibi held the mayoralty throughout: re-elected in 1927, he served until 1934, when Hussein Fakhri al-Khalidi was elected [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi].

Two months after the violence of August 1929, Palestinian organization widened. On 26 October 1929 the first Palestine Arab Women's Congress met in Jerusalem, with about 300 delegates. Salma al-Husseini led it; Wahida al-Khalidi and Amina al-Husseini organized it. The congress called for repeal of the Balfour Declaration and an end to Jewish immigration, and it elected a fourteen-member executive [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_womens]. Its demands matched those of the national congresses. What was new was that women had built their own national body to press them.

The Executive's next move was diplomatic. In 1930, while London weighed the findings of its inquiries into 1929, an Arab Executive delegation went to London. Nashashibi travelled with it in March 1930, and Jamal al-Husseini sat on the delegation that negotiated there in April and May [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi] [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_jamal]. The sources used here do not record what the delegation obtained. The policy London published that autumn, and its withdrawal in February 1931, are told in the section on the Passfield White Paper.

In December 1931 the rivalry took international form. The General Islamic Congress opened in Jerusalem on 7 December 1931 with 130 delegates from 22 countries, according to an account in Middle East Eye, and Hajj Amin al-Husseini, the Mufti, was among its main organizers. It resolved to defend the holy places, to boycott Zionist goods, to found an Islamic university in Jerusalem and to form a company to buy Palestinian land [cite:rs_rd3_mee_congress_1931]. The same account records Britain's response: High Commissioner Wauchope questioned the Mufti about the delegates' political speeches; Britain deported Abd al-Rahman Azzam and refused entry to the former caliph Abdulmecid II; and the university project stalled for lack of funds in 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_mee_congress_1931]. The Mufti's congress did not speak for all Palestinians. In the same month Nashashibi held a rival conference in Jerusalem to counter it [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi].

Land was a recurring concern. In 1932 the Executive launched a "Piaster Project", a fund to keep land in Arab hands [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress]. Like the Islamic Congress's land company, it answered the purchases described in [scene:rd3_land|land and labour, 1922–1933].

The sharpest challenge to the Executive came from inside the national movement. In August 1932 eleven founders, among them Rashid al-Haj Ibrahim, Awni Abd al-Hadi and Muhammad Izzat Darwaza, officially announced the Istiqlal (Independence) Party. It called for full Arab independence and Arab unity, and named British imperialism as the main enemy [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_hajibrahim] [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_istiqlal]. That was a change of target. The congresses had petitioned Britain; Istiqlal treated Britain itself as the adversary, and it attacked the established leadership for inaction in 1932–33 [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim]. Its reach was limited. According to a reference account drawing on Porath, Khalidi, Kedourie and others, the party never built a large membership; its influence peaked among young educated Palestinians in early 1933 and then fell, partly through Husseini hostility and partly for lack of funds [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_istiqlal]. Its founders remained active: until June 1933 Awni Abd al-Hadi was representing the tenants of Wadi al-Hawarith in court, and Istiqlal joined the protests when the eviction order came [cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith].

Open confrontation with the government came in October 1933, called not by Istiqlal but by the Arab Executive. Following its call for protest demonstrations, disturbances broke out in Jerusalem on 13 October, in Jaffa, Haifa and Nablus on 27–28 October, and in Jerusalem again on 28–29 October. The Murison Commission, which investigated them, found that they were "aimed against the Government and not against the Jews" [cite:rs_rd3_murison_palquest]. Its count was exact. One policeman and 26 members of the public were killed; 56 police and 187 members of the public were injured. All the public deaths were by gunfire, 15 of them at Jaffa, where police fired 102 rifle and 43 revolver rounds [cite:rs_rd3_murison_palquest]. Musa Kazim al-Husseini, the Executive's president, was injured by British forces at the Jaffa protest [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim].

The commission reported on 4 January 1934 and found that the police had acted with restraint [cite:rs_rd3_murison_palquest]. The two readings of October 1933 rest on the same report. In Murison's own judgment, the police acted "with restraint and forbearance" and no officer departed from regulations. The protesters' case draws on its other findings: the demonstrations were political protest against government policy, called openly by the national leadership, and every one of the 26 civilian dead was killed by police gunfire, while one policeman died [cite:rs_rd3_murison_palquest]. The commission found that the force used was lawful and measured. Its figures show who bore the cost of that force. A reader can hold both findings at once; the report does not resolve them into one verdict, and neither does this book.

Musa Kazim al-Husseini died in March 1934, and the Arab Executive dissolved that year [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]. Over fifteen years the congresses and their Executive had sent petitions and delegations asking for an elected government; Britain recognized the Zionist Organization's Jewish Agency but never recognized the Palestinians' own Executive [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]. Its end left the field to parties built around the old rivalries.

Nashashibi moved first. Having lost the mayoralty of Jerusalem to Hussein Fakhri al-Khalidi in 1934, he founded the National Defense Party in early December 1934. Its program rejected the Balfour Declaration, Jewish immigration and land sales, and called for parliamentary government; Nashashibi was close to Emir Abdullah [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_nashashibi]. The Husseini camp answered within four months. The Palestine Arab Party was officially established on 27 March 1935, and Jamal al-Husseini, close to the Mufti, was unanimously chosen as its leader [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_jamal]. By the end of 1935, five Arab parties stood where one Executive had been. Late that year they formed a united front, which the American consul general in Jerusalem, Leland Morris, described in April 1936 as a response to a "national emergency" [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512]. How that front met the High Commissioner's offer of a legislative council, and how it held, is the subject of [scene:rd3_council_1935|the legislative council of 1935–36].

The structure that emerged had two poles and a third force. The Husseini pole ran through the Mufti, who held religious office and had organized the Islamic Congress, and through the Palestine Arab Party under Jamal al-Husseini, who was close to him. The Nashashibi pole ran through the National Defense Party, which held to the same rejection of the Balfour Declaration but looked toward parliamentary government and stood close to Emir Abdullah. Istiqlal, small and short of money, named British imperialism as the main enemy. The united front of late 1935 joined them without dissolving them.

Who could act in this structure, and through what, is part of the story. The Palestinian leaders had no recognized channel to government. They had congresses, delegations, the press, demonstrations, and from 1934 parties; the mandatory power had police, deportation and the power to refuse entry. The Islamic Congress shows both sides of that balance. It could gather 130 delegates and pass resolutions on land, boycott and a university; the High Commissioner could question its organizer and Britain could expel a delegate, and the university project stalled in 1935 for lack of funds [cite:rs_rd3_mee_congress_1931].

Several questions the record does not settle. The sources do not say what the 1930 London delegation won or lost in its negotiations. The figures for the Islamic Congress and for Istiqlal's decline come from secondary accounts and are attributed as such. The sources do not name the two smaller parties of the 1935 front, or show how far the front reconciled the Husseini and Nashashibi camps rather than suspending their rivalry. And the Murison report leaves open the question at the center of October 1933: whether a police force that fired 145 rounds at Jaffa and killed 15 people there acted with restraint, as the commission found, or whether the toll itself is the better measure. What the record does show is a political movement with named leaders, recorded demands and organized institutions, making its own choices, and divided against itself, through the years before 1936.
