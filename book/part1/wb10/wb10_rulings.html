The Rulings

The Security Council, 2016. On 23 December 2016 the Security Council adopted Resolution 2334 by 14 votes to none, with the United States abstaining rather than using its veto. It found that the settlements have "no legal validity" and are a "flagrant violation" of international law, and called on all states to distinguish, in their dealings, between Israel and the territories occupied since 1967. The Council had addressed the settlements before, in Resolutions 446 and 465. Netanyahu called the resolution "part of the swan song of the old world that is biased against Israel," and the government said it would not comply. The resolution asked the Secretary-General to report every three months; the first report, in March 2017, found that no steps had been taken to stop settlement, and that announcements had increased. [cite:wb_wiki_2334]

The World Court, 2004. In July 2004 the International Court of Justice, by 14 votes to 1, advised that the barrier's route inside the West Bank was contrary to international law, that the settlements "have been established in breach of international law," and that the Fourth Geneva Convention applies. [cite:wb_asil_icj2004] Part Six followed what became of the barrier. [scene:wb6_barrier|The wall]

The World Court, 2024. On 19 July 2024 the Court went further. It found Israel's continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory unlawful, and said Israel must end it "as rapidly as possible," immediately cease all new settlement activity, evacuate all settlers, and make reparation. It found that the settlements breach the Fourth Geneva Convention, as does legalizing outposts, and that Israel's restrictions on Palestinians amount to "systemic discrimination." It said all states must not recognize the situation as legal or help maintain it. Eleven of the fifteen judges agreed with all its findings; the Court's vice-president, Julia Sebutinde, dissented. [cite:wb_livelaw_icj2024] [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] Netanyahu answered: "The Jewish people are not occupiers in their own land, including in our eternal capital Jerusalem." [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024]

The General Assembly, 2024. On 18 September 2024 the General Assembly voted 124 to 14, with 43 abstentions, to demand that Israel end its presence in the occupied territory within twelve months. The United States voted against, calling the text "inflammatory"; Israel's ambassador, Danny Danon, called it a shameful decision backing the Palestinian Authority's "diplomatic terrorism." [cite:wb_ahram_unga2024] The twelve months ended in September 2025. Part Nine set out what happened in them: record settlement approvals and E1. [scene:wb9_annex|Since October 2023]

The International Criminal Court. Palestine joined the Court in 2015, and on 3 March 2021 the prosecutor opened an investigation into the situation in Palestine. Israel, which, like the United States, is not a party to the Court's statute, said it does not recognize the Court's authority. [cite:wb_aj_icc_timeline] In November 2024 the Court issued arrest warrants for Netanyahu and his former defense minister, Yoav Gallant, over the war in Gaza. [cite:gz_un_icc] In February 2025 President Trump declared a national emergency over the Court, calling the Netanyahu warrant "baseless," and sanctioned its officials; four of its judges were sanctioned in June 2025, and in June 2026 three judges sued in a US federal court. [cite:wb_hrw_iccjudges] [cite:wb_jurist_iccsuit]

Why None of It Is Enforced

The record does not show inaction. It shows rulings and resolutions, each of which stopped at a specific point. The table sets out each one, what it asked, and where it stopped.

Three mechanisms run through the table. The World Court's opinions on this question were advisory opinions, not judgments in a case between states. [cite:wb_asil_icj2004] [cite:wb_livelaw_icj2024] The General Assembly's resolutions are not binding. [cite:wb_ahram_unga2024] And the bodies that can bind or punish, the Security Council and national governments, depend on the positions of states, above all the United States, whose positions have changed with each administration, as the timeline above shows. Israel's answer, consistently, has been that the territory is not occupied in law and that the settlements are lawful, and it has said so to each body that ruled otherwise.
