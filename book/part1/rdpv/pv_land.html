A Country With People in It

Every stage of this book has followed the road that led to the state: the congresses, the funds, the declarations, the votes. That road ran through a country that already had people in it, and they watched it being built across their fields. This part tells the same years from where they stood. It uses their own words where the record keeps them: letters, newspapers, petitions, the speeches of their deputies and the demands of their congresses. It also uses the reports of the British officials who governed them. It draws on facts already told in the stages, gathered here into one account, and it holds to the rule that governs the rest of the book: what is documented is stated, what is disputed is shown from both sides, and what one man did is not charged to a people.

The people of the land were Arabs, overwhelmingly Muslim, with a large Christian minority and a small, old Jewish community among them. Their language, Arabic, belongs to the same Semitic family as Hebrew and Aramaic. [cite:sm_wp_semitic] The Ottoman records of 1878 counted 462,465 residents of the districts that later made up Palestine, of whom 15,011, about 3 percent, were Jews. [cite:m_wp_oldyishuv] Most were villagers who farmed land they had worked for generations, much of it registered to landlords who lived in the towns or in other provinces altogether. [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine]

They were not living under foreign occupation when the first Zionist colonies were planted in 1882. They were subjects of the Ottoman Empire, as Palestine's Jews were, and after the revolution of 1908 they elected deputies to its parliament. Ruhi al-Khalidi was one of three deputies elected for Jerusalem that year. [cite:rs_rd1_ruhi_wiki] Occupation came later and from elsewhere. British forces took Jerusalem in December 1917, and a British military administration governed the country from October 1918 until July 1920, when it gave way to a civil administration under a High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel. [cite:pv_wp_oeta] From then until 1948 Palestine's Arabs lived under a foreign power that had promised their country to someone else and refused them an elected government of their own.

The question this part asks is the one their own record poses. They saw what was happening early, said so in writing, petitioned, boycotted, went to court, struck and finally rose in arms. What did they see, what did they ask for, and what were they told?
