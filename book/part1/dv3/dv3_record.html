The Record, Pope by Pope: 1904 to 2026

The Holy See's dealings with the Zionist movement and the State of Israel run through eleven pontificates, from Pius X to Leo XIV. Read in order, they fall into three periods: open refusal, from Herzl's audience in 1904 to the founding of the state; non-recognition with growing practical contact, from 1948 to 1993; and full relations, held alongside a parallel relationship with the Palestinians, from 1993 to the present. This section sets out what each pope and his Secretariat of State did, in their own words where the record preserves them.

Refusal, 1904–1948. On 25 January 1904 Pius X received Theodor Herzl and told him: "We cannot prevent the Jews from going to Jerusalem but we could never sanction it." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] Thirteen years later, on 4 May 1917, six months before the Balfour Declaration, Benedict XV received the Zionist leader Nahum Sokolow and told him that the problem of the holy places "is of extraordinary importance for us." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] That sentence marks the Holy See's consistent priority for the next seventy years: not the political future of Palestine as such, but who would control the sites of Christ's life and what would happen to the Catholic institutions and communities around them.

When the League of Nations was drafting the Mandate, the Holy See said so directly. On 15 May 1922 Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, Secretary of State, sent the League a note stating that the Holy See could not agree to "the Jews being given a privileged and preponderant position in Palestine vis-à-vis the Catholics." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The League approved the Mandate on 22 July 1922 regardless, and the Vatican, by the account in the same source, came to accept it as the lesser evil. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The position held through the next war. In May 1943 Cardinal Maglione of the Secretariat of State wrote to the apostolic delegate Cicognani that a Palestine "under Jewish predominance, would bring new and grave international problems." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] That letter was not a public statement made for effect. It was internal correspondence between two of the Holy See's own diplomats, which is why it matters for the question taken up in the next section.

The founding years, 1947–1949. The Holy See's diplomacy in the decisive years concentrated on Jerusalem. It had argued for an international status for Jerusalem and Bethlehem since 1937, and the partition plan adopted by the UN General Assembly on 29 November 1947 included a version of that idea, a corpus separatum under international administration. [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] The Holy See, which was not a UN member, avoided interfering in the partition vote itself, probably to protect that outcome. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] After the war began, Pius XII used encyclicals to press the point. In In multiplicibus curis, on 24 October 1948, he called for Jerusalem to have "an international character"; in Redemptoris nostri cruciatus, on 15 April 1949, he called for "a juridical statute internationally guaranteed" for Jerusalem and the holy places elsewhere, asked for help for the refugees "living in exile and even in concentration camps," and did not mention Israel at all. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] [cite:dv3_wiki_redemptoris]

The Holy See also worked through Catholic governments, and Irish government files released in 2004 show how cautiously. In December 1948 Franco's Spain proposed that the Spanish, Portuguese and Irish envoys to the Holy See press for Catholic countries to govern Jerusalem; Dublin judged the idea "ill-conceived and unrealistic." In April 1949 Italy, Spain, Portugal and Ireland held talks at the Vatican, but the initiative stalled, because the Vatican would not reveal its position and the four would not act without it. The Irish file records the reason: the Holy See was reluctant to make demands "which might lead to the Vatican itself being saddled with responsibility for promoting further disturbance in Palestine." [cite:dv3_dawn_irish_files] On 9 December 1949 the General Assembly voted 38 to 14, with 7 abstentions, to restate a permanent international regime for Jerusalem; Israel, the United States and Britain voted against, and the resolution was never carried out. [cite:dv3_wiki_res303] David Ben-Gurion is recorded as saying that year that a major religion "has to settle with us a historical reckoning." [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The Holy See did not recognize the new state.

Non-recognition with contact, 1949–1993. The next four decades combined formal refusal with growing practical dealings. Israel spent its first decade trying to win even a modest level of recognition; by the account of a later Israeli ambassador to the Holy See, "Nobody in Jerusalem dared to dream about full-fledged relations." [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] On 5 January 1964 Paul VI became the first pope to visit the Holy Land, describing himself and his party as pilgrims come "to venerate the Holy Places"; the visit still reflected non-recognition, and Israel's president was not addressed by his title. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] The theological change came the next year, with Nostra Aetate on 28 October 1965. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] After 1967, when Israel took control of all of Jerusalem, the Holy See developed what the same ambassador calls a pragmatic policy alongside its declared non-recognition. [cite:dv3_lewy_2009] On 15 January 1973 Paul VI received Prime Minister Golda Meir. By September 1987, when John Paul II received Jewish leaders at Castel Gandolfo, sources close to the pope said there was "no longer any theological obstacle to full relations with Israel." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The remaining obstacles were political: borders, refugees, Jerusalem and the legal position of the Church in the Holy Land.

Recognition, 1993. The Fundamental Agreement between the Holy See and the State of Israel was signed on 30 December 1993 by Monsignor Claudio Maria Celli for the Holy See and Deputy Foreign Minister Yossi Beilin for Israel. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] Diplomatic relations were established on 19 January 1994; the agreement entered into force on 10 March 1994; and the Holy See agreed to exchange ambassadors only after the Cairo Agreement between Israel and the PLO on 4 May 1994. [cite:dv3_wiki_fund_agreement] [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] The order is telling. Recognition followed the start of the Israeli-Palestinian peace process, and the exchange of ambassadors waited for its next step. A Legal Personality Agreement followed on 10 November 1997, giving Catholic bodies in Israel legal standing under Israeli law. The economic and fiscal agreement promised in 1993, on Church property and taxes, had still not been concluded in 2019, and the Knesset has never written the Fundamental Agreement into Israeli domestic law. [cite:dv3_wiki_fund_agreement] John Paul II visited in March 2000; at Yad Vashem on 23 March he said: "Only a godless ideology could plan and carry out the extermination of a whole people." [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican]

Two relationships, 2000–2026. The Holy See built its relationship with the Palestinians in parallel. After the General Assembly's vote of November 2012 it began to treat Palestine as a state, and on 26 June 2015 it signed a Comprehensive Agreement with the State of Palestine, in force from 2 January 2016, covering the life and activity of the Church in Palestinian territory and reaffirming support for a negotiated solution. [cite:dv3_vr_palestine_2016] [cite:gulfnews_vatican_palestine_accord] Israel protested at the time. [cite:gulfnews_vatican_palestine_accord] During the Gaza war Pope Francis telephoned the Holy Family parish in Gaza almost every night until two days before his death in April 2025, and on 17 July 2025 an Israeli strike on that church compound killed three people. [cite:cbc_pope_nightly_calls] [cite:washingtonpost_holy_family_strike] Leo XIV received President Isaac Herzog on 4 September 2025, and the Holy See's statement called for the release of the Israeli hostages, a permanent ceasefire and humanitarian access, and described two states as "the only way out of the ongoing war." [cite:dv3_ewtn_herzog_2025] When Israel publicly rebuked the Secretary of State's remarks on Gaza in October 2025, the pope backed his Secretary of State. [cite:dv3_catholicherald_parolin] Flying from Turkey to Lebanon at the start of December 2025 he summed up the position: "We are also friends with Israel and we are seeking to be a mediating voice between the two parties," while acknowledging that Israel "does not yet accept" the two-state proposal. [cite:dv3_allisrael_leo_2025]

What the sequence shows. Across eleven pontificates the Holy See's stated priorities barely moved: the holy places, the Catholic communities of the Holy Land, Jerusalem's status and, after 1948, the refugees. What moved was the theology about Jews, in 1965, and the political setting, in 1993. Recognition came forty-five years after the state's founding, and it came only when the Palestinian question had entered a negotiation the Holy See could support. Since then the Holy See has kept both relationships, and in the Gaza war it used words, pastoral presence and diplomacy, not the withdrawal of recognition, a tool it had used for nearly half a century.
