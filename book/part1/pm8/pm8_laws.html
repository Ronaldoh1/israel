Thirty-Eight States: The Laws Against Boycotting Israel

The most durable result of pro-Israel advocacy below the federal level is not a sum of money but a body of law. Between 2015 and 2024, thirty-eight American states passed laws or issued executive orders discouraging boycotts of Israel. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] The laws answer the boycott, divestment and sanctions movement, known as BDS, which campaigns for economic pressure on Israel over its treatment of Palestinians. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] This section sets out where the laws came from, what they require, how the courts have treated them, and why the federal versions failed.

Origin. Illinois became the first state to explicitly penalize boycotts of Israel when its governor signed SB 1761 on 23 July 2015; South Carolina had a law of its own the same year. Later in 2015 the Israel Allies Foundation drafted a model act combining the two, and the laws spread quickly. AIPAC, the Israel Action Network and local Jewish federations lobbied for them, and a lobbyist for the Israeli-American Council drafted or pushed bills in several states. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] By 2024 the count stood at thirty-eight. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds]

What the laws require. The laws take two forms. Contract laws require businesses and individuals who contract with the state to certify that they are not boycotting Israel; some exempt small contractors or contracts below a dollar threshold. Investment laws require state pension and investment funds to avoid or sell holdings in companies that boycott Israel, and several states, Arizona, Florida, Illinois, Indiana and Colorado among them, keep published lists of companies they have found to be boycotting. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] Arkansas's law, for example, applies not only to Israel but to "Israeli-controlled territories," which brings boycotts limited to the occupied territories within its reach. [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip]

The courts. The laws raised a First Amendment question, whether a consumer boycott is protected political expression, and the courts have not answered it the same way. In Kansas a federal court enjoined the state's law as likely unconstitutional, and the legislature narrowed it in 2018. In Arizona a court enjoined the law in 2018, and it too was amended, ending the case. In Texas a federal court ruled in April 2019 that the state's law was an unconstitutional restriction on speech based on viewpoint. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] The case that went furthest came from Arkansas. In 2018 the Arkansas Times, a weekly newspaper that said it was not boycotting Israel, refused on principle to sign the certification required to renew an advertising contract with a state college. A three-judge panel of the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals held the law unconstitutional, but the full court, rehearing the case, upheld it in June 2022, reasoning that purchasing decisions in a boycott are not expressive enough to be protected, even though speech supporting a boycott is. On 21 February 2023 the Supreme Court declined to hear the newspaper's appeal. [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] The law stands, and so do most of the others.

The federal attempts. Congress tried and failed to pass a federal version. The Israel Anti-Boycott Act of 2017 died in Congress. The Combating BDS Act passed the Senate in January 2019 but was never taken up by the House. The House did pass, in July 2019, a non-binding resolution condemning the boycott movement, by 398 votes to 17. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] The anti-boycott law is therefore almost entirely a state-level achievement, which is why the state legislators who write and vote for such laws are of particular interest to the sponsors of the trips described in Part Seven. One of them, an Idaho legislator, said he believed he was invited to Israel at the Israeli government's expense in 2025 because he had co-sponsored his state's law. [cite:pm_idcap_50states]

The laws' supporters argue that states, like any purchaser, may decide whom they do business with, and the Eighth Circuit accepted that the Arkansas law served commercial purposes. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] Their opponents, including the American Civil Liberties Union, argue that the laws single out one side of a political debate for penalty and that the right to boycott, recognized for civil-rights boycotts, should not depend on its target. [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] For this volume the relevant fact is the scale of the achievement: in less than a decade, a policy goal pursued by pro-Israel organizations became the law of three-quarters of the states, without a federal statute.
