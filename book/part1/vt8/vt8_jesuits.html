The Society of Jesus: an Order Under the Pope

The Society of Jesus is the body most often cast as the hidden hand behind the papacy. Part Six tested the "Black Pope" nickname and the office of the Jesuit Superior General against the record. [scene:vt6_black_pope|Part Six: the "Black Pope"] This section sets out the order itself: how it was founded, what its members promise and to whom, how a pope abolished it and another restored it, how large it is now, what it runs and what it does not, and what changed and did not change when one of its members became pope in 2013.

Founding: from a vow in Paris to a papal bull

The order began as a group of students. On 15 August 1534 Ignatius of Loyola, a Basque former soldier, and six companions, among them Francis Xavier, Diego Laínez and Peter Faber, took vows of poverty and chastity in a chapel at Montmartre, outside Paris. They called themselves the Compañía de Jesús. The date often given as the order's foundation is therefore the date of a private vow; the order existed in Church law only from 27 September 1540, when Pope Paul III approved it, and Ignatius was chosen as its first Superior General. [cite:vt8_wiki_sj]

The approving bull, Regimini militantis Ecclesiae, carried the group's own charter, the Formula of the Institute. It limited the Society to sixty members and bound Jesuits to accept any mission the pope gave them through their superior, rather than arranging missions with the pope directly. The limit on numbers was lifted by Julius III's bull Exposcit debitum of 21 July 1550, which gave the order its final approval. [cite:vt8_wiki_regimini] The military language of the charter, which speaks of serving as "a soldier of God beneath the banner of the Cross", has often been read as describing an army. It describes a religious order whose members take vows and are sent where their superiors decide. [cite:vt8_wiki_sj]

The fourth vow, and the vows against ambition

Members of most Catholic religious orders take three vows: poverty, chastity and obedience. Jesuits who reach full incorporation, the "professed", take a fourth, of special obedience to the pope concerning missions. James Martin, a Jesuit writer, explains it as a commitment to go wherever the pope sends them, because Ignatius believed the pope had the widest view of where the Church needed workers; the historian John O'Malley describes it as a vow about worldwide mobility. [cite:vt8_am_vows] The Society's 35th General Congregation restated in 2008 that the vow's whole purpose "was and is with regard to missions". [cite:vt6_gc35_d4]

The professed then take five further private vows. Three of them concern ambition: not to seek Church dignities such as a bishopric, not to seek high office in the Society, and to report any Jesuit who does. [cite:vt8_am_vows] These are the founding documents that conspiracy writing about the order most often leaves out. Read as written, they bind Jesuits to the pope's direction and bar them from campaigning for the offices through which the Church is governed.

Expelled by kings, abolished by a pope, restored by a pope

The order's greatest crisis came from Catholic monarchies, not Protestant ones. Portugal expelled the Jesuits in 1759 and deported about a thousand to the Papal States; France dissolved the Society in 1764; Spain and Naples expelled it in 1767, and Parma in 1768. Under pressure from those courts, Pope Clement XIV issued the brief Dominus ac Redemptor on 21 July 1773, which declared: "we abolish and suppress the oft-mentioned Society". [cite:vt8_wiki_suppr] The Superior General of the day, Lorenzo Ricci, died a prisoner in the papal fortress of Castel Sant'Angelo in 1775. [cite:vt6_wiki_ricci]

The order survived because two non-Catholic rulers refused to publish the papal brief. Catherine II of Russia kept the Jesuits in her newly annexed Belarusian lands and allowed them a novitiate, and Frederick II of Prussia barred the brief's distribution for some years. [cite:vt8_wiki_suppr] Pope Pius VII restored the Society throughout the Church by the bull Sollicitudo omnium ecclesiarum of 7 August 1814. [cite:vt8_wiki_sollicitudo] The order's legal existence was ended by one papal act and restored by another, forty-one years apart.

How large it is now

The Society is the largest male religious order in the Catholic Church, but it is about a third of the size it was sixty years ago. Figures drawn from the Holy See's yearbook, the Annuario Pontificio, give a peak of 36,038 members in 1965 and 1966, then a steady fall: 14,839 in 2021, 14,195 in 2023 and 13,768 in 2025, of whom 9,987 were priests. [cite:vt8_ch_sj] The Society's own count for 1 January 2022 was 14,439 Jesuits: 10,432 priests, 2,587 scholastics in training, 837 brothers and 583 novices. [cite:vt8_sj_numbers2022] Earlier material on this platform gave 17,000 members; that figure is out of date by more than a decade.

The order is governed from its General Curia in Rome by a Superior General elected for life by a General Congregation of delegates, through provincial superiors he appoints. [cite:vt8_wiki_sj] [cite:vt6_wiki_supgen] As of October 2026 the Superior General was Arturo Sosa Abascal, a Venezuelan elected on 14 October 2016; on 16 October 2026 the Ghana News Agency reported his visit to Accra, and no convocation of a 37th General Congregation, which would be needed to elect a successor, was found. [cite:vt8_gna_sosa2026] [cite:vt6_wiki_gc]

What it runs, and what it does not

The Society's weight lies in education. The Jesuit education networks' own 2024 count lists 177 universities in the International Association of Jesuit Universities, 937 schools in the Jesuit Global Network of Schools, 388 schools of the Jesuit Refugee Service and 2,127 schools of Fe y Alegría, a popular-education movement founded by a Jesuit: 3,629 institutions in all. [cite:vt8_em_map2024] Other figures in circulation (190, "200+", 228, "250+" universities) count by different definitions or have no source; Part Thirteen examines the universities in detail. [scene:vt13_jesuit_universities|Part Thirteen: the Jesuit universities]

A Jesuit school is not necessarily a school the order owns or controls. In the United States, control of several leading Catholic universities passed in the late 1960s from the religious orders that founded them to independent boards of trustees made up mostly of lay men and women, and that became the norm across Catholic higher education in the 1970s. Joseph O'Hare, a Jesuit and former president of Fordham, described the change as a response to institutional complexity and to fewer, older religious. [cite:vt8_am_ohare2007] The order's influence in such institutions runs through sponsorship, mission and the Jesuits who work there, not through ownership.

The Society holds no office in the government of the Holy See. Individual Jesuits have served in the Curia, as cardinals and as editors of papal publications, but they do so as appointees of the pope, removable by him, and the order as such has no seat in the Secretariat of State or any dicastery. [cite:vt6_gc_curia] When the pope and the order have disagreed, the record shows the pope prevailing: after Superior General Pedro Arrupe suffered a stroke on 7 August 1981, John Paul II set aside the vicar general Arrupe had chosen, Vincent O'Keefe, and installed two Jesuits of his own choosing, Paolo Dezza and Giuseppe Pittau; the order was allowed to elect a successor only two years later. [cite:vt8_am_kolvenbach]

The first Jesuit pope

Jorge Mario Bergoglio entered the Jesuit novitiate in Buenos Aires on 11 March 1958, was ordained on 13 December 1969 and led the Argentine province from 1973. He became a bishop in 1992 and a cardinal on 21 February 2001, and was elected pope on 13 March 2013, taking the name Francis. [cite:vt8_va_francis_bio] He was the first Jesuit to become pope, 473 years after the order's approval. [cite:vt8_usccb_francis] His election shows a Jesuit reaching the papacy once; it does not show the order steering the conclave, and a Jesuit pope had taken the vow not to seek such office. Francis died on 21 April 2025, and the conclave of May 2025 elected Robert Prevost, an Augustinian, as Leo XIV. [cite:vt6_wiki_conclave2025] [cite:vt6_vn_leobio]

The myths, graded

The idea of a secret Jesuit plan is older than most of the order's schools. Its first famous text, the Monita Secreta ("secret instructions"), appeared in Kraków in 1614 or 1615, presented as the order's hidden rules for winning wealth and influence. Its likely author was Jerome Zahorowski, a Polish Jesuit expelled from the Society in 1613, and many scholars, as well as the British Museum's cataloguers, have treated it as a forgery. [cite:vt8_wiki_monita] Modern versions add that the order rules the world "through their Jewish and Masonic proxies", which joins an anti-Catholic theory to an antisemitic one. [cite:vt6_splc_phelps] The table grades the main claims against the documents.

The Society's own position, stated by its General Congregation, is that its distinctive bond is availability to the pope for mission. [cite:vt6_gc35_d4] The documented record is consistent with that statement: an order large in schools and universities, shrinking in members, and subject to papal decisions it did not choose.
