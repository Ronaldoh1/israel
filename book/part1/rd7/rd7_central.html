Following the Money to the Central Banks

A common claim about the founding is that central banks stood behind it: that the institutions which issue a nation's money also financed or steered the movement that built a state. The claim can be tested the way every other claim in this appendix is tested. The search started from each central bank that could plausibly have been involved and followed only documented ties outward, one step at a time: a person who sat on its board, a payment it made, an order it gave. It looked for a route from the bank to the Zionist Organization, the Jewish Agency or the decisions of 1947 and 1948. Where no route was found, that is recorded as a finding, not as a gap to be filled.

What the search found

The Reichsbank, Germany's central bank, is the one documented case of central-bank money reaching the movement. Under the Transfer Agreement of 1933, told in [scene:hv_s01|the Transfer Agreement, 1933–1939], German Jews who emigrated to Palestine paid their capital into a trust account in Germany; the money bought German goods, which were sold in Palestine, and the emigrants were repaid there. By the Encyclopaedia Judaica's account the transfer came to £P8.1 million in all, of which the Reichsbank provided about £P2.6 million, roughly a third, and some 60,000 German Jews reached Palestine with its help. [cite:cb_jvl_haavara] The bank's interest was its own regime's: the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported in 1934 that the Reichsbank kept control of the capital even in Palestine and that the scheme paid German export bills, earning Germany scarce foreign currency. [cite:cb_jta_1934] In April 1936 a German government order took the Reichsbank out of the transactions and handed the German end to two private banks, one of them the Warburg bank of Hamburg. [cite:cb_jta_1936] Its head, Max Warburg, had been appointed to the Reichsbank's General Council in 1924 and helped emigrants move their money through the Palestine trust company; he left for New York in 1938, after the regime closed his bank. [cite:cb_jvl_mwarburg]

The Bank of England left no documented tie to the movement's funds or decisions, and Britain's monetary authorities acted against the new state at its birth. From 1926 the Palestine pound was issued at par with sterling by a Palestine Currency Board in London, appointed by the Colonial Secretary, and the board was dissolved in May 1948. [cite:cb_wiki_palpound] On 22 February 1948 the British government blocked Palestine's sterling balances, about £100 million, leaving £7 million for current needs, and took Palestine out of the sterling area. [cite:cb_frus48] Israel's first banknotes, in August 1948, were issued not by a central bank but by the Anglo-Palestine Bank, the Zionist Organization's own bank; the Bank of Israel was founded only in 1954. [cite:cb_jvl_boi]

The Federal Reserve has no documented tie to the movement. The often-repeated claim that the Fed's founding in 1913 and Zionist finance were linked is examined in [scene:s04ga|the Federal Reserve, 1913], which finds a coincidence of dates and no institutional or financial link. Paul Warburg, who was present at Jekyll Island in 1910 and sat on the Fed's first board, has no documented role in the movement, and his brother Felix and his father-in-law Jacob Schiff were prominent non-Zionists in the same years. [cite:wiki_paul_warburg] [cite:schiff_zionism_wiki] Eugene Meyer, the Fed's chairman from 1930 to 1933 and in 1946 the first president of the World Bank, left no recorded position on Zionism or Palestine. [cite:cb_wiki_meyer]

The Banque de France connects only through a family. Alphonse de Rothschild was one of the bank's regents from 1855 until his death in 1905. [cite:cb_wiki_alphonse] His youngest brother, Edmond, was the largest single funder of the early colonies. But no document ties the bank to Edmond's colonies, and a family relationship is not a tie the map counts, because it would turn kinship into evidence.

What the map shows

On the map, removing every central bank with a documented tie (the Reichsbank, the Banque de France, the Palestine Currency Board, the Federal Reserve and the Bank of England) leaves the chance of a founding in 1948 at about 93 percent, essentially unchanged. Removing the whole Haavara transfer, of which the Reichsbank's share was about a third, lowers it to about 91 percent. The money behind the founding came from private donors, Jewish appeals, the movement's own banks and funds, and, in its last years, governments acting through diplomacy and arms rather than through their central banks.

The search does not settle every question that can be asked about private bankers, and it was not meant to. Bankers appear throughout this book, from Edmond de Rothschild's colonies to the Kuhn, Loeb partners who opposed the movement, and each is mapped by what the documents say he did. What the search does show is narrower and firmer: a central bank's money reached the movement only once on the record, and it was the central bank of the regime that was driving Germany's Jews out.
