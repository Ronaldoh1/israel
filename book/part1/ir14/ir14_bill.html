The American Bill, 1979–2026

Two earlier accounts in this library already count much of what the United States has paid in the Middle East since 1983. The Hezbollah volume sets out the Beirut embassy and Marine barracks bombings of 1983, the kidnappings of the 1980s, the militia attacks of 2023–24 and the money spent from October 2023; The One-Way Mirror sets out the American interceptors fired in Israel's defence in 2024 and 2025. [scene:hz12_costs|The American bill, 1983–2026] [scene:ow7_iran|Defending Israel, fighting Iran] This section does not repeat them. It adds the direct record between the United States and Iran, keeping apart three things that are easily blurred: Americans killed by Iran's forces or by forces the United States says Iran armed; people killed by American forces in the same conflict; and costs the United States took on by its own decisions.

The hostages and Desert One, 1979–1981

The conflict began with Americans held captive, not killed. Fifty-two staff of the American embassy in Tehran, seized on 4 November 1979, were held for 444 days. President Carter called the hostage-taking "blackmail". On 24–25 April 1980 he sent a rescue force, Operation Eagle Claw; it was aborted at a desert staging site, and during the withdrawal a helicopter struck a transport aircraft, killing eight American servicemen, five airmen and three Marines. No Iranian fire was involved. The hostages were released on 20 January 1981, minutes after Carter left office. [cite:ir14_wiki_eagleclaw] Iran's government's handling of the crisis is told in Part Nine. [scene:ir9_record|Iran's own record]

The tanker war, 1987–1988

The first American combat with Iran came in the last years of the Iran–Iraq war, and it began with a request from Kuwait. In December 1986 Kuwait asked the Reagan administration to protect its oil tankers from Iranian attack; on 7 March 1987 the administration agreed, and Kuwaiti tankers were re-registered under the American flag. The Navy's escort operation, Earnest Will, ran from July 1987 to 26 September 1988. On its first convoy, on 24 July 1987, the reflagged tanker Bridgeton struck an Iranian mine; no one was hurt, and Iran's prime minister, Mir Hossein Mousavi, called it an "irreparable blow to America's political and military prestige." [cite:ir14_wiki_earnestwill]

The deadliest attack on an American ship in the Gulf in those years was not Iranian. On 17 May 1987 an Iraqi jet fired two Exocet missiles into the frigate USS Stark, killing 37 sailors and injuring 21; Iraqi officials said the targeting was accidental. [cite:ir14_wiki_earnestwill] It belongs in the ledger because it shows what the deployment cost whoever fired, but it is not an Iranian death toll, and earlier material in this library that counts it as one is wrong.

On 18 October 1987 American destroyers shelled two Revolutionary Guard positions at the Rostam oil field (Operation Nimble Archer). [cite:ir14_wiki_earnestwill] On 14 April 1988 the frigate USS Samuel B. Roberts struck a mine, and ten sailors were severely injured. Four days later the United States answered with Operation Praying Mantis: it destroyed the Sassan and Sirri platforms, which the Revolutionary Guard used for surveillance, sank the fast-attack craft Joshan and the frigate Sahand, and disabled the frigate Sabalan. [cite:ir14_nhhc_mantis] [cite:ir14_usni_mantis] Two Marine aviators, Captains Stephen C. Leslie and Kenneth W. Hill, died when their AH-1T Sea Cobra crashed at night south-west of Abu Musa; Navy officials reported no evidence of hostile fire, and the cause has never been settled. [cite:ir14_pas_mantiskia]

Iran Air 655, 3 July 1988

The largest single loss of life in the whole American–Iranian conflict before 2026 was caused by the United States. On 3 July 1988 the cruiser USS Vincennes fired two missiles at Iran Air Flight 655, an Airbus flying from Bandar Abbas to Dubai, and killed all 290 people on board: 274 passengers and 16 crew, among them 254 Iranians and 65 children. The United States said the crew had mistaken the airliner for an attacking Iranian F-14; Iran's government said the shooting was deliberate and unlawful, and at the least negligent. President Reagan expressed regret, but the United States never formally apologised or admitted wrongdoing, and the ship's captain later received the Legion of Merit, in a citation that did not mention the downing. [cite:ir14_wiki_ia655]

Iran took the United States to the International Court of Justice in 1989. In 1996 the United States agreed to pay $131.8 million to end that case and other claims, of which $61.8 million went to the families of the 248 Iranians killed. It paid ex gratia, that is, without admitting liability. [cite:ir14_wiki_ia655]

Iraq, 2003–2011: a claim, not a count

The largest figure in the ledger rests on an American assessment that has not been published. In April 2019 a Pentagon spokesman, Commander Sean Robertson, said the Defense Department "assessed that at least 603 U.S. personnel deaths in Iraq were the result of Iran-backed militants", up from an earlier official estimate of about 500; the State Department put this at 17 per cent of all American deaths in Iraq from 2003 to 2011. The deaths were attributed to explosively formed penetrators (EFPs), armour-piercing roadside bombs the United States says Iran made and supplied, and to other bombs, rockets, mortars and small arms. [cite:ir14_mt_603] The Pentagon did not give Military Times the report itself, so the figure could not be checked; under President Bush, officials had shown images of Iranian-made EFPs but had not proved that Iran supplied them to the militias, and the reports of the 2019 figure carried no Iranian response. [cite:ir14_mt_603] [cite:ir14_tp_603] The figure is the United States government's own claim and is given as such; the militias themselves are described in the Hezbollah volume. [scene:hz10_iraq|The Iraqi militias]

Soleimani and Ain al-Asad, 2019–2020

The second direct clash began in Iraq. After a rocket attack killed an American contractor in late December 2019, the United States blamed Kata'ib Hezbollah and struck it, killing 25 of its fighters; militia supporters then besieged the American embassy in Baghdad. On 3 January 2020 an American drone killed Qassem Soleimani, commander of the Revolutionary Guard's Quds Force, at Baghdad airport; the Pentagon called it "decisive defensive action", and Khamenei promised "harsh retaliation". [cite:hz10_fox_pentagon2020] [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] On 8 January Iran fired 15 ballistic missiles at two bases in Iraq, ten of them at Ain al-Asad. It had warned first: Iraq's prime minister said he received an "official verbal message" that the response "had begun or would begin shortly" and would be limited to places where American troops were. No one was killed. [cite:ir14_fn_tbi] [cite:ir14_na_warning] President Trump wrote "All is well!" and at first said no Americans had been harmed; by February the Pentagon reported 109 service members diagnosed with mild traumatic brain injury. [cite:ir14_fn_tbi] [cite:ir14_stripes_tbi] Part Twelve asks what this calibrated answer says about whether Iran wants war with the United States. [scene:ir12_question|Does Iran want war with America?]

From 2024: defence, two wars, and the pump

The costs of 2024–2026 are counted in full elsewhere and summarised here. Three American soldiers were killed at Tower 22 in Jordan on 28 January 2024 by a drone that Washington blamed on Kata'ib Hezbollah. [cite:hz10_abc_tower22] Iran's attacks on Israel of April 2024, which followed a suspected Israeli strike on Iran's consulate in Damascus on 1 April that killed a Revolutionary Guard general and six other officers, and of October 2024 drew American forces into Israel's air defence. Israel did not comment on the Damascus strike, and the United States told Iran it had no involvement. [cite:ir14_cbs_damascus] [cite:ow7_dod_apr2024] The June 2025 war, including Operation Midnight Hammer, cost the United States $2.04 billion to $2.25 billion by the Costs of War project's estimate, most of it in THAAD interceptors fired in Israel's defence. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] The 2026 war had cost $43.6 billion by 3 September on Central Command's estimate, and by 9 October the Pentagon listed 21 American dead and 865 wounded. [cite:ow7_ap_cost2026] [cite:ow7_inq_deaths2026] The decision, the war and Congress's votes are told in the Hezbollah volume and in Part Eleven. [scene:hz12_wars|Two wars with Iran] [scene:ir11_war|The 2026 war]

The 2026 war also reached households directly. Petrol averaged about $2.98 a gallon before 28 February; on 31 March, with the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut, AAA's national average passed $4 for the first time since 2022, and diesel had risen from about $3.76 to $5.45. [cite:ir14_star_mar31] Petrol reached its 2026 high of $4.564 on 21 May. [cite:ir14_we_may21] The government's Energy Information Administration reported in October that petrol had averaged $4.35 and diesel $6.29 in September, and forecast an average of $3.91 for petrol in 2026 against $3.10 in 2025, with Brent crude at $96 a barrel against $69, on the assumption that Gulf oil flows would stay constrained through the end of the year. [cite:ir14_eia_steo] At the forecast, the average American paid about 81 cents a gallon more for petrol in 2026 than in 2025, an increase of about a quarter.

What the ledger shows

Set side by side, the record cuts both ways for Ron's thesis. Iran's government and forces the United States says it armed did kill and injure Americans: the Pentagon's Iraq figure, if accurate, is the largest single entry, and Iran's hostility to the United States began in 1979, long before Israel and Iran became open enemies. [cite:ir14_mt_603] [cite:ir14_wiki_eagleclaw] But most American deaths came where American forces had been sent into someone else's war, to escort Kuwait's oil in 1987, to occupy Iraq in 2003, and to fight alongside Israel in 2026; the single deadliest act of the whole conflict before 2026 was an American cruiser's destruction of an Iranian airliner; and the largest sums, the interceptors, the war of 2026 and its effect on fuel prices, followed decisions taken in Washington. [cite:ir14_wiki_earnestwill] [cite:ir14_wiki_ia655] [cite:ow7_ap_cost2026] [cite:ir14_eia_steo] Who argued for those decisions, and who took them, is the subject of the next section.
