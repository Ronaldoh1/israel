Two Girls, Forty-Six Days Apart

This part deals with two missing teenagers and with what was said about them for more than forty years. It is not a part about money or treaties, and it is not here because it proves anything about the Holy See. It is here because Emanuela Orlandi was a citizen of Vatican City State and the daughter of a Vatican employee, because the Holy See became a party to the case within days, and because the theories that grew around it have been used to make claims about popes, cardinals, the Vatican bank and organised crime. The rule for the part is the one Part One set for the whole volume ([scene:vt1_how_to_read|how this volume reads the record]): what the record shows is kept apart from what has been alleged, and each allegation carries its grade. Both girls' families are still waiting for an answer. Neither girl has been found, alive or dead, as of October 2026. [cite:vt15_agi_dec2025]

Mirella Gregori, 7 May 1983

Mirella Gregori was fifteen and lived with her family in central Rome. On 7 May 1983, after school, she told her mother she was going out briefly to meet a friend named Alessandro. She was last seen near a bar below the family's apartment. She had no connection to the Vatican. [cite:vt15_cwr_senate] Her case was joined to Orlandi's only later, when callers and written communiqués in 1983 claimed to be holding both girls. [cite:vt15_cwr_senate] [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] The Gregori family's lawyer, Nicodemo Gentile, has argued that the two cases should not be merged and that Mirella's disappearance was a local matter. Investigative attention in her case has turned to the bar on Via Volturno, run by the parents of the friend who last saw her, and to memoranda that another lawyer and a criminologist sent to the parliamentary commission in December 2025 on a police sketch and on a pattern of minors who went missing in Rome in May and June 1983. [cite:vt15_agi_dec2025] None of those lines has produced a charge.

Emanuela Orlandi, 22 June 1983

Emanuela Orlandi was also fifteen. She lived with her family inside Vatican City. Her father, Ercole Orlandi, was a lay employee of the Prefecture of the Papal Household and a citizen of Vatican City State, as she was. [cite:vt15_cwr_senate] [cite:vt15_npr_reopen] On the afternoon of Wednesday 22 June 1983 she went to her flute lesson at the music school on Piazza Sant'Apollinare, in the centre of Rome. From a public telephone she called home to say that a man had offered her 375,000 lire to hand out publicity for a cosmetics firm at a fashion show. She was last seen at about 7.30 that evening at a bus stop on Corso del Rinascimento, in front of the Senate's seat at Palazzo Madama. Her father reported her missing. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] [cite:vt15_wiki_case]

The family put up about 3,000 posters across Rome on 1 July. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] Two men who called themselves "Pierluigi" and "Mario" telephoned the family in late June, describing a girl called Barbara who sold cosmetics; one of the calls was recorded. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] [cite:vt15_wiki_case]

The pope's appeal and the "Amerikano"

On Sunday 3 July 1983 John Paul II spoke about her at the Angelus. He prayed that Emanuela "possa presto tornare incolume", might soon come home unharmed, and appealed to those responsible. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] It was the first time a pope had spoken publicly of the case, and it changed its character: from then on it was treated in public as a kidnapping. [cite:vt15_wiki_case]

On 5 July a man with an English-speaking accent, whom the press called "l'Americano", telephoned the Vatican press office, claimed to be holding Emanuela, and asked for a direct line to the Vatican. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] The Vatican installed one. On 19 July 1983 it confirmed that it had set up a telephone line for contact with the presumed kidnappers. A tape on which a girl's voice could be heard was released, and a voice on it demanded the release of Mehmet Ali Ağca, the Turkish gunman serving a life sentence for shooting John Paul II in St Peter's Square on 13 May 1981. [cite:vt15_csm_1983] From August 1983 into 1984, written communiqués arrived in the names of a "Turkish Anti-Christian Liberation Front" calling itself Turkesh, of a group calling itself Phoenix, of a "new Muslim organisation" and of the Turkish nationalist Grey Wolves. They repeated the demand for Ağca's release. None of them ever supplied proof that its authors held Emanuela. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] Some of these messages claimed both girls, which is how Mirella Gregori's name entered the Orlandi file. [cite:vt15_wiki_case]

The President of the Republic, Sandro Pertini, appealed for the girls' release on 20 October 1983. On 24 December 1983 John Paul II visited the Orlandi family at home and described the disappearance as a case of international terrorism. [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] [cite:vt15_wiki_case] That phrase was the pope's reading of the demands that had been made, not a finding by any investigator. No court has found who made the calls or whether they had anything to do with the girl's disappearance.

Why the case touches the Holy See

Three facts tie the Orlandi case to the Holy See, and none of them is a theory. Emanuela was a citizen of Vatican City State, so her disappearance concerned a sovereign whose territory begins at the edge of Rome ([scene:vt4_holy_see_vs_city|Part Four on the Holy See and the City]). The calls were made to the Vatican and the Vatican answered them, including by installing a line on request. And the pope himself took up the case in public, twice in its first six months. [cite:vt15_csm_1983] [cite:vt15_ilpost_2013] Everything else that came to be said about the Vatican's part in the affair, from a mobster's tomb in a basilica to a disputed expense memo, is examined in the next section, where each claim is graded.

The case also lasted. A first Italian investigation was dismissed in 1997, a second ran from 2008 and was archived in 2015, and in 2023 three inquiries opened at once: one by the Vatican's Promoter of Justice, one by Rome's prosecutors and one by a commission of the Italian Parliament. [cite:vt15_wiki_case] [cite:vt15_fq_2015] [cite:vt15_npr_reopen] Those are covered in [scene:vt15_investigations_2023|the third section]. Ercole Orlandi died in 2004 without learning what happened to his daughter. [cite:vt15_wiki_case] Her brother Pietro has led the family's public campaign since, and the family's own statements are reported in this part as theirs.
