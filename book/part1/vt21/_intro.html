Part Twenty-One follows the eighty years in which the Christian churches of the Roman Empire passed from persecution to establishment. It begins with Diocletian's Great Persecution of 303, when the state itself burned Christian scriptures, and follows Constantine from his proclamation in 306 through the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312, comparing the two contemporary accounts of his conversion, to the Milan letter of 313 and his deathbed baptism in 337. The second section tests the popular claims that cluster around him: that he merged sun worship with Christianity, that his Sunday law of 321 created Christian Sunday worship, that Christmas and the Eucharist were taken from Sol Invictus and Mithras, and that the popes inherited his title of Pontifex Maximus. The third section sets out what imperial favour gave the bishops (immunities, courts, buildings and land), the first imperial edict against named Gnostic and other dissenting groups, and the laws of Theodosius that by 395 made Nicene Christianity the only lawful public religion, ending with a table of the documented state orders to burn religious books.
