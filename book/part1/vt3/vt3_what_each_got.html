What Each Side Got in 1929

The Lateran Pacts were three documents, not one. The Treaty settled sovereignty and closed the dispute over Rome; the Concordat set the place of the Catholic Church inside Italy; the Financial Convention paid the Holy See for what it had lost in 1870. All three were signed on 11 February 1929 by Cardinal Pietro Gasparri for Pius XI and by Benito Mussolini as head of the Italian government, and all three are published together, in Italian, on the Holy See's own website. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] How the negotiation was conducted is told in [scene:vt2_pacts_signed|Part Two]. This section reads the texts for a narrower purpose: to list what each side received, article by article, before turning to the commentary that has grown around them.

What the Holy See received

The first gain was a state. Article 3 of the Treaty recognized the Holy See's "full ownership and exclusive and absolute power and sovereign jurisdiction" over the Vatican, which became Vatican City. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] What that sovereignty means in international law is set out in [scene:dv1_sovereignty|The Divine 3+1]; here the point is that Italy granted it by treaty, after nearly six decades of dispute over Rome ([scene:vt2_roman_question|the Roman Question]).

The second gain was a ring of protected property outside the walls. Article 13 left to the Holy See the patriarchal basilicas of St John Lateran, St Mary Major and St Paul Outside the Walls, with their annexed buildings; Article 14 added the papal palace and Villa Barberini at Castel Gandolfo; Articles 15 and 16 gave the seats of named curial offices the immunities of foreign embassies and exempted listed pontifical institutions, among them the Gregorian University and the Biblical Institute, from expropriation and from taxes. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] These properties are not Vatican territory; they are Italian land with special status, a distinction that matters again in [scene:vt4_holy_see_vs_city|Part Four].

The third gain was money. Article 1 of the Financial Convention obliged Italy to pay 750,000,000 lire in cash and to hand over 5 per cent consolidated state bonds with a nominal value of one billion lire, at the exchange of ratifications. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] Those are the treaty figures. Conversions into pounds or dollars that circulate in popular accounts (30 million pounds, 92.1 million dollars, "1.75 billion lire in cash") are not in the text, and any modern equivalent has to state its date and method. How the money was invested belongs to [scene:vt9_apsa_governorate|Part Nine].

The fourth gain, and the largest in daily life, was the Concordat's place for the Church in Italian law. The Treaty's first article reaffirmed the principle of the 1848 Statute that "the Catholic, apostolic and Roman religion is the only religion of the State." [cite:vt3_lateran_text] The Concordat then gave civil effect to the sacrament of marriage as governed by canon law (Article 34); declared that Italy considered "the teaching of Christian doctrine" the "foundation and crown of public education" and extended it from elementary to secondary schools (Article 36); committed the state to keep supplementing the incomes of poorly endowed benefices, the system of clergy pay known as the congrua (Article 30); and promised that the government would prevent in Rome anything contrary to "the sacred character of the Eternal City" (Article 1). [cite:vt3_lateran_text]

What Mussolini's government received

Italy's first gain was the end of the Roman Question on its own terms. In Article 26 the Holy See recognized "the Kingdom of Italy under the dynasty of the House of Savoy with Rome as capital of the Italian State" and declared the question "definitively and irrevocably" closed. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] Since 1870 the popes had refused to accept the loss of Rome; after 1929 no pope could claim it back.

The second gain was a Holy See that undertook to stay out of politics between states. In Article 24 the Holy See declared that it "wishes to remain and will remain extraneous to the temporal competitions between other States," and Vatican City was to be treated as neutral territory. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] That clause still stands, and its bearing on the Holy See's conduct over Israel and Gaza is examined in [scene:vt18_why_no_sever|Part Eighteen].

The third gain was a measure of control over the clergy inside Italy. Under Article 19 of the Concordat the Holy See chose bishops freely but communicated each name to the government in advance, "to make sure that it has no reasons of a political character to raise" against the appointment. Under Article 20 every bishop, before taking possession of his diocese, swore before the Head of State "fidelity to the Italian State." [cite:vt3_lateran_text] Article 43 recognized the organizations of Catholic Action and, in the same article, renewed for Italian clergy and members of religious orders "the prohibition on enrolling and being active in any political party." [cite:vt3_lateran_text] The Concordat's political clause binds the clergy; it does not mention any party by name.

The fourth gain was the least tangible: approval. Two days after the signing, Pius XI told professors and students of the Catholic University of the Sacred Heart in Milan that perhaps "also a man was needed like the one Providence has caused us to meet" to reach the settlement, and that with the Concordat he believed he had "given God back to Italy and Italy to God." [cite:vt3_pius_13feb1929] The passage does not name Mussolini, but its subject is plain from the context. Reviewing David Kertzer's history of the two men, the historian Saul Friedländer described the accords as "advantageous for both sides" and wrote that Mussolini could thereafter "rely on the Holy See's support of his regime and his policies," a benefit he called priceless "in a deeply Catholic country." [cite:vt3_tnr_kertzer] That is a historian's reading of the documents, not a clause in them, and it is given here as such.

The bargain tested: 1931

Within two years the clauses on Catholic Action were tested. After a press and police campaign that the Catholic Action historical institute dates from 19 March 1931, the regime dissolved all of Catholic Action's youth circles, male and female, on 30 May 1931. [cite:vt3_isacem_1931] Pius XI answered with the encyclical Non abbiamo bisogno, dated 29 June 1931. He protested the dissolution of the youth and university associations, accused the regime of seeking to "monopolize completely the young," and called its doctrine "a true, a real pagan worship of the State," which he named "Statolatry." [cite:vt3_nab_1931] He declared the oath demanded of party members "as it stands, is unlawful" and suggested that Catholics add a reservation for "the laws of God and of the Church." [cite:vt3_nab_1931]

The encyclical also drew a line. "We have not said that We wished to condemn the party as such," Pius XI wrote; what he condemned were the doctrines and practices he judged incompatible with Catholic teaching. [cite:vt3_nab_1931] An agreement signed on 2 September 1931 ended the crisis, and Catholic Action survived in reduced form; the same institute records that harassment of individual members continued until the regime fell in 1943. [cite:vt3_isacem_1931]

The episode is the best single test of how the 1929 settlement should be described. It was not a merger of Church and regime: within two years each side was defending its share of the bargain against the other, in public, over the one thing both wanted, the education of the young. Nor was it a rupture: the pope condemned an ideology while declining to condemn the party, and the dispute ended in a new agreement rather than a denunciation of the Concordat. The documents support a narrower description, the one Friedländer gives: a bargain between two authorities that needed each other and each expected to gain. [cite:vt3_tnr_kertzer] [cite:vt3_nab_1931] The Holy See's own stated aim, in Pius XI's words of 13 February 1929, was religious rather than political: to give "God back to Italy and Italy to God." [cite:vt3_pius_13feb1929]
