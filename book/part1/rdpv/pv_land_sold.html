Sold From Under Them: The Land, 1910–1933

The land passed out of Arab hands through sales, and most of the sellers were landowners who did not live on it, many of them Arab families in the towns or in other provinces. That is the heart of the Palestinian experience of this period, and it is harder than either of the familiar stories about it. Britain did not hand the land to Zionist buyers. It was bought, lawfully and privately, from landowners, most of them absentees, by Zionist institutions that then held it permanently. [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance]

The law that made this possible was older than Zionism. Under Ottoman land law most farmland was miri, state land held on a kind of lease, treated in practice as owned by whoever held the registered rights. Families who had worked the same fields for generations, paying tithes to landlords they might never see, typically held no registered title at all. When the landlord sold, the family had no ownership claim to assert; the sale and the eviction were the same lawful transaction. [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] [cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws]

The largest seller was a family in Beirut. In 1872 the Ottoman government sold the Jezreel Valley, Marj ibn Amir, to the Sursocks, wealthy Greek Orthodox Christians. Between 1901 and 1925, mainly in the 1920s, they sold some 60,000 to 75,000 dunams to Zionist purchasers, for a fraction of the land's later value. Those sales alone account for about 22 percent of all the land Jews acquired in Palestine up to 1948. [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:sursockhouse_rothschild_purchases] On the other side of the table were men of means too: the Jewish National Fund, which held land as the inalienable property of the Jewish people; the land company whose buyer, Yehoshua Hankin, negotiated the Sursock deals; and the Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, founded on Edmond de Rothschild's money, which became the largest single Jewish landowner in the country. [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]

The people on the land were not parties to either side of the contract. In the largest single group of Sursock sales, 1,746 Arab farming families, 8,730 people, were evicted. They were owed nothing under the law; they were paid about $17 a person as a goodwill settlement. Across all the Sursock-linked purchases an estimated 20 to 25 villages were emptied. [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] Some Arabs saw selling to Jewish buyers as a betrayal, and in at least one case negotiations stalled for years because the sellers did not want to be seen as responsible for the eviction. [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases]

What the evicted could not do was go back to work on the same land. The Jewish National Fund's draft lease required its tenants to cultivate "only with Jewish labour," on penalty of a fine for each breach and repossession after three. [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal] Britain's own inquiries measured the result. The Shaw Commission found in 1930 that the protective ordinances had failed and that the evictions were creating a landless class, "a potential danger to the country." [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_e] Sir John Hope Simpson's survey that year found that in 104 villages, 6,940 of 23,573 families, 29.4 percent, held no land at all; of 688 Arab families displaced by land sales, only 379 were farming again. [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal] The Jewish Agency contested his statistics, and the two positions stand side by side. [cite:rs_rd3_cojs_ja_1931] Britain passed five laws between 1920 and 1940 to protect tenants from eviction, and they "failed to provide the protection that Palestinian Arabs demanded, often due to loopholes." [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance]

When the people of the land fought a sale, they fought it in the courts of the power that governed them. In 1929 the Jewish National Fund bought 30,800 dunams at Wadi al-Hawarith at auction from absentee owners. The Bedouin tenants refused to leave. Awni Abd al-Hadi, a leading national figure, represented them through years of litigation, and the Istiqlal Party, the Palestine Communist Party and the Congress of Youth called strikes in protest. The final eviction order was issued on 15 June 1933. [cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith] The case was lost under the Mandate's own law.

Seen from the village, the story was simple, even if the law was not. A family farmed land its grandparents had farmed. A man in Beirut or Jaffa sold it to a fund in Jerusalem backed by money from Paris, London and New York. The family was paid off, or not, and could not be hired back. The government that might have stopped it was foreign, unelected and bound by its Mandate to facilitate the other side's settlement. By 1947 land in Jewish ownership was still under a tenth of the country, about 7 percent by one account. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] For the people who had lived on that tenth, the loss was already complete.
