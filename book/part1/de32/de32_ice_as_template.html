ICE as the Template

Every design for an executive without a veto runs into the same practical question: who carries out the orders. Curtis Yarvin's answer, set out in [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight], was a board holding cryptographic keys able to "disarm all government weapons." [cite:de8_kofman_ny] Weapons, though, are carried by people, and the book's worst-case reading, the Sophia platform's hypothesis and never a finding, holds that a sovereign executive would not need to build a new force. It would repurpose the ones that exist. This part examines those forces in turn, beginning with the one that grew fastest in 2025 and 2026. Capability is not intent: what follows documents what each force is, what it did and what limited it, and keeps separate any claim about what it might be used for.

What ICE is and where it came from

Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) opened on 1 March 2003 as part of the new Department of Homeland Security, created by the Homeland Security Act that President George W. Bush signed on 25 November 2002 "in response to 9/11." It took over the enforcement work of two older agencies, the Immigration and Naturalization Service and the Customs Service, and in its own words holds "a unique combination of civil and criminal authorities." [cite:de32_ice_history] Its arrest-and-removal arm, Enforcement and Removal Operations (ERO), handles civil immigration cases; its investigative arm, Homeland Security Investigations, handles crimes. [cite:de32_ice_history] The software ICE has run on since 2014, and the data that feeds it, are traced in [scene:ow9_ice|the library's account of ICE and its contracts] and [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty]. This section concerns the people.

The money and the hiring surge

The budget law signed on 4 July 2025, known as H.R. 1, gave about $170 billion to immigration and border enforcement over four years, roughly $75 billion of it to ICE. [cite:us6_brennan_obbba] Within that sum were $45 billion for detention, a $29.9 billion lump sum for enforcement and removal including hiring, and funding for 10,000 new ICE officers over five years, with money available until 30 September 2029. [cite:de32_aic_hr1] How the budget compares with earlier years is set out in [scene:us6_asylum|the library's chapter on asylum].

The hiring came faster than the law required. On 5 January 2026 the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) said ICE had hired "more than 12,000 officers and agents," taking its workforce from about 10,000 to about 22,000 out of more than 220,000 applicants; its spokeswoman called it "a 120% increase." These are the agency's own figures and have not been independently audited. [cite:de32_kwtx_hiring] Training was shortened to match. ICE's own recruitment page had described almost five months of training for new officers: five weeks of language training and sixteen weeks of law enforcement. In January 2026 a senior DHS official said ERO officer training "is 8 weeks long." The Atlantic, citing unnamed officials, had reported 47 days; DHS called that "false," and a fact-check rated the 47-day claim mostly true because the confirmed schedule, six days a week for eight weeks, comes to about the same. [cite:de32_aj_training]

Agents borrowed from other agencies

ICE did not rely on its own hires alone. An ICE disclosure obtained by the Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank, counted 42,153 people diverted to help ERO between 5 and 28 August 2025, 28,390 of them from federal law-enforcement agencies. Cato's analyst David Bier estimated the shares by agency: about 2,840 from the FBI, 2,181 from the Drug Enforcement Administration (close to half its agents), 1,778 from the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (more than two-thirds), 650 from the US Marshals and 6,198 from Homeland Security Investigations; a DHS memorandum had also deputised up to 600 State Department security agents. [cite:de32_cato_diverted] The chief of the IRS's criminal investigators said in December 2025 that 250 of his agents had been working on immigration since May. [cite:de32_ap_irsci] Moving agents between priorities is lawful. What the figures document is that a single executive priority could redirect a large share of federal law enforcement within months, without new legislation.

In the streets

The new force was used in surges into cities, often led by Border Patrol units under Commander Gregory Bovino. In Chicago, after the autumn 2025 operation called Midway Blitz, Judge Sara Ellis issued a 233-page opinion finding that "video disproves Defendants' contentions" about protesters, that Bovino admitted lying about a rock striking him before he used tear gas, and that one agent had used ChatGPT to write a use-of-force report. [cite:de32_cbs_ellis] The Seventh Circuit called her injunction "overbroad" and later vacated it after the plaintiffs dropped the suit. [cite:de32_yahoo_7th] In Los Angeles, a lower court had barred stops based on apparent race or ethnicity, accent, location or type of work; on 8 September 2025 the Supreme Court stayed that order 6–3, Justice Kavanaugh writing that such factors can "constitute at least reasonable suspicion" and Justice Sotomayor, dissenting, that people who "look a certain way" could now be stopped. [cite:de32_scotusblog_perdomo]

In Minneapolis, during the operation DHS called its largest, an ICE officer shot and killed Renee Good on 7 January 2026, and federal agents shot and killed Alex Pretti on 24 January; both were US citizens. Federal officials said there would be no federal investigation of Good's death, and evidence in both cases reached the county prosecutor only in July 2026, after a state lawsuit; as of that month no charging decision had been made. A separate ICE agent was charged by the state with assault and filing a false report over a January shooting that wounded a Venezuelan man; he has not been tried and is presumed innocent. [cite:de32_fox9_mn] ProPublica counted 506 US citizens held by immigration agents by October 2026, including at least 70 children; 36 were held for a day or more without contact with a lawyer or family, and about a dozen, mostly children, were deported. The government does not publish a count of its own. [cite:de32_dn_propublica]

Agents often worked masked. California banned face coverings for officers; on 10 February 2026 Judge Christina Snyder blocked the ban as applied to federal agents because it exempted state police, while upholding California's rule that agents display their agency and badge number, and noting that "federal officers can perform their federal functions without wearing masks." [cite:de32_ktvu_masks]

Deaths in custody

Twenty-nine people died in ICE custody in the fiscal year that began in October 2025 by mid-April 2026, passing the previous record of 28 in fiscal 2004. Senators Jon Ossoff and Raphael Warnock counted 49 deaths since January 2025. At Camp East Montana, the tent camp at Fort Bliss described in [scene:de32_the_military_at_home|the military section], the county medical examiner ruled the death of Geraldo Lunas Campos a homicide, by "asphyxia due to neck and torso compression," and the FBI opened an investigation. DHS said the rise reflected the number held and put the death rate at 0.009 percent of the detained population. [cite:de32_npr_deaths]

If it were connected

Each join is graded documented, probable, possible or not supported. ICE's arrest force joined to its data platforms: documented, through the case-management and targeting systems built by Palantir and the broker contracts mapped in [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty's ecosystem map]. Other federal law-enforcement agencies joined to ICE's priorities: documented, by ICE's own disclosure of 28,390 diverted personnel. [cite:de32_cato_diverted] The same enlarged force joined to the domestic-terrorism framework of NSPM-7, which names "opposition to law and immigration enforcement" among its indicators: probable, because the memorandum and the Attorney General's implementing order already point federal task forces at that category, though those task forces are the FBI's and no document shows ERO officers assigned to political cases. [cite:de30_bondi_memo] ICE pointed at political opponents as such, beyond immigration: not supported. Its statutory mandate is immigration and customs; turning it elsewhere would need a new authority, a designation of the kind traced in [scene:de30_nspm7_and_detachment|Part Thirty-Two], or the Insurrection Act discussed later in this part. What stands in the way is set out in [scene:de32_what_stops_it|the final section].
