The Hawk Decision, 1960–1962: From Refusal to Offer

Israel could pay Morocco to open a door. It could not buy American weapons of any consequence: for most of the period covered by this chapter, Washington gave money, not arms. American aid ran in the tens of millions of dollars a year, with only small military loans, as the section on the 1961 aid law shows. In August 1962 that changed. President Kennedy decided that Israel could buy the Hawk, an American surface-to-air missile, an anti-aircraft weapon. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_hawk] [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] The decision took more than two years to reach, was argued over inside the administration at every step, and was tied, in the memorandum that carried it to the President, to an American plan to settle the Palestinian refugee question.

What kind of weapon. One account carried elsewhere in this library describes the 1962 sale as "the first US sale of offensive weaponry to the Israeli military." [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] That description is wrong on the record and should be corrected at the outset. The Hawk was an air-defence missile. Kennedy himself, in May 1961, called it "a defensive weapon." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] The State Department's circular of 7 September 1962 instructed American posts to present it as purely defensive, a response to the Soviet build-up. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] What made the Hawk a threshold was not that it was offensive. It was that it was a missile, and that it was the first major American weapons sale to Israel. Kennedy's own sentence of 1961 holds both halves: "while the Hawk is a defensive weapon it is also a missile." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] The later account's framing, in which the Hawk opens a line of offensive sales continued under Johnson, is examined in [scene:s11d|what Johnson actually did].

The refusals of 1960. Israel had been asking for some time. According to the Center for Israel Education, negotiations for the Hawk had begun in 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_hawk] In 1960 the answer was no. The Pentagon said that Hawks were unavailable, and Livingston Merchant recommended against a sale that would cost "close to $40 millions" for six batteries. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The figure set the problem. A White House staff note of 8 May 1961 put the cost higher, at "$50 million," and observed that "our largest previous sale to Israel had a ceiling of $1 million." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] Israel was asking for a purchase forty or fifty times larger than anything the United States had sold it before.

Kennedy at the Waldorf, May 1961. Ben-Gurion put the request to the new President in person, at the Waldorf-Astoria in New York on 30 May 1961, a meeting whose nuclear and refugee exchanges are told in [scene:cw4_waldorf_1961|the Waldorf meeting]. On missiles, the official memorandum records Kennedy's answer in three parts. The Hawk was "a defensive weapon" but "also a missile"; "we are reluctant to introduce missiles into the Middle East"; and "we do not want to see Israel at a disadvantage." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_memcon] The first two clauses explained the refusal. The third can be read as leaving the door open: if the balance shifted against Israel, the reluctance could yield.

Deferral, June 1962. A year later the State Department still advised waiting. A paper of 7 June 1962 recommended deferral, judging that "Israel is in little actual danger of an Arab assault now." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The judgment was a military one: on its reasoning, if Israel was not in danger, there was no reason to bring American missiles into the region.

Rusk's review, 7 August 1962. Within two months the department's position had changed. Rusk's review of 7 August 1962 recommended offering the Hawk "after consultation with the British and discussion with the UAR." His grounds were intelligence that the United Arab Republic, Egypt, was obtaining comparable missiles from the Soviet Union, and the judgment that the Hawk would "reduce considerably" Israel's vulnerability to surprise air attack. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The argument had turned from Israel's danger in general to a specific Soviet supply. On that reasoning, if Egypt was getting Soviet missiles, the United States would no longer be the first to bring missiles into the region.

Feldman's memorandum, 10 August 1962. Three days later Myer Feldman, who had sat in on the Waldorf meeting, sent Kennedy a memorandum that tied the missiles to something else. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug10] At issue was a plan for the Arab refugees, known as the Johnson plan. Feldman wrote that "The only chance the plan has for success" lay in telling Ben-Gurion that the United States would guarantee Israel's security and provide Hawks. He urged that the missiles be available "no later than mid-1964," since an offer for 1966 "would be worse than no offer at all." [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug10]

Feldman did not expect the plan to be welcomed by anyone. On the Israeli side he anticipated objection to the plan's lack of any numerical limit on refugees who might return, and to its burden on a state that had, he noted, absorbed some 500,000 Jewish refugees from Arab countries and about 50,000 Arab refugees. On the Arab side he anticipated insistence on full return. He called Ben-Gurion "the most violent opposition." And he estimated the plan's cost to the United States at "about $30 million a year—if we are lucky." [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug10] In Feldman's design the Hawk was the price of Ben-Gurion's consent: a security guarantee and a missile to make acceptable a refugee plan that Israel's Prime Minister was expected to fight.

The meeting of 19 August 1962. Feldman carried the message himself. His telegram, sent under the name "Barnes," reports a meeting of three and a half hours on 19 August 1962 with Ben-Gurion, the Foreign Minister Golda Meir and Teddy Kollek. Feldman told them that the President had decided Israel could have the Hawk, with the terms to be settled later. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] Ben-Gurion's reply put the arms question in his own terms: he "would gladly agree to no missiles at all if Nasser would agree to arms limitations and controls." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks]

On the refugee plan the Israelis gave little. Ben-Gurion said that no area would accept anything except repatriation. Meir said Israel would accept it only if Nasser resettled designated refugees in the UAR and refrained from propaganda urging repatriation. Feldman answered with the American estimate that "not more than one refugee in ten would seek repatriation." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_rusk_aug21] The refugees themselves, whose return or resettlement was the subject of the conversation, do not speak in these documents. Their position appears only as Feldman's expectation of an Arab insistence on full return and as Ben-Gurion's account of what they would accept. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19]

"A statement in principle," 21 August 1962. Two days later Rusk explained the decision to the American embassy in Cairo. His telegram described the offer as "a statement in principle in the absence of arms limitation" and "not an undertaking for delivery at specific time of specific number of missiles." It noted that Israel might prefer Britain's Bloodhound missile instead. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_rusk_aug21] The language was careful. It presented the Hawk as conditional on the absence of an arms agreement, which matched Ben-Gurion's own offer to forgo missiles if Nasser accepted controls, and it committed Washington to no date and no number.

Approval and presentation. The sale was approved on American credit in late August 1962. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] A State Department circular of 7 September instructed American posts to present the Hawk as purely defensive, a response to the Soviet build-up. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The first missiles were delivered in early 1965. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] Israel was believed, according to the Center for Israel Education, to have become the first nation outside NATO to obtain the Hawk. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_hawk] Robert Komer, in his oral history of 1969, later said that "the Hawks marked the watershed." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks]

The money. The sale was a sale, not a gift, and it was made on credit. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The Congressional Research Service series shows the military line in these years, though it does not say what the loans paid for. American military aid to Israel, all of it in the form of loans from 1959 onward, was $0.4 million in 1959 and $0.5 million in 1960, and $13.2 million in 1962; in 1963 it was $13.3 million. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] [cite:rs_cw4_cie_hawk] The estimates of the Hawk's cost, close to $40 million in 1960 and $50 million in 1961, were far larger than any single year's military line in that series. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] The sale's final price and its credit terms are not in the sources used here, so the chain from the cost estimates to the yearly loan figures cannot be traced.

The timeline of the aid relationship shown above summarizes these years in two rows: "1948–1961: economic aid and loans only," and "1962: first US military aid, as loans." The CRS figures refine both. The first row is right in substance but not to the letter, since the series shows small military loans of $0.4 million in 1959 and $0.5 million in 1960. The second row is right if "first military aid" means the first of any size: $13.2 million in 1962, the year of the Hawk decision. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] The difference between the two readings is the third of the disputes below.

Three disputes. The first is whether Nasser was told. Feldman, in his oral history of 1967, said that "Nasser was not told about it." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] The documents of August 1962 point the other way, or at least to an intention: Rusk's review of 7 August recommended the offer "after consultation with the British and discussion with the UAR," and the telegram of 21 August went to the embassy in Cairo, explaining the decision as a statement in principle. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_rusk_aug21] A telegram of instruction to an embassy shows what Washington planned to say; it does not by itself show what Nasser was told, or when. Feldman's recollection, five years later, is a participant's memory; the telegrams are contemporary but record intention. The sources used here do not settle which is right.

The second dispute is why Kennedy sold the Hawk at all. The documents of August 1962 give two reasons. One is the military balance: Soviet missiles and aircraft were reaching the UAR, and the Hawk would reduce Israel's exposure to surprise air attack. The other is the refugee plan: Feldman's memorandum makes the Hawk the inducement for Israeli acceptance of the Johnson plan. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] Against these, Ari Lance Buchman's 2013 honors thesis at Emory University argues that the decision was driven mainly by domestic politics, to win the backing of pro-Israel opinion, and that it cost Kennedy his attempt at a rapprochement with Nasser; his title calls it "a missed opportunity." [cite:rs_cw4_buchman_emory] The strongest form of the first case is that every contemporary document gives security and the refugee plan as the grounds, and that the decision followed a specific intelligence finding about Soviet supply. The strongest form of the second, as the documents themselves allow it to be put, is that the decision reversed the department's judgment of June that Israel was in little actual danger, and that the refugee plan Feldman tied to the Hawk did not win Israel's consent at the meeting of 19 August, yet the offer stood. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19] [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d18] [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] Buchman's argument is known here from his thesis's abstract only, and the evidence he relies on for the domestic motive is not among the sources fetched.

The third dispute is when American military aid to Israel began. On the CRS series, it began in 1959, with a military loan of $0.4 million. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] On the account of Komer and of the documents compiled by Martin Kramer, it began in 1962, with the Hawk, the first major sale: "the Hawks marked the watershed." [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_hawks] Both are accurate on their own terms. One counts the first dollar; the other counts the first weapon system that changed the relationship.

What came next. The decision opened a new phase in the relationship, and its terms were worked out in the months that followed, including the talks of December 1962, told in Stage Five in [scene:cw5_palm_beach|the terms of the relationship, 1962–63]. In the same months the administration was pressing Israel over Dimona; the two tracks, arming Israel conventionally while seeking to restrain its nuclear program, ran side by side from here on. [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] [cite:state_frus1964_68v18]

What the record does not settle. On the sources used here, the sale's final price and credit terms are not established. Arab and Soviet reactions to the decision are not documented in them, beyond the August instructions to the Cairo embassy. Whether Nasser was told, and when, remains in conflict between Feldman's later memory and the contemporary telegrams. The domestic-politics reading rests on a thesis known here only by its abstract. And the Johnson plan, to which Feldman tied the missiles, is described in these documents only through the objections each side was expected to raise and the estimate that "not more than one refugee in ten would seek repatriation"; the plan's own text is not among them. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_feldman_aug19]
