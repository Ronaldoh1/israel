Part Thirty-One established that the tithe was not a custom of early Christianity. It became church law at Mâcon in 585, with excommunication for refusal, and a royal obligation at Herstal in 779, and it was abolished in France in 1789 and ended in England only in 1977; Germany's Catholic dioceses still received €6.751 billion in church tax in 2025. [cite:vt31_shuler2012] [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] [cite:vt31_wiki_herstal] [cite:vt31_wiki_august] [cite:vt31_pillar_tax2025] Beside the tithe ran annates, Peter's Pence and indulgences, whose sale the papacy itself abolished in 1567, fifty years after Albrecht of Brandenburg's Fugger-financed indulgence provoked Luther. [cite:vt31_wiki_annates] [cite:vt31_wiki_albrecht] [cite:vt31_brit_indulgence] Attendance moved from custom to the brief exclusion of Elvira's canon 21, the yearly confession and Easter communion imposed under excommunication in 1215, and the "grave sin" of the present Catechism. In Germany since 2012, leaving the church-tax roll costs a Catholic the sacraments. [cite:vt31_elvira21] [cite:vt31_lateran4] [cite:vt31_ccc2181] [cite:vt31_nss_2012] Against this, the Gospel of Mary forbids rules beyond the Saviour's, and no tithe or attendance law is recorded among the groups that read such texts. Thomas, Ptolemy, Pistis Sophia and the Cathars' own bishops and vows show that the inner traditions were not free of discipline or office.
