Britain: The Friends of Israel and the Petition

Britain has no organization like AIPAC's super PAC, and none of the sources used here records outside spending against a sitting MP at a British election. Pro-Israel influence in Westminster runs instead through donations to MPs and sponsored trips, both of which MPs declare in their Register of Members' Financial Interests, the record on which the count below is based. The main vehicles are the parliamentary Friends of Israel groups attached to the parties. This section sets out what the declarations show, according to the one systematic count of them, and the parliamentary argument the count helped provoke in 2026.

The count. In June 2024 the investigative outlet Declassified UK published an analysis of MPs' declarations in the 2019–24 Parliament. It found that about 180 of the 650 MPs had received funding from pro-Israel groups, individuals or Israeli state institutions, worth more than £1 million in all, including more than 240 paid trips to Israel costing more than £500,000. By party the count was 130 Conservatives, 41 Labour, 3 Liberal Democrats and 3 from the Democratic Unionist Party. Conservative Friends of Israel had funded 118 sitting Conservative MPs on 160 trips costing more than £330,000; Labour Friends of Israel had funded trips for 32 Labour MPs costing more than £64,000; more than 40 MPs had received funding from Israeli state institutions. [cite:pm_declassified_quarter]

The party leadership. The count also traced funding to the leadership of the Labour Party, which took office in July 2024. Sir Trevor Chinn, a businessman, gave £50,000 to Keir Starmer's campaign for the party leadership in 2020 and went on to finance eight members of his front bench; seven members of Starmer's cabinet had accepted trips to Israel funded by Labour Friends of Israel. [cite:pm_declassified_quarter] [cite:pm_aljaz_ukdebate] The declarations show the gifts. As with the American recipients in Part Four, they do not show that any position was taken because of them.

The petition and the debate. In early 2026 a parliamentary e-petition calling for a public inquiry into pro-Israel and Israeli state-linked influence on British politics gathered 118,306 signatures, more than enough to be considered for a debate. [cite:pm_aljaz_ukdebate] The government said in April that existing rules already provided transparency and that it did not support an inquiry, adding that the Prime Minister had asked the Ethics and Integrity Commission to review lobbying, disclosure and access to government. [cite:pm_aljaz_ukdebate] [cite:pm_jn_petition] The Jewish Leadership Council said the petition offered "no credible evidence" for its claims and drew on tropes widely recognized as antisemitic, and asked for it to be taken down; the request was refused. [cite:pm_jn_petition] MPs debated it in Westminster Hall on 22 June 2026. The Conservative MP who opened the debate for the Petitions Committee, John Lamont, said he did not support the petition: it singled out one country when many lobby in Britain, it echoed old claims of Jewish control of politics, and the clearest documented cases of foreign interference involved Russia and China. He also said Israel's government could be criticized and that rules on lobbying and donations should apply to every country alike. As with every debate on a petition, there was no vote. [cite:pm_wb_ukdebate]

Both sides of the argument rest on a fact the other accepts. The money and the trips are on the public register, which is how Declassified UK could count them, and which is the government's reason for saying no inquiry is needed. The difference from the United States lies elsewhere. The British record shows nothing like the primary spending of Part Five; what it shows, on a large scale, is funding that reached more than a quarter of the House of Commons in a single Parliament, much of it in the form of the instrument described in Part Seven, the paid trip.
