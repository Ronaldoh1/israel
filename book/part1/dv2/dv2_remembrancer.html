The Remembrancer: The City's Officer in Parliament

On 6 February 1571 the Corporation of the City of London created a new office and gave it an old-fashioned name. The Remembrancer was to keep the City's important business "in remembrance." The first holder was Thomas Norton, and his original task was bookkeeping: bringing the Corporation's journals and records up to date. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] Over the following centuries the office became something else. It is now the channel through which the Lord Mayor and the City deal with the Sovereign, the Royal Household and Parliament, and it serves as the City's chief of protocol. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] It is the most discussed of the City's institutions, because no other local authority in Britain has an officer whose permanent job is to watch Parliament on its behalf.

What the office does in Parliament is a matter of record. The Remembrancer is a parliamentary agent. The office monitors legislation, reports to the Corporation on bills that touch the City's interests, briefs members of Parliament, submits evidence to select committees, and drafts and promotes the Corporation's own private bills. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] In 2011, according to reporting in The Guardian, the department had a budget of £6 million and employed six lawyers to scrutinize prospective legislation and give evidence to committees. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] The most recent holder recorded in the sources used here is Paul Double, the 45th Remembrancer, in post since 2003; in November 2022 he submitted the City of London (Markets) Bill to Parliament, to which the London Borough of Havering objected. [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer]

Where the Remembrancer sits is another matter, and here the popular account and the record part company. A widely repeated claim holds that the Remembrancer has a seat on the floor of the House of Commons, beside or behind the Speaker's chair, the only person who is not a member allowed there. Versions of the claim have appeared in print, including in a textbook published in 2017. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] The House of Commons Commission has stated otherwise. The Remembrancer may watch proceedings from the visitors' under-gallery, near the Serjeant at Arms, by the Speaker's permission and not by right; he has no access beyond the bar of the House, and none to the floor. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] Not being a member, he can neither speak in debates nor vote. [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer]

The same correction applies to a second part of the legend. The Remembrancer is notified of private bills that affect the City, as any affected party is. The office is not shown public bills before they are published. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] In 2013 the Green Party member of Parliament Caroline Lucas wrote to the Speaker, John Bercow, asking him to consider removing the Remembrancer from the floor of the House and to end claimed privileges of seeing draft legislation. [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] Her letter is part of the record; so are the documents, set out below, that bear on the two privileges it described.

The correction does not make the office trivial. An officer with a budget of millions and a team of lawyers, whose only job is to track legislation for one client and to argue that client's case to members and committees, is a lobbyist, and a permanent one. George Monbiot described the Remembrancer in 2011 as "an official lobbyist." [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] The author Nicholas Shaxson has called the office "the world's oldest institutional lobbyist." [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] The difference from other lobbyists is not a privileged seat. It is age, continuity and institutional standing: an office that has been doing this work since 1571, on behalf of a body that is itself part of the state's local government.

The office's own history shows what that work has looked like when the City felt threatened. In 1854 it spent £2,750 on "private opposition" to a government bill. Between 1835 and 1875, by one account, its lobbying helped defeat bills and reports that would have reformed the City on at least twelve occasions. [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] In 1884 a government-backed bill to reform the government of London ran out of parliamentary time after a campaign largely run by the Remembrancer of the day, Gabriel Goldney, which cost £19,500. Goldney later admitted to a select committee that he had paid a go-between to have men break up meetings. [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] The office did not always win. In 1863 it failed to stop a bill letting the Metropolitan Board of Works drive Queen Victoria Street through the City's territory. And it has used the same machinery for ends few would object to: in 1878 the Epping Forest Bill it promoted passed Parliament, and the forest it protected is still managed by the Corporation today. [cite:dv2_wanderer_remembrancer] [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts]

What the record does not show should be stated as precisely. The sources used here describe the office's functions, its budget in one year, and several historical campaigns. They do not measure how often, in recent decades, its work has changed the text of a law, and they do not establish any role for the Remembrancer in British policy toward Palestine or Israel. The office's documented concern has been the City's own powers, privileges and property. Its importance to this volume is structural. It shows that the body which governs the country's financial district has, alone among British local authorities, a permanent and long-established channel to the legislature, and it shows that the channel has been used, when the City's privileges were at stake, with persistence and money. Whether that channel shaped any particular decision on finance is a question the documents leave open.
