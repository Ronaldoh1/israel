Washington Retreats: The Trusteeship Proposal of March 1948

On 18 March 1948 Harry Truman received Chaim Weizmann at the White House. He had refused to see the Zionist leader through official State Department channels, and the meeting happened only because Eddie Jacobson, his former partner in a Kansas City clothing store, asked him to. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] The meeting is widely credited with securing Truman's commitment to the Jewish state. [scene:s08|The president and his advisers] tells how it came about. The next day, 19 March, the American ambassador to the United Nations stood up in the Security Council and announced that the United States was stepping back from partition.

Warren Austin told the Council that the partition plan "cannot now be implemented by peaceful means." In its place, he said, "a temporary trusteeship for Palestine should be established under the Trusteeship Council." He asked the Council to request a special session of the General Assembly, under Article 20 of the Charter, and to instruct the United Nations Palestine Commission to "suspend its efforts to implement the proposed partition plan." [cite:rs_rd5_avalon_austin]

A common shorthand holds that the United States backed the Jewish state steadily from the partition vote to recognition. The record of these four months shows something else: a government that voted for partition in November, proposed to suspend it in March, and recognized the new state in May, with its own delegation at the United Nations still arguing for trusteeship when the recognition was announced. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition]

The scale of the retreat is clearest set against what the United States had voted for. On 29 November 1947 it was among the 33 states, with 13 against and 10 abstaining, that adopted the partition resolution. That resolution set a timetable. The Mandate was to end "as soon as possible but in any case not later than 1 August 1948." Provisional councils of government were to be in place by 1 April 1948. The two states were to come into existence two months after the British evacuation and "not later than 1 October 1948." A five-member Palestine Commission, made up of Bolivia, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, Panama and the Philippines, was to carry it out. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] Austin's statement asked that body to stop, less than two weeks before the date the resolution had set for the provisional councils.

Trusteeship was not a new idea, and it had not belonged to one side. In 1944 the American Jewish Committee had proposed an international trusteeship instead of a Jewish commonwealth. [cite:djn_1944_hearings] In April 1946 the Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry recommended continuing the Mandate toward a United Nations trusteeship, and declared that Palestine should be neither a Jewish nor an Arab state. [cite:avalon_aac] In May 1948 Hannah Arendt would argue in print for a binational federation under United Nations trusteeship, and Judah Magnes would meet Truman to press the same case. [cite:rs_rd5_manne_2026] What was new in March 1948 was that the government that had led the vote for partition now offered trusteeship as its replacement.

The reasons the State Department gave for caution were on record well before Austin spoke. Loy Henderson, director of Near Eastern Affairs, Under Secretary Robert Lovett and Secretary of State George Marshall opposed early recognition of a Jewish state, arguing that it would damage American relations with the Arab world and endanger the oil supply. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Austin's own stated ground was narrower and practical: partition could not be carried out without force. [cite:rs_rd5_avalon_austin] By then fighting between Palestine's Jewish and Arab communities had gone on since 1 December 1947 [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition], and nine days before Austin spoke the Haganah had issued Plan Dalet, its plan for the period of the British withdrawal ([scene:pd_s01|Plan Dalet]).

The two events of 18 and 19 March collided because they came from two different parts of one government. The meeting with Weizmann ran through a personal channel, an old friend reaching a tired president, while the professional foreign service was strongly advising the other way. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] The statement at the Security Council came from the diplomatic machinery that Henderson, Lovett and Marshall directed. The material gathered for this book does not show whether Truman knew that Austin would speak on 19 March, what he had told Weizmann in detail, or who drafted the statement and cleared its timing. Those questions are left open here rather than answered by inference.

The reactions were immediate. The Secretary-General, Trygve Lie, saw the new American position as "a rebuff to the United Nations." According to his memoir, as footnoted in the State Department's documentary history, he suggested that he and Austin should both resign; Austin declined. [cite:rs_rd5_frus_austin_1948] An American memorandum recorded that the Arab representatives "were pleased with this latest turn of events, but they did not gloat." [cite:rs_rd5_frus_austin_1948]

For the Jewish Agency, Abba Hillel Silver answered in the Council the same day. He called the proposal "a shocking reversal" of the American position and warned that it was likely "to lead to increased violence in Palestine." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_trusteeship] On 20 March David Ben-Gurion went further, and his answer amounted to a refusal:

We shall not agree to any kind of trusteeship, whether permanent or temporary, even for the shortest time

He called the reversal "a surrender to the terrorism of the Arab gangs" and said it would not "prevent the establishment of the Jewish State." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_trusteeship] The two characterizations of what had moved Washington could hardly be further apart. Austin spoke of the impossibility of a peaceful partition. Ben-Gurion spoke of surrender to force. Each was stating his own government's or movement's case, and neither statement settles the motives of the officials who drafted the policy.

The Soviet Union took the opposite course from Washington. When the United States moved toward trusteeship, Andrei Gromyko told the Security Council that the rapid creation of two states was the only way to reduce bloodshed, and Moscow stood behind partition. [cite:yated_stalin_israel] [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel] The Soviet calculation is taken up in [scene:so_s01|the Soviet section]; here it matters that in March 1948 the two powers that had voted together in November were on opposite sides of the question.

Austin's proposal did not stop the Jewish Agency from preparing for statehood, as Ben-Gurion had said it would not. On the same day Austin spoke, Golda Meyerson returned from her American fundraising tour with a reported $50 million, spent largely on weapons. [cite:jwa_golda_50m] Plan Dalet's operations began on 1 April. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Eight weeks later the United States extended de facto recognition to Israel eleven minutes after its proclamation, while the American delegation at the United Nations was still working for trusteeship. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition]

The record assembled here leaves gaps. It holds Austin's statement and the first reactions to it, but not the proceedings of the special session he requested or what became of the trusteeship draft there. It does not document the internal deliberations that produced the statement, and it cannot say how far Truman's private meeting with Weizmann and his ambassador's public retreat were known to each other at the time. What it does establish is that in the spring of 1948 the United States held two positions at once, and that both were visible to everyone watching.
