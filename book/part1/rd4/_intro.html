The years between 1936 and 1945 moved the center of the question. At their start came an armed uprising in Palestine against British rule and Jewish immigration, a royal commission formed to investigate it, and a first official proposal to divide the territory. By their end, the arguments were being made in a New York hotel ballroom, in the White House, and in the platforms of both American parties.

In between came the catastrophe. Nazi Germany turned in November 1938 from legal exclusion of Jews to open, organized violence. That same year thirty-two nations met at Évian and nearly all declined to take more refugees, and Britain, in its White Paper of 1939, capped Jewish immigration to Palestine just as escape became a matter of survival. Refugee ships were sunk or turned back. A clandestine fleet tried to carry people past the British limits. Inside the Zionist movement, the careful language of a "national home" gave way to an explicit demand for a state.

This stage asks how that shift happened: why the movement's center of gravity moved toward the United States, what Britain, the Vatican and the American president actually did and said, and how much of what is commonly remembered about these years survives contact with the documents.
