The People Already There: Herzl, al-Khalidi and the Arab Question, 1895–1905

A common belief holds that the founders of political Zionism imagined Palestine as empty, and that the existence of its Arab population came as a later surprise. The record of the movement's first decade does not support that belief. Herzl, his correspondents and his allies wrote about the people living in the land, sometimes privately and sometimes in print, from the movement's earliest days. What they disagreed about, and what historians still argue over, is what they intended to do about them.

The question had been put to the movement before Herzl came to it. In 1891 Asher Ginsberg, writing as Ahad Ha'am, published an account of his visit to Ottoman Palestine reporting that the land was farmed and lived on by a substantial Arab population, who would not simply acquiesce to displacement; his warning, and his wider quarrel with Herzl, are told in [scene:s04|the Basel section].[cite:wiki_ahad_haam] The historian Steven Beller, reviewing Derek Penslar's biography of Herzl, notes that Ahad Ha'am raised at the time a point Herzl never fully absorbed: Herzl, in Beller's words, "lacked a realistic view about the antagonistic effect" a Jewish settlement would have on the local Arab population.[cite:rs_rd1_beller_arab]

The phrase most often attached to this period has an older and non-Zionist origin. The earliest published version of "a land without a people for a people without a land" appeared in an 1843 book by Alexander Keith, a Church of Scotland clergyman and an advocate of Jewish "restoration," who had himself travelled to Palestine in 1839 and knew it was populated. Lord Shaftesbury used similar wording in 1853. The first use by a Zionist came in 1901, when Israel Zangwill wrote that Palestine was "a country without a people."[cite:meq_land_without_people] [cite:aijac_slogan_origin] Historians debate whether the phrase meant a land literally empty or a land "without a nation." Whatever its authors meant, the Ottoman counts of the period, set out in [scene:rd1_ottoman|the opening section], show a populated country. The slogan's later career, and the other talking points that grew up beside it, are examined in [scene:sq_s05|three claims checked].

Herzl's own first recorded thoughts on the matter came before the movement existed. In June 1895 he wrote a passage in his diary that has become one of the most argued-over sentences in the history of Zionism. As the Israeli researcher Shaul Arieli quotes it, by way of Edward Said, it reads: "We shall try to spirit the penniless population across the border by procuring employment for it in the transit countries, while denying it any employment in our country."[cite:rs_rd1_arieli_transfer]

Two serious readings of that passage stand against each other, and each has its evidence. Arieli, in a 2025 survey, places it at the head of a chronicle: in his account the idea of moving the Arab population out was a long-standing strand of Zionist strategic thinking, and this 1895 entry is where it begins.[cite:rs_rd1_arieli_transfer] Beller, drawing on Penslar, accepts that Herzl was "quite prepared to remove any local population," but adds two qualifications that matter. Herzl preferred to do it peacefully, through the employment mechanism the passage itself describes rather than by force. And "the context in which he was discussing this was not Palestine": the passage concerned South America.[cite:rs_rd1_beller_arab] The strongest form of Arieli's case is continuity: whatever territory Herzl had in mind, the passage shows that the founder of the movement treated a resident population as something to be moved, and later Zionist thinkers returned to the same idea. The strongest form of Beller's case is context: a private note about an unchosen territory, preferring economic pressure to coercion, is not the same thing as a plan for Palestine. Both scholars quote the same sentence. Neither the exact day of the entry nor its full surrounding text is given in the sources used here, so this book does not go beyond Arieli's quotation.

Four years later the question arrived at Herzl's desk from Jerusalem itself. The writer was Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi. According to the reference account of his career, he served as mayor of Jerusalem in 1870–76, 1878–79 and 1899–1906, sat for Jerusalem in the first Ottoman parliament, and had taught at the Oriental Academy in Vienna.[cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki] On 1 March 1899 he wrote to Zadoc Kahn, the chief rabbi of France, asking that the letter be passed to Herzl. It was not a hostile letter. Al-Khalidi called the Zionist idea in itself "natural, beautiful and just." But he set two facts against it: Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire, and it was inhabited by others. He closed with a plea: "in the name of God, let Palestine be left alone."[cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki] [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_intercept]

Herzl answered on 19 March 1899. His reply, as quoted, did not deny that the land was inhabited. His argument was that Jewish immigration would benefit the people already there: "It is their well-being, their individual wealth, which we will increase by bringing in our own." And he asked: "who would think of sending them away?"[cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_intercept] [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki]

How to read that reply is itself disputed. Taken on its own terms, it is a statement of good intent and a prediction: Jewish capital and labor would enrich the country, and no one proposed expulsion. The historian Rashid Khalidi, a descendant of al-Khalidi's family, reads it otherwise. He calls the reply condescending and argues that it did not answer al-Khalidi's central objection, that the people of Palestine would not accept being displaced, whatever the economic benefits offered them.[cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_intercept] The exchange set the founder of political Zionism against a senior Jerusalem notable, and the two men were, in a sense, writing past each other: one about sovereignty and consent, the other about prosperity.

Herzl returned to the argument in fiction. His novel Altneuland, published in German in 1902, imagines Palestine in 1923, run by a cooperative "New Society" in which land is publicly owned. The book has one Arab character, Reschid Bey. According to the standard summary of the novel, Reschid Bey says that Arabs had no reason to oppose Jewish immigration, because the newcomers "developed the country and raised everybody's standard of living."[cite:rs_rd1_altneuland_wiki] The character speaks almost the same argument Herzl had put to al-Khalidi three years earlier. A reader sympathetic to Herzl can see in the novel a sincere vision of a shared, prosperous country without expulsion. A reader persuaded by Rashid Khalidi can see the same objection unanswered: the only Arab voice in the book is one that agrees.

A blunter statement came from Zangwill, the writer who had first given the "country without a people" phrase a Zionist voice. In 1905, as Arieli quotes him, Zangwill wrote that "Palestine itself is already occupied," and that Zionists must be prepared either "to drive out by the sword the tribes in possession as our forefathers did" or "to grapple with the problem of a large alien population, mostly Mohammedan and accustomed to despise us."[cite:rs_rd1_arieli_transfer] The quotation reaches this book through Arieli's survey; its original place of publication is not confirmed in the sources used here. That same year Zangwill broke with the Zionist Organization over the East Africa question and went looking for a territory elsewhere.

Read in order, these documents settle the first question a reader is likely to ask. The founders knew Palestine was inhabited. Keith had seen it in 1839; Ahad Ha'am reported it in 1891; al-Khalidi told Herzl in writing in 1899, and Herzl answered him without denying it; Zangwill, the first Zionist to use the slogan, wrote in 1905 that the land was "already occupied." What they said about it ranged widely: from Herzl's assurance that no one would send the inhabitants away, through Altneuland's picture of shared prosperity, to Zangwill's choice between the sword and the "problem" of a large population. These are the words of individuals, in a diary, a letter, a novel and Zangwill's own statement, not resolutions of a Zionist Congress.

What the record does not settle is the question historians still divide on: what Herzl himself intended for the Arabs of Palestine. Arieli reads the 1895 passage as the start of a continuous strand of thinking about transfer; Beller and Penslar read it as a note about South America by a man who preferred peaceful means. Herzl's 1899 letter denies any intent to send the inhabitants away, and Rashid Khalidi reads that denial as evasion of the real objection. The sources here give the passages these readings rest on, not Herzl's full diary entry or his letter in its entirety, and a reader should weigh the competing readings with that limit in mind. What can be said plainly is that the warning al-Khalidi put to Herzl, that the people of Palestine would not accept displacement, was on paper in March 1899, and that the movement's founder answered it with an economic argument rather than a political one.
