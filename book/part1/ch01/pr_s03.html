Occupation, Apartheid, Genocide: What the Words Mean in Law, and Who Has Found What

The differences in legal standing traced in the last two sections are the ground on which three words are fought over. Occupation, apartheid and genocide run through almost every public argument about Israel and the Palestinians. Two opposite habits surround them. One holds that they are slurs with no legal meaning, used to wound rather than to describe. The other holds that all three have already been proven in court. Neither is accurate. Each word has a definition in international law, set down in a treaty or a statute. Each has been applied to Israel by bodies of very different standing: a court, a United Nations commission, human-rights organizations, an association of scholars. And each finding has been rejected by Israel. The first task in weighing them is to keep those bodies apart. A court's opinion, a UN commission's conclusion, a non-governmental report and a government's denial are different kinds of statement, and this book labels each one for what it is.

Occupation. In international law, occupation is the control of foreign territory by a state's armed forces. The question that matters is whether a particular occupation is lawful. On 19 July 2024 the International Court of Justice, the UN's highest court, gave its answer for the territory Israel holds.[cite:icj_press_19jul24]

The court was responding to a request from the UN General Assembly. It found that Israel's continued presence in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, meaning the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza, is unlawful. It said that Israel must bring that presence to an end as rapidly as possible, cease all new settlement activity immediately, evacuate all settlers, and make reparation. It added obligations for everyone else: all states and international organizations must not recognize the situation as legal, and must not assist in maintaining it.[cite:icj_press_19jul24] [cite:un_icj_ao_record] Several of these findings passed by 14 votes to 1.[cite:un_icj_ao_record] [cite:diakonia_icj_summary]

The reasoning is as important as the result, because it answers a common defense. The court noted that occupation law itself sets no time limit. Duration alone did not make the presence unlawful. What did were Israel's policies and its assertion of sovereignty: the court reaffirmed its 2004 opinion that the settlements and the regime attached to them violate international law, held that the prolonged occupation breaches the Palestinian people's right to self-determination, and found that the policies amount to annexation.[cite:transcend_icj_ao] [cite:adalah_icj_ao] It also found that they violate the prohibition on racial segregation and apartheid, a finding that bears on the second word.[cite:11kbw_icj_ao]

The opinion's limits are part of the record too. An advisory opinion is not a binding judgment. The court left it to the General Assembly and the Security Council to decide how to act on it.[cite:adalah_icj_ao] It therefore binds no one by itself, yet it is the world court's answer to whether the occupation is legal, and no body has enforced it. The day before the opinion was delivered, the Knesset passed a resolution calling Palestinian statehood an "existential danger."[cite:adalah_icj_ao] The familiar description of the occupation as a temporary security arrangement whose legality is a matter of opinion is contradicted by the court's advisory opinion. The court's opinion is told in full in a later volume: [scene:icj_s01|the ICJ's 2024 opinion].

Apartheid. Apartheid is a crime under international law, defined in the 1973 Apartheid Convention and in the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court. In both, it means an institutionalized regime of systematic domination by one racial group over another.[cite:fara_amnesty_explainer] Because the definition describes a structure of domination, the argument over the word turns on the different legal situations of the people Israel governs: citizens, residents of East Jerusalem, and Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, set out in [scene:pr_s02|the previous section].

Three human-rights organizations have concluded that Israel commits apartheid: Human Rights Watch in 2021, the Israeli group B'Tselem in 2021, and Amnesty International in February 2022.[cite:fara_amnesty_explainer] These are reports by non-governmental organizations, not judgments. The nearest a court has come is the ICJ's 2024 finding that Israeli policies breach the prohibition on racial segregation and apartheid.[cite:11kbw_icj_ao] That finding is a breach of a prohibition stated in an advisory opinion; it is not a criminal conviction of any person for the crime of apartheid. Israel rejects the apartheid label.[cite:fara_amnesty_explainer] [cite:11kbw_icj_ao]

Genocide. Under the 1948 Genocide Convention, genocide means specified acts committed with intent to destroy a group, in whole or in part. Since late 2024 a series of bodies has concluded that genocide, or acts of genocide, have been committed in Gaza. Amnesty International did so in December 2024. Human Rights Watch, also in December 2024, concluded that Israel had committed acts of genocide through the deprivation of water. In July 2025 two Israeli organizations, B'Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights–Israel, reached the same conclusion. In August 2025 the International Association of Genocide Scholars passed a resolution to that effect, and in September 2025 a UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that acts of genocide had been committed.[cite:ips_amnesty_hrw_genocide] [cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] [cite:news9_un_coi]

These findings were challenged. More than 500 scholars called on the genocide scholars' association to retract its resolution, citing the process by which it was adopted.[cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] Israel rejects all of the findings.[cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] [cite:news9_un_coi]

No court has decided the question. The case brought by South Africa at the ICJ remains pending. In 2024 the court ordered provisional measures, which found that some rights were plausibly at risk; that order did not decide whether genocide had occurred, and it is not a verdict.[cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] It must be said plainly: as of the research behind this edition, completed in 2026, the genocide case is undecided. The claim that genocide "has been proven in court" is false, and so is the claim that the word has no legal footing in this case. Both a court proceeding and a set of formal findings by UN and non-governmental bodies exist, and the court has not ruled.

The sequence of legal steps is easiest to follow in order. The panel below traces it from South Africa's filing at the ICJ in December 2023, through the provisional-measures order of January 2024 and the July 2024 opinion on the occupation, to the UN Commission of Inquiry's finding in September 2025. It also records a separate track at a different court, the International Criminal Court, whose November 2024 arrest warrants for Netanyahu and Gallant concern war crimes and crimes against humanity, not genocide; that track is told in later volumes. The panel's last line is about enforcement, and the point it makes matches what the ICJ's own design implies: findings have been made, and no mechanism has carried them out.

Israel's stated position belongs beside those findings, as part of the same record. Israel rejects all of the genocide findings.[cite:jta_un_coi_genocide] [cite:news9_un_coi] As the next panel summarizes it, Israel also contests the ICJ's orders and the ICC's jurisdiction, and says its operations target Hamas, which it accuses of embedding in civilian areas. It rejects the apartheid label, and its parliament declared Palestinian statehood an existential danger on the eve of the occupation opinion.[cite:fara_amnesty_explainer] [cite:adalah_icj_ao]

"The only democracy in the Middle East." The legal findings sit uneasily beside one of the most repeated claims about Israel: that it is the only democracy in its region. For citizens, the indices largely bear the claim out. In the V-Dem Institute's 2024 report Israel remained the only electoral democracy in the Middle East and North Africa, a region where 98% of people live under autocracies.[cite:globes_vdem] Israel holds competitive elections and has a free press, and Arab citizens vote and sit in the Knesset.

The same report, however, demoted Israel from "liberal democracy" to "electoral democracy" for the first time in about 50 years. It cited government attacks on the judiciary and a "substantive decline" in freedom from torture.[cite:toi_vdem] Freedom House's score for Israel fell from 80 in 2017 to 73. The Israel Democracy Institute, an Israeli think tank, called the downgrade "troubling, but not catastrophic," noting that countries such as Austria and Portugal sit in the same category.[cite:idi_vdem]

The larger gap is structural, and it is the point at which the democracy indices meet the apartheid argument. Both V-Dem and Freedom House assess Israel without the occupied territories. Palestinians in the West Bank live under Israeli military control but cannot vote in Israel's elections. By one count, from PRIO, of roughly 15.5 million people under Israel's control, about 5.5 million lack citizenship.[cite:prio_democracy] Both facts stand together. Israel is a democracy for its citizens, and it governs millions of people who cannot vote for the government that governs them. The talking point states the first fact and leaves out the second, and the apartheid argument, as the previous section showed, turns on differences of this kind.

What the record does not settle. The most important open question is the one before the ICJ: whether genocide has been committed in Gaza. No court has answered it, and the formal findings by UN and non-governmental bodies, however detailed, are not a judgment. On apartheid, no court has convicted any person of the crime; the ICJ's finding concerns a breach of a prohibition in an advisory opinion, and the non-governmental reports are disputed by Israel. On occupation, the law is the clearest of the three, since the world court has spoken, but its opinion is advisory, and whether and how any state or UN organ will act on it remains open. These are live matters, and their status here is as of the research behind this edition in 2026. What a reader can take away is a way of reading the next headline: ask which word is being used, which body used it, and whether that body was a court, a UN commission, a human-rights organization or a government.
