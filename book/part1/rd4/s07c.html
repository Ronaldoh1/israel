From Resolution to Movement Policy

A resolution passed at a single conference can easily remain just that, a statement by the people who happened to be in the room. The Biltmore Program, adopted in 1942 and discussed earlier in this book, did not stay there. Within months it had been made the formal platform of the organized Zionist movement, and within two years its language had reached the platforms of both major American political parties.

The familiar account holds that Zionist political organizing in America was informal and marginal until the founding of Israel in 1948 forced President Truman's hand. The record of 1942 to 1944 does not fit that account. The decisive institutional step came in October 1942, when the Zionist General Council formally adopted the Biltmore Program as official policy [cite:biltmore_enc]. That ratification converted Biltmore from the position of the delegates who attended into the binding platform of the entire organized Zionist movement worldwide. It was the mechanism by which one conference's resolution became years of political activity. The record documents the top-level ratification; it does not detail how each national Zionist body adopted and applied it in turn.

The next step was to turn the platform into politics in the United States, and here the speed is striking. By 1944 both major American party platforms included planks addressing the Palestine question. Abba Hillel Silver's biographical record documents that in 1944 he lobbied for a Palestine resolution, and that the same year saw Palestine written into both parties' platforms [cite:azec_case]. Two years separated a resolution adopted at a hotel conference from language in the platforms of both major parties alike. The record establishes that both platforms included Palestine-related planks. It does not, in this account, compare the wording the two parties chose.

That two-year turnaround is a measure of how effective the organizing behind Biltmore already was. It also places the origins of organized American political support for a Jewish state well before 1948. The Biltmore position was being carried into American party politics while the war was still being fought.

The work of translating Biltmore into American political practice ran through a specific organization, the American Zionist Emergency Council. That body is the institutional bridge between the 1942 declaration and the domestic political machine examined next, in [scene:s07f|the American Zionist Emergency Council]. Its leaders, its methods and its internal quarrels show how a conference resolution became a working program of pressure on Congress, the parties and, in time, the White House.
