The Mass Market: Prophecy Books and Religious Television

From 1970 the end-times system reached far more people through books and broadcasts than through any school. Three channels carried it: a prophecy bestseller by a Dallas graduate, a series of novels that turned the same scheme into fiction, and religious television, where Pat Robertson's Christian Broadcasting Network was the pioneer. This section measures each by what can be counted and records what its best-known figures said about Israel. Sales and audiences show reach. They do not show what readers and viewers came to believe, which only surveys can measure, and the one survey of the American public quoted here is given with its date.

The Late Great Planet Earth, 1970

Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth, written with Carole C. Carlson and published by Zondervan in 1970, applied the dispensational timetable to the news. The book appeared three years after the war of June 1967, and Lindsey read Israel's rebirth as the key to the prophetic clock. [cite:ts7_wrap_lindsey] He linked Jesus's saying in Matthew 24 that "this generation" would not pass away to the founding of Israel and treated a biblical generation as forty years, which some readers took to mean the end would come no later than 1988; the book was made into a film narrated by Orson Welles, released in January 1978. [cite:ts7_wiki_lgpe]

The sales figures vary with the source and the year. One history gives 28 million copies sold by 1990; Lindsey's obituary in TheWrap gave "an estimated 35 million copies by 1999." [cite:ts7_wiki_lgpe] [cite:ts7_wrap_lindsey] The book is regularly described as the bestselling nonfiction book of the 1970s, a judgment attributed to The New York Times; this volume found the attribution only in secondary works, not in the newspaper itself, and gives it as reported. [cite:ts7_wiki_lgpe] Lindsey's later prophecy books sold well but never matched the first. [cite:ts7_wrap_lindsey]

Lindsey moved to television. On the Trinity Broadcasting Network he hosted a programme called International Intelligence Briefing until the network pulled it for December 2005. Lindsey said that "some at the network apparently feel that [his] message is too pro- Israel and too anti- Muslim"; TBN's owner, Paul Crouch, disputed this and said the show had been pre-empted for Christmas programming. Lindsey resigned on 1 January 2006, broadcast on smaller networks, and returned to TBN in January 2007. He died on 25 November 2024, aged 95. [cite:ts7_wiki_lindsey] The episode shows that even inside religious broadcasting the weight given to Israel was argued over, and that the network's own account differed from his.

Left Behind, 1995–2007

The Left Behind novels, written by the pastor Tim LaHaye and the novelist Jerry B. Jenkins and published by Tyndale House, ran to 16 volumes between 1995 and 2007. [cite:ts7_sa_lahaye] They are thrillers "about Evangelical Christians living through an end-times apocalypse inspired by the Book of Revelation": the dispensational sequence of rapture, tribulation and return, told as a story. [cite:ts7_brit_leftbehind] The sales figures again conflict. The Christian Post reported at LaHaye's death in July 2016 that the series had sold "over 50 million copies", about 50 million of them by 2002; Shelf Awareness, a book-trade newsletter, gave "some 80 million copies" the same week. [cite:ts7_cp_lahaye] [cite:ts7_sa_lahaye] The difference probably reflects whether spin-off and children's editions are counted, but neither source says so. The figure of "65 million" printed in earlier works in this library has no source and is not used here.

Pat Robertson and the Christian Broadcasting Network

Pat Robertson launched the Christian Broadcasting Network on 1 October 1961; CBN's own announcement of his death spoke of the network he founded in 1960. He hosted its flagship programme, The 700 Club, from 1966 to 2021, and died on 8 June 2023, aged 93. [cite:ts7_tvi_robertson] The historian Joseph Williams notes that Robertson played a crucial role in the Religious Right's rise in the 1980s and that he, like John Hagee, was closely identified with the pentecostal-charismatic movement. [cite:ts7_williams_pent] The political career that followed belongs to the next part. [scene:ts8_orgs|Part Eight: the organisations]

Robertson's best-documented statement on Israel shows both the theology and its limits as a shield. On 5 January 2006, the day after Prime Minister Ariel Sharon suffered a stroke, Robertson told The 700 Club audience that "God considers this land to be his" and suggested that a prime minister who gave up land would face God's opposition; Sharon had withdrawn Israel's settlers from Gaza the previous summer. [cite:ts7_cbs_robertson] [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement] Israel's government did not welcome the theology. The tourism minister, Abraham Hirchson, said Robertson's help was no longer wanted for a planned Christian Heritage Center in the Galilee, for which Robertson was leading a group of evangelicals who had pledged to raise $50 million; a ministry official said the government remained "outraged". Robertson wrote to Sharon's son Omri: "I ask your forgiveness and the forgiveness of the people of Israel." [cite:ts7_cbs_robertson] A Christian broadcaster who claimed to speak for God about Israel's land had been rebuked by Israel's own ministers when the claim was turned on an Israeli leader.

Broadcasting beyond the United States

Williams's study in the journal Church History (2015) argues that pentecostal and charismatic ritual and media, more than formal end-times doctrine alone, helped forge Christian Zionism into a cohesive movement in the United States and abroad. A 1990s survey of evangelical leaders by James Guth and colleagues found that pentecostal and charismatic leaders gave Israel the highest political support, and that the strongest Christian Zionism went with premillennial eschatology. [cite:ts7_williams_pent] In 1994 CBN Conferences held a Feast of Tabernacles-style event in Virginia Beach with speakers from the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem; programmes broadcasting Israel-themed worship became "permanent fixtures on pentecostal-charismatic radio and television", and US-based evangelists and broadcast ministries spread this expression of Christian Zionism "well beyond North America", helping to "catalyze a transnational, global movement". [cite:ts7_williams_pent] The study gives no audience figures for those broadcasts, and none is offered here.

What the American public said

A survey shows how widely the core belief had spread by the early 2000s, though not what spread it. In a Pew survey of July 2003, 44 per cent of Americans said God gave the land that is now Israel to the Jewish people, and 36 per cent said Israel's existence fulfilled biblical prophecy about Jesus's second coming; white evangelical Protestants were the most likely to hold both views. [cite:ts7_pew_2003] The belief in the land as a divine gift was thus held by far more people than had bought a prophecy book, and by Americans of several traditions. Later surveys, including those that show younger evangelicals less attached to Israel, are taken up in Part Eleven. [scene:ts11_generations|Part Eleven: the generations]

The record supports a limited conclusion. Between 1970 and 2007 two publishing events put the dispensational timetable, with Israel at its centre, into tens of millions of copies, and religious television gave its preachers a daily platform. Those channels are documented by sales and by what their best-known figures said. The claim that they manufactured American support for Israel is a different claim; it would need surveys that tie exposure to belief, and none is cited in the sources this volume examined.
