Peter's Pence: What the Collection Pays For

Peter's Pence (the Obolo di San Pietro) is the annual collection that dioceses, foundations, religious institutes and private donors send to the pope. It is the one stream of Holy See money that ordinary churchgoers pay into directly, and so it is the one whose use donors have questioned most sharply. The answer is in the Holy See's own reports, which since 2021 have broken the fund's income and spending down each year. Most of the money supports the running of the Holy See, and a smaller part goes directly to charity. The Holy See says so itself. [cite:vt9_vn_guerrero2021] [cite:vt9_vn_pp2024]

What the Holy See says the fund is for

Fr Juan Antonio Guerrero Alves, then Prefect of the Secretariat for the Economy, set out the official account in an interview with Vatican News on 25 June 2021. Peter's Pence, he said, supports the pope's charity in two ways. Part of it goes directly to local churches, institutions, families and people in need. Part of it pays for offices of the Curia that serve the universal Church but earn little or no income: those responsible for doctrine, liturgy and the Church's courts, the papal communications offices, and the Vatican Library and Archives. "People have the right to know how we spend the money that is given to us," he said. [cite:vt9_vn_guerrero2021] Francis's decree of 26 December 2020 had already made the fund a sub-account of the Papal Funds administered by APSA. Under it, money is paid out at the Secretariat of State's request, within the approved budget, and spending outside the budget needs the Secretariat for the Economy's approval. [cite:vt9_mp_2020]

The disclosures began in a crisis of trust. Reuters reported in June 2022 that giving had fallen 23 per cent between 2015 and 2019 and a further 18 per cent in 2020. Many Catholics said they had stopped giving because of the Vatican's financial scandals, above all the London building purchase then at the centre of the trial covered in [scene:vt10_london_trial|Part Ten]. [cite:vt9_reuters_pp2021] Guerrero acknowledged that Peter's Pence investments had been mixed with other Secretariat of State funds, so that it was hard to tell which assets belonged to which. At the end of 2020 the fund held about 205 million euros, partly in illiquid investments that included the London building. [cite:vt9_vn_guerrero2021] His position was that the losses on the London building "did not weigh on" Peter's Pence but on other Secretariat of State funds. [cite:vt9_vn_guerrero2021] When the building was sold to Bain Capital for 186 million pounds, announced on 1 July 2022, the Holy See said the capital loss had been charged entirely to the Secretariat of State's reserve funds and that Peter's Pence "has not been touched". [cite:vt9_vn_sloane] These are the Holy See's own statements. The court record on the investment is the subject of Part Ten.

The numbers, year by year

In 2021, according to Reuters' reading of the first detailed report, contributions came to about 46.9 million euros and spending to about 65.3 million. About 55.5 million euros went to Vatican offices, nunciatures, communications and local churches, and about 10 million to 157 direct-aid projects. The deficit was covered from other Holy See income. [cite:vt9_reuters_pp2021] For 2023 the report gave income of 52 million euros: 48.4 million in donations and 3.6 million in financial income. Spending was 109.4 million euros, of which 90 million supported the apostolic mission through 68 Holy See bodies and 13 million went to 236 projects in 76 countries. The 2022 income figure, 107 million, was inflated by a one-off capital gain on the sale of property the fund owned. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2023]

For 2024 the fund collected 58 million euros: 54.3 million in donations and 3.7 million in financial and other income. It spent 75.4 million. Of that, 61.2 million went to the work of the dicasteries, about 17 per cent of the Holy See's 367.4 million euros of mission costs, and 13.3 million went to 239 projects in 66 countries. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2024] For 2025, the report published on 30 June 2026 gave revenue of 57.6 million euros, with donations of 54.5 million, and spending of 59.8 million: a deficit of 2.2 million, which it attributed to exchange rates. Of the spending, 41.2 million euros supported the Holy See's mission, about 10 per cent of the 404.5 million spent on that mission in 2025, and 13.3 million went to 252 projects in 74 countries. Africa received the largest regional share, 5.2 million euros for 73 projects. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2025]

The share that goes to charity

The share of spending that went directly to charity projects can be worked out from the reports themselves. It was about 15 per cent in 2021 (10 of 65.3 million), about 12 per cent in 2023 (13 of 109.4 million), about 18 per cent in 2024 (13.3 of 75.4 million) and about 22 per cent in 2025 (13.3 of 59.8 million). [cite:vt9_reuters_pp2021] [cite:vt9_vn_pp2023] [cite:vt9_vn_pp2024] [cite:vt9_vn_pp2025] These are this volume's own divisions. They depend on what is counted as spending: the 2023 report, for example, gives total disbursements of 103 million beside spending of 109.4 million. A scene in Observe The System's earlier simulation stated that "roughly 10%" of the 2024 collection went to charitable works. That figure matches neither the 2024 report nor the general pattern; it is closest to the 2021 and 2023 shares. In every year the cash projects took a minority of the money and the Holy See's offices took the majority.

Two points keep this in proportion. First, the Holy See does not hide the split. Its reports put the mission figure first, and Guerrero's account counts paying for the Curia's non-earning offices as part of the pope's service. A donor who expects the whole gift to reach the poor will not find that promise in the Holy See's own description of the fund. [cite:vt9_vn_guerrero2021] [cite:vt9_vn_pp2024] Second, "mission" spending is not all administration. In the consolidated accounts, 37 per cent of the 2024 mission budget supported local churches in difficulty and 10 per cent went to the service of charity. [cite:vt9_vn_hs2024] The reports do not say how much of Peter's Pence money ended up in each of those lines.

Who gives

Dioceses give most of the money. In 2023 they gave 31.2 million euros (64.4 per cent of donations) and foundations 13.9 million. By country, the United States led with 13.6 million euros (28.1 per cent), followed by Italy with 3.1 million and Brazil with 1.9 million. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2023] In 2024 donors in the United States gave 25.2 per cent, France 15 per cent, Italy 5.2 per cent, Brazil 3 per cent and Germany 2.8 per cent. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2024] In 2025 dioceses gave 34.7 million euros (63.6 per cent), foundations 13 million, private donors 5.4 million and religious institutes 1.4 million. The United States again gave the most, 14.2 million euros. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2025] Set beside the Holy See's budget, Peter's Pence is modest. Its 54.5 million euros in donations for 2025 were about a tenth of the Holy See's non-hospital revenue, which was 546.5 million euros in 2024, and about a twentieth of the 1.06 billion euros that the Italian Bishops' Conference received from the otto per mille in 2026, money that goes to the Church in Italy, not to the Holy See ([scene:vt3_otto_per_mille|Part Three]).
