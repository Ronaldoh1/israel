The American Palestine Committee, 1932

In 1932, while the Zionist movement in Palestine was building its labour federation, its banks and its defence force, one of its executives in the United States began building something of a different kind: a committee of prominent Americans who were mostly not Jewish. Emanuel Neumann, a member of the Zionist Executive, founded the American Palestine Committee that year to organize prominent, mainly non-Jewish Americans in support of a Jewish homeland [cite:aja_neumann_bio]. The committee's existence and its founder are documented in the records of the American Jewish Archives [cite:aja_neumann_bio].

The committee matters for what it shows about method. A common account of the 1940s holds that the wave of Christian and elite American support for a Jewish state arose on its own. The record shows that the most visible bodies expressing that support were founded and later revived by one Zionist official, working from inside the movement's political office. The support was real; so was the organization behind it. The two facts are not in tension, and this section keeps both.

The sources gathered here say little about the committee's first decade. They record its founding in 1932 and its purpose, and they do not record its membership, its activities or its funding in the 1930s. What they do record is what happened when Neumann returned to it. In January 1941 he joined the Emergency Committee for Zionist Affairs as head of public relations and political action, and from that post he revived the American Palestine Committee, with Senator Robert Wagner as its chairman [cite:aja_neumann_bio]. The mechanism is plain in the titles. The person charged with the Zionist emergency body's public relations and political action was the same person who rebuilt the committee through which prominent non-Jewish Americans would speak.

In December 1942 Neumann organized a second, parallel body for clergy, the Christian Council on Palestine [cite:aja_neumann_bio]. Its founding minutes describe it as an "autonomous committee working in cooperation with the American Palestine Committee" [cite:aja_ccp_minutes_1942]. By the end of the war the American Palestine Committee claimed some 6,500 members, among them senators, congressmen, cabinet members and governors, and by one description mayors; the Christian Council claimed about 3,000 clergy [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee] [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby]. These are the committees' own membership claims, not independent counts. In 1946 the two merged into the American Christian Palestine Committee, which disbanded after 1948 [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee]. How these committees fitted into the wider American campaign of the 1940s is told in [scene:s07f|the American Zionist Emergency Council].

The dispute is over a single word. The Christian Council's founding minutes call it "autonomous" [cite:aja_ccp_minutes_1942]. The historian Peter Grose described the committees' operations as "hardly autonomous" [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby]. Reporting that draws on Grose's work states that Zionist funds, reaching up to $150,000 by 1946, sustained them [cite:counterpunch_origins_lobby]. That figure is reported, not drawn from a primary financial record, and it should be read that way: this book has not seen the accounts it describes.

The strongest case for "autonomous" is that the committees' members were real people who lent their own names. Nothing in the record gathered here suggests that any of them signed on against their convictions. The strongest case for "hardly autonomous" is structural: both committees were founded by the same Zionist official, the larger one was revived from the Emergency Committee's political office, and, according to reporting on Grose's work, the money that kept them running came from Zionist funds. A body can be sincere in its membership and organized from outside in its operations. The distinction this book draws is between real names lent and organized direction, and the record shows both.

The same arrangement, organized support presented as independent, appears again in later decades, and this library documents it for the predecessors of AIPAC in its own place. The scale should be kept in proportion. The reported $150,000 appears as a bar, labelled $0.15 million, on the chart of documented dollar flows embedded in [scene:hv_s01|the Transfer Agreement section], where it sits beside sums up to several hundred times larger. One reading is that its significance lay not in its size but in what it bought: a public voice that appeared to come from outside the movement.

Several questions remain open on the evidence gathered here. The record does not say what the committee did between 1932 and 1941, whether it was active or dormant, or who paid for it in those years. It does not give a year-by-year account of the reported $150,000 or name its paying body. And it does not show how much direction Neumann's office gave the committees day to day, which is the real substance of Grose's phrase.
