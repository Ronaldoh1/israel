Two Subjects Behind One Word

"The Vatican" is used in news reports, in popular books and even in official sources for at least three different things: the government of the Catholic Church, a small state in the middle of Rome, and the Catholic Church itself. Most errors about how the Vatican works begin with that slippage. In law there are two subjects, and the 1929 treaty that created the state keeps them apart in its first articles. The Holy See is the papal office and the government around it. Vatican City State is a territory of 0.44 square kilometres over which the Holy See is sovereign. Part One of the companion volume has already set out why the Holy See counts as a subject of international law under the usual tests, and that argument is not repeated here. [scene:dv1_sovereignty|The Divine 3+1: what makes a state] This section shows, as concretely as the documents allow, which of the two does what.

What the treaty says

The Lateran Treaty, signed on 11 February 1929, deals with the two subjects in two separate articles. In Article 2, Italy recognizes the sovereignty of the Holy See "in the international field" as something inherent in its nature, in keeping with its tradition and the needs of its mission in the world. That is a recognition of a sovereignty Italy did not create. In Article 3, Italy recognizes the Holy See's full ownership and "exclusive and absolute" sovereign power and jurisdiction over the Vatican, "constituted" for that purpose as Vatican City. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The order matters. The Holy See comes first and already exists; the city is a territory handed to it so that its independence would rest on ground no other state controlled. Part Three described what each side gained from this bargain. [scene:vt3_what_each_got|Part Three: what each side got]

The same article of the treaty also shows how small the territory's independence is in practice. St Peter's Square belongs to Vatican City, but it stays normally open to the public and under the powers of the Italian police, whose officers stop at the foot of the basilica steps and go no further unless invited. When the Holy See closes the square for a ceremony, the Italian authorities withdraw behind the outer line of Bernini's colonnade. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] Inside the sovereign state, in other words, the largest open space is policed by the neighbouring country by treaty.

Which one signs

The Holy See conducts the diplomacy. As of 9 January 2026, 184 states maintained diplomatic relations with it, along with the European Union and the Sovereign Military Order of Malta, and 93 of the embassies accredited to it had their chancelleries in Rome. [cite:vt4_press_relations2026] It is the Holy See, not the city, that holds observer status at the United Nations, since 6 April 1964. [cite:dv1_wiki_holysee_un] [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] It is the Holy See that signed the Fundamental Agreement with Israel on 30 December 1993 and the Comprehensive Agreement with the State of Palestine in 2015, the two documents at the centre of this volume's question, which Parts Seventeen and Eighteen read in detail. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] [cite:vt1_rv_palestine2015] [scene:vt17_fundamental_agreement|Part Seventeen: the Fundamental Agreement] [scene:vt18_state_of_palestine|Part Eighteen: the State of Palestine]

Vatican City State signs very little in its own name, and when it does, the Holy See signs for it. The Secretariat of State publishes a list of the Holy See's agreements from the Lateran Pacts of 1929 to 2009; of about fifty-nine entries, only one carries the city's name in its title: the Monetary Agreement between the European Union and the Vatican City State of 17 December 2009. [cite:vt4_vatva_agreements_list] That agreement was signed in Brussels by Archbishop André Dupuy, the apostolic nuncio to the European Union, for the Holy See acting "as representative" of the city, and by Commissioner Joaquín Almunia for the Union. [cite:vt4_rv_monetary2009] The euro is a matter for the territory, which is why the city is the party; the Holy See is still the hand that signs. The Fundamental Law states the rule outright: representation of the state abroad and the making of treaties are reserved to the Pope, who exercises them through the Secretariat of State. [cite:vt4_wipo_fl2000]

How large, and who lives there

The Governorate, the city's own administration, gives the territory's area as 0.44 square kilometres, or 44 hectares, partly enclosed by walls. [cite:vt4_vcs_geography] A second figure, 49 hectares, is also in circulation and goes back to a 1930 atlas; the Governorate's own page uses 44. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] Forty-four hectares is about 109 acres. Some popular accounts give "44 acres", which confuses the unit and shrinks the state by more than half.

The population is small and unusual. On the Governorate's figures the state has 673 citizens, of whom 458 live inside the walls, 120 of them members of the Swiss Guard. Some 30 per cent of Vatican citizens do not live in the state at all; most of them are diplomats at their posts abroad. Sixty-six cardinals held Vatican citizenship at the end of 2024, and ten of them lived inside. Counting everyone who lived in the territory, citizen or not, there were 882 residents on 31 December 2024. [cite:vt4_vcs_population] The Governorate does not give a single date for all its figures, so the gap between 458 resident citizens and 882 residents, roughly four hundred people living in the city without its citizenship, is an approximation from its numbers, not a count it publishes. Section three explains why so many residents are not citizens and so many citizens are not residents: in the Vatican, citizenship follows the job.

The people who work there are far more numerous than those who live there. Il Post, reporting in May 2025, put the workforce at nearly 5,000, about 2,000 in the Curia, which serves the Holy See, and the rest employed by the state, including some 700 at the Vatican Museums. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] The two employers sit in the same buildings, but they are different: a curial official works for the Holy See, a museum guard for Vatican City State.

Outside the walls: the extraterritorial properties

The Holy See's property in Italy does not stop at the walls. Articles 13 to 16 of the Lateran Treaty created a second category of places that are not Vatican City but enjoy special status inside Italy. Italy recognized the Holy See's ownership of the three other patriarchal basilicas, St John Lateran, St Mary Major and St Paul Outside the Walls, of the papal palace at Castel Gandolfo with the Villa Barberini, and of buildings on the Janiculum hill facing the Vatican. Article 15 gives these, along with the palaces of the Datary, the Cancelleria, Propaganda Fide on Piazza di Spagna, the Holy Office and the Vicariate, the immunities that international law grants to the embassies of foreign states. Article 16 adds that they may not be expropriated without the Holy See's consent and are exempt from taxes, and extends those two protections to a list of pontifical universities and colleges. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The Governorate calls the zones around such buildings "extraterritorial areas". [cite:vt4_vcs_geography]

The list has grown by later agreement. Santa Maria di Galeria, outside Rome, holds the shortwave transmitters of Vatican Radio and has extraterritorial status under agreements with Italy of 1951; on 31 July 2025 the two sides signed a further agreement for a solar plant there, which the Italian Senate approved for ratification on 7 January 2026. [cite:vt4_vcs_galeria] The Bambino Gesù children's hospital, owned by the Holy See since 1924, sits on extraterritorial ground on the Janiculum, and the plan to move it is discussed in section four. [cite:vt4_wiki_bambino] An embassy-like immunity is not sovereignty: these buildings remain in Italy, and the treaty itself provides for the handover of suspects who take refuge in them, as section three shows. [cite:vt4_lateran_text]

The distinction has a direct bearing on this volume's question. When the record says that "the Vatican" recognized a state, protested a strike in Gaza or signed an agreement, it was the Holy See acting, through the Pope and the Secretariat of State, on behalf of a church that spans every continent. The 44 hectares are what guarantee that the Holy See answers to no other government while it does so; they are not where its weight comes from.
