The Histadrut and "Hebrew Labor"

In December 1920, in Haifa, the two labor Zionist parties of the day, Ahdut HaAvoda and Hapoel Hatzair, joined forces to found the General Federation of Hebrew Workers in the Land of Israel, known by its Hebrew short name, the Histadrut [cite:wiki_histadrut]. It began small. Membership stood at about 4,400 in 1920 and about 8,400 in 1922, in a country where, by a Zionist historian's estimate, there were roughly 50,000 Arab workers [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss]. Those workers were outside the federation by rule, not by accident: from its founding, membership was restricted to Jews [cite:wiki_histadrut].

Labor Zionism is often remembered as a socialist workers' movement, committed to class solidarity across national lines. The Histadrut, however, was not a union of the country's workers. It was a union of one community's workers, and much of its work in the 1920s and 1930s was to build that community's separate economy. The question this section answers is how a trade union came to be, in practice, a government-in-waiting.

The decisive figure was David Ben-Gurion, elected secretary of the federation at the end of 1921 [cite:wiki_histadrut]. In the account of the historian Zeev Sternhell, Ben-Gurion turned the Histadrut into a national instrument for realizing Zionism rather than a conventional class union [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Its campaign in the labor market went by the name "Hebrew labor," avoda ivrit. The Histadrut pressed Jewish employers, especially citrus growers and builders, to hire only Jewish workers [cite:wiki_histadrut]. The demand was made of Jewish employers; the workers it was meant to exclude were Arab. This was separation in the labor market long before there was any separation in law.

The federation did not stop at organizing workers. From December 1920 it also took responsibility for defence, and the Haganah was placed under its direct control; through the 1920s the Haganah had only "a few hundred" volunteer members [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. How that militia grew after 1929, and how part of it broke away, is told in [scene:s07e2|the armed line].

The Histadrut also owned the places where its members worked. It built the construction and public-works company Solel Boneh. In association with the World Zionist Organization it founded the Workers' Bank, Bank Hapoalim. It ran the Kupat Holim health fund, on which many Israelis later depended for their health insurance, and it gathered industrial holdings that later became Koor Industries [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Read one way, a union that ran a bank, a building contractor, a health system and its own militia was a parallel economy with its own means of protection. The scale it eventually reached shows what this structure made possible: by 1989 the Histadrut employed about 280,000 workers [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss].

Land was the other half of the same design. The Jewish National Fund held land that could not be resold to non-Jews and leased it on terms requiring Jewish labor; that clause is set out in [scene:rd3_land|the land section]. Taken together, the land fund and the labor federation meant that the Yishuv had separate economic institutions decades before 1948: land that stayed in Jewish hands, a labor market organized for Jewish workers, its own bank and its own health system.

How to read this is disputed, and both readings deserve their strongest form. The movement described itself as socialist. Pinhas Lavon, the Histadrut's secretary-general in 1960, described its founders as young people seeking work in a country "which rejected its inhabitants." On this reading the Histadrut was what young immigrants built in order to find work at all, and its enterprises were a means of employing them.

The critical reading starts from the same facts. On Sternhell's account, under Ben-Gurion the Histadrut became a national instrument for realizing Zionism rather than a conventional union; its socialism served the national project, not the other way round [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Critics including the BDS movement go further, describing the pre-state Histadrut as a national-exclusivist rather than a socialist body, and "Hebrew labor" as national exclusivism in place of worker solidarity [cite:bds_histadrut_critique]. That is their characterization; it is not a finding of this book. What is not in dispute is the membership rule. A labor federation that excluded the majority of the country's workers from the day of its founding cannot be described, on the record, as practicing class solidarity across national lines, whatever its members believed about themselves.

The Histadrut was also a school for government. Ben-Gurion went on to chair the Jewish Agency from 1935 and became prime minister in 1948. He was followed by two Histadrut veterans, Levi Eshkol and Golda Meir [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss]. The membership rule outlasted independence. Arab workers were admitted to full membership only in 1959, eleven years after the state was founded. By 1983 about 170,000 of the federation's 1.6 million members were Arab [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss].

The record leaves some questions open. The sources give no year-by-year membership series between 1922 and the 1950s, so the federation's growth through the period of this chapter cannot be traced here. Nor do they measure how far "Hebrew labor" actually succeeded: how many Jewish employers complied, how many Arab workers lost work in Jewish-owned groves and building sites, or what the policy cost employers in wages. The campaign is documented; its economic effect, in numbers, is not.
