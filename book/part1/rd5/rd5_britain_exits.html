Britain Lets Go, February–September 1947

While Washington argued over the President's motives, London was deciding to give the problem up. In the space of eight months in 1947, Britain went from governing Palestine to announcing that it would leave. The steps were formal and dated: a Cabinet decision in February, a letter to the United Nations in April, and a statement to the General Assembly's Ad Hoc Committee in September. Between them came hangings, kidnappings, riots in British cities and a refugee ship turned back to Europe. The question historians still argue over is how these events related to one another, and in particular how far the Jewish armed campaign drove Britain out.

By the start of 1947 the policy that Ernest Bevin had staked his name on in November 1945 had run out of road; that earlier course, from the Anglo-American Committee to the London conference, is traced in [scene:rd5_bevin|Bevin and Palestine, 1945–1946]. On 14 February 1947 Bevin told the Arab delegates in London that the Cabinet had agreed the question should go to the United Nations without British recommendations, "inasmuch as plan offered by British Delegation had met with no acceptance from either Arabs or Jews". Britain, he said, would present the matter "as impartially as possible" [cite:rs_rd5_frus_gallman_1947]. The words survive in a telegram from the American embassy in London to the Secretary of State.

Four days later, on 18 February 1947, Bevin explained the decision in the House of Commons. In the text reproduced by the Center for Online Judaic Studies, which gives no archival citation, he described "an irreconcilable conflict of principles". For the Jews, the essential principle was a sovereign Jewish state. For the Arabs, it was to resist Jewish sovereignty anywhere in Palestine. He called the Mandate "unworkable in practice", and he put the country's population at about 1,200,000 Arabs and 600,000 Jews [cite:rs_rd5_cojs_bevin_1947]. Because that text could not be checked against Hansard, its wording should be read as reported rather than verified. The historian Raphael Langham adds that in the Commons Bevin blamed the Jews and the United States for the failure [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit].

The formal handover followed. On 2 April 1947 Sir Alexander Cadogan, for the United Kingdom, asked the United Nations to "place the question of Palestine on the Agenda of the General Assembly at its next regular Annual Session", and to "summon, as soon as possible, a special Session" to constitute a special committee [cite:rs_rd5_un_a286]. The letter was circulated as document A/286, dated 3 April 1947. The mechanism matters. Britain did not surrender the Mandate in April. It asked the Assembly to take up the question and to set up a committee, while it went on governing. The committee that resulted, UNSCOP, spent the summer in Palestine and the region; its work is told in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair].

Inside Palestine the violence that ran alongside the diplomacy moved in a cycle of death sentences and reprisals. The dates below come from a single encyclopedia entry and are given here as reported, without the casualty and riot figures that the same source carries but which could not be confirmed. On 16 April 1947 Britain executed four members of the Irgun: Dov Gruner, Yehiel Dresner, Mordechai Alkahi and Eliezer Kashani. On 4 May the Irgun broke into Acre prison. Three of the attackers who were captured, Avshalom Haviv, Yaakov Weiss and Meir Nakar, were sentenced to death and hanged on 29 July 1947 [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_sergeants].

By then the Irgun held two British hostages. On the night of 11 to 12 July 1947 it kidnapped two Intelligence Corps sergeants, Clifford Martin and Mervyn Paice, in Netanya. On 30 July, the day after the Acre men were hanged, it hanged the sergeants, and booby-trapped their bodies [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_sergeants]. The killings provoked a reaction in Britain itself. From 1 August 1947 anti-Jewish rioting broke out, beginning in Liverpool and spreading to other cities [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_sergeants]. It should be named for what it was: antisemitic violence in British cities, aimed at Jews in Britain in response to killings carried out by the Irgun in Palestine.

The same month brought an episode that, in the Holocaust Memorial Museum's account, helped shift diplomatic sympathy. In July 1947 a ship formerly called the President Warfield, renamed Exodus 1947, sailed from Sète in France with more than 4,500 Jewish passengers, most of them displaced persons and Holocaust survivors [cite:rs_rd5_ushmm_exodus] [cite:rs_rd5_yv_july1947]. It was one vessel in the clandestine immigration fleet whose wider history is told in [scene:ab_s01|Aliyah Bet]. When the Royal Navy boarded it, three Jews were killed, one crew member and two passengers according to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, and dozens were wounded [cite:rs_rd5_ushmm_exodus] [cite:rs_rd5_yv_july1947].

The two institutions that record the boarding do not agree on its date. Yad Vashem states that the ship was attacked on 17 July in international waters, that three people were killed, and that it reached Haifa on 18 July [cite:rs_rd5_yv_july1947]. The Holocaust Memorial Museum dates the clash to 18 July 1947 and specifies one crew member and two passengers among the dead [cite:rs_rd5_ushmm_exodus]. Neither source explains the difference, and it is left standing here.

What Britain did next made the ship a symbol. The passengers were moved onto three navy transports and taken back to Port-de-Bouc in France. France declined to remove them by force. After a hunger strike of 24 days and about three weeks in the summer heat, they were carried to Hamburg and interned in camps in the British zone of Germany [cite:rs_rd5_ushmm_exodus]. Langham records that Bevin insisted the passengers be returned to their port of embarkation, and suggests that prejudice may have played a part [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit]. That is Langham's suggestion, and he frames it as a possibility rather than a finding. Yad Vashem states that the voyage was meant to draw the attention of the UNSCOP members then working in the country; the Holocaust Memorial Museum says the "public embarrassment for Britain" helped shift diplomatic sympathy [cite:rs_rd5_yv_july1947] [cite:rs_rd5_ushmm_exodus].

The decision to leave outright, rather than to hand over a question for advice, was announced at the United Nations. On 26 September 1947 Arthur Creech Jones, the Colonial Secretary, told the Ad Hoc Committee that "in the absence of a settlement" Britain had to plan for "an early withdrawal of British forces and of the British administration". Britain, he said, "was not prepared to undertake the task of imposing a policy in Palestine by force of arms" [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_creechjones]. That sentence is the hinge of the whole year. The Assembly could recommend what it liked; Britain would not enforce it. When the Assembly voted on partition on 29 November 1947, Britain abstained [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit].

There was also a bill. The funding ledger compiled for this library puts the cost of the forces in Palestine in 1945–47 at about £100 million, with some 100,000 troops deployed and about 750 British killed over the period; in today's money the cost is roughly $6.7 billion. The ledger's sources are listed with the panel below, and the full account of Britain's bill appears in the next stage of this book. The panel below sets it beside the other large sums of the pre-state years, for scale rather than as a total. Britain's military bill for 1945–47 is the second-largest item on it, behind only the Jewish capital invested in Palestine between 1921 and 1937, and well above the United Jewish Appeal's wartime giving of 1939–45. The sources used here do not say how much weight the Cabinet gave to that cost when it decided to leave; they show only that the cost was being paid.

How much did the Jewish insurgency, and the sergeants affair in particular, cause the withdrawal? Two positions have serious support. On one view the armed campaign was decisive or close to it. The historian J. Bowyer Bell called the sergeants affair the "straw that broke the Mandate's back", and Creech Jones himself, writing in 1961, listed the sergeants affair as one of four major factors behind the Cabinet's September 1947 decision to evacuate [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_sergeants]. The strength of this case is the testimony of the minister who announced the withdrawal, and the timing: the sergeants were hanged on 30 July, riots broke out in Britain on 1 August, and the withdrawal was announced eight weeks later.

On the other view the insurgency was one factor among several. Britain's own stated reason, in February, was the irreconcilable Arab and Jewish positions and the failure of its own plan; the Cabinet had agreed to hand the question to the United Nations before the executions, the Acre break-in, the kidnapping or the Exodus [cite:rs_rd5_frus_gallman_1947]. Langham points to strategic considerations and Britain's relations with the Arab states [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit]. The strength of this case is the sequence of documents: the decision to give up the problem predates the events of the summer. The two readings are not wholly opposed. February's decision referred the question; September's decision ended the Mandate; the events between may explain why the second followed the first so fast, even if they did not cause the first.

Britain's departure did not end Bevin's part in the story. In May 1948 he told the Commons, "there are, after all, no Arabs in the House", and Britain recognized Israel only in January 1949, with Bevin's acceptance, in Langham's word, "grudging" [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit].

Several things remain unsettled on this record. The Commons text of 18 February 1947 is known here only through a reproduction without archival citation. The Cabinet's own minutes for February and September 1947 are not among the sources used, so the weight the ministers themselves gave to cost, to the insurgency, to American pressure or to Arab opinion cannot be measured from them. Creech Jones's four factors are known from a later account, written in 1961, and only one of the four is named in the source. The exact date of the Exodus boarding differs between two reputable institutions. And whether prejudice shaped Bevin's handling of the Exodus is, in the one historian's account that raises it, a possibility and not a demonstrated fact. What the documents do show is the order of events: Britain referred the question because its plan had failed with both sides, endured a summer of executions, reprisals and a refugee ship that embarrassed it before the world, and then announced that it would not stay to enforce whatever the world decided.
