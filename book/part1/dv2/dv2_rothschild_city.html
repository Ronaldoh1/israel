Inside the City's Institutions: Emancipation, the Gold Fix and the Mansion House

The Rothschild name carries more weight in the popular story of London's power than any other, and much of that weight is legend. The best-known episode, Nathan Rothschild racing news of Waterloo to the stock exchange, traces back to a pamphlet published in 1846 to stir hostility toward Jews; [scene:bc_s02|the London house's record] sets out what the documents show instead. That record is one of loans to states, a supply of coin to the Bank of England in the panic of 1825, and the £4 million that bought Britain the Khedive of Egypt's Suez Canal shares in 1875. [cite:wiki_dairnvaell_satan_pamphlet] [cite:jwatte_rothschild_myths_record] [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023] [cite:dv2_wiki_lionel] This section follows a different thread: how a Jewish banking family, and Jewish citizens of the City more broadly, entered the City's own institutions, and how those institutions in turn became a public stage for Jewish causes in the century before Zionism.

The oath. Until the 1830s, the City's offices and Parliament's seats were closed to Jews by a religious test. Office-holders had to swear an oath "upon the true faith of a Christian." The first breach came inside the City. In 1835 David Salomons was elected one of the City's two Sheriffs. He could not take the Christian oath, and Parliament passed the Sheriffs' Declaration Act later that year so that he could serve, making him the first Jewish Sheriff of the City of London. [cite:dv2_wiki_salomons] [cite:dv2_wiki_emancipation] In December 1835 he was elected an Alderman, and the same oath disqualified him. He was elected again in 1847, after the Religious Opinions Relief Act amended the oath, and this time he took his place. The Aldermen chose him as Lord Mayor, and he served from 10 November 1855 to 8 November 1856, the first Jewish Lord Mayor of London. [cite:dv2_wiki_salomons]

Salomons was also a founder of the London and Westminster Bank, and in 1851 he tested the same barrier in Parliament. Elected member for Greenwich, he took his seat without the Christian words of the oath, voted three times, was removed by the Serjeant-at-Arms and was fined £500. [cite:dv2_wiki_salomons] The City's own electorate was making the same point at the same time. In 1847 its voters returned Lionel de Rothschild, head of the London house, as one of the four members of Parliament for the City of London. He could not take his seat. He was returned again and again, and in 1849 he resigned and stood once more at a by-election to strengthen his claim. In 1850 he entered the chamber and was allowed to swear on the Hebrew Bible, but was required to leave when he left out the words "upon the true faith of a Christian." [cite:dv2_wiki_lionel] [cite:dv2_wiki_emancipation]

The Jews Relief Act of 1858 broke the deadlock by allowing each House to decide its own oath. On 26 July 1858 Lionel de Rothschild took the oath with his head covered, said "so help me, Jehovah," and took his seat as the first Jewish member of Parliament, sitting for the City of London. [cite:dv2_wiki_lionel] [cite:dv2_wiki_emancipation] In 1885 his son Nathaniel became Lord Rothschild, the first Jewish peer. [cite:dv2_wiki_emancipation]

This history matters to the question of power for a reason that runs against the legend. The barrier these men faced was a law written to exclude Jews from public life, and its removal came from a political campaign fought in the open, through elections the City's voters kept repeating. The documents show a family and a community gaining ordinary civic rights late and against resistance. They do not show a family that already controlled the institutions it was trying to enter.

The gold fix. The Rothschild house's most visible place in the City's machinery came later, in the market for gold. On 12 September 1919 the first London Gold Fixing was held at N M Rothschild & Sons' offices at New Court. Representatives of the five members of the London gold market, N M Rothschild & Sons, Mocatta & Goldsmid, Pixley & Abell, Samuel Montagu & Co. and Sharps Wilkins, each sat at a desk with a telephone and a small Union Jack flag. By tradition the Rothschild representative sat at the center as chairman. [cite:dv2_ra_goldfix] For eighty-five years the London gold price was fixed in a Rothschild office. In 2004 the firm decided to withdraw from the fixing; its last participation was on 4 May 2004. [cite:dv2_ra_goldfix]

The fixing was a real position of influence, and the family's own archive describes it. It was also a shared one: five firms took part, and the house left the arrangement by its own decision. Read in isolation, "the Rothschilds set the price of gold" sounds like command. Read with the record, it describes the chairmanship of a market committee.

The Mansion House. The City's institutions also gave Jewish causes a public platform, and the Lord Mayor's residence was its usual setting. On 3 July 1840 a meeting summoned by the Lord Mayor at the Mansion House protested the ritual-murder accusation against the Jews of Damascus, in which two of the accused had died under torture, and demanded the release of those still held. The Lord Mayor sent the meeting's resolutions to the foreign ambassadors in London. [cite:dv2_je_mansion_house] [cite:rs_ch01_ej_damascus] The Damascus affair itself, and the mission of Moses Montefiore and Adolphe Crémieux that ended it, are told in [scene:ch01_hatred|the history of the old hatred]. In 1882, after a series of articles by Joseph Jacobs on the persecution of Jews in the Russian Empire, the Lord Mayor convened another Mansion House meeting; the fund it launched raised £108,000 for the persecuted Jews of Russia. On 10 December 1890 a meeting at Guildhall deplored "the renewed sufferings of the Jews in Russia" and sent a memorandum asking the tsar to grant his Jewish subjects equality. The tsar refused to receive it, and it came back through the Foreign Office. About £100,000 was collected, and the committee that managed it became the Russo-Jewish Committee. [cite:dv2_je_mansion_house]

These were humanitarian meetings, not Zionist ones. Political Zionism did not yet exist in 1840 or 1882, and nothing in the record presents the meetings as aimed at a Jewish state. Their significance for this volume is the precedent they set. By the 1880s the City's highest office had become a recognized place from which British public opinion spoke about the treatment of Jews abroad, and the Lord Mayor's name carried that opinion to foreign governments. When the Zionist movement arrived in London a generation later, it found a city whose institutions already had a habit of engagement with Jewish questions, and a banking community, Jewish and Gentile, long accustomed to financing causes overseas.

The gaps should be stated. The sources used here do not name the Lord Mayors who chaired the meetings of 1840 and 1882, and they do not give the date in 1882 on which the Mansion House meeting was held. They describe the gold fixing's form and its Rothschild chairmanship, not how much influence the chairman had over the price on any given day. And they do not connect any of the episodes in this section to the later decisions of the British government on Palestine, which were taken elsewhere, by other people, for reasons set out later in this part.
