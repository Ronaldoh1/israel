The American Machine: Hadassah, Brandeis and the ZOA, 1912–1918

While Filastin was finding readers in the villages of the Jaffa district, a different public was being organized in the United States. A familiar assumption holds that American support for Zionism became organized and significant only after 1948, or at the earliest after the Holocaust. The institutional record places its foundations three decades earlier. By 1918 the movement in the United States had a national women's organization raising money for medical work in Palestine, a Justice of the Supreme Court as its most influential advocate with direct access to the President, and a merged national political organization reporting more than 147,000 members.[cite:zoa_wiki] [cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] None of this was built in Palestine. It was domestic American institution-building, carried out years before there was a British Mandate to build under.

The first piece was built by women. On 24 February 1912 Henrietta Szold founded Hadassah in New York. Sources give the number of founding members variously, from 38 to 122; on any count it was a meeting of fewer than 130 people.[cite:ushmm_youth_aliyah] The organization moved quickly from meeting to practice. In 1913 it sent the movement's first two nurses to Palestine, to provide maternity care and to treat trachoma.[cite:ushmm_youth_aliyah]

Hadassah's significance for the architecture of the movement lies in the channel it created as much as in the clinics it funded. Most of the financial and political machinery traced elsewhere in this stage, the Jewish Colonial Trust, the Jewish National Fund and, from 1918, the ZOA's own fundraising, was built and run by men. Hadassah was a distinct American fundraising channel, financed specifically by American Jewish women and directed to medical and settlement infrastructure in Palestine. It ran alongside the male-led institutions rather than through them.[cite:hadassah_wiki] It did not operate in isolation, however: in 1918 Hadassah became one of the founding member organizations of the Zionist Organization of America, so the two channels were joined institutionally while remaining distinct in organization and purpose.[cite:zoa_wiki] [cite:hadassah_wiki]

Szold's own arc reached far beyond this period. She later directed Youth Aliyah, founded in 1933, which the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum credits with bringing more than 14,000 unaccompanied children to Palestine and Britain between 1933 and 1945.[cite:ushmm_youth_aliyah] That work belongs to a later volume. It is recorded here because it shows the scale an organization founded at a small New York meeting in 1912 would eventually reach, and with the caution that a child-rescue and resettlement operation of that size involved real hardships for the children, a fuller story than a founding-to-scale summary can tell.

The second piece was a single convert. In 1914 Louis Brandeis, who would join the United States Supreme Court in 1916, moved into active Zionist leadership after a meeting with Jacob de Haas, who had been Herzl's secretary.[cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] The line from Herzl to Brandeis ran through one of Herzl's own staff. From 1914 Brandeis led the American movement, and he remained its leader until 1921.

What Brandeis brought was access. He worked with the Reverend William Blackstone on the Blackstone Memorial of 1916 to influence President Wilson. In 1917 he met Balfour, and during the drafting of the Balfour Declaration he worked as a conduit between the British government and the President.[cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] His direct line to Wilson in those months is documented by multiple historians; the channel itself is not in dispute.[cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] How the Declaration was drafted, and what Brandeis's part in it was, is told with [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration].

How much that access mattered is a separate question, and the answer offered most often is an assessment rather than a finding. Nahum Goldmann later argued that without Brandeis "the Balfour Declaration would probably never have been issued."[cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] That is one historian's judgment about a counterfactual. It may be right, but no document can prove what Britain would have done without him, and it should be read as an argument, not as settled fact.

Why Brandeis made the turn is a third question, and it remains open. His personal motives for converting to Zionist leadership are contested among historians. Some argue that the conversion was partly career-strategic, a reading that outlets such as Mondoweiss have cited in critical treatments of his role.[cite:brandeis_zionism_wiki] No primary source in the record this book has definitively settles his motivation. The distinction matters for how the American story is told: that a Supreme Court Justice had direct access to a sitting President is a documented, checkable fact about influence; why he chose to spend that access on this cause is a matter of interpretation on which historians can reasonably differ.

The third piece was consolidation. In 1918 the Federation of American Zionists, Young Judaea, Hadassah and other bodies merged into the Zionist Organization of America. Brandeis became honorary president and Julian W. Mack president. The new organization reported 147,551 members in 1918.[cite:zoa_wiki]

The size of the merged organization is the figure that the summary of the movement's whole build carries for these years: in the step-by-step account of how the state was built, 1912–18 is "the American machine," made up of Hadassah, Justice Brandeis and the ZOA at 147,000.[cite:zoa_wiki] That compressed row follows directly from the documents above, and it sits between the bank and land fund of 1899–1902 and the promise and endorsement of 1917–22. Its placement makes a point the familiar account misses. The American institutions were not a response to the Balfour Declaration; they were built before it, and one of their leaders was working the channel to the President while it was drafted.

The unity of 1918 did not last. Brandeis and his adherents favored private economic investment over the Zionist Organization's model of national fundraising, and at the Cleveland convention of 1921 they split away. Louis Lipsky then became president of the ZOA.[cite:zoa_wiki] The movement's most famous American member thus lost the argument over what kind of institution it should be within three years of its founding; the dispute and its consequences belong to the next stage.

Several things remain unsettled. The number of Hadassah's founders depends on which source is counted, from 38 to 122. Brandeis's motives remain contested, and Goldmann's claim that the Declaration would probably not have been issued without him cannot be tested. The record establishes that the 1921 split concerned private investment against national fundraising, but not every argument made on either side.
