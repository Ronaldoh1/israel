By the autumn of 1962 the Kennedy administration had committed itself to two things in the Middle East at once. It had offered Israel the Hawk anti-aircraft missile, the first American military aid to Israel, extended as loans, and in late September it had sent American scientists on a brief second visit to the reactor at Dimona, which the State Department reported to its posts as finding "no evidence" of preparation for weapons. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d87] It had also, since the spring of 1961, been courting Gamal Abdel Nasser. Kennedy had written to six Arab heads of state offering help on the refugee question "on the basis of the principle of repatriation or compensation," and American food aid to Egypt under PL-480 had reached $180.3 million in fiscal 1962. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_jfk_arab_letter] [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d249] On 8 October 1962 a multi-year food agreement with the United Arab Republic was signed, with Kennedy asking for "minimum publicity." [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70] Neither line of policy had yet been tested against the other, and the question of what the United States would require of Israel in return for its support had not been asked in writing.

This stage follows the months in which it was asked. Between November 1962 and November 1963 Washington confronted Israel's government over Dimona and confronted the American body that received the Jewish Agency's money over the Foreign Agents Registration Act, while it tried to hold Cairo and Riyadh on opposite sides of a war in Yemen. The question the stage answers is what those confrontations settled, and what they left in place, by the time the President was killed in Dallas on 22 November 1963. That date ends the stage as a fact of timing. The record reached for this book connects it to neither dispute as a cause.

The actors are named states, offices and organizations. In Washington stood the President; his brother Robert F. Kennedy, whose Justice Department issued the order of 21 November 1962 to the American Zionist Council; Robert Komer of the National Security Council staff; the intelligence analysts who wrote NIE 30-63 and Sherman Kent's memorandum of March 1963; Ambassador Barbour in Tel Aviv; and Senator J. William Fulbright, whose Foreign Relations Committee examined the agents of foreign principals in thirteen parts. [cite:israellobby_org_fara_doj_order] [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] In Israel stood David Ben-Gurion, Shimon Peres and Golda Meir; Isser Harel, head of the Mossad until his forced resignation in 1963; and Levi Eshkol, who succeeded Ben-Gurion in 1963. [cite:rs_cw5_enc_harel] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] Among American organizations stood the American Zionist Council, the Jewish Agency's American Section, already a registered agent, Isaiah L. Kenen's committee, and the American Council for Judaism, which, according to Grant F. Smith, welcomed the order. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] In the region stood Nasser, Crown Prince Faisal of Saudi Arabia, the Ba'athist officers who seized Baghdad and Damascus in February and March 1963, and Ahmad Shukairy, chosen in 1963 as Palestine's representative at the Arab League, who spoke at the United Nations in November. [cite:rs_cw5_riedel_yemen] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] [cite:rs_cw5_palquest_shuqayri]

The sections open with [scene:cw5_nasser_outreach|Kennedy's wager on Cairo] and the war in Yemen that tested it. They then take up the order to the American Zionist Council and the question of which body was which, the terms Kennedy set with Meir at Palm Beach, and the estimates that turned Washington's view of Dimona from reassurance to alarm. The Senate inquiry is set out as a whole before its part on the Jewish Agency. Between them come the German scientists in Egypt and the fall of Harel, the two Ba'athist coups and the short-lived Cairo union, and [scene:s11b|the battle of the letters] between Kennedy and Ben-Gurion, which ended with a letter that was never delivered and a resignation. Eshkol's answer, the dissent of the American Council for Judaism and Shukairy's speech at the United Nations follow, and the stage closes with [scene:s11c|22 November 1963], told as a question of timing.
