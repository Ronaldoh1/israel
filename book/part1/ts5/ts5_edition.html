The Edition: Oxford, 1909, 1917 and 1967

By 1909 the system described in Part Three already had its conferences, its creed and its Bible institutes ([scene:ts3_niagara|Part Three]). What it lacked was a single book that a reader could carry into church and open at any verse. The Scofield Reference Bible supplied that book. It first appeared in 1909 under the imprint of Oxford University Press, and Scofield revised it in 1917. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] Oxford published both, and a much-revised third edition in 1967. [cite:ts5_dunlap] This section sets out how the edition came to Oxford, what kind of object it was, and how widely it sold. It also tests two figures repeated in earlier accounts, including this library's own.

How the book came to Oxford

The route to Oxford ran through the same Brethren circles that paid for the work (see [scene:ts5_funding|who paid]). According to a history by David Dunlap, which draws on Arno Gaebelein's 1943 account and Trumbull's 1920 biography, Henry Frowde, the Oxford publisher, was himself in Brethren fellowship and advised Scofield that Oxford was the only house able to handle the work. [cite:ts5_dunlap] A passage quoted by the fact-checking site Check Your Fact adds that Frowde coordinated publication with John Armstrong in New York, head of Oxford's American branch. [cite:ts4_cyf] The Library of Congress copy of the 1917 edition carries the imprint "New York, Oxford University Press, American Branch; London, H. Milford". [cite:ts5_ia_1917] The book was therefore an Oxford product in the ordinary commercial sense: a university press publishing, printing and selling a study Bible prepared by an outside author. What Oxford's governing Delegates discussed about it, if anything, has not been found in any source used for this volume, and nothing in the record shows that the press reviewed or endorsed Scofield's theology.

Notes on the page

The book's power lay in its layout. Scofield's Bible carried a running commentary set on the same page as the King James text: headings, references linking one theme across both Testaments, and notes that told the reader what a passage meant and where it fitted in a scheme of seven dispensations. [cite:ts5_ia_1917] [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] A reader did not have to buy a separate commentary or attend a prophecy conference to meet the system. It was printed beside the verse, in the same binding, under the name of a university press. The scheme itself is examined in the next section ([scene:ts5_notes|what the notes say]).

The title page added a further mark of authority: a list of seven consulting editors, Henry G. Weston, James M. Gray, W. J. Erdman, A. T. Pierson, W. G. Moorehead, Elmore Harris and Arno C. Gaebelein, all known names in the premillennial conference movement. The historian Ernest Sandeen wrote in 1970 that their role "has been the subject of some debate" and that "Apparently Scofield only meant to acknowledge their assistance", though some suspected he wanted to win support from both wings of the millenarian movement. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] The notes were Scofield's.

The 1917 revision, and a claim that fails

Scofield revised the work in 1917. The changes most noted by historians concern chronology: the 1917 edition attempts to date the events of the Bible and printed dates derived from Archbishop Ussher, with creation at 4004 BC, alongside a note favouring the "gap theory" of Genesis 1, which set off a debate among fundamentalists. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] The 1917 notes are now in the public domain in the United States, and the 1917 edition has remained the best-selling Scofield edition in the United Kingdom and Ireland. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb]

Earlier accounts in this library, following the simulation, said the 1917 edition incorporated "references to the just-issued Balfour Declaration" ([scene:s05|Road to 1948]). That claim has no source. The reference work cited for it does not mention Balfour or Zionism in connection with the revision. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] The 1917 notes examined for this part, on Genesis 12 and 15, Deuteronomy 30, Ezekiel 38 and Romans 11, make no reference to the declaration and still describe Israel as "now in the third dispersion", awaiting restoration at Christ's return. [cite:ts1_sac_gen15] The timing also tells against it: the declaration is dated 2 November 1917 and a revision prepared during that year would have had at most weeks to absorb it. The claim is dropped here as unsupported. The declaration itself, and the Christian restorationists around it, are the subject of [scene:ts6_balfour|Part Six].

How many copies

The book sold steadily. Gaebelein recorded in 1943 that sales had exceeded two million copies by the end of the Second World War, and Dunlap, citing the same source, gives Oxford's figure of 1,925,000 copies published within thirty years of first issue. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] [cite:ts5_dunlap] By 1967 Christianity Today reported that the original had "sold more copies than any other title issued by that press in the States" and spoke of "several million copies in print". [cite:ts5_ct_1967] Book 1 of Ron's earlier series said Oxford had published "tens of millions"; no source for that figure has been found, and it is not used here. The documented figure, about two million by 1945, is large for a study Bible with dense notes, and it sold through churches, Bible institutes and correspondence courses rather than general bookshops (see [scene:ts7_schools|Part Seven]).

The New Scofield, 1967

Fifty years after Scofield's revision, Oxford published a new edition prepared by a committee of nine: E. Schuyler English, Frank E. Gaebelein, William Culbertson, Charles L. Feinberg, Allan A. MacRae, Clarence E. Mason, Alva J. McClain, Wilbur M. Smith and John F. Walvoord. [cite:ts5_ct_1967] Christianity Today's review of 14 April 1967 listed the changes. Hundreds of archaic King James words were replaced and marked; Ussher's dates were dropped, so no dates appear before about 2000 BC; book introductions were rewritten. The seven dispensations were kept, with the sixth renamed from "Grace" to "Church"; the distinction between Israel and the Church was maintained; and the pre-tribulational, premillennial scheme was unchanged. A note that seemed to teach salvation by works in some dispensations was removed, and a new note rejected the "Anglo-Israel" theory that the British were the lost tribes. [cite:ts5_ct_1967] Other notes were softened: according to Campbell's 2010 study, as summarised in Wikipedia, the note on John 1:17 was rewritten so that it seemed to say the opposite of Scofield's original. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb]

Later Oxford editions, including the Scofield Study Bible III of the early 2000s, moved the 1967 changes to the King James text into the margin. [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] The book has therefore existed in several forms. Statements about "what the Scofield Bible says" need a date: the passages quoted in the next section are from the 1917 text, the version most reprinted and the one now in the public domain.
