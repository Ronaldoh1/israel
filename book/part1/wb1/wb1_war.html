The War in the Hills

A quiet understanding. Before the fighting began, the ruler whose army would take the hill country had already met the Zionist leadership. In early November 1947, at Naharayim on the Jordan, King Abdullah of Transjordan told Golda Meyerson (later Meir) that he intended to annex the Arab areas of Palestine; she replied that the Zionist leaders would not oppose this if there were no clashes between Jewish and Transjordanian forces. On 10 May 1948 Meyerson crossed to Amman in disguise; Abdullah said he was now "one of five," bound to the other Arab states, and asked, "why don't you wait a few years?", urging her to drop the demand for free immigration. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_golda]

Plan D. Fighting between the communities followed the partition vote. On 10 March 1948 the Haganah finalized Plan D, whose stated objective was "to gain control of the areas of the Hebrew state and defend its borders," including Jewish settlements outside them, and which, for enemy villages used as bases, provided for "destruction of villages," especially those "difficult to control continuously," and, where resistance was met, for the population to be "expelled outside the borders of the state"; "in the absence of resistance," garrison troops were to occupy the village. The Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi argues that it created "the military fait accompli upon which the state of Israel was to be based." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Benny Morris concluded that there was no explicit, general Israeli decision to expel. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] On 9 April, at Deir Yassin, a village outside Jerusalem, fighters of the Irgun, the underground led by Menachem Begin, and of Lehi, with Haganah and Palmach support, killed about 100 to 110 villagers, by the estimates of Benny Morris and a Birzeit University study, and expelled the rest. [cite:wb_wiki_deiryassin] Contemporary accounts put the dead at 254; a letter to the New York Times signed by Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and others repeated the figure then current, "240 men, women, and children," and described the village as "not a military objective." [cite:rs_rd5_einstein_nyt]

The Arab Legion. When the Arab armies entered on 15 May, Transjordan's Arab Legion, according to the US State Department's historians, fought only in areas allotted to the Arab state or to the international zone of Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] It held the hill country and the road to Jerusalem at Latrun, where it repelled three Israeli frontal attacks between 25 May and 9 June, and on 28 May took the Jewish Quarter of Jerusalem's Old City. Iraqi forces held the triangle of Jenin, Nablus and Tulkarm in the north. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] On 13 May, the day before the Mandate ended, the Arab Legion and local villagers overran Kfar Etzion, in the Gush Etzion bloc south of Bethlehem; about 127 defenders died, some of them killed after surrendering, and the bloc's other three settlements surrendered the next day, their people taken to Jordan as prisoners. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_cw] [cite:wb_wiki_kfaretzion]

Lydda and Ramle. The towns of Lydda and Ramle lay inside the planned Arab state, on the plain below the hills. In July, Operation Dani took them. On 12 July, after Arab Legion armoured cars entered Lydda and a brief firefight followed, Israeli troops opened fire in the town; Benny Morris, citing the Palmach's own history, puts the dead at about 250, and Palestinian accounts give between 80 and 176 killed at the Dahmash mosque and more than 400 in the town. [cite:wb_wiki_lydda] [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] Yitzhak Rabin, who signed the expulsion order, gave two accounts of how it was decided: in a 1977 interview, that Ben-Gurion, asked by Yigal Allon about the inhabitants, waved his hand and said "Expel them"; in his memoir manuscript, that Ben-Gurion waved his hand without speaking. Allon denied asking, and the historian Yoav Gelber attributes the decision to Allon. Israeli censors removed the episode from Rabin's memoir. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war] [cite:wb_wiki_lydda] The towns held 50,000 to 70,000 people. Palestinian sources estimate that about 70,000 were forced out on 13 July and that close to 1,000 died in the towns and on the march east, toward the hills held by the Arab Legion; Morris puts the deaths on the march at "a handful and perhaps dozens." [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] [cite:wb_wiki_lydda]
