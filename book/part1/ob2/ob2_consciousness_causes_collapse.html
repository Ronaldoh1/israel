Consciousness Causes Collapse: The Idea and the Retreat

The idea has a respectable origin. In 1932 the mathematician John von Neumann, setting quantum mechanics on a rigorous footing, showed that the line between the system being measured and the apparatus measuring it could be drawn anywhere along the chain from particle to detector to the observer's eye and brain. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc] Somewhere the chain must end in a definite result. In 1939 Fritz London and Edmond Bauer placed the end in the observer's consciousness. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc]

The name most associated with the view is Eugene Wigner, a Nobel physicist who argued for it in the early 1960s. His thought experiment, now called Wigner's friend, imagined a friend inside a sealed laboratory making a measurement while Wigner waits outside: if collapse is purely physical, the friend's result is not settled for Wigner until he opens the door, and Wigner argued that a being with a mind could not be left in that unsettled state. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc]

The retreat

Wigner did not hold the view to the end. By the late 1970s he had moved away from it, and in 1982 he described it as leading towards solipsism, crediting the work of the German physicist Dieter Zeh on decoherence for changing his mind. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc] The proposal is now, in the words of its encyclopedia entry, largely discarded by modern physicists. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc] A poll of 33 researchers at a 2011 quantum foundations conference found two who held it. [cite:ob_wiki_ccc]

The record here cuts both ways. The idea that the mind completes the measurement was not invented by mystics; it came from the founders of the mathematics, and the gap it was meant to fill has not been closed. But the physicists who proposed it did not prove it, and the most famous of them gave it up. A teaching that cites quantum mechanics as proof that consciousness creates reality is citing a hypothesis that its own champion withdrew.
