Migrant and Palestinian Workers

Both labour markets depend on workers who are not citizens and do not have the rights citizens have. In Israel they are of two kinds: Palestinians from the West Bank and, until October 2023, Gaza, who entered on daily or seasonal permits issued by the military authorities; and foreign workers recruited abroad for set sectors. In the United States the largest group is unauthorised immigrants. The two cases differ in law and in scale, and each is stated on its own terms here; the status of Palestinians under Israeli control generally is the subject of Part Nine. Palestinian workers: permits, then the closure

After 1967 Israel integrated the territories' economy with its own, and from 1967 to 1990 between 35 and 40 per cent of employed Palestinians worked in Israel, mostly in low-paid jobs. In the early 1990s Israel introduced a closure regime under which every worker needed a permit subject to security clearance; the Oslo Accords did not end it. [cite:gz_carnegie_labor] [scene:gz2_economy|The economy after 1967] Permits are issued through the Civil Administration and COGAT, and a permit is tied to a named employer. [cite:wb_wiki_civadmin] [cite:us13_972_workers] Gaza's workers were barred from 2007 to 2014; about 18,500 Gazans held permits on the eve of the war, and all were revoked on 10 October 2023. [cite:gz_carnegie_labor]

The West Bank figures are the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics'. Before the war about 172,000 West Bank Palestinians worked in Israel and the settlements, about 127,000 of them with permits. In the last quarter of 2023 the number fell to about 25,000; by the last quarter of 2025 it had partly recovered to about 51,000, of whom only about 14,000 held permits. West Bank unemployment rose from about 13 per cent in the third quarter of 2023 to about 28 per cent at the end of 2025. In Gaza it rose from about 45 per cent to about 68 per cent by the end of 2024, the last figure the bureau gives. [cite:us13_pcbs_workers2026] Other counts of the pre-war workforce differ. +972 Magazine gives about 150,000 West Bank permit holders and about 50,000 working without permits, and reports that about 8,000 permits for work inside Israel were issued in 2025 and that more than 10,000 Palestinians still work in settlements. [cite:us13_972_workers] The difference turns on whether settlements and unpermitted work are counted; the PCBS survey is the only one of these that is a published statistical series.

Foreign workers: the replacement

Israel has recruited foreign workers since the closures of the 1990s; by 2000 there were about 240,000, documented and undocumented, roughly a tenth of the labour force. [cite:us13_972_workers] The state sets quotas by sector, about 30,000 each in agriculture and construction, with no cap in home care, and visas, typically for five years, are tied to the employer. [cite:us13_972_workers] After October 2023 the government turned to recruitment abroad to fill the jobs Palestinians had held. A government decision in May 2024 raised the quota for foreign workers to 3.3 per cent of the population; by The Media Line's account of figures from the Population and Immigration Authority, almost 61,000 new permits were issued in 2025, bringing the total to 227,044, and the authority still described a shortfall of about 100,000. [cite:us13_tml_piba] The workers' rights group Kav LaOved counts about 270,000 migrant workers in all; planned recruitment of about 65,000 workers from India, Sri Lanka and Uzbekistan was under discussion in 2026. [cite:us13_972_workers]

The law on paper covers them. Israel's Minimum Wage Law applies, by its own terms, to foreign and undocumented workers as to anyone else. [cite:us13_kz_minwagelaw] The record of compliance is weaker. Kav LaOved estimated in 2014 that migrant farm workers received on average about 70 per cent of the wages they were owed, and +972 reports withheld wages, unfit housing and pesticide exposure without protection. [cite:us13_972_workers] Foreign agricultural workers also bore part of the war's toll: Thai workers were among the people killed and taken hostage on 7 October 2023, and at least three migrant workers were killed by Iranian missiles in the 2026 war. [cite:us13_972_workers]

The United States: unauthorised workers

The American counterpart is larger in absolute terms and built on a different legal fact: most of these workers have no permit at all. The Pew Research Center estimated 8.3 million unauthorised immigrants in the American labour force in 2022, just under 5 per cent of all workers, up from 3.6 million in 1995 and level with its earlier peak in 2008 and 2011. [cite:us13_epi_unauth] The Economic Policy Institute argues that without regular status, employers can use the fear of retaliation and deportation to pay them less, and that work authorisation raises wages and standards for the workers concerned. [cite:us13_epi_unauth] Enforcement against them, and the detention and deportation system, are covered in Parts Four and Six. [scene:us6_asylum|Part Six: asylum and the border] [scene:us4_detention|Part Four: detention]

The comparison is uneven in one respect that matters. The unauthorised American worker lives inside the country that sets the rules and can, in principle, be regularised by its legislature; the Palestinian permit worker lives under military rule in territory Israel controls and has no vote in Israel's elections, so the permit regime is set by a state in which the worker has no political voice. Both systems rely on workers whose status depends on someone else's decision; only one also governs where those workers live. [cite:us13_pcbs_workers2026] [cite:gz_carnegie_labor]
