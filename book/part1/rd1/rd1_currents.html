For and Against: Jewish Currents, 1885–1914

The years between the Basel Congress and the First World War are easy to tell as the story of one movement gathering a people behind it. The record shows something less tidy. Jewish opinion on Zionism in this period was plural, organized and argued in public, and several of its currents were founded expressly to oppose the movement or to contest what it should be. The first wave of opposition, in 1897, came from the German rabbis of the Protestrabbiner declaration, from the Bund and from Ahad Ha'am, and is told in [scene:s04|the section on Basel]. This section follows the currents that took shape around it, from a rabbinical platform in Pittsburgh in 1885 to a newspaper declaration in Berlin in 1914. Some stood outside Zionism and rejected it on principle. Others stood inside the Zionist Organization and fought over its program. A third group opposed both the movement and its religious wing.

Reform in America: "no longer a nation." The earliest of these positions predates Herzl. In 1885 the Reform movement in the United States adopted the Pittsburgh Platform, which declared: "We consider ourselves no longer a nation but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine" nor the restoration of the laws of a Jewish state.[cite:rs_rd1_reform_wiki] The position was put again in the year of the First Congress. In 1897 the Central Conference of American Rabbis resolved: "We reaffirm that the object of Judaism is not political nor national, but spiritual." Isaac Mayer Wise described Judaism as "not tied down to a piece of land here or there."[cite:rs_rd1_reform_wiki] These words come to this book through a reference work quoting N. W. Cohen's study of the subject; the original text of the 1897 resolution has not been checked for this volume. The substance is clear enough. For these rabbis the Jews were a religious community whose object was spiritual, and a national program, in Palestine or anywhere, contradicted that self-definition. Their objection was not to a particular piece of land. It was to the idea that Jews were a nation needing one.

Inside the movement: culture and the Democratic Faction. Within the Zionist Organization the argument was over what Zionism should do besides diplomacy. At the Fifth Congress in Basel in 1901, the Congress that also founded the Jewish National Fund, a group known as the Democratic Faction, among them Leo Motzkin, Martin Buber and Chaim Weizmann, pressed for Hebrew culture and for more democracy inside the organization.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] That December the Congress declared cultural work an essential part of Zionism.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] Whether a national movement should take charge of Jewish culture at all was a question on which Zionists themselves divided, and the division soon had an organization of its own.

Mizrachi: religious Zionism, 1902. Three months later, on 4–5 March 1902, a convention in Vilna called by Rabbi Isaac Jacob Reines founded Mizrachi, the religious-Zionist faction within the Zionist Organization.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_mizrachi] Tobias Grill counts 72 delegates at Vilna, 24 of them rabbis.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] The faction grew fast. In its first year it built 210 branches in the Russian Empire, and its first world conference, held at Pressburg on 21–23 August 1904, drew about 100 delegates. Its motto set out the synthesis it stood for: "The Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel."[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_mizrachi] Mizrachi worked inside the Zionist Organization, and its motto put the Torah at the center of the national program.

The arrangement held for nine years and then broke at the point where it had started. When the Tenth Zionist Congress, meeting in Basel in 1911, added cultural work to the organization's formal program, all the Mizrachi delegates walked out. A later Mizrachi conference decided not to leave the Zionist Organization; some leaders who disagreed with that decision broke away.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_mizrachi] The episode shows how the organization actually worked. Program was set by majority vote in the Congress; a faction that lost could protest, walk out, or stay, and Mizrachi as a body chose to stay.

Orthodox opposition: Frankfurt and the Wahnjudenthum pamphlet. Religious Zionism had religious opponents from its first year. In 1902–03 Orthodox leaders of the Free Association, based in Frankfurt, declared Mizrachi's principles incompatible with traditional Judaism. A pamphlet by Raphael Breuer published in 1903 carried the title Nationaljudenthum ein Wahnjudenthum!, which branded national Judaism a delusion.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] Here the objection ran opposite to Reform's. Reform rejected Jewish nationhood in the name of a spiritual religious community; the Frankfurt Orthodox rejected national Judaism as a counterfeit of the traditional faith, and rejected Mizrachi for trying to reconcile the two.

Agudat Israel, 1912. Orthodox opposition acquired an international organization a decade later. Agudat Israel was founded at Kattowitz in late May 1912 as a worldwide association of Torah-observant Jews. Grill describes it as founded largely as a countermeasure to Mizrachi.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] Another account in this library puts the emphasis more broadly: Agudat Israel was founded to strengthen Torah-observant Judaism as a separation from the emerging secular Jewish nationalist movement, which its founders regarded as a danger to Torah-observant Jewish life.[cite:unpacked_neturei_karta_history] [cite:wiki_neturei_karta] The two descriptions do not exclude each other; one names the religious-Zionist faction as the immediate rival, the other names the secular nationalism that faction had joined. Either way, organized Orthodox opposition to political Zionism on religious grounds existed thirty-six years before the State of Israel did. It was not a reaction to the state, which did not yet exist, but a theological position taken toward a movement that controlled no territory at all. The organization's later history, and the more uncompromising current that split from it, are followed in [scene:df_s13|a later volume].

Germany: the Antizionistisches Komitee, 1912–1914. In the same year liberal German Jews organized against Zionism on political grounds. In October 1912 they founded a league to combat Zionism, soon renamed the Antizionistisches Komitee. Its first pamphlet, issued early in 1913, reached about 45,000 copies by July of that year. An appeal of 1913 carried about 300 signatures, and the committee's declaration appeared in the Berliner Tageblatt on 5 February 1914. Before the war it reportedly had more than 1,000 members.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] Its instruments were those of a liberal civic organization: pamphlets, signed appeals and a declaration in a major daily newspaper.

Britain, 1917. The argument outlasted the outbreak of war. About twelve days after the Balfour Declaration, in November 1917, anti-Zionist British Jews formed the League of British Jews.[cite:rs_rd1_grill_currents] The Declaration itself belongs to the next stage; see [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration].

What the plurality shows. Set side by side, these currents answer a common assumption: that Zionism in its first two decades spoke for the Jews of the world, and that its Jewish critics were a fringe. The record shows at least four distinct positions, each organized and each documented in its own words. American Reform rejected Jewish nationhood as such. Liberal German Jews organized a committee against it. Frankfurt Orthodoxy and then Agudat Israel rejected it as a departure from the faith. And within the movement, secular nationalists and Mizrachi fought over whether culture or Torah would define it. The strongest case on the Zionist side is also in the record. Mizrachi's 210 branches in a single year show that religious Jews in the Russian Empire could be organized for the movement in large numbers, and the decision after 1911 to stay inside the Zionist Organization shows that the movement could absorb deep disagreement without splitting. The strongest case on the other side is that its opponents were not marginal cranks but the established rabbinical bodies of American Reform, leading Orthodox circles in Germany and an international Orthodox association, each stating a principled objection in public.

What the record does not settle. The sources give the founding dates, the counts of delegates, branches, pamphlets and signatures, and the key texts. They do not measure what share of the world's Jews held each position. The figures that exist count organizers and printed copies, not adherents, and they cannot be added up into a census of opinion. Nor does the material here say how these organizations were funded. The 1885 and 1897 Reform texts are quoted through a secondary source, and the AZK's membership figure is reported rather than established. What is established is that the argument existed, that it ran in several directions at once, and that each side made it in its own name.
