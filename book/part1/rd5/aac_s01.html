The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry, 1945–1946

It is sometimes said that no international body heard the Arab case, or the case for a shared state, before the United Nations voted to partition Palestine in November 1947. The record shows otherwise. More than a year and a half before the vote, a joint British and American committee heard the Jewish Agency, Arab spokesmen, Jewish advocates of a binational state, Christian and Jewish organizations, and the refugees themselves. It then produced a unanimous report that rejected both a Jewish state and an Arab state. What happened next is the more important part of the story. Washington took the one recommendation it wanted and left the rest; London attached a condition to even that one; and the follow-up plan pleased no one.

Twelve members, two governments. The committee was announced in November 1945 by the Foreign Secretary, Ernest Bevin, as a "joint Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry, under a rotating chairmanship" (the announcement and its context are told in [scene:rd5_bevin|the section on Bevin]). [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_bevin_1945] It was created as Washington pressed London to admit Jewish displaced persons to Palestine, and it was built to share the question between the two governments. It had six American and six British members. The Americans included Bartley Crum and James McDonald; the British included Richard Crossman. [cite:w_aac]

Hearings on three continents. Between January and March 1946 the committee held hearings in Washington, London, the displaced-persons camps of Europe, Cairo and Jerusalem. [cite:w_aac] It took evidence from governments, the Jewish Agency, Arab states and the Arab Office, Christian and Jewish organizations, and refugees in the camps. It was, in the judgment of the account this book follows, the most complete hearing of competing cases before 1947. [cite:w_aac]

Three of the cases put to it deserve naming. The Jewish Agency's leaders presented the Zionist case. Arab representatives presented theirs, among them the Arab Office, where the historian Albert Hourani gave testimony that the record describes as notable. [cite:w_aac] The sources used here do not give the substance of what Hourani said, so this book does not paraphrase it. The third case came from Jews who opposed a Jewish state. Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, and the Ihud association argued that Arabs and Jews should share one state with political parity. [cite:w_aac] Their testimony shows that a Jewish alternative to partition was argued, in person, before an official body. It was not a position invented afterward by critics of the state that emerged.

The April 1946 report. The committee reported unanimously in April 1946. It recommended four things. First, the immediate issue of 100,000 immigration certificates for Jewish refugees. Second, the repeal of the 1940 land regulations, which restricted land transfers. Third, the continuation of the Mandate toward a United Nations trusteeship. And fourth, as a principle of the future constitution, that Palestine should be neither a Jewish state nor an Arab state, with neither people dominating the other. [cite:avalon_aac]

The report was a package. The 100,000 certificates answered the plight of the survivors in Europe; the repeal of the 1940 land regulations lifted restrictions on land transfers; trusteeship and the "neither Jewish nor Arab state" principle expressed the report's principle that neither people should dominate the other. Unanimity mattered: the six Americans and six Britons had agreed on all of it together.

What Washington kept, and what London required. President Truman publicly endorsed the 100,000 and was silent on the rest. [cite:w_aac] The admission of refugees was popular with American voters. The constitutional recommendation against a Jewish state was not something he endorsed. Prime Minister Attlee answered by tying any admissions to the disarmament of the Jewish and Arab armed groups in Palestine. [cite:w_aac] The British condition was not abstract. At the time, the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi were acting together against British rule, as told in [scene:rd5_revolt|the Jewish Resistance Movement]. By making the 100,000 depend on disarmament, London made the one recommendation Washington wanted depend, in part, on the surrender of arms by Jewish groups then in open revolt against British rule.

Morrison–Grady. The two governments tried once more. In July 1946 a follow-up Anglo-American plan, known as Morrison–Grady, proposed provincial autonomy for Palestine. Zionist and Arab leaders both rejected it, and Truman declined to back it. [cite:w_morrisongrady] With that, the joint Anglo-American attempt to settle Palestine was finished. The question went to the United Nations the following year. [cite:w_morrisongrady]

The mechanism. The episode shows how the decision was structured in 1946. The committee could hear anyone and recommend anything, but it could decide nothing. The two governments could decide, but each took from the report what served its own position. Washington, which did not govern Palestine, could press on the one point that cost it nothing at home and had public support. London, which governed Palestine, could attach conditions. The people heard by the committee, whether the Jewish Agency, the Arab Office, Magnes and Ihud, or the refugees in the camps, had a hearing but no hand in what followed. The binational option that Magnes argued, and that the report's "neither Jewish nor Arab state" principle partly echoed, had no government willing to carry it.

The two readings. One reading of this record is that the hearing was real and the outcome was a failure of politics: the committee did its work, and the governments that commissioned it then ignored most of it. Another reading, found in the source this book follows, puts it more sharply: what survived into American policy was the part with domestic political value. Both readings start from the same facts. They differ on how much weight to give Truman's own reasons, a question taken up in [scene:wh_s01|the section on the White House]. What neither reading supports is the claim that the Arab and binationalist cases went unheard before 1947. They were heard; they were not adopted.

What the record does not settle. The sources used here name only three of the twelve members, and do not record the committee's internal debates or how it reached unanimity. They do not give the content of Hourani's testimony or of the Jewish Agency's. They do not say what, if anything, London said about the land regulations, trusteeship or the "neither Jewish nor Arab state" principle once the report was published, beyond its disarmament condition on the 100,000. And they do not show whether any of the 100,000 certificates were issued as a result of the report.
