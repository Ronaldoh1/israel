A Corporation Older Than the Kingdom It Sits In

The oldest document in the City of London's keeping is a strip of vellum about six inches long and an inch and a half wide. It was written in 1067, a few months after William of Normandy was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066, and it was written in Old English rather than in the conqueror's own Norman French. It is addressed to "William the bishop and Godfrey the portreeve and all the burgesses within London both French and English." [cite:dv2_lma_william] It has been in the City's archive continuously ever since, and it is the place to begin, because it shows something that the rest of the City's history repeats. The charter granted nothing new. It confirmed what the citizens already had: that they were "law-worthy, as you were in the days of King Edward," that every child would be his father's heir, and that the king would not suffer any person to do them wrong. [cite:dv2_lma_william] The new king was not creating London's liberties. He was promising not to take them away.

That pattern, a power that already existed being recognized and preserved by each new authority, is the core of what makes the City unusual. It also explains why the City is so often misdescribed. A body whose privileges were confirmed by a conqueror, and later by kings and Parliaments, can look from a distance like a body that stands above them. The documents show the opposite relationship, and they show it repeatedly.

The mayoralty came next. The first recorded Mayor of London was Henry Fitz-Ailwyn, in 1189. [cite:dv2_col_mayoralty] Before him, the Crown appointed the sheriffs who answered for the City; until 1199 they were royal appointments, and after the creation of the mayoralty they became subordinate to the Mayor. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013] In 1215 the City gained the right that defined its later independence. King John granted it a charter, early in May of that year, allowing the citizens to elect their own Mayor. The legal historian Anthony Arlidge notes that John asked for no payment for it. [cite:dv2_col_mayoralty] [cite:dv2_gresham_arlidge_2015]

The timing mattered. John was then at war with his own barons, and he was trying to keep the capital on his side. He failed. The rebels reached London's walls on 17 May 1215, and the gates were opened to them. The chronicler Roger of Wendover wrote that the rich citizens favored the rebels while the poor were afraid to speak against them. [cite:dv2_gresham_arlidge_2015] On 15 June 1215, at Runnymede near Windsor, the king sealed Magna Carta; under the peace the barons were to hand London back by 15 August, and they refused. [cite:dv2_wiki_magna_carta] The charter's thirteenth clause guaranteed the City of London its ancient liberties and free customs. [cite:dv2_gresham_arlidge_2015] When the charter was reissued and confirmed in 1297, in the version now held by the United States National Archives, the provision appeared as clause 9: "The city of London is to have all its ancient liberties and customs." [cite:dv2_nara_mc1297] Who the Mayor was that summer is recorded two ways. Arlidge identifies the Mayor who had sided with the rebels as probably Serlo the mercer, while the standard list of the twenty-five barons charged with enforcing the charter includes "William Hardell, Mayor of the City of London." [cite:dv2_gresham_arlidge_2015] [cite:dv2_wiki_magna_carta] The sources used here do not reconcile the two. Either way, the City's chief officer stood with the barons against the king.

The office acquired its grander title slowly. "Dominus major" appears in thirteenth-century Latin, "Lord Mair" in English in 1414, and the prefix "Right Honourable" was in use by the sixteenth century. Some seven hundred men and two women have held it, almost always for a single year. [cite:dv2_col_mayoralty] The current holder, Dame Susan Langley, is the 697th. [cite:dv2_wci_lordmayor]

Behind the mayoralty stood the elected bodies. Aldermen consulted the citizens of their wards from the thirteenth century. Regular meetings of a common council began in 1376, and the council has been elected since 1384. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013] By the time England had a Parliament in anything like its modern form, the City already had a government with elected members, its own officers and its own courts.

What the City does not have is a founding document. No charter survives that first created the Corporation, and the law treats it as incorporated "by prescription": it exists because it has always existed, and its legal existence has been continuous for centuries. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] One consequence is unusual in British public law. The Corporation can alter its own constitution by an Act of its own Common Council. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] Another followed in 1835. That year Parliament passed the Municipal Corporations Act, which swept away and rebuilt the old closed corporations of England's towns. The City was left out. It was not reformed then, and it has never been reformed on the general model since. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation]

The result is a local authority whose shape is medieval. The City covers 1.12 square miles, and at the 2021 census 8,583 people lived there. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] It is surrounded on every side by Greater London, but it is not one of Greater London's boroughs, and it remains separate from them. It is a ceremonial county in its own right, and where every other county has a Lord-Lieutenant representing the Crown, the City has a Commission of Lieutenancy headed by the Lord Mayor. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] Inside its boundaries sit two smaller anomalies, the Inner Temple and the Middle Temple. They are among the few surviving "liberties," treated as independent enclaves outside any parish, and the Corporation is not given general authority over them, although many of its statutory functions reach into them. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] An enclave within an enclave is about as far as English local government goes in the direction of a separate jurisdiction.

It is not far enough to make a state. The popular claim, examined in an earlier volume of this library, holds that the City is a sovereign city-state beyond the reach of Parliament and the Crown. [scene:bc_s01|The Square Mile] sets out why the documents do not support it, and [scene:bc_s06|Three enclaves, one trope] shows how the claim travels. The short version is visible in the City's own statute book. When the City wanted to reform its own elections in 2002, it had to go to Parliament, and the Act that resulted says plainly: "The objects of this Act cannot be attained without the authority of Parliament." [cite:dv2_wiki_remembrancer] A sovereign does not ask another legislature for permission to change its franchise. The Corporation does, and has done so for centuries. Its liberties survive because Parliament keeps choosing to preserve them, usually by writing an express exception for the City into each new Act. [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation]

The four standard tests of statehood, set out in the Montevideo Convention of 1933, are a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. [cite:dv1_montevideo] The City has the first three in miniature. It does not have the fourth. It sends no ambassadors and receives none, signs no treaties, and holds no seat in any international body. What it has instead is something the Convention does not measure: a privileged and formal position inside the government of a state that does all of those things. That position, and the people who fill it, are the subject of the next two sections.

The record leaves some things unsettled. The sources used here give the 1067 charter's date and contents but not the circumstances of its drafting, and they date King John's charter only to "early May" 1215, without resolving the exact day. The claim that the City is older than Parliament is true in the sense set out above, that the City had elected councils before Parliament took its modern form; it should not be stretched into a claim that the City preceded English law or the Crown, which it did not.
