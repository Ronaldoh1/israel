The Pacts Signed: Who Designed the 1929 Deal, Who Paid, Who Controlled It

On 11 February 1929, in the Lateran Palace, Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, Secretary of State, signed for Pius XI and Benito Mussolini, "Prime Minister and Head of Government," signed for King Victor Emmanuel III. They signed three instruments: a Treaty of 27 articles that created the State of Vatican City and closed the Roman Question, a Concordat of 45 articles that governed the Church's position inside Italy, and a Financial Convention that settled the money. Ratifications were exchanged in the Apostolic Palace on 7 June 1929, and the new state existed from that day. [cite:vt2_lateran_text] [cite:vt2_vn_dallatorre2019] This section covers why both sides wanted the deal, who designed it, who paid, and who controlled the result then and now; Part Three follows the terms to the present. [scene:vt3_what_each_got|Part Three: what each side got]

Why the pope wanted it, in his own words

Pius XI explained the deal twice in the week it was signed. On 11 February, to the parish priests of Rome, he said the territory he had asked for was "very little, very very little, the least possible," and compared it to the minimum of body needed to keep a soul attached. Asking for little, he said, was meant to show that no earthly greed drove the request. He defended the money on the same ground: the Holy See "has the right to provide for its own economic independence." [cite:vt2_pius11_19290211]

Two days later, on 13 February 1929, he told professors and students of the Università Cattolica in Milan that the pacts had "given God back to Italy and Italy to God," and added a sentence about Mussolini that has followed the papacy since: "perhaps also a man was needed like the one Providence has caused us to meet." [cite:vt2_pius11_19290213] The phrase is usually shortened to "the man of Providence." In context it credits Mussolini with making the settlement possible; it is not, on its face, an endorsement of fascism as a doctrine. The historian David Kertzer reads it more broadly: in his account Pius XI came to see Mussolini as the leader who might reverse the Church's losses since 1870 and as "the best bet, perhaps, to prevent a socialist takeover of Italy." [cite:vt2_npr_kertzer2014]

Why Mussolini wanted it

Mussolini had been, in Kertzer's words, "a committed anti-cleric." Once in power from 1922 he came to see the value of the pope's support: fascist rallies opened with Mass, and religious instruction in schools was one of his early gestures toward the Church. In Kertzer's judgement the Church lent the regime "organizational strength and moral legitimacy." [cite:vt2_npr_kertzer2014] The treaty text records what the Italian state gained in law. The Holy See recognised "the Kingdom of Italy under the dynasty of the House of Savoy with Rome as capital of the Italian State," declared the Roman Question eliminated, and accepted the repeal of the 1871 Law of Guarantees (Art. 26). [cite:vt2_lateran_text]

Who designed it

The negotiation ran from 1926 to 1929 and was conducted for the Holy See by a layman. Francesco Pacelli, a lawyer and professor born in 1874, led the talks with the Italian government at Pius XI's wish from 1926. [cite:vt2_vn_pacelli2019] He reported to the pope in more than two hundred long audiences while the text passed through some twenty successive drafts. [cite:vt2_wiki_fpacelli] Gasparri directed the Holy See's side and signed. Francesco's brother, Eugenio Pacelli, then nuncio in Germany and later Secretary of State and Pius XII, was not a negotiator; Francesco kept up a private correspondence with him about the talks. Francesco's own account is his Diario della Conciliazione, published in 1959. [cite:vt2_vn_pacelli2019]

Pacelli also drafted the new state's internal law. With the jurist Federico Cammeo he prepared the first laws of Vatican City, issued on 7 June 1929. He was rewarded with a seat as general counsellor of the state and, by papal brief of 9 June 1929, the hereditary title of marquess. [cite:vt2_vn_pacelli2019] [cite:vt2_wiki_fpacelli] The design is documented to named people: Pius XI, Gasparri, Francesco Pacelli and, on the other side, Mussolini.

The party that was gone before the deal

Sophia's earlier module said the deal included "the dissolution of the Catholic Centre Party." Both halves are wrong. Italy's Catholic party was the Partito Popolare Italiano, founded by the priest Luigi Sturzo in January 1919; the Centre Party (Zentrum) was German, and its fate belongs to the 1933 Reichskonkordat, a separate treaty. [cite:vt2_wiki_ppi] And the PPI was not dissolved by the Lateran Pacts. Sturzo resigned as its leader on 10 July 1923 after consulting the Holy See, following Mussolini's threats of reprisals against the clergy, and left for exile in London on 25 October 1924, an exit Gasparri arranged. [cite:vt2_wiki_sturzo] The party itself was dissolved on 5 November 1926 under the "most fascist laws," which made the National Fascist Party the only party, suppressed freedom of association and the press and created a special tribunal. [cite:vt2_wiki_ppi] [cite:vt2_museo_cronologia] That was twenty-seven months before the pacts were signed.

The Holy See's role in the PPI's decline is contested. The historian John Molony argued that the fascist state and the Vatican "worked hand in hand to help destroy the People's Party." Other accounts describe Pius XI's stance as ambiguous, and Sturzo as a priest already inclined to step aside. [cite:vt2_wiki_ppi] [cite:vt2_wiki_sturzo] What the documents establish is the sequence: the party's founder stepped down after consulting the Holy See and left Italy with Gasparri's help, the regime then dissolved the party, and the negotiations went ahead with no Catholic political party left in Italy.

The Concordat then wrote that outcome into law. Article 43 recognised the organisations of Azione Cattolica (Catholic Action) on condition that they act "outside any political party," and barred clergy and religious from joining or campaigning for any party. [cite:vt2_lateran_text] The clause did not end the friction. In 1931 the regime's police dissolved Catholic youth and university associations, and Pius XI answered with the encyclical Non abbiamo bisogno of 29 June 1931, accusing the regime of seeking to "monopolize completely the young," of "a real pagan worship of the State," and declaring the fascist oath, as it stood, "unlawful." [cite:vt2_pius11_nonabbiamo] The deal was a settlement between two powers, not a merger.

Who paid, and how much

The Italian state paid, from public funds. Article 1 of the Financial Convention obliged Italy to pay the Holy See 750,000,000 lire in cash on the exchange of ratifications and to hand over, at the same time, 5 per cent Italian state bonds of a nominal value of 1,000,000,000 lire. Article 2 records that the Holy See accepted this as the "definitive settlement" of its financial claims arising from "the events of 1870." The preamble adds that the pope had limited his request "to the strictly necessary," taking account of the state's finances and the economic condition of the Italian people after the war, and that the sum was far lower than what the state would have owed under the 1871 law. [cite:vt2_lateran_text]

Who controlled it then

In 1929 control was concentrated in one man. The pope was sovereign of the new state, and the money went into a body that answered only to him. On 7 June 1929 Pius XI created the Special Administration of the Holy See to manage the Financial Convention funds, reporting directly to the pope, and put Bernardino Nogara in charge; Nogara ran it until 1954. [cite:vt2_wiki_specialadmin] The historian John Pollard records that the endowment transformed papal finances at once, that losses followed within two years, which he attributes to ambitious spending, weak controls and the Wall Street crash, and that Nogara then rebuilt the portfolio. [cite:vt2_pollard1999] Paul VI merged the Special Administration into the new Administration of the Patrimony of the Apostolic See (APSA) in 1967. [cite:vt2_wiki_specialadmin] [scene:vt9_apsa_governorate|Part Nine: APSA and the Governorate]

Who controls it now

The Treaty still stands and was, in the words of Giuseppe Dalla Torre, then president of the Vatican City tribunal, "substantially unchanged" when the Concordat was revised in 1984. [cite:vt2_vn_dallatorre2019] [scene:vt3_concordat_1984|Part Three: the 1984 Concordat] The pope remains the sovereign. Pius XI's fundamental law of 1929 was replaced by John Paul II's in 2000 and by Francis's of 13 May 2023, in force from 7 June 2023, under which only the pope holds "powers" and the bodies of the state exercise "functions" on his behalf. [cite:vt2_open_fundlaw2023] Pope Leo XIV, elected on 8 May 2025, signed a further fundamental law dated 31 July 2026, under which he appoints the president of the Governorate, the members of the Pontifical Commission and the judges of the state's courts; as of October 2026 the Governorate is headed by Sister Raffaella Petrini. [cite:vt2_or_leo14] [cite:vt2_sussidiario_fundlaw2026] [scene:vt4_fundamental_law|Part Four: the Fundamental Law]

One clause signed in 1929 bears directly on this volume's central question. In Article 24 the Holy See declared that it "wishes to remain and will remain extraneous to temporal competitions between other States," and Vatican City is to be considered "always and in every case" neutral and inviolable territory. [cite:vt2_lateran_text] Any test of whether the Holy See's later refusal to back Zionism was a front, and of why it has never broken with Israel over Gaza, has to start from that written commitment, made nineteen years before the State of Israel existed.
