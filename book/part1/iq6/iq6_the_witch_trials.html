The Witch Trials

Between about 1450 and 1750 European courts tried roughly 100,000 people for witchcraft and executed somewhere between 40,000 and 60,000 of them. About 80 per cent of those convicted were women, most of them over forty. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] The charge joined two older ideas: harm done by magic to a neighbour's child, cow or harvest, and the theologians' newer claim that witches had made a pact with the Devil and met him at night in sabbaths. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials]

The papacy's clearest part is real. On 5 December 1484 Innocent VIII issued Summis desiderantes affectibus, confirming the authority of two Dominican inquisitors, Heinrich Kramer and Jacob Sprenger, to proceed against witches in five German church provinces. [cite:vt30_fordham_summis] Kramer then wrote the Malleus Maleficarum, printed in 1486 or 1487, and placed the bull at its front, which gave readers the impression of papal approval for a book the bull never mentioned. The book taught that women were more prone to the Devil, and recommended torture, leading questions and deception. In 1490 clergy at Cologne condemned it, and according to the historian Wolfgang Behringer the Inquisition "denied any authority to the Malleus". [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus]

Most of the killing was done by the courts of territorial rulers, towns and kingdoms, Catholic and Protestant. About half of all prosecutions fell in the Holy Roman Empire. Protestant Scotland hunted witches hard; in south-western Germany between 1561 and 1670, 2,527 of 3,229 recorded executions took place in Catholic territories. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] Some of the worst hunts were in lands where a Catholic bishop was also the prince: in the prince-bishopric of Würzburg a commission executed an estimated 900 people between 1626 and 1631, among them at least 49 children under twelve. [cite:vt30_wiki_wurzburg]

The inquisitions behaved differently. The Roman Inquisition restrained Italian courts; its Venetian tribunal never convicted anyone of harmful magic, and in 1635 the Roman congregation admitted it had "found scarcely one trial conducted legally". [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] In Spain, after six people were burned alive at Logroño in 1610, the inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías toured the Basque country, collected nearly 2,000 confessions, 1,384 of them from children, found no objective proof of witchcraft, and argued that no one should be sentenced "unless the case can be proven with external and objective evidence". In August 1614 the Spanish Inquisition adopted stricter rules of evidence, which ended witch-burning in Spain. [cite:vt30_wiki_basque] [cite:vt30_audit_spanishinq]

The figure of nine million comes from a Quedlinburg lawyer, Gottfried Christian Voigt, who in 1784 took about thirty local trials, assumed the same rate for every town in Europe over many centuries, and multiplied; the historian David Luebke calls it "a colossal error of misguided statistical extrapolation". [cite:vt30_luebke_ninemillion] The real toll needs no inflation, and the inflated one hides the documented victims, whose names survive in the trial records.
