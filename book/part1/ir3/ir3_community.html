Two Documented Facts

The question this part examines is often put as a paradox. Israel and the United States went to war in 2025 and again in 2026 against a state whose constitution recognises its Jewish community by name and reserves that community a seat in parliament. [cite:ow7_npr_epicfury] [cite:ir3_const_en] The same state executed the community's best-known businessman in its first months, tried thirteen Jews as Israeli spies in 1999 and 2000, convened a conference of Holocaust deniers in 2006, and in the summer of 2025 summoned dozens of Jews for questioning about their relatives in Israel. [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] [cite:ir3_ap_shiraz2000] [cite:ir3_wiki_conf2006] [cite:ir3_fh_2026] The simulation behind this volume calls these "two documented facts, not a paradox": how a constitution treats a minority, and what a government's officials say and do, are separate questions with separate records. This part sets out both records in full. This section gives the first: the community, its numbers, its legal standing and its public voice. The next section gives the second. [scene:ir3_facts|The other fact]

Two thousand seven hundred years

Jewish life in what is now Iran is older than Islam, older than Persia's conversion to it, and older than most of the cities where Iranian Jews now live. Accounts that give "over 2,700 years" count from the Assyrian conquest of the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE, when, according to the Bible (2 Kings 18:11), some of the deported Israelites were settled in "the cities of the Medes", in what is now western Iran. [cite:ir3_wiki_history] The firmer historical marker is the Persian king Cyrus, who conquered Babylon in the sixth century BCE and allowed the exiled Jews to return to Judea; many stayed under Persian rule. [cite:ir3_wiki_history] The Book of Esther is set at the Persian court. In Hamadan, the ancient Ecbatana, a mausoleum believed to date from the 1600s is held by Iranian Jews and Christians to contain the tombs of Esther and her cousin Mordecai, and it is the most important Jewish pilgrimage site in the country. The tradition is not supported in the Talmud, and the archaeologist Ernst Herzfeld argued the tomb belonged to Shushandukht, the Jewish wife of the Sasanian king Yazdegerd I. [cite:ir3_wiki_tomb] On 14 March 2025 Iran's chief rabbi read the Book of Esther there for Purim; the site had been the target of an arson attempt in May 2020 and a firebomb in April 2024, both causing minor damage. [cite:ir3_wiki_tomb] The 2,700-year figure therefore rests on the biblical account; the continuous record from the Persian empire onward, about 2,500 years, is not in dispute.

How many: censuses and estimates

No figure for Iran's Jewish population is exact, and the sources that give them count differently. Iran's first national census, in 1956, counted 65,232 Jews. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] For the eve of the revolution, figures range from about 60,000, attributed to Iran's Statistical Centre, through about 80,000 to "over 100,000", the figure given by the Iranian-American writer Roya Hakakian. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] [cite:ir3_wiki_history] [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] The census figures since then are much lower. The 2011 census, released on 29 July 2012, counted 8,756 Jews, against 117,704 Christians and 25,271 Zoroastrians. [cite:ir3_afp_census2012] The 2016 census counted 9,826. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] The US State Department's 2014 religious freedom report gave both kinds of figure side by side: "fewer than 9,000 Jews" by Iran's census, "as many as 25,000" by media estimates. [cite:ir3_usip_irf2014]

The higher estimates come from earlier years or from individual community members. In December 2006 the then Jewish member of parliament, Maurice Motamed, spoke of about 25,000 Iranian Jews; in 2009 the State Department gave 20,000 to 25,000. [cite:ir3_wiki_conf2006] [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] In 2014 Hakakian wrote that the government reported 25,000 and that she estimated no more than 10,000. [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] The Tehran Jewish Committee, as cited by the State Department, estimated about 9,000, while a community member visiting the United States in 2021 had given 20,000. [cite:ir3_iranwire_rallies] Reporting during the wars ranged from about 15,000 (Ynet, June 2025) to 8,000 to 10,000 (Times of Israel, June 2025) and 8,000 to 15,000 (Religion News Service, April 2026). [cite:ir3_ynet_2025] [cite:ir3_toi_summoned] [cite:ir3_rns_synagogue] The figure of "over 20,000" Iranian Jews today is therefore not supported by any census and matches only estimates made between 2006 and 2009 and one individual's claim in 2021. The census counts since 2011 put the community at under 10,000, and current estimates from outside Iran run from about 8,000 to 15,000. One study estimated that more than 80 percent of the Jews in Iran at the revolution had left by 2006. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] AFP describes the remaining community as the largest in the Middle East outside Israel, a ranking that depends on which estimate is used and whether Turkey is counted. [cite:ir3_olj_ghahremani]

What the constitution says

The constitution of the Islamic Republic, adopted in 1979 and amended in 1989, makes Twelver Shia Islam the official religion (Article 12). Article 13 states: "Zoroastrian, Jewish, and Christian Iranians are the only recognized religious minorities," free within the law to perform their rites and to follow their own rules in personal status and religious education. Article 14 requires the government and Muslims to treat non-Muslims with "Islamic justice and equity" and to respect their human rights, but only those who do not conspire against Islam or the Islamic Republic. [cite:ir3_const_en] [cite:ir3_const_es] Article 64 reserves seats in the parliament, the Majlis: "The Zoroastrians and Jews will each elect one representative," the Assyrian and Chaldean Christians share one, and Armenian Christians elect two. [cite:ir3_const_en] The text does not mention Zionism. Some earlier accounts, including the series this volume builds on, describe the constitution as distinguishing Jews from Zionists; that distinction is in Khomeini's reported words, discussed below, not in the constitutional text. [cite:ir3_const_es]

The Jewish seat has been held since 2020 by Homayoun Sameh Najafabadi, who in 2024 told Voice of America that state aid to the community's institutions had "increased greatly" in recent years. [cite:ir3_wiki_sameh] He was still the community's representative in April 2026. [cite:ir3_rns_synagogue] About 100 synagogues remained in Iran as of 2025, along with Jewish schools and Tehran's Jewish hospital, Dr Sapir. [cite:ir3_wiki_history] Freedom House records that the recognised minorities are "relatively free to worship." [cite:ir3_fh_2026] The schools run under limits: their heads are appointed by the state, and most have been Muslim; and until 4 February 2015 they were required to open on Saturdays. [cite:ir3_wiki_history]

Khomeini's assurance: three wordings

The founding statement of the Islamic Republic's position on its Jews is a remark Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini made to a Jewish delegation in Qom in May 1979, days after the execution of the community leader Habib Elghanian. [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] [cite:ir3_wiki_history] It survives in more than one wording, and no primary transcript was found for this book. Hakakian, drawing on the delegates' accounts, dates the visit to 10 May 1979 and gives his words as: "We recognize our Jews as separate from those godless, bloodsucking Zionists." She reports that by nightfall the sentence was painted on synagogue walls, Hebrew schools and kosher butcher shops. [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] The historian Trita Parsi quotes the same sentence. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] Another account places the meeting three days after the execution, with a delegation led by Yedidia Shofet, and reports that the newspaper Ettela'at headlined the next day: "We do not believe that all Jews are Zionists." [cite:ir3_wiki_history] A third, softer wording ("we make a distinction between the Jewish community and the Zionists") and a description of the remark as a formal fatwa circulate in later secondary material; this book did not find either traced to a primary record. What is consistent across the attested versions is the distinction itself: a protected Jewish community on one side, Zionism and Israel on the other.

The community's public voice in the wars

During Israel's strikes in June 2025, the community's leadership issued two statements condemning Israel. Sameh wrote that Israel "is committing war crimes — crimes against humanity," and the senior rabbi Younes Hamami Lalehzar published a similar letter. [cite:ir3_ynet_2025] The state news agency Mehr published photographs of Jews gathered at Tehran's Abrishami synagogue in support of the Supreme Leader and the armed forces. [cite:ir3_toi_summoned] After an Israeli strike destroyed a Tehran synagogue on 7 April 2026, the Iranian Jewish Association called the attack "heinous" and affirmed its "historic unity with the Leader, the Iranian nation, and the courageous defenders of our country." [cite:ir3_nw_assoc2026] The Leader in question was by then Mojtaba Khamenei, designated after his father was killed in the opening strikes of 28 February 2026. [cite:hz8_wiki_iranwar]

These statements are the community's documented public position. They are not evidence of what its members privately think, and the record shows why they cannot be read that way. In 2023 the State Department said it had reports that Iranian authorities "coerced Iranian Jewish leaders and their communities" into anti-Israel rallies, and called this "abhorrent." [cite:ir3_iranwire_rallies] An Israeli radio host who knew of one 2025 gathering said it was meant to "avert any suspicion of spying or, heaven forbid, betrayal." [cite:ir3_toi_summoned] In December 2025 Sameh said he had been summoned by the security agencies over Jewish users' likes and comments on Israeli content, according to Iran International, a London-based opposition-leaning outlet. [cite:ir3_iranintl_summons] Ynet's anonymous sources described a community whose main fear in the war was vigilante violence. [cite:ir3_ynet_2025] A community that speaks under these conditions has a public voice; it does not have a free one.
