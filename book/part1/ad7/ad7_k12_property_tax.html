Schools Paid for by the House Next Door

The education promise begins long before college. Civics and economics standards teach that investment in "human capital", the skills and knowledge a person carries, raises productivity and living standards, and that a public school system open to every child is how a society makes that investment fairly. [cite:ad1_c3] [cite:ad1_cee2010] [scene:ad1_as_taught|The Dream as taught] What the standards do not usually explain is how the money for a given school is raised. In most of the United States a large part of it comes from a tax on the value of the land and buildings in the school district, so the price of the houses around a school sets much of what that school can spend. This section shows how that mechanism works, how the Supreme Court came to leave it in place in 1973, what the gaps it produces look like by the main published measures, and where the record shows that state money has narrowed them.

How the money is raised

In the 2024 fiscal year American public schools took in $994.9 billion. States provided 45.2 per cent, local sources 43.2 per cent and the federal government 11.6 per cent, and property taxes made up 63.3 per cent of local revenue. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] The arithmetic shows the weight of the property tax in the whole system: 43.2 per cent of $994.9 billion is about $429.8 billion of local money, and 63.3 per cent of that is about $272 billion, or roughly 27 cents of every dollar spent on public schools. Average current spending was $17,619 per pupil, but the average hides a range that runs from $31,918 in New York to $11,060 in Idaho. [cite:us17_census_fy2024] A school's local money depends on two things: the tax rate voters or officials set, and the taxable value of the property in its district. A district with expensive homes, offices or industry can raise a great deal at a modest rate; a district of low-value homes can tax heavily and still raise little. State aid formulas exist to offset this, and the federal government adds a smaller share, but the local base comes first and shapes everything that follows.

This is designed friction, not a hidden one. Local funding of schools by local property was a deliberate feature of American public education, defended as local control: the people who pay for a school decide how much to spend on it. The problem is that the base each community can tax is not equal, and that the value of housing was itself shaped by federal and local rules about who could live where. The federal underwriting manual of 1938 told appraisers that schools attended in large numbers by "inharmonious" groups lowered the value of a neighbourhood, tying school composition to property value in the government's own lending rules. [cite:ad6_fha_manual] [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six: redlining and the suburbs]

San Antonio v. Rodriguez, 1973

The constitutional test came from San Antonio. Parents in the Edgewood district, a poor district of about 22,000 pupils, sued over the Texas system. Edgewood taxed its property at the highest rate in the area and raised about $26 per pupil from it; Alamo Heights, a wealthier district of about 5,000 pupils, raised $333 per pupil at a lower rate. With state and federal money added, Edgewood spent $356 per pupil and Alamo Heights $594, a gap of $238, or 67 per cent more for each Alamo Heights child. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez] [cite:ad7_aba_funding] A federal district court found the system unconstitutional. On 21 March 1973 the Supreme Court reversed it, five votes to four. [cite:ad7_wiki_rodriguez]

Justice Lewis Powell, writing for the majority, held that education "is not among the rights afforded explicit protection" by the federal Constitution, that the Texas scheme did not single out a definable class of poor people for disadvantage, and that the state's system therefore needed only a rational relationship to a legitimate purpose, which local control supplied. He left one door open: a system that produced an "absolute denial of educational opportunities" might be different. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez] [cite:ad7_wiki_rodriguez] Justice Thurgood Marshall's dissent called the decision "a retreat from our historic commitment to equality of educational opportunity", and noted that state constitutions remained open to challenge. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez]

That note set the course of the next half-century. Because the federal route was closed, school-finance fights moved to state courts under state constitutions, and most states have faced such suits. [cite:ad7_aba_funding] In Texas itself the state Supreme Court ruled in 1989, in a case led by Edgewood, that children in poor and rich districts must have "a substantially equal opportunity" to school funds; Texas courts found the system unconstitutional again in 1991, 1992 and 2005 and upheld it in 1995 and 2016. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez] [cite:ad7_aba_funding] A 2020 federal appeals panel suggested a federal right to basic literacy might exist, but the full court vacated the ruling after Michigan settled. [cite:ad7_edweek_rodriguez] As of October 2026, Rodriguez remains the federal law: there is no federal constitutional right to equal school funding.

How large are the gaps?

Measuring the gap depends on what is compared, and the main studies compare different things. The Education Trust, an advocacy organisation for low-income students and students of color, compared state and local money only, leaving out federal funds, and found in 2018 that the highest-poverty districts received about $1,000, or 7 per cent, less per pupil than the lowest-poverty ones; after adjusting for the extra cost of educating children in poverty, the gap was about $2,000, or 16 per cent. Districts serving the most students of color received about $1,800, or 13 per cent, less. [cite:ad7_edtrust_2018] EdBuild, a school-finance research group, compared districts by racial composition after adjusting for local costs and reported in 2019 that predominantly white districts received $23 billion more than districts serving mostly students of color, about $2,226 less per pupil in the average nonwhite district. It found that high-poverty districts serving mostly students of color were about $1,600 per pupil below the national average, against about $130 for high-poverty, mostly white districts, and it tied part of the pattern to district size: mostly white districts averaged just over 1,500 pupils, mostly nonwhite ones more than 10,000, so small affluent districts could keep their tax base to themselves. [cite:ad7_npr_edbuild]

The Urban Institute measured the same question a third way, asking what the average poor pupil's district spends compared with the average non-poor pupil's, counting all sources. On 2013–14 data, the districts poor pupils attended received $12,961 per pupil against $12,640 for non-poor pupils, about 2.5 per cent more. The local money alone was about 15 per cent lower for poor pupils, a gap that had narrowed from 18 per cent in 1994–95, and state money about ten percentage points higher, more than closing it. [cite:ad7_chingos_ednext] The authors also found that many states with progressive formulas on paper did not achieve them in practice. [cite:ad7_urban_2017] Their method was later revised to correct how charter-school money was counted, which turned Pennsylvania from a state where poor pupils received 4 per cent more to one where they received 3 per cent less. [cite:ad7_chalkbeat_urban]

These findings are not contradictory. Read together they say that the local property-tax base is strongly unequal, that state aid, and to a lesser degree federal aid, offsets most or all of it on average, and that the offset is weaker in some states and for districts serving mostly Black and Hispanic pupils, where segregation by district line keeps poor children concentrated together. They also say that "equal dollars" is not "equal opportunity": children in poverty cost more to educate to the same standard, which is why a district can receive slightly more money and still be underfunded relative to need. The Education Law Center reported in 2021 that 14 states spent more per pupil in low-poverty districts than in high-poverty ones; in Nevada the figures were $12,798 against $8,731. [cite:ad7_aba_funding]

Does the money matter?

A long-running argument in school finance held that spending had little relation to results. The strongest recent evidence comes from court-ordered reforms that changed spending for reasons unrelated to a district's own choices, which allows a cleaner test. C. Kirabo Jackson, Rucker Johnson and Claudia Persico followed children who grew up through such reforms and found that a 10 per cent increase in per-pupil spending in every one of a child's twelve school years led to about 0.27 more years of completed education, 7.25 per cent higher adult wages and a 3.67 percentage-point lower chance of being poor in a given adult year, with much larger effects for children from low-income families. [cite:ad7_jjp_nber] That result is why the funding gap is a mechanism of the Dream and not only a budget question: money spent on a child's school shows up in that child's adult earnings.

The results the system produces have weakened recently across the board. On the 2024 national assessment, twelfth-grade mathematics scores were the lowest since 2005 and the gap between high and low performers the widest on record, and on the international PISA test of 2025 the United States had the largest gap between its strongest and weakest readers of any participating country. [cite:us17_chalkbeat_naep] [cite:us17_edweek_pisa2025] Both patterns fit a system in which where a child lives shapes what is spent on the child, though neither test result by itself shows how much of the spread comes from funding rather than from family income, the pandemic or other causes. [scene:us17_schools|US vs Israel: schools, money and scores]

Where it works as promised

The taught version holds in important respects. Public schooling remains free at the point of use for every child, and on the Urban Institute's all-sources measure the average poor pupil attends a district with slightly more money, not less, because state formulas push money towards weaker tax bases. [cite:ad7_chingos_ednext] The local-revenue gap between poor and non-poor pupils narrowed between 1994–95 and 2013–14, and in 35 states the formula adds money for low-income pupils. [cite:ad7_chingos_ednext] State courts, after Rodriguez, forced repeated reforms in Texas and elsewhere, and the evidence from those reforms is that added money reached children and changed their adult lives. [cite:ad7_aba_funding] [cite:ad7_jjp_nber] The mechanism is unequal by design, but the correction built on top of it is real and measurable; the record shows where it falls short, district by district and state by state, rather than a system in which the correction never happens.
