Who Pays for Hezbollah

The plainest statement of where Hezbollah's money comes from was made by Hezbollah. In a televised speech on 24 June 2016, marking forty days since the death of its commander Mustafa Badreddine, Hassan Nasrallah said the group was open about the fact that its budget, income and expenses, "everything it eats and drinks, its weapons and rockets, come from the Islamic Republic of Iran." He added: "As long as Iran has money, we have money." [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] He was answering a US law, the Hezbollah International Financing Prevention Act, signed by President Obama on 18 December 2015, which threatened sanctions on banks that knowingly handled significant transactions for the group. Nasrallah's reply was that the law could not touch Hezbollah because "we don't transfer our money through the Lebanese banking system" and kept no money in Lebanese banks. [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] Four years earlier, in February 2012, he had acknowledged Iran's "moral, and political and material support in all possible forms" since 1982, while insisting that Hezbollah took support, not orders, from Tehran. [cite:hz6_wilson_record]

How much money that is has never been published by Hezbollah or Iran, so every figure in circulation is an outside estimate, almost all of them from the US government. In about 2010 US officials put Iran's support at $100 million to $200 million a year. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] [cite:hz6_wiki_hziran] In June 2018 Sigal Mandelker, the Treasury's under secretary for terrorism and financial intelligence, told a meeting of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies that "Iran provides upwards of $700 million a year to Hezbollah", far above the earlier figure of about $200 million. [cite:hz6_jp_mandelker] The State Department's Country Reports on Terrorism for 2018 repeated "an estimated $700 million per year" and said this was "the overwhelming majority of the group's annual budget". [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018] On 13 November 2018 Nathan Sales, the State Department's counterterrorism coordinator, put Iran's spending on armed allies across the region at close to $1 billion a year, about $700 million of it for Hezbollah and $100 million for Palestinian groups. Iranian officials answered that US sanctions would not stop Iran supporting its allies in Syria, Lebanon, Palestine and Iraq. [cite:hz6_rferl_sales]

The jump from about $200 million to $700 million should be read with care. The higher figure was announced in 2018, the year the United States left the nuclear agreement with Iran and began its "maximum pressure" campaign of sanctions, by officials of the administration running that campaign, and none of the statements cited here gives the method behind it. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] That timing does not show the figure is wrong; it is a reason to treat it as a government estimate rather than an audited number. What is not in doubt, because Hezbollah's own leader said it, is that Iran is the source of most or all of its budget and its weapons. The dispute is over the size of the sum, not its origin.

Sanctions and the crime accusation

The legal basis for most US measures against Hezbollah's money is older than any of these estimates. On 8 October 1997 Secretary of State Madeleine Albright designated "Hizballah", also listed as "Party of God" and "Islamic Jihad", a Foreign Terrorist Organization, in the first list issued under the 1996 anti-terrorism law; it was added to the Specially Designated Global Terrorist list in October 2001. [cite:hz6b_fr_fto1997] [cite:hz6b_dea_lcb2011] Hezbollah rejects the label, presenting its armed wing as resistance to Israel. Beyond the 2015 banking law, the US kept widening its financial measures. In October 2018 President Trump signed a law widening the range of people who could be sanctioned for dealing with Hezbollah, at an event marking the 35th anniversary of the 1983 Marine barracks bombing (see [scene:hz2_1983|the 1983 bombings]), and on 15 October Attorney General Jeff Sessions named Hezbollah one of five top transnational organised crime threats to the United States. [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] That designation reflects a long-running US accusation that Hezbollah raises money through money laundering and the drug trade as well as from Iran. Nasrallah rejected the accusation in February 2012, saying Iran met the group's needs. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Rebeiz, the Dickinson College scholar, describes the financial network that grew around Hezbollah as involving money laundering and drug trafficking. [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz] The accusation is the US government's and Hezbollah denies it, so the individual cases behind it need to be taken one at a time, each at its own strength.

The strongest is the Lebanese Canadian Bank. On 10 February 2011 the Treasury named the bank, which had 35 branches in Lebanon and assets above $5 billion, a "primary money laundering concern" under Section 311 of the USA PATRIOT Act. It said a network run by Ayman Joumaa, designated a drug kingpin that January, had used the bank to launder up to $200 million a month in narcotics proceeds from cocaine moved from South America through West Africa to Europe and the Middle East, and that "U.S. Government information indicates" Hezbollah drew financial support from the network. [cite:hz6b_dea_lcb2011] The bank was sold, and in a civil case in Manhattan the US alleged that from 2007 to early 2011 at least $329 million had been wired to the United States to buy used cars for resale in West Africa, with the proceeds, mixed with drug money, returned to Lebanon through "Hizballah-controlled money laundering channels". On 25 June 2013 the bank agreed to forfeit $102 million of $150 million the US had seized in August 2012. [cite:hz6b_sdny_lcb2013] That is a settlement, not a trial verdict, and the Justice Department's announcement does not say the bank admitted wrongdoing; the link to Hezbollah rests on the US government's allegations and its intelligence, which were never tested in court.

The most disputed is Project Cassandra. In December 2017 Politico, in a report by Josh Meyer, described a Drug Enforcement Administration campaign launched in 2008 whose agents believed Hezbollah was collecting about $1 billion a year from drug and weapons trafficking, and alleged that the Obama administration had let the group "off the hook", declining to pursue key suspects, in order to secure the nuclear agreement with Iran. [cite:hz6b_snopes_cassandra] Former officials disputed it. Kevin Lewis, a spokesman for Obama and a former Justice Department official, said there had been "a consistent pattern of actions taken against Hezbollah" before and after the deal; Derek Maltz, a former DEA special-operations chief, attributed the program's end partly to turf battles between law enforcement and intelligence agencies. [cite:hz6b_snopes_cassandra] Daniel Byman of Georgetown, writing in Lawfare, found much of the material on Hezbollah convincing but judged the sourcing one-sided, relying heavily on DEA personnel, and the evidence stronger for interagency rivalry than for White House pressure; Daniel Benjamin, the former State Department counterterrorism coordinator, called it a "woefully one-sided recitation from DEA". [cite:hz6b_lawfare_byman] The claim that Washington went easy on Hezbollah for the sake of the Iran deal is contested, and the $1 billion figure is the agents' belief as reported, not a documented sum.

The newest is Captagon, an amphetamine pill made in Syria and sold across the Gulf. Here the documented finding concerns the Assad government more than Hezbollah. The Treasury said on 28 March 2023 that Maher al-Assad, the president's brother, and the army's Fourth Division he commanded facilitated Captagon production and trafficking, and that the Assad family dominated the trade. [cite:hz6b_tr_captagon2023] OCCRP traced a 2020 seizure of more than 14 tonnes at Salerno to the president's family network, and the New Lines Institute called the government "the main supplier and sponsor of the industry", with exports worth at least $5.7 billion in 2021. [cite:hz6b_occrp_salerno] [cite:hz6b_nl_endgame] [cite:hz6b_newarab_57bn] The Hezbollah-specific claims are narrower and weaker. The same Treasury action sanctioned two Lebanese men, saying one, Hassan Daqqou, ran operations with the Fourth Division "with cover reportedly provided by Hizballah" and the other had ties to "certain members of Hizballah". [cite:hz6b_tr_captagon2023] Daqqou told Lebanese investigators he worked with Hezbollah, but the judge who sentenced him found no evidence implicating Syrian officials, and OCCRP noted that many Lebanese officials were reluctant to tie Hezbollah directly to the trade. [cite:hz6b_occrp_drugwar] After Assad fell, US and Arab officials told the Wall Street Journal that Hezbollah "facilitated trafficking in areas under their control", while saying its profits were unknown. [cite:hz6b_wsj_captagon] Nasrallah called the reports "baseless lies" in May 2023 and said Captagon was "religiously prohibited". [cite:hz6b_voa_nasrallah2023] On the public record, Assad-government production is well documented; Hezbollah's part is alleged by the US and Arab governments and named investigators, and denied by Hezbollah, without the kind of court finding that exists for the Lebanese Canadian Bank.

The pressure campaign on Iran did reach Hezbollah's books. According to the AP in October 2018, the group was cutting costs while continuing to pay stipends to the families of its dead, medical care for its wounded and retirement payments, and could still draw on tens of thousands of donation boxes placed in Shia neighbourhoods of Lebanon. Nasrallah admitted that the sanctions "will have an effect for sure", and Kataib Hezbollah, an Iraqi militia, offered to help it financially if needed (see [scene:hz10_iraq|the Iraqi militias]). [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] NOW reported in 2022 that the sanctions had sharply cut the money reaching Hezbollah and forced austerity. [cite:hz6_now_welfare]

Its own bank, and the state's money

Nasrallah's statement that Hezbollah keeps its money outside Lebanese banks points to the parallel system it built instead. Al-Qard al-Hassan, a lending association, is described by the researcher Yara Asmar as Hezbollah's own alternative to the Lebanese banking system. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] The US Treasury designated it on 24 July 2007, saying "Hizballah has used AQAH as a cover to manage its financial activity" and had moved part of that activity there after earlier designations in 2006. [cite:hz6b_tr_2007] On 11 May 2021 it designated seven more people: Ibrahim Ali Daher, named as head of Hezbollah's central finance unit, and six Al-Qard al-Hassan officials. Treasury said the association held a licence from the Interior Ministry as a non-profit but offered bank-like services without banking regulation, that sanctioned Hezbollah figures had registered accounts in its officials' names, and that accounts held by five of those officials had moved more than $500 million through Lebanon's banks over a decade. [cite:hz6b_tr_2021] The two statements sit uneasily together: Nasrallah said Hezbollah kept its money out of Lebanese banks, while the Treasury says its lending arm used them through individuals' accounts. The Treasury's account is the US government's finding under its own sanctions law, not a court ruling.

The state's money also reaches Hezbollah's institutions, legally. Under Prime Minister Hassan Diab's government in 2020, the health ministry, then run by Hezbollah according to NOW, added fifteen Hezbollah-sponsored medical centres to the list of providers the state pays, and state payments to Hezbollah-run hospitals nearly doubled, from about 16 billion to 30 billion Lebanese pounds. In 2020 the US refused to send aid to the health ministry while Hezbollah held it. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] The two streams, Iran's money for weapons and fighters and the Lebanese state's money for services, show how Hezbollah sits both outside and inside the state at once (see [scene:hz6_state|A Party With an Army]).
