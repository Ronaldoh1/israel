The Chorus: Other Israeli Officials on the Timeline

Part Five set Benjamin Netanyahu's dated warnings against what followed. [scene:ir5_predictions|Always a year away] He was not the only Israeli leader to put a date on Iran's bomb, and the same test has to be applied to the others. This section collects the dated statements of other Israeli leaders, generals and intelligence services on how close Iran was to a weapon and whether Israel should strike. Each is given with its date and venue, and with whether it was framed as a condition ("if it decides") or as a flat forecast, because a conditional warning that did not come true is not the same failure as a dated prediction that did not.

Shimon Peres: The First Date and the Last Word

The earliest dated Israeli forecast found for this volume came from Shimon Peres, then foreign minister in Yitzhak Rabin's government. In 1992 he told French television, as The Christian Science Monitor's timeline of the warnings records it, that Iran was set to have nuclear warheads by 1999. He called Iran "the greatest threat and greatest problem in the Middle East" because it "seeks the nuclear option while holding a highly dangerous stance of extreme religious militancy." [cite:ir6_csm_peres1992] The same year Netanyahu, then an opposition member of the Knesset, was reported to put Iran three to five years away. [scene:ir5_predictions|Netanyahu's 1992 forecast] 1999 passed with no Iranian warhead; the United States intelligence community later judged that Iran had worked on weapons design until 2003 and then halted it. [cite:hz11_wh_nie2007]

Peres kept up the warning for fifteen years. As president, in a written statement on 18 October 2007, he compared the moment to the failure to stop Stalin and Hitler: "We are nearing a similar turn of events with Ahmadinejad. The world must open its eyes before it is too late." [cite:ir6_ynet_peres2007] Yet when the question became whether Israel should strike alone, Peres was among the first senior figures to say publicly that it should not. On 17 August 2012, at the height of the debate over an Israeli attack, he told Channel 2: "We can delay the Iranian nuclear plan, but it is clear to us that we need to go with the United States," adding that President Obama was determined to stop Iran "and Israel cannot do it." Members of Netanyahu's coalition answered him directly; the minister Gilad Erdan called the remarks "an unabashed and explicit attack on the official policy of the government of the State of Israel." [cite:ir6_jta_peres2012] Peres shows in one career what this part finds across the establishment: agreement that the programme was a danger, disagreement over what to do.

Ehud Barak: Windows, and the "Zone of Immunity"

Ehud Barak, defence minister from 2007 to 2013, was Netanyahu's partner in the planning of a strike, and his statements show both a date and a doubt. According to US diplomatic cables published by WikiLeaks and summarised by AFP in December 2010, Barak told American officials in June 2009 that there was a window of six to eighteen months to stop Iran from getting nuclear weapons; at the same meeting the Mossad estimated Iran would have the technological capability to build one by 2010 or 2011. [cite:ir6_csm_yaalon2010] In August 2010 US officials said they had persuaded their Israeli counterparts that Iran was at least a year from a bomb; Gary Samore of the National Security Council told The New York Times that Iran's technical problems gave it at least a year of "dash" time, and Israel was reported to have previously believed Iran could have a weapon by the following March. [cite:ir6_jta_samore2010] None of those windows produced a weapon.

Barak did not, however, describe Iran as a threat to Israel's existence. In an interview with Yedioth Ahronoth in September 2009 he said: "I am not among those who believe Iran is an existential issue for Israel." [cite:ir6_jta_barak2009] His argument for acting was about time rather than survival. By early 2012 he had given it a name. Speaking in Tokyo on 18 February 2012, he urged tighter sanctions "before the Iranians fully enter this immunity zone," meaning the point at which so much of the programme was buried deep enough, at sites such as Fordow, that an Israeli attack could no longer reach it. [cite:ir6_ahram_barak2012] The zone of immunity was a claim about Israel's military reach, not about Iran's intent, and it could be true even if Iran never decided to build a weapon. The US defence secretary, Leon Panetta, said the same week that Israel had "indicated they're considering" a strike and that Washington had "indicated our concerns." [cite:ir6_ahram_barak2012]

Moshe Ya'alon: A Date Pushed Back

Moshe Ya'alon, a former chief of staff who was strategic affairs minister in 2010 and defence minister from 2013 to 2016, gave the clearest example of an Israeli estimate being revised in public. On 29 December 2010 he told Israeli public radio that Iran "does not have the ability to create nuclear weapons by itself at the moment," that "technological challenges and difficulties" had "postponed the timetable," and that a weapon was "likely to happen in the next three years, if it is successful." [cite:ir6_csm_yaalon2010] He did not name the difficulties; the report noted that the Stuxnet computer worm had struck the enrichment plant at Natanz the year before (see Part Eight). [scene:ir8_shadow|The shadow war] His three years ran out at the end of 2013, the month after Iran signed the interim agreement that froze much of its programme (see Part Seven). [scene:ir7_deal|The deal]

Gantz, Bennett and Lapid: The Threshold Years

Benny Gantz, chief of staff from 2011 to 2015, gave Haaretz in April 2012 the most reassuring assessment any serving Israeli commander offered in those years. "The Iranian leadership is composed of very rational people," he said, and of Ali Khamenei: "I don't think he will want to go the extra mile." Barak answered within days that Iran's leaders were not "rational in the Western sense of the word," and Gantz's aides said his words had been "taken out of context" and that he and Netanyahu saw Iran "in the same way." [cite:ir6_csm_gantz2012] Nine years later, as defence minister, Gantz used a timeline of his own. On 4 August 2021, after the United States had left the 2015 deal and Iran had expanded enrichment, he told ambassadors from the UN Security Council that Iran was "only around 10 weeks away from acquiring weapons-grade materials necessary for a nuclear weapon," and called for "diplomatic, economic and even military deeds." [cite:ir6_toi_gantz2021] His claim was about fissile material, not an assembled bomb, and it rested on enrichment the IAEA itself was recording (see Part Four). [scene:ir4_programme|The programme]

The prime ministers who followed Netanyahu in 2021 and 2022 kept the same line in the same venue. Naftali Bennett told the UN General Assembly on 27 September 2021 that "Iran's nuclear weapon program is at a critical point. All red lines have been crossed," and that "words do not stop centrifuges from spinning"; he gave no date. [cite:ir6_un_bennett2021] Yair Lapid told the Assembly on 22 September 2022 that "the only way to prevent Iran from getting a nuclear weapon is to put a credible military threat on the table." [cite:ir6_mm_lapid2022] Neither made a timeline prediction that can be tested; both made the argument for force.

The Military's Case in June 2025

When Israel struck Iran on 13 June 2025, its military gave an intelligence basis. It said information gathered "in recent months" showed Iran "approaching the point of no return," and its spokesman, Brigadier General Effie Defrin, said Iran was "rushing toward a nuclear bomb" and that Israel had uncovered a secret programme in which scientists worked on weapon components, which he called "unequivocal proof." [cite:ir6_afp_idf2025] Israel did not publish the intelligence. The IAEA's director general said six days later that the agency had not found "an active, systematic plan to build a nuclear weapon." [cite:ir5_tbs_grossi] In September 2026 Ynet quoted an unnamed senior Israeli official as saying the weaponization group had been "very far from developing a nuclear weapon"; that account cannot be checked independently. [cite:ir5_ynet_sep2026] Part Ten sets out the war itself. [scene:ir10_strikes|The twelve days]

Americans Who Echoed the Timeline

Some American officials gave short timelines too, usually framed as a condition. Leon Panetta said in an interview with 60 Minutes recorded on 17 December 2011 that if Iran's leaders decided to proceed at full speed they could have a weapon in about a year, sooner if they had a hidden enrichment site; "That's a red line for us," he said. [cite:ir6_afps_panetta2011] Hillary Clinton, by then the Democratic nominee, told Israel's Channel 2 on 8 September 2016 that Iran had been "weeks away from breakout" before the 2015 deal, and that the deal "has made Israel safer." [cite:ir6_toi_clinton2016] In June 2025 President Trump said Iran was "very close" to a bomb. [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] Panetta's and Clinton's claims, like Gantz's, concerned breakout, the time to make enough fissile material, not a finished weapon.

The flat dated forecasts (Peres's 1999, the Mossad's 2010–11, the March 2011 estimate) did not come true, as Netanyahu's did not. Most later statements were claims about capability or about Israel's ability to strike, which Iran's expanded enrichment after 2019 partly bore out. What the record does not show is a single Israeli view: the same years produced Israeli voices that rejected both the timelines and the case for war, the subject of the next section.
