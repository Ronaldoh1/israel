The Police Exchanges

American police do not train in Israel under any US–Israeli government agreement. The trips that have taken police chiefs, sheriffs and senior officers to Israel since the 1990s were organized by three private bodies: a programme based at Georgia State University, a Washington defence think tank, and a Jewish civil-rights organization. Public money has paid for some of the travel, but the programmes themselves are private, and that shapes what can be known about them. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] [cite:ow6_jpost_adl]

GILEE. The oldest is the Georgia International Law Enforcement Exchange, founded in 1992 at Georgia State University by the criminologist Robert Friedmann as Atlanta prepared for the 1996 Olympic Games. It has run exchanges with several countries, including China before the 2008 Beijing Olympics and, at the State Department's request, post-Soviet states such as Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan; Israel is its best-known partner. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] Counts of its participants differ because they measure different things. GILEE's own website, as reported in 2022, said over 1,100 officials had completed 290 training programmes; a 2023 count put participants in its exchanges at about 1,250 executives and attendees at its workshops and conferences in the United States at about 43,000. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] [cite:ow6_wiki_gilee] The foundation of the Home Depot co-founder Bernard Marcus gave GILEE at least $1.25 million from 2005, and the Atlanta police department spent at least $25,500 a year on it from 2017 to 2021; the department says it uses no federal money for Israel training. [cite:ow6_reason_cops]

JINSA. The Jewish Institute for National Security of America began bringing American police to Israel in the summer of 2002, in a programme created by a former assistant director of the FBI, Steven Pomerantz. JINSA pays participants' costs; the Marcus foundation gave it at least $2.38 million from 2011. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] The Justice Department's community-policing office described the programme in 2013 as teaching intelligence gathering and sharing, early detection of plots, post-attack forensic investigation and the ideologies of terrorist groups, on the premise that "if properly trained, U.S. law enforcement can better prevent and respond to terrorist threats". [cite:ow6_cops_leep] In 2026 JINSA said its trips focus on management and policy, not physical tactics. [cite:ow6_cs_nypd2026]

The ADL. The Anti-Defamation League set up an Advanced Training School in 2002 and began its Israel seminars in 2004. A leaked internal memo put the cost at about $115,000 a trip, plus some $200,000 a year in staff time; departments paid about $5,000 an officer. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] [cite:ow6_jpost_adl] The 2016 seminar included meetings with Israeli police, a tour of Jerusalem, visits to Gilboa prison and Hebron, and a meeting with Palestinian police in Bethlehem. [cite:ow6_reason_cops]

Public money. The trips are private, but not wholly privately funded. Reason found that Homeland Security grants had paid $480,466 for police travel abroad since 2015, more than 80 per cent of it for training in Israel. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] Records are hard to obtain: when Georgia State students sought GILEE's records in 2011, the request was treated as a security matter, and Georgia later passed a law allowing such records to be sealed. [cite:ow6_reason_cops]

What the sponsors say. The organizers describe the programmes as counterterrorism and leadership education drawn from a country with long experience of attacks. Friedmann has said Israel was "more than willing to share its lessons learned", and that Israeli use-of-force rules are "much more restrictive" than American ones; Pomerantz wrote that Israel's lessons "remain pertinent" to American policing. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] The ADL says it does not conduct practical field training. [cite:ow6_reason_cops]

What the critics say. In August 2016, after the Justice Department's report on the Baltimore police, Amnesty International USA noted that Baltimore officials had received training from Israel's police, military and intelligence services, and that since 2002 the ADL, the American Jewish Committee's Project Interchange and JINSA had taken police chiefs from more than a dozen states to Israel. It argued that the trips exposed American officers to forces with documented records of excessive force and weak accountability, and concluded that for de-escalation and the policing of protest, "Israel is not such a partner". [cite:ow6_amnesty_2016] Jewish Voice for Peace, an advocacy group opposed to Israeli policy, launched its "Deadly Exchange" campaign in 2017 and argued in a 2018 report that "these programs facilitate an exchange of methods in state violence and control". [cite:ow6_reason_cops] That is the campaign's characterization, not a finding.

What the evidence shows. The strongest specific claims do not hold up. Reason's 2022 investigation found less evidence that the training causes American excessive force than its critics allege, and no evidence for the claim that American officers learn chokeholds in Israel; Israel's national police, JINSA and the ADL all deny it. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] Where the record is clearer is surveillance and secrecy: the investigation found that the trips helped police justify more surveillance, and the architect of the New York police's Demographics Unit, which mapped Muslim communities, described it as modelled on Israeli intelligence work in the West Bank. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] That account is reported, not documented in a policy paper; the next section sets out what is documented about New York.

The responses. The campaign changed some decisions. On 16 April 2018 the Durham, North Carolina, city council unanimously adopted a statement that it "opposes international exchanges with any country in which Durham officers receive military-style training"; the police chief said Durham had not joined any Israel exchange under her leadership. [cite:ow6_aj_durham] In December 2018 the Vermont State Police and the police chief of Northampton, Massachusetts, withdrew from the ADL's New England seminar in Israel after local objections. [cite:ow6_toi_newengland] The ADL paused its trips in 2019. A June 2020 draft memo by two of its vice-presidents recommended ending them, warning however that doing so "could imply culpability when ADL does not believe we did anything wrong"; the ADL decided instead to continue with a revised curriculum, but as of 2022 no trip had resumed. [cite:ow6_jpost_adl] [cite:ow6_reason_cops] This book found no later report of a resumption.
