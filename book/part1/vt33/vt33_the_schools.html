The Schools: What the Mysteries and the Philosophers Taught About the Soul

A widely repeated claim holds that the ancient world possessed "mystery schools" which taught, under oath of secrecy, a science of the soul: how it descends into a body, how it can be purified, and how it can rise again toward the divine, and that this knowledge was deliberately closed down when the Roman Empire became Christian. Before the closings can be weighed, the schools themselves have to be described from the record. That record is uneven. Some of these institutions kept their secret so well that almost nothing of their inner teaching survives; others, the philosophical schools, wrote a great deal down. This section sets out what can be documented about each, using their own texts where they exist and their critics' texts where they do not. The Eleusinian rites, the Isis initiation in Apuleius, the Hermetica and the general pattern of ascent across traditions are treated in an earlier volume and are not repeated here. [scene:ts13_mysteries|The Mysteries and the Ascent]

Two kinds of school

The word "school" covers two different things, and the distinction matters for everything that follows. The mystery cults (Eleusis, Samothrace, Isis, Mithras and others) were rites. A person was initiated, usually once, sometimes in stages, and was bound not to reveal what was shown. They had priesthoods and sanctuaries but no doctrinal books that have come down to us. The philosophical schools (Pythagorean, Platonic and later Neoplatonic) were teaching institutions. They had heads, pupils, lectures and libraries, and their doctrine of the soul's ascent was argued in writing. When the Christian empire acted against "the mysteries", it acted against both, but in different ways and with different results: the rites ended, while much of the philosophy passed into the hands of the people who had closed the temples. [cite:vt33_sep_pythagoreanism] [cite:vt33_sep_proclus]

Eleusis: the grade of seeing

At Eleusis, initiation had more than one level. Among those who attended the Greater Mysteries were first-time initiates and those who had returned and attained the epopteia, the state of the epoptai, "those who have seen", described as having learned the secrets of the greatest mysteries of Demeter. [cite:vt33_nwe_eleusis] What they saw is not recorded. What the initiates said they gained is. Cicero, himself an initiate, wrote that the mysteries had shown Romans how to live joyfully and had taught them "how to die with a better hope". [cite:vt33_wh_eleusis] That phrase, a better hope at death, is the most secure statement of what Eleusis offered: not a doctrine, but a changed relation to mortality, conferred by an experience.

Samothrace: open to all who came

The Sanctuary of the Great Gods on the island of Samothrace ran the second most famous Greek mystery cult. Its distinguishing feature, in the record, is who it admitted. Anyone who could reach the island and was free of blood guilt could be initiated, "whether enslaved, free, Greek, non-Greek, male, or female". Most ancient sources say the gods offered protection at sea; the historian Diodorus Siculus claimed initiates became "more pious and more just and better in all ways". Philip II of Macedon and Olympias, the parents of Alexander, were initiated there; Plutarch says they were initiated at the same time and that Philip fell in love with her and was betrothed to her. [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] [cite:vt33_audit_plutarch_alex2] What the island did not experience, on the present evidence, is a dramatic closure. The last securely dated list of initiates belongs to the later second century CE, activity may have continued into the third or fourth, and the excavator Bonna Wescoat's view is that the cult "gradually died out". [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] Samothrace is the first reminder in this part that not every mystery ended by force.

Orphic tablets and Pythagorean souls

The most direct surviving instructions for the soul's journey after death are not in a book at all. About forty thin gold tablets, most from the fourth to the first centuries BCE, were placed in graves in southern Italy, Sicily, Greece and Crete, on the chest, mouth or hand of the dead. They tell the deceased to avoid one spring and to drink instead from the cold water of the Lake of Memory, and they give a password to say to the guardians: "I am the son of Earth and Starry Heaven. But my race is heavenly." The label "Orphic" is a modern one; none of the tablets names Orpheus, though they reflect initiations associated with Orpheus and probably Dionysus. [cite:vt33_getty_goldtablets] The teaching they carry is clear enough: the soul has a heavenly origin, forgetting is the danger, and the initiate who remembers can claim a better lot.

Pythagoras, in the sixth century BCE, taught a related doctrine in a philosophical community. He held that the soul is immortal and passes into new bodies, human or animal (metempsychosis). Some Pythagorean teaching was secret, and a letter attributed to Lysis urged that the master's doctrines not be presented in public to the uninitiated, but much was openly taught. Late sources describe a division between the akousmatikoi, who followed oral rules for living, and the mathematikoi, who studied mathematics; some scholars think that split is a later invention. The connection between Orphic writings and Pythagoreans is real but, in the Stanford Encyclopedia's word, "elusive". [cite:vt33_sep_pythagoreanism]

Mithras: a ladder of seven gates

The Roman cult of Mithras, which flourished from the first to the fourth century CE among soldiers, officials and traders, left hundreds of underground sanctuaries and almost no texts. [cite:vt21_wiki_mithraism] Two pieces of evidence describe its inner structure. The first is a hostile literary witness at two removes. The second-century philosopher Celsus, as quoted by the Christian writer Origen in his reply, described a Mithraic "ladder with seven gates and, at the top, an eighth gate", each gate made of a metal and assigned to a planet: lead to Saturn, tin to Venus, copper to Jupiter, iron to Mercury, a mixed alloy to Mars, silver to the Moon and gold to the Sun. Celsus presented it as a symbol of the soul's passage through the heavens. Origen dismissed it. [cite:vt33_harland_celsus]

The second is archaeological. In the Mithraeum of Felicissimus at Ostia, Rome's port, installed probably in the second half of the third century, a floor mosaic runs from the entrance toward the altar in seven panels. Each pairs a grade of initiation with a planet and its symbols: Raven with Mercury, Bridegroom with Venus, Soldier with Mars, Lion with Jupiter, Persian with the Moon, Sun-Runner with the Sun, and Father with Saturn. An inscription names the donor, Felicissimus, who made it in fulfilment of a vow. [cite:vt33_ostia_felicissimus] The two orders do not match; Celsus's ladder and the Ostia floor rank the planets differently, which is itself evidence that the cult was not uniform. Both, however, document the same idea: a member advanced through seven ranks tied to the seven planets, and the walk from door to altar was a walk upward through the sky.

Isis

The cult of Isis spread from Egypt across the Roman world and is the one mystery whose initiation has a first-person literary account, the eleventh book of Apuleius's novel the Metamorphoses. Its narrator says he approached the boundary of death, passed through the elements, saw the sun shining at midnight and returned. [cite:ts13_cam_apuleius] Its great temples, including the one at Philae on the Nile, outlived most others; how the last of them closed is set out in the next section.

The philosophers of ascent

The fullest written doctrine of ascent belongs to the Neoplatonists. Plotinus, who taught in Rome in the third century, described the soul's return to the One through purification, the awakening of intellect and, finally, a union that cannot be forced. [cite:ts13_mac_plotinus] His pupil and editor Porphyry reported that during the six years he spent in the school, Plotinus four times attained union with the God "who has neither shape nor form". [cite:vt33_voegelin_plotinus] This is the closest the ancient sources come to a documented claim of repeated mystical experience by a named teacher.

The next generation divided over method. Porphyry wrote critically about ritual; Iamblichus, in the work known as On the Mysteries, answered that the soul is raised not by thought alone but by theurgy, rites that bring the soul into contact with the gods. [cite:ts13_wiki_iamblichus] Theurgy became the practice of the later schools. Proclus, who headed the Platonic school at Athens as its diadochos, Plato's "successor", for almost fifty years until his death in 485, practised it according to his pupil Marinus, wrote hymns to the gods and composed the Elements of Theology, a chain of more than two hundred propositions deriving all reality from the One. [cite:vt33_sep_proclus] In Proclus the two kinds of school met: a philosopher who argued the ascent in writing and also performed the rites that were meant to accomplish it.

The table shows why "the mystery schools" is a modern umbrella rather than an ancient institution. There was no single network, no shared hierarchy and no common text. There was a widely shared idea, documented in very different forms: that the soul is not at home in the body, that it can be prepared, and that a rite or a discipline can change what happens to it. That idea is what the next section follows into the decades when the temples were closed, and the final section follows into the institutions that, in the end, kept much of it.
