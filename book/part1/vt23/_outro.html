Part Twenty-Three established that the Christian canon was sorted over three centuries by named bishops and scholars in Lyon, Alexandria, Antioch, Caesarea, Bethlehem and Carthage, with Rome asked to confirm Africa's list in 419 and doubting Hebrews in Eusebius's day, and was made binding for Catholics only at Trent on 8 April 1546, by 24 votes to 15. [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt23_na_carthage] [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] Measured by gospels and revelations, most of the books named in the sources were left out: four of at least twenty-one gospels and one of five revelations were kept. Measured against the books bishops actually debated, most were kept: 27 of the 38 titles in Eusebius's catalogue. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt23_bt_origen_luke] The documents excluded books from public worship, warned against them and pronounced anathemas, but none examined here ordered a gospel destroyed; the documented burning order of the period was Constantine's against Arius's writings. [cite:vt23_na_festal39] [cite:vt23_na_socrates1] Today's Bibles still differ, from the Protestant 66 to the Ethiopian 81, which keeps Enoch and Jubilees. [cite:vt23_wiki_ot] [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] Part Twenty-Four turns to the people who read many of the excluded books: the teachers of gnosis whom the Sophia platform calls the Evangelical Gnostics.
