Oslo in Gaza, 1994–2000

The Oslo agreements came to Gaza first. The Declaration of Principles signed in Washington on 13 September 1993 promised Palestinian self-government for an interim period of up to five years, beginning with an Israeli withdrawal from Gaza and the West Bank town of Jericho, with talks on Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, security and borders deferred. [cite:gz_wiki_oslo1] This section sets out what the agreement for Gaza transferred, what Israel kept, and how the economic arrangements and closures of those years kept the decisions that governed the strip's livelihood outside it.

The Cairo agreement. On 4 May 1994 Yitzhak Rabin, now prime minister, and Yasser Arafat signed the Agreement on the Gaza Strip and the Jericho Area. It created the Palestinian Authority, provided for a Palestinian police force, and required Israeli forces to withdraw from parts of the Gaza Strip and from Jericho within three weeks. Israeli troops remained at the Rafah crossing into Egypt and around the Israeli settlements. The agreement also carried the Paris Protocol, which set the economic relations between the Authority and Israel and integrated the Palestinian economy into Israel's. [cite:gz_wiki_gazajericho] On 1 July 1994 Arafat returned to Gaza City, his first visit in twenty-five years, and on 5 July the Authority was formally inaugurated. In 1995 the agreement was folded into the broader interim agreement known as Oslo II. [cite:gz_wiki_gazajericho]

Closure and permits. The agreements did not change the arrangement that governed the strip's largest source of wages. From the early 1990s Israel had imposed a regime of closures under which Palestinian workers needed permits subject to security clearance, and the Oslo accords left that regime in place. [cite:gz_carnegie_labor] A worker's income now depended on a permit that Israel could suspend at any time, on a security judgement that was Israel's alone. The pattern, of a Palestinian government responsible for the strip's people while the strip's borders, crossings and access to work were controlled from outside, is the one that runs through the rest of this volume.

The interim period was meant to end on 4 May 1999 with a permanent agreement. [cite:gz_wiki_oslo1] It did not. The second intifada began on 28 September 2000, after Ariel Sharon's visit to the Temple Mount, known to Muslims as the Haram al-Sharif, and is usually dated to the truce of February 2005. By 30 April 2008, B'Tselem had counted 4,745 Palestinians killed by Israeli security forces and about 1,053 Israelis killed by Palestinians since September 2000. From Gaza, armed groups fired Qassam rockets and mortars at Israeli towns such as Sderot, and Israeli operations in Rafah destroyed more than 1,500 houses, according to Human Rights Watch. [cite:gz_wiki_intifada2] Before the uprising was over, the Israeli government had decided on a course that no agreement required: to leave Gaza on its own terms.
