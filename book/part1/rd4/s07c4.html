Henry Wallace and Zionism

In 1944 Henry Wallace, Roosevelt's vice president, was replaced on the Democratic ticket by Harry Truman, the man who would later recognize the state of Israel. Given that sequence, it is natural to wonder whether Wallace's views on Palestine played a part in his removal. One version of the story holds that Zionist-aligned donors and organizations preferred Truman, after Wallace said something unfavorable about Palestine or about Jews. The documented record runs the other way at almost every point.

Wallace's sympathy for Zionism was personal and long-standing. It began in the 1930s, when he was Secretary of Agriculture, through his association with the soil scientist Walter Clay Lowdermilk, who had examined agricultural conditions in British Mandatory Palestine. Wallace later recalled of Lowdermilk: "I don't think he has any Jewish blood, but he was the most complete Zionist convert anyone could ask for." Under that influence, Wallace came to see Jewish settlers in Palestine as frontiersmen in the American mold. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] [cite:algemeiner_wallace_zionist] The conviction predated the politics of 1944 by roughly a decade. That establishes where it came from; it does not mean every later public statement he made on the subject was free of political calculation.

His removal in 1944 is documented as a fight over something else. According to historical accounts, Roosevelt grew wary of Wallace's left-wing, pro-Soviet political tilt, and that broader concern, not any statement about Palestine, Jews or Zionism, drove the push to replace him. It was not a quiet decision. Labor organizations, including the CIO and the UAW, kept actively supporting Wallace at the convention itself, and a Gallup poll taken on the day of the convention showed 65% of delegates still favoring him over Truman. [cite:wiki_1944_dnc] [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] The sources describe the general pattern historians identify as the main driver of his removal. They do not account for every reason every delegate opposed him, but they show no documented link to the Palestine question.

Four years later, Wallace returned to the story from an unexpected direction. Running for president in 1948 as the candidate of the Progressive Party, he attacked Truman from the pro-Israel side. His campaign literature included a booklet, Meet Henry Wallace, written by James Waterman Wise, the son of Rabbi Stephen Wise. It featured a half-page photograph of Wallace "helping the Jewish settlers in Galilee harvest their fields." At rallies, Wallace accused Truman of "playing politics with the lives of the people of Israel," aiming directly at Truman's arms embargo on the newly declared state. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] Israel was not the center of his campaign, which was built mainly around opposition to Cold War policy and support for continued engagement with the Soviet Union. But it was a real and deliberate part of his appeal to Jewish voters.

The appeal had measurable effects. New York newspapers reported widespread talk of Jewish voters leaving Truman for Wallace out of dissatisfaction with Truman's record on Palestine. Truman's share of the New York Jewish vote was the lowest for any Democratic presidential candidate in modern history, except Jimmy Carter's. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] [cite:algemeiner_wallace_zionist] The Palestine record is credited as one contributing factor in that decline, not the only one; other parts of Wallace's campaign, and broader dissatisfaction with Truman, likely played a part as well.

Taken together, the record reverses the intuitive version of events twice. Wallace's removal in 1944 concerned his general ideological direction, not Palestine, and Wallace himself held a genuine, decade-old sympathy for Zionism. In 1948 he did not fade from the story. He became one of Truman's most direct pro-Israel critics, at a documented cost to Truman in New York. If the episode reveals an electoral incentive, it points away from the one commonly assumed. In 1948, what the record shows carrying a price at the ballot box was being seen as insufficiently pro-Israel, not being too closely associated with it.
