Begin and "Judea and Samaria", 1977–1982

In May 1977 the Likud, led by Menachem Begin, won an Israeli general election for the first time, ending the Labor movement's hold on government since 1948. Begin came from the Revisionist current described in Part Two, and he brought into office a vocabulary as well as a programme. Within days of the vote he set out his position in public. As reported in the Detroit Jewish News of 27 May 1977, he said that Judea and Samaria, the biblical names he used for the West Bank, "cannot be given up in any part". He also said that their Arab residents "can have complete cultural autonomy" and that "it is not an occupied country". [cite:gi5_djn_begin77] Soon after the election he visited the Elon Moreh group of Gush Emunim, which the previous government had allowed to stay at the Kadum army camp after the stand-off at Sebastia, and told the settlers: "Soon, there will be many Elon Morehs." [cite:gi5_akevot_tzipori] [cite:wb_ej_gush] [scene:gi4_movement|Part Four: the Land of Israel Movement and Gush Emunim]

The words mattered because they changed the frame. Labor governments after 1967 had spoken of the West Bank as territory held pending a settlement. They had built settlements, mostly in the Jordan Valley and around Jerusalem along the lines of the Allon Plan, which offered the populated hills back to Jordan. [cite:wb_wiki_allon] Begin rejected the word "occupied" and spoke of the land as the Jewish people's by right. That was the language of the platform on which he had been elected, examined in the next section. [cite:gi5_jvl_likud1977] [scene:gi5_likud|The platform and the party]

Settlement as government policy

The new government turned the movement's demand into state planning. Gush Emunim presented an "emergency" settlement plan. Its blueprint of October 1978 resembled the plans drawn up by Matityahu Drobles of the Jewish Agency's settlement department and by Ariel Sharon, Begin's agriculture minister. Unlike the Allon Plan, these placed settlements in the hill country itself, among the Palestinian towns. [cite:wb_ej_gush] [cite:wb_wiki_allon] The settler population outside East Jerusalem, about 4,400 when Begin took office, reached 23,700 by 1983, by Peace Now's count. The West Bank volume tells that story in detail. [cite:wb_peacenow_pop] [scene:wb4_movement|The West Bank, Documented: the movement and the government]

The United States objected from the start, and Begin answered on the record. In a message handed to Secretary of State Cyrus Vance and cabled to the embassy in Israel on 9 January 1978, Begin reported that his cabinet had decided "to establish four additional settlements, all of them in military camps". He relayed Foreign Minister Moshe Dayan's account of a meeting with President Carter, in which Carter spoke of "the illegality of the settlements and their being an obstacle to peace" and Dayan replied that "there cannot be a government in Israel that will not establish settlements." [cite:gi5_frus_begin78] Begin's own position in the same message was that his government kept "scrupulously" to every promise it had made. The American position, as Dayan reported it, was that the settlements were illegal. Both are recorded in the US government's own documentary history. [cite:gi5_frus_begin78]

The courts set a limit. In the Elon Moreh case of 1979 Israel's Supreme Court held that taking private Palestinian land for a settlement was unlawful when the motive was political and ideological rather than military. After that ruling the authorities turned to declaring "state land" under the Ottoman Land Code, a mechanism explained in the West Bank volume. [cite:wb_dweikat] [cite:wb_wiki_stateland] [scene:wb4_land|The West Bank, Documented: how the land was taken]

The autonomy plan: sovereignty claimed, then left open

Begin's formal proposal for the territories did not annex them. On 28 December 1977 his government put forward a 26-point "Self-Rule Plan" for the Arab residents of Judea, Samaria and Gaza. It offered administrative autonomy under an elected council. Security and public order would remain "the responsibility of the Israeli authorities", and residents would have "free choice of either Israeli or Jordanian citizenship". [cite:gi5_ecf_selfrule] It also stated that "residents of Israel will be entitled to acquire land and settle" in the territories. On the central question it made a claim and then set it aside. Israel, the plan said, stood by its claim of sovereignty over the areas but, knowing that other claims existed, proposed for the sake of peace that "the question of sovereignty in these areas be left open". The principles were to be reviewed after five years. [cite:gi5_ecf_selfrule]

That formula carried into the Camp David Framework of 17 September 1978, which provided for "transitional arrangements for the West Bank and Gaza for a period not exceeding five years" and "full autonomy to the inhabitants" under an elected self-governing authority. [cite:gz_campdavid] [scene:gz2_economy|Gaza, Documented: Camp David and the territories] The autonomy talks that followed produced no agreement. The pattern of Begin's years is that sovereignty was asserted in speeches and the platform and was not legislated for the West Bank and Gaza, while settlement there expanded under government plans. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:wb_peacenow_pop]

Two laws and one withdrawal

In Begin's years the Knesset did legislate on two other territories. In 1980 it passed the Jerusalem Law, a Basic Law on the capital that entrenched the application of Israeli law to an enlarged East Jerusalem in June 1967; Security Council Resolution 478 declared it null and void. [cite:wb_wiki_ejannex] [scene:wb3_jerusalem|The West Bank, Documented: East Jerusalem] On 14 December 1981 the Knesset passed the Golan Heights Law by 63 votes to 21. Its operative sentence applied "the Law, jurisdiction and administration of the State" to the Golan. [cite:gi5_wiki_golanlaw] The law does not use the word annexation. The Security Council answered three days later with Resolution 497, adopted unanimously, which declared the law "null and void and without international legal effect". The United States suspended the strategic cooperation memorandum it had signed with Israel two weeks earlier. [cite:gi5_wiki_golanlaw] [cite:ow1_wiki_1981isr] The Golan front, including the American recognition of 2019, is covered in Part Eight. [scene:gi8_golan|Part Eight: the Golan]

In the same years Begin's government gave back far more land than it annexed. Under the peace treaty with Egypt it withdrew from the whole of Sinai, completing the handover on 25 April 1982. The settlement town of Yamit was evacuated and levelled, and Sharon, by then defence minister, said: "In Sinai, in Yamit, we have reached the red line of our concessions." [cite:gi1_csm_sinai1982] [scene:gi12_withdrawals|Part Twelve: the withdrawals] The record of Begin's government does not show the land-claim language of 1977 applied everywhere Israel held territory. It shows that language applied to the land west of the Jordan, the land of the platform. There the government settled, claimed sovereignty and left the claim formally open. Sinai, outside that claim, was traded for peace.
