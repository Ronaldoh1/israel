Three Functions, One Test

A volume that calls three cities the parents of a state owes the reader a definition of the word. Without one, any contact between a city's institutions and the Zionist movement or the State of Israel can be counted as parenthood, and the claim becomes impossible to test because nothing could count against it. This section sets out the definition the volume uses, the three functions it assigns to the three cities, the kinds of document that would count as evidence for each, and the single test that each city's part ends with. It also states, in its strongest form, the hypothesis about Rome that gives this volume its sharpest question.

What a parent is here. In this volume a parent is a power that supplied something the Jewish national home, and later the State of Israel, could not have had without it, at a time when no one else was supplying it. Three parts of that definition do the work. The thing supplied must be specific and documented: a letter, a law, a loan, a vote, a shipment. It must have been needed: something the movement or the state could not readily have found elsewhere. And it must be dated, because the same power can be indispensable in one decade and obstructive in the next, as Part Two shows for London. [scene:dv2_parent_test|London's test] is the model for the others.

Three functions. Each of the three cities is assigned one function, because each stands, in the popular telling, for one kind of power.

London stands for finance: money, credit, and the legal and institutional forms through which money moves. Evidence for this function would be companies chartered there, loans raised there, currency issued or reserves held there, and the acts of the government seated there that gave the movement a legal frame in which its money could work.

Rome stands for theology: the religious ideas that gave a political project legitimacy in the eyes of Christians, and the institutional decisions of the Catholic Church about the Jewish people, the Holy Land and the state. Evidence for this function would be papal and curial acts, the Holy See's diplomatic record, doctrinal statements, and the documented reception of those statements by governments and believers. Because Christian Zionism, the movement that gave Zionism its broadest Christian support, grew mainly out of Protestant theology, the part on Rome has to ask whose theology did the parenting, not only what the Vatican said. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism]

Washington stands for force: arms, money for arms, diplomatic protection and recognition. Evidence for this function would be the acts of the United States government: recognition, votes and vetoes at the United Nations, aid agreements, appropriations, and arms transfers, each dated and sourced.

The fourth step matters most. The test is built to look as hard for what a city withheld as for what it gave. That is not a gesture of balance. It is the only way to tell a parent from a power that happened to share some interests with the movement for a time. A city whose institutions supplied something indispensable and never opposed the project would be a parent in the fullest sense. A city that supplied something and later fought the project, as London did after 1939, is a parent of a more limited kind. And a city whose documented acts were mainly opposition would not be a parent at all, unless the opposition can be shown to have been a cover.

The front hypothesis. That last possibility is the subject of this volume's hardest chapter. The publicly documented record of the Holy See toward Zionism, as the earlier volumes of this library show, is largely one of refusal. In January 1904, according to Herzl's diary, Pius X told him that he could not support Zionism, because the Jews had not recognized Christ; in April 1949 Pius XII asked the world's Catholics to press for an international status for Jerusalem; and the Holy See did not recognize the State of Israel until it signed the Fundamental Agreement on 30 December 1993. [cite:dv1_ej_piusx] [cite:dv1_rnc_1949] [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The hypothesis this volume tests is that this refusal was a public facade: that the Holy See was connected to the project from the start, and kept its support out of view so that no one would question it. It is a serious hypothesis about how power behaves, and it is stated here in its strongest form so that it can be tested fairly.

A hypothesis of this kind makes predictions, and that is what allows it to be tested. If the refusal was a front, the private record should contradict the public one: there should be documents in which papal diplomats encouraged what they publicly opposed, money or favors moving from the Holy See to the movement, or a pattern in which public opposition never cost the movement anything it needed. If the refusal was genuine, the private record should match the public one, and the opposition should have been costly: it should have spent the Holy See's own influence, provoked complaint from Zionist leaders, and left traces in the archives of governments the Holy See lobbied. The archives of Pius XII's pontificate, opened to researchers in 2020, are the most important new source on this question, [cite:wiki_pius_xii_holocaust] and Part Three draws on what has been published from them.

One piece of evidence often cited for the hypothesis needs to be placed correctly from the start. The documented loans between the papacy and the Rothschild houses in the nineteenth century ran from the bankers to the Pope, not the other way, beginning with the loan agreed in 1831 with the Papal States. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] They show a financial relationship between a Jewish banking family and the papal government half a century before political Zionism existed. Whether that relationship bears on the Holy See's later conduct toward Zionism is one of the questions Part Three takes up; [scene:bc_s03|the library's account of the papal loans] sets out what is known about them and how thin the sourcing is.

What the test does not assume. The test is applied to each city on its own evidence. It does not assume that the three cities coordinated, and it does not assume that they did not. If documents show coordination, the composite part of this volume will report them. [scene:bc_s06|The library's earlier examination of the three-enclaves claim] found none, and the parts that follow begin from that finding without treating it as final. What the volume will not do is infer coordination from parallel interests. Three governments can each support the same outcome for reasons of their own, and the record of such convergence is a finding in its own right, not evidence of a single hand.

Some claims about the three cities are kept out of this volume unless new documents support them, because no record has been found for them. They include the claim that the Orsini family descends from the biblical Nimrod, the claim that an Act of Congress of 1871 turned the United States into a corporation, the reading of the District of Columbia's flag as an emblem of the three cities, the claim that the Rockefeller and Rothschild fortunes are one, and the claim that a single coordinated group directs all three cities. Each is named so that the reader knows it was considered and why it is absent.

The test is the same in every part, and so is its last question. For London the answer is already in: a financial and imperial parent, through the British state and the London market rather than the City Corporation, decisive between 1917 and 1939 and then withdrawing. Rome and Washington are tested in the same way, and the fourth sovereign, the child, is examined in its own right before the volume sets the three verdicts side by side.
