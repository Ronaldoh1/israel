The Nuremberg Laws: Persecution Given Legal Form

A familiar assumption holds that Nazi persecution began with violence and that the legal apparatus came afterward, almost as an afterthought. The sequence was the reverse. Before the mass violence of Kristallnacht, before the camps, the regime built a legal architecture of exclusion. In September 1935 the Reichstag passed, unanimously, two laws that defined who counted as German by blood and stripped German Jews of citizenship. Law came before the pogrom.

Both laws were passed on September 15, 1935, at a special session of the Nazi-controlled Reichstag held during the party rally at Nuremberg [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_laws]. The first, the Reich Citizenship Law, defined a citizen as a person "of German or related blood" who proves through conduct a willingness to serve the German people and Reich [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. That single definition reduced German Jews from citizens to "subjects" without full political rights. No violence was required. A population lost its legal standing through a statute.

The second, the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honor, reached into private life. It banned marriage between Jews and "subjects of the state of German or related blood," and declared such marriages invalid even if they were concluded abroad in order to evade the law. It criminalized "extramarital intercourse" between the two groups. It barred Jews from employing non-Jewish German women under 45 in their households. Violations were punishable by imprisonment [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. The regime's own term for the criminalized category was Rassenschande, "race defilement," a word that reflects the law's ideological framing [cite:wiki_rassenschande]. The statute itself is documented; its enforcement varied in intensity across regions and years, and the text alone does not record every prosecution brought under it.

The laws did not define a Jew by belief. Under the Nuremberg Race Laws, a person with three or four Jewish grandparents was Jewish, whatever his or her religious practice [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. Germans who had not practiced Judaism in decades, and even people who had formally converted to Christianity, could be classified as Jewish. Conviction, conversion and long non-observance offered no legal escape. Ancestry alone decided.

The bureaucracy then refined the categories. On November 14, 1935, the First Supplementary Decree to the Reich Citizenship Law created the Mischling, or "mixed-blood," classification for people with one or two Jewish grandparents, with its own separate legal restrictions [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. Different degrees of ancestry carried different legal consequences. Charts were produced to help officials, and ordinary citizens, determine a person's racial status under the law [cite:facinghistory_nuremberg_laws]. A person's legal existence was reduced to a genealogical calculation. The full administrative detail of the Mischling categories goes beyond what is set out here; what matters is that the system existed and how it worked.

The Reichstag that passed these laws was the same body whose surrender of authority in 1933 is described in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse]. Having voted away its power to check the government, it now met to pass, unanimously, the laws that excluded Jews from citizenship.

The order of events is the point. By September 1935 Germany had a complete legal framework, passed without dissent, that defined Jewish identity by blood, stripped citizenship and criminalized intermarriage. That framework came years before the mass violence of Kristallnacht, which escalated what the statutes had established. Exclusion was legislated first and then enforced through escalating violence. Only weeks before the Nuremberg session, in August 1935, the Zionist Congress at Lucerne had voted to continue its transfer agreement with the same state, as described in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement].
