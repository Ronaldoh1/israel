Who Bought the Land, From Whom, and at What Cost

A common account holds that the British government sold Palestinian land to outside Zionist buyers, and that it did so with little scrutiny and little resistance. Almost every part of that sentence fails against the documents. The land was bought in private transactions, overwhelmingly from absentee landowners who had acquired large estates under Ottoman rule. The British, when they came, regulated the process rather than selling into it. And resistance was there from the first large purchase: an Ottoman governor tried to block it in 1910, and two decades later tenants fought an eviction through the courts for years. The mechanism that did the work was assembled before 1917, and its first test came in the Jezreel Valley.

The chain of ownership began before Zionism existed as an organized movement. In 1872 the Ottoman government itself sold the Jezreel Valley, Marj ibn Amir, to the Sursock family of Beirut, wealthy Greek Orthodox Christians.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] From that moment the valley was no longer, in any sense, land the government held; it was the private estate of a family that lived in another city. Between 1901 and 1925, and mainly in the 1920s, the family sold roughly 60,000 to 75,000 dunams to Zionist purchasing organizations, for a fraction of the land's later value. The Sursock Purchases alone represent approximately 22 percent of all Jewish land acquired in Palestine through 1948, and 58 percent of all land bought specifically from absentee landlords.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:sursockhouse_rothschild_purchases] The chain ran through three parties and contained no government sale to Zionist buyers: the Ottoman state sold to a Beirut family, and the family later sold to Zionist purchasers.

That last step is the hinge of the whole mechanism. Ottoman property law recognized two main categories of land. Mulk was true private freehold. Miri was state land held on a kind of lease, but treated in practice as owned by whoever held the registered rights to it. Most of the agricultural land that tenant farmers worked fell into the miri category, and the registered rights belonged to the landlord, even when the landlord had never once worked the land himself. Families who had cultivated the same fields for generations under hereditary arrangements, paying tithes to absentee owners like the Sursocks, typically held no registered title at all. When the landlord sold, the tenant had no ownership claim to assert.[cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] [cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws] [cite:wiki_jewish_land_purchase_full] The sale was lawful, and so was the eviction that followed; under the law of the time they were the same transaction. Nothing about this was a hidden device. It was a documented feature of Ottoman land tenure that predated Zionist land purchase entirely, and the record does not show that it was created for that purpose. What the record shows is that it was used, extensively, once purchase began on a large scale.

It would be wrong, though, to say that no law stood in the way. Starting in 1881–82 the Ottoman government issued a restriction specific to Palestine: foreign Jews could settle anywhere else in the empire, but not there. By March 1883 only Ottoman Jewish citizens could buy land in Palestine at all; foreign citizens were barred outright. In 1892 Sultan Abdul Hamid II went further, requiring imperial approval even for Ottoman Jewish citizens to buy.[cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws] [cite:wiki_jewish_land_purchase_full] [cite:state_gov_1892_naturalization]

The Ottoman authorities explained their reasoning to foreign diplomats, and the explanation survives in American correspondence from the 1890s. American diplomats reported the Ottoman position that individual visitors of any background were welcome, but that the government "does not regard it to its interest to have too large a number of any race or nationality established there." What it opposed was organized, large-scale colonization aimed at a concentrated territorial presence.[cite:state_gov_1892_naturalization] That distinction, between individual presence, which was tolerated, and organized mass settlement toward territorial concentration, which was resisted, was the government's own. It was also an exact description of what Herzl's movement had been organized to achieve.

The restrictions were evaded by three documented routes. First, Ottoman Jewish citizens, who were still permitted to buy, purchased land and later transferred it to Zionist organizations. Second, Jews holding British, French or German passports could sometimes invoke the capitulations, the extraterritorial privileges that European powers had negotiated with the Ottoman Empire, and claim consular protection that Ottoman domestic law could not easily override. Third, and most directly documented, United States State Department correspondence from the 1890s records Russian Jewish emigrants who traveled to America, completed naturalization, and returned to Palestine as American citizens in order to claim American consular protection for residence and land purchase. One named case, that of Simon Ben Nachman Lowenstein, was argued over directly between American and Ottoman diplomats.[cite:state_gov_1892_naturalization] [cite:wiki_jewish_land_purchase_full]

Scholars who have examined the period agree that the restrictions "created real obstacles without constituting an absolute prohibition," and the totals bear that out. After roughly four decades of organized purchasing against these laws, Jewish-held land in Palestine in 1918 came to approximately 418,000 dunams, around 2 percent of the territory.[cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws] [cite:wiki_jewish_land_purchase_full] The law did not stop the process. It slowed it considerably.

Three named organizations did the buying and holding, and it is worth being exact about which did what. The Jewish National Fund, founded in 1901, was the holding body: land went in and, by design, never came back out, held as inalienable Jewish national property. The negotiating was done by the Palestine Land Development Company, led by Yehoshua Hankin, who personally negotiated the Sursock deal and most of the major Jezreel Valley acquisitions. The third, the Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, or PICA, was founded in 1924 by Baron Edmond de Rothschild, a man who had opposed Herzl personally; it ran a separate, parallel operation on Rothschild money and became the single largest Jewish landowner in Palestine.[cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]

Hankin was not an anonymous institutional process but a named individual at the center of the whole mechanism. His working relationship with the Sursock family dated to 1891, and in 1932 he became head of the land development company.[cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] The point of naming him and the companies is not to allege illegality, since the purchases were lawful under the property law of the time, but to document who did the buying and how.

The answer to a reader's obvious question, how the buyers knew where to buy, is bureaucratic. In 1907 the Zionist Organization sent Arthur Ruppin, a German-trained lawyer and economist, to make a pilot study of colonization in Palestine. In 1908 he opened the Palestine Office in Jaffa, and the same year, by this account, founded the Palestine Land Development Company as its operational purchasing arm; other sources date the company to 1909, a discrepancy taken up with the office's founding in [scene:s04g|the Palestine Office and its land company]. Ruppin identified the strategic importance of the Jezreel Valley as early as 1907, years before the large Sursock sales closed.[cite:wiki_arthur_ruppin] His office coordinated directly with the Jewish National Fund, negotiated with Ottoman and later British authorities on behalf of Jewish immigrants, and, through the land company, handled the surveying, valuing and acquisition of land before settlers arrived. It turned land purchase from an individual pursuit into a coordinated institutional program, and Ruppin became known as "the father of Jewish settlement in Palestine."[cite:wiki_arthur_ruppin]

The same documented record contains a second fact about Ruppin, and it has to be stated with equal precision. His Wikipedia biography says that early in his career he "accepted the crude racial views of his age, including the idea that Jews were an inferior race," and that he later "believed that realization of Zionism required 'racial purity' of Jews and was influenced by works of antisemitic thinkers, including some Nazis." A peer-reviewed article in History of European Ideas examines these views at length. Haaretz, in its own retrospective, states plainly: "Arthur Ruppin believed that the realization of Zionism demanded 'racial purity' among the Jews." He is also documented as holding Ashkenazi Jews to be racially superior to Sephardi and Yemenite Jews, a view that, according to the same record, shaped in practice whom his office prioritized for settlement.[cite:wiki_arthur_ruppin] [cite:haaretz_ruppin_retrospective] [cite:tandfonline_ruppin_weltanschauung] Three independent kinds of source, an encyclopedia entry, peer-reviewed scholarship and the mainstream Israeli press, agree on this. The finding is about one individual who held real operational authority over settlement and land selection. It does not show that everyone involved in Zionist settlement shared his views, or that the purchase program as a whole was organized around them. Both facts about Ruppin, the institutional achievement and the racial ideology, are part of the accurate account; neither cancels the other.

The first large purchase in the valley shows the mechanism working, and shows that it was contested from the start. Ruppin recalled, in a memoir written in May 1929, that in February 1910 Hankin concluded an agreement for 9,500 dunams at Fule, al-Ful, the site that became Merhavia. The Jewish National Fund took over 3,500 dunams and the land company the rest.[cite:rs_rd1_ruppin_emek] The seller was Elias Sursuq, of the same Beirut family; one account describes the land as about 2,500 acres.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] Disputes over evicted tenant farmers followed this first significant acquisition, though the sources used here give no count of the tenants concerned.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases]

Ruppin's own account records the opposition. The Turkish government, he wrote, refused to authorize the sale, and "the Governor in Nazareth, a rabid anti-Zionist," declared that he would fight it. Permission was finally obtained through Hankin's friends in Constantinople.[cite:rs_rd1_ruppin_emek] The governor, according to the biographical record, was Shukri al-Asali, district governor (qaimaqam) of Nazareth from 1910, who tried and failed to stop the sale.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] He did not drop the matter. In 1911 he resigned to win a by-election to the Ottoman parliament for Damascus, and there he argued that Jewish settlers held Ottoman citizenship alongside foreign citizenship and were taking control of rural villages. He sat until April 1912.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] His argument about dual citizenship can be read as describing, from the other side, the evasion routes the American consuls had been documenting since the 1890s. On 6 May 1916, by order of Jamal Pasha, al-Asali was hanged in Marjeh Square in Damascus with a group of Arab nationalists, a wartime story told with [scene:rd1_wwi|Palestine at war], where the dating of those executions is also discussed.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki]

Ruppin's memoir also gives the long arc of prices and quantities, and the figures show how the valley moved from a single deal to a program. Hankin, he wrote, had first reached agreement as early as 1891 for 160,000 dunams at 15 francs a dunam. In 1920 about 70,000 dunams were bought at £3 a dunam for unirrigated land and £6.5 for partly irrigated land, about £300,000 in all. By 1929 about 290,000 dunams had been bought in the Emek and the Acre plain since 1910, for £970,000.[cite:rs_rd1_ruppin_emek] Elsewhere the record gives a broader benchmark: land sold by absentee landlords to Jewish buyers went for roughly six Palestine pounds a dunam by the early Mandate period.[cite:emory_zionist_land_acquisition] That figure sits at the upper end of Ruppin's 1920 prices, and either way the scale explains why a dedicated financial system existed.

Settlers were not, in the main, buying the land out of their own savings. Individual circumstances varied, and some settlers may have used personal savings alongside institutional support, but the purchases at this scale ran through a purpose-built banking and philanthropic system. The Jewish Colonial Trust, founded in 1899, was the Zionist Organization's overall financial instrument. Its subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, established in 1902 with Jaffa as its base, did the work on the ground: financing land purchases directly and running a network of credit unions that gave long-term agricultural loans to Jewish colonists. Herzl put the principle in one line: "Settlers in the Land of Israel need credit rather than charity." On the philanthropic side, from 1920, Keren Hayesod raised donations specifically for settlement infrastructure.[cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust] [cite:palquest_wzo_funding] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust]

Ownership of this system and control of it were deliberately separated. The Trust was not the property of one family or a small circle of bankers: over 100,000 people had subscribed for shares by 1899, so many that London's Somerset House needed special accommodation for the shareholder register. But the first 100 shares were designated founders' shares, carrying voting rights equal to all the other shares combined at any general meeting. However widely the rest were held, the founding Zionist leadership kept permanent control.[cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] [cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust] The preparatory committee that launched the Trust was David Wolffsohn, the designer of what became the Israeli flag, Bodenheimer of Cologne, and Rudolf Schauer of Mainz. Zalman David Levontin, whom Herzl personally summoned to a director's seat in 1901 and who had sold his own earlier land holdings to Edmond de Rothschild in 1883, ran the Jaffa branch from 1903.[cite:encyclopedia_levontin] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] The pattern lasted for decades. As late as 1969 the Trust controlled 88.3 percent of the voting ordinary shares of Bank Leumi, the bank's descendant, while holding only 23.5 percent of its total share capital, a structure that lasted until the late 1980s, when it was sold to private investors.[cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust]

The system was not always planned from the center. Purchasing agents like Hankin sometimes bought land first, at personal financial risk, and secured reimbursement from the Jewish Agency or the Jewish National Fund only afterward.[cite:wiki_jewish_colonial_trust] [cite:emory_zionist_land_acquisition]

How the sales looked depends heavily on who is describing them, and the two surviving accounts of al-Fula should be read side by side. Ruppin's 1929 memoir presents willing sales by owners who wanted cash, through a buyer they trusted: Hankin, he wrote, "enjoyed the confidence of the Arabs." His account does not mention the tenants on the land at all.[cite:rs_rd1_ruppin_emek] Al-Asali's record describes a sale that the Ottoman district governor tried to block and later raised in parliament as a danger to rural villages.[cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] Both can be true at once: the owners in Beirut may well have wanted cash and trusted Hankin, while the official responsible for the district saw the transaction as a threat to rural villages. Ruppin's case, at its strongest, is that this was ordinary commerce, lawful and voluntary on both sides of the contract. The opposing reading is that the contract had only two sides because the law left the cultivators off it.

Sellers, too, felt the pressure of their own society. Selling land to Jewish purchasers was regarded by some in the Arab community as a serious betrayal, and in at least one documented case negotiations stalled for years because the selling landowners did not want to be seen to take responsibility for evacuating the tenants.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]

No documented consultation preceded any of this. There is no documented record of a formal negotiation in which Zionist organizations sought the consent of the existing Arab political leadership, or of the Old Yishuv's leadership, before organized purchase began. Within the movement the warning had been given early: Ahad Ha'am, a committed cultural Zionist, wrote in 1891, in "Truth from Eretz Israel," that the Arab population was being treated as effectively invisible in Zionist planning, years before political Zionism was formally organized (his critique is set out with [scene:s04|the founding Congress and its critics]). What took place was land acquisition and settlement building toward a territorial and political fact, not a negotiated agreement, which is precisely why the Ottoman restrictions existed.

The Jewish communities that had lived in Palestine before organized Zionist immigration were neither uniform supporters of the purchases nor absent from them. The clearest documented window is Avraham Albert Antébi, a Sephardi Jew active in Jerusalem from 1897 to 1916. He ran the Caisse de Prêts, a low-interest loan fund that served both the Old Yishuv, the Sephardi and Ashkenazi communities already in the country, and the settlers of the New Yishuv. That role gave him working relationships with affluent Arab landowners and Ottoman officials as well as with the Zionist institutions. Research on his surviving correspondence, published in Shofar: An Interdisciplinary Journal of Jewish Studies, examines Sephardi reaction to Zionist land purchases and the Arab-Jewish tension that followed, and finds a community financially and socially entangled with every side of the transaction.[cite:shofar_antebi_sephardim] Antébi does not speak for every Old Yishuv resident; views varied by person, community and moment. What his record establishes is that some of the Jews already in Palestine watched the displacement from inside the deals themselves.

The human cost of the Sursock purchases is documented in specific figures. In the largest single group of Sursock-related purchases, 1,746 Arab farming families, 8,730 people, were evicted. Under the tenure system described above they were owed no compensation, since they held no registered title to compensate; they were paid roughly $17 a person as a voluntary goodwill settlement. Across the full span of Sursock-linked purchases, an estimated 20 to 25 villages were depopulated.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] These are the figures for the best-documented single group, not an estimate for every transaction of the period, which varied case by case; nor does the record show that every evicted family received exactly that amount.

Most of that cost fell after 1917, and the later history is told in later volumes, but its outline belongs here because the mechanism built before the war carried it. The main Sursock sales came in 1920–25, negotiated directly with Hankin.[cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] Britain, as the Mandate power, enacted five separate laws between 1920 and 1940 meant to protect tenant cultivators from eviction after a sale. Analysis of the period concludes that they "failed to provide the protection that Palestinian Arabs demanded, often due to loopholes, which allowed individuals and agencies to circumvent the legislation."[cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine]

Wadi al-Hawarith became the case that tested the system. In 1929 the Jewish National Fund bought 30,800 dunams there at auction from absentee landlords, which the historian Weldon Matthews counts as the third-largest single acquisition of the Fund in the whole Mandate period. The Bedouin Palestinian tenants who lived on the land refused to leave, and the Fund began formal eviction proceedings. Awni Abd al-Hadi, a significant Palestinian national political figure, represented the residents through a multi-year court battle. The final eviction order was issued on 15 June 1933, and the response was immediate: the Istiqlal Party, the Palestine Communist Party and the Congress of Youth called strikes and issued public declarations of protest.[cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith] The case does not show that every sale met organized opposition; many proceeded with less. It shows that resistance existed, was organized, and reached formal legal representation and cross-party protest when the stakes were high enough.

In 1938 the Mandate authorities moved in the opposite direction from their tenant laws. They granted Zionist officials direct permission to copy land-registration and taxation records documenting Arab land ownership, explicitly to facilitate further Zionist acquisition.[cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] Britain was neither seller nor bystander: it first attempted regulation that failed, then took a specific, dated step of facilitation. That does not mean Britain orchestrated the purchase program from the start, or that 1938 was the whole of its policy; its immigration restrictions in the same years, the 1939 White Paper among them, cut sharply the other way. The Mandate's record on land was inconsistent rather than a single coherent policy.

The end point of the land series puts the whole mechanism in proportion. By 1947–48 total Jewish holdings came to roughly 2 million dunams, about 7 percent of Mandate Palestine's roughly 26 million dunams, of which the Jewish National Fund held approximately 936,000.[cite:jnf_wiki] [cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] The panel below sets the 7 percent against the rest of the land; its second chart, the 1947 partition plan, belongs to a later volume. The Sursock land, around a fifth of everything acquired, was the single largest component of that total; and the restrictions, the evasions, the bank and the office that produced it were all in place before 1917.

What the record does not settle is substantial. Ruppin's memoir does not count the tenants at al-Fula, and the sources used here give no figure for them or for the price paid in 1910. The documented eviction figures, 1,746 families at about $17 a person, describe one large group of purchases, not the period as a whole, and there is no comparable count for the 418,000 dunams held by 1918. How the owners in Beirut weighed cash against the objections of officials like al-Asali survives only in the buyer's account. And how far Ruppin's racial views shaped individual settlement decisions, beyond the documented fact that they shaped his office's priorities, is not set out case by case in the record this book has.
