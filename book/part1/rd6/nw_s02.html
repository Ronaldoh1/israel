The Network of the American Build-Up, 1897–1948

A common picture of American support for Zionism before 1948 is of a loose and scattered sympathy: individuals of goodwill, a few friendly senators, some generous donors, acting each on their own. The documented record shows something more organized than that. Its pieces were connected, and the connections can be named: who founded which body, who chaired which committee, who sponsored which resolution, who raised which money and who carried which message to the President. Laid out by distance from the center, they form a network with a clear structure and two loops that fed themselves.

A word first on what such a map shows. Every tie below is a documented relationship of a specific kind: an organizational founding, a chairmanship, a sponsorship, an appointment, a transaction. None is inferred from shared identity, religion or background. A senator appears because he chaired a named committee or sponsored a named resolution; a donor appears because he raised a named sum. Where the record does not establish a tie, the map does not draw one.

At the apex stood the Jewish Agency and the World Zionist Organization, led in these years by Weizmann and Ben-Gurion. They set the movement's program and goals. [cite:aja_azec_local_1945] One later document defines the financial side of the relationship more sharply than anything from the 1940s. In November 1962 the Justice Department ordered the American Zionist Council to register as a foreign agent, alleging that it had received more than $5 million from the Jewish Agency over the preceding eight years. [cite:azc_fara_doj] The precision matters. The 1962 finding concerned the American Zionist Council, the body into which the wartime emergency council was renamed in 1949, and the years it covered; it was not a finding about the emergency council of the 1940s, and the record here does not document Agency funding of that earlier body. The later case is traced in [scene:s07g|the organization after statehood].

At the first degree, bound to the apex by direct organizational ties, stood three American bodies. The American Zionist Emergency Council, co-chaired by Abba Hillel Silver and Stephen Wise, ran the national political campaign of the 1940s. The Zionist Organization of America was the mass-membership body; it had reached 147,000 members by 1918. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944] Hadassah completed the first ring.

At the second degree were bodies created or coordinated by those first-degree organizations. The American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine presented themselves as independent voices of American and Christian opinion. Both were organized by the Zionist executive Emanuel Neumann, and one historian has described them as "hardly autonomous." [cite:hnn_clark_truman] The United Jewish Appeal, under its executive Henry Montor, was the fundraising engine described in [scene:gf_s01|the ledger of who paid]. The Sonneborn Institute and its front company, Materials for Palestine, were set up at Ben-Gurion's 1945 meeting with American donors, to buy arms. Abraham Feinberg, who appears again at the third degree, was one of the original seventeen members of the Sonneborn Institute. [cite:emory_feinberg_thesis]

At the third degree were the political and financial endpoints, the people through whom the network reached decisions. In Congress, Senator Robert Wagner chaired the American Palestine Committee, and Senator Robert Taft co-sponsored the 1944 Palestine resolution. Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and Representative Hamilton Fish III gave their names to the 1922 resolution endorsing the Balfour Declaration. Sol Bloom, chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, was a target of the telegram campaigns. [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation] In the White House, the aides David Niles, Clark Clifford and Max Lowenthal, and Truman's friend Eddie Jacobson, carried the Zionist case to the President. [cite:wiki_david_niles] Among donors, Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann raised the $100,000 that Truman's 1948 campaign needed, and Golda Meyerson's tour of 1948 raised about $50 million through Montor's network, largely for arms. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] [cite:israelaffairs_italy]

The diagram simplifies in one respect. The White House channel, Niles, Clifford, Lowenthal and Jacobson, sat at the third degree but did not hang beneath any one second-degree body; it connected to the legislators on one side and, through the 1948 campaign money, to the donors on the other. It is left out of the tree for legibility and shown in the network drawn with this section.

Two parts of the structure were circular, and these loops are the most telling finding in the map. The first was a commitment loop. The emergency council collected statements of support from legislators; printed them in its book America and Palestine; mailed the book to every member of Congress; used it to press for votes and platform planks; and so gathered new statements, which went into the next round. Each public commitment became leverage for the next one.

The second was the Montor loop, and it ran through one fundraiser. Henry Montor gathered the donors for the 1945 Sonneborn meeting; that meeting produced the arms network; Montor then secured Golda Meyerson's platform before American audiences in 1948; and her tour raised about $50 million, largely for arms, which returned the money to the purpose the 1945 meeting had set. [cite:israelaffairs_italy] One man's network ran from the founding of the arms channel to its largest single injection of money. Feinberg's place inside it continued after 1948: the FBI file released decades later documents his ongoing meetings with Sonneborn, and in 1952 he was ordered to register as a foreign agent over a publication he ran, a story told in [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg]. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file]

The headline figures above give the scale of what this network sat on top of. Two of them belong to it directly: the Zionist Organization of America's 147,000 members by 1918, the mass base of the first degree, and the roughly $50 million of Golda Meyerson's 1948 tour, the largest single return of the Montor loop. [cite:aja_azec_local_1944] [cite:israelaffairs_italy] The land and population figures in the same panel, the share of land in Jewish ownership in 1947 and the share the 1947 partition plan allotted, are taken up in the chapter's closing account of the founding narratives.

The money panel sets four documented sums side by side: Golda Meyerson's tour at about $50 million, the transfer of German Jewish capital in the 1930s, about $14.5 million for Czechoslovak arms in 1948, and the $5 million raised by the American delegation of 1921–22 that met President Harding. [cite:israelaffairs_italy] They are separate events in different years and currencies, shown together for scale. Two of them, the tour and the Czechoslovak purchases, are the financial output of the second-degree arms and fundraising network in its final year.

What the map establishes is structure, not control in the conspiratorial sense. It shows a movement that built its own institutions, created allied committees that presented themselves as independent, organized the money and the arms channel through a small number of named people, and reached Congress and the White House through named legislators, aides and a friend of the President. Each tie is a public or documented relationship. Against it stood the State Department, the Joint Chiefs and the Defense Secretary, who opposed the policy in writing; the network's success was political, and it was contested inside the government it sought to move.

What the map does not establish is how much each tie weighed. It records that Niles and Clifford carried the case to Truman, not how far they changed his mind; that legislators made statements, not what moved each of them to make one; and that the Agency funded the American Zionist Council in the decade before 1962, not whether or how it funded the wartime council before 1949. Those questions are left where the documents leave them.
