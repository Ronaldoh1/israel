Between 1936 and 1945, the center of the question moved. The period opened in Palestine, with an armed uprising against British rule and against Jewish immigration. A royal commission was formed to investigate it, and it produced the first official proposal to divide the territory. By the period's close, the arguments were being made far from Palestine: in a New York hotel ballroom, in the White House, and in the platforms of both American parties.

In between came the catastrophe. In November 1938, Nazi Germany turned from the legal exclusion of Jews to open, organized violence. That same year, thirty-two nations met at Évian to discuss refugees, and nearly all declined to take more. Then Britain, in its White Paper of 1939, capped Jewish immigration to Palestine at the very moment escape became a matter of survival. Refugee ships were sunk or turned back. A clandestine fleet tried to carry people past the British limits. And inside the Zionist movement, the careful language of a "national home" gave way to an explicit demand for a state.

This part asks how that shift happened. Why did the movement's center of gravity move toward the United States? What did Britain, the Vatican and the American president actually do and say? And how much of what is commonly remembered about these years survives contact with the documents?
