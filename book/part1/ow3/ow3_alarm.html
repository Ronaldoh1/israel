What the CIA Itself Recorded, 1968–1987

The partnership described in the previous section was never the whole of the CIA's view of Israel. In the same decades the agency's analysts and directors produced a separate record: a request to investigate a uranium loss, a report on Israel's intelligence services that named the United States as a target, and a damage assessment after an Israeli-run spy was caught inside the US Navy. None of these documents ended the liaison. Together they show what the agency knew, when it knew it, and what it did with the knowledge.

NUMEC, 1968–1978. The shortage of highly enriched uranium at the NUMEC plant in Apollo, Pennsylvania, was found in 1965, and the first years of the case are told in [scene:fis_s07a|the NUMEC shortage]. The CIA's part began in 1968. On 2 April that year the Director of Central Intelligence, Richard Helms, asked Attorney General Ramsey Clark to "initiate a discreet intelligence investigation of an all source nature" of the company's president, Zalman Shapiro; J. Edgar Hoover approved a new FBI inquiry on 6 May. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] On 10 September 1968 four Israelis visited the plant, among them Rafi Eitan, who was later exposed as head of the scientific-intelligence bureau that ran Jonathan Pollard. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] On 3 September 1969 Hoover told Helms that the FBI was discontinuing its active investigation. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec]

The CIA's own judgment was firmer than the FBI's. A CIA memorandum of March 1972 called diversion "a distinct possibility", and in 1976 Carl Duckett, the agency's former deputy director for science and technology, briefed the Nuclear Regulatory Commission on the CIA's late-1960s conclusion that Israel's first weapons "owed in some measure to uranium from the NUMEC plant." [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] On the other side stand the Atomic Energy Commission, which told Congress in February 1966 that it had "found no evidence of theft"; President Carter's national security adviser, Zbigniew Brzezinski, who wrote in 1977 that there was "no evidence—despite an intensive search for some—to prove that one did" occur; and the General Accounting Office, which concluded in December 1978 that "a diversion could not be ruled out." [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] The editors of the fullest documentary collection judge the evidence "strong but not incontrovertible," and note that "the CIA has released none of its internal operational documents" behind its conclusions. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] No official body has found that a diversion occurred, and no one was charged. The question is officially unresolved, and this book treats it so.

The 1979 report. The second document reached the public by accident. When Iranian militants occupied the US embassy in Tehran in 1979, they seized classified papers and later published them. In February 1982 the Washington Post, in a story by Scott Armstrong, summarized one of them: a 47-page CIA report on Israel's intelligence services. [cite:ow3_jta1982] According to the summary, the report said Israel's collection of secret American policy information ranked "only second and third in priority" after the Arab states, and that its collection was "especially concentrated in the Soviet Union and the United States as well as the United Nations." [cite:ow3_jta1982] The Christian Science Monitor reported that the Post and the Boston Globe described papers saying Israeli services had "used blackmail, bugs, wiretaps, and bribe offers to US government employees"; the CIA had no comment. [cite:ow3_csm1982]

Israel's government rejected the report. Prime Minister Menachem Begin's spokesman, Uri Porat, called it a "cheap invention not worthy of comment"; an unnamed official in Jerusalem said the source, Iran, "cannot be described as a reliable source by any stretch of the imagination"; and a former Israeli intelligence officer said it was "highly unlikely" the CIA would have kept such a document in Tehran. [cite:ow3_jta1982] Both positions belong in the record. The document's authenticity has not been confirmed by the CIA, and the Monitor reported four years later that a classified 1979 CIA report "reportedly" named secret American policy information and scientific intelligence as Israel's top priorities in the United States. [cite:ow3_csm1986] The report's existence is therefore attested by repeated press summaries rather than by an official release.

A Justice Department veteran gave the same picture from the American side. John Davitt, who led the department's internal-security section and resigned in 1980, told the New York Times that "the Israeli intelligence service, when I was in the Justice Department, was the second most active" in the United States, after the Soviet Union's. [cite:ow3_merip] Stansfield Turner, CIA director under Carter, said after the Pollard arrest: "Of course it goes on." [cite:ow3_csm1986] Davitt's ranking and the later National Intelligence Estimate that placed Israel third, discussed in Part Four, come from different eras and different sources; they are not in conflict, and neither is a public finding.

Pollard, 1987. The third document is the most complete. After the FBI arrested Jonathan Pollard, a civilian analyst for the Navy, in November 1985, the CIA's Foreign Denial and Deception Analysis Committee wrote a damage assessment dated 30 October 1987. [cite:ow3_nsa_pollard] The CIA withheld most of it in 2006; the Interagency Security Classification Appeals Panel ordered much of it released in 2012. [cite:ow3_nsa_pollard] It found that Pollard had passed about 800 documents, many top secret or codeword, plus some 1,500 intelligence summary messages; that the bureau running him was LAKAM, then headed by Rafi Eitan; and that his Israeli taskings concerned Arab states, Pakistan and the Soviet Union. [cite:ow3_nsa_pollard] It also recorded what Israel did not ask for: the Israelis "never expressed interest in US military activities, plans, capabilities, or equipment." [cite:ow3_nsa_pollard] The prosecution, the sentence and the later release are told in Part Five.

What the documents show about the mechanism. In each case the CIA's knowledge travelled no further than an assessment or a referral. The agency could ask the FBI to investigate, as Helms did in 1968, and it could assess damage after an arrest, as in 1987. It had no authority of its own to act inside the United States, and the decisions that followed, whether to prosecute, to press Israel or to continue the partnership, were taken elsewhere. McConnell cited a 1978 Senate Intelligence Committee study finding that the FBI investigated "friendly" services only in response to specific complaints, that what it learned rarely reached prosecutors, and that the CIA and the State Department discouraged penalties against friendly services. [cite:ow3_merip] Melman and Raviv, writing from inside the relationship, acknowledge that American law enforcement "busted several conspiracies run by Israelis to procure defense and high-tech secrets," mostly run by military officers and LAKAM rather than the Mossad. [cite:ow3_tablet] Throughout these years the liaison itself continued.

What the record does not settle. Whether NUMEC uranium reached Israel remains unproven in either direction, and the CIA's operational files remain closed. The 1979 report has never been officially acknowledged, and its text is known only through press summaries of a document published by a hostile government. And no public document records any decision, in any administration, to limit the CIA–Israel liaison because of what these three files contained.
