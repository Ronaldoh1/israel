What the Apps and the AI Companies Keep

The records in [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|the previous section] were mostly collected about people. This section turns to what people hand over themselves, through the apps on their phones, the artificial-intelligence assistants they type into, and the shops they buy from. The questions stay the same: what each company says it keeps, who can reach it, and what changed when regulators or courts stepped in. The companies' own documents come first; where a claim rests only on a lawsuit's allegations, it is called an allegation.

Inside the phone

An app can see what its permissions allow: precise or approximate location, camera, microphone, contacts, photos. Even without permissions it receives the phone's network address, model and settings. Many apps also carry a software development kit, or SDK, a ready-made block of code supplied by another company, often to count users, report crashes or serve advertisements; an "analytics SDK" reports how people use the app back to its supplier, and it inherits every permission the host app holds. That is how location data from a prayer app reached the military. Motherboard reported, in an investigation it followed up in January 2021, that Muslim Pro, downloaded more than 96 million times, had sent users' precise locations to the broker X-Mode, which sold location data to defence contractors, and that US Special Operations Command had bought access to products built on commercial phone-location data. Apple and Google banned X-Mode's code from their stores in December 2020. [cite:de24_vice_xmode] The market that grew from such code, and the federal agencies that bought from it, are followed in [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Five].

Apps have also grown heavy. The analytics firm Sensor Tower found that the ten most-downloaded iPhone apps in the United States averaged about 2.2 gigabytes in May 2021, against about 550 megabytes at the start of 2016; TikTok and Instagram grew about sixfold and Gmail nineteenfold, which Sensor Tower attributed to new features in each year's iPhone software. [cite:de24_imore_size] Part of the weight is machine learning: a trained "model", a large file of numbers that lets a program recognise images, sounds or language, can now run on the phone itself. Meta's engineers described in July 2025 models inside Instagram that cut a person out of a photo, inside WhatsApp that estimate network speed for calls, and inside Messenger that detect a message's language so that translation works while messages stay end-to-end encrypted, meaning only the sender and recipient hold the keys to read them. Meta presented this as privacy, "keeping data on users' devices." [cite:de24_meta_executorch] The size of an app is not in itself evidence of spying; most of it is code, models and stored media. What matters is what the code sends, and to whom.

The AI assistants

A chatbot receives the full text of every question and every uploaded file, along with the account and the network address, from which a rough location follows. The differences between the companies lie in how long they keep it, whether people read it, whether it trains future models, whether it feeds advertising, and who else can obtain it. OpenAI trains on the chats of its free and individual paid ChatGPT users by default, unless they switch off a setting called "Improve the model for everyone"; business and developer accounts are excluded, and even "temporary" chats may be kept for up to 30 days. [cite:de24_kaspersky_openai] Anthropic told users of its consumer Claude plans in August 2025 that they had until 28 September 2025 to choose whether their chats would train its models; chats from those who agreed would be kept for five years instead of 30 days, and business and education plans were excluded. [cite:de24_macrumors_anth] Google says a sample of Gemini conversations is read by human reviewers and that those are kept for up to three years even if the user deletes his or her history; with history switched off, chats are still held for 72 hours, and a general location is always collected. [cite:de24_gemini_hub] Meta announced on 1 October 2025 that from 16 December 2025 people's conversations with Meta AI would be used to personalise advertisements and content, with no opt-out except not using the assistant, and with conversations on religion, politics, health, sexual orientation and similar subjects excluded from ad use. [cite:de24_9to5_metaai]

Perplexity, an AI search company, says in its privacy policy of 31 October 2025 that it collects prompts, device identifiers, approximate location and network address, may use them to improve its models unless a user opts out, and receives data from "consumer marketing databases or other data enrichment companies" to tailor its own advertising; it states that it does not "sell" or "share" personal information as California law defines those words. [cite:de24_pplx_policy] Its chief executive, Aravind Srinivas, said on a podcast in April 2025 that the company's new browser was meant "to get data even outside the app to better understand you," adding, "we plan to use all the context to build a better user profile." [cite:de24_mb_srinivas]

All of it can be reached by legal process. OpenAI's policy requires a subpoena or court order for account details and a search warrant for content, and promises users notice unless the law forbids it. [cite:de24_openai_lep] In the second half of 2025 it received 75 requests for content and produced data in 62, and 224 requests for other records, producing data in 146. [cite:de24_openai_tr] Anthropic, much smaller, reported two content requests in the same period, both answered, and twenty for other records, seven answered. [cite:de24_anthropic_tr] A private litigant can reach further than either. In the New York Times' copyright suit, a magistrate judge ordered OpenAI on 13 May 2025 to preserve "all output log data that would otherwise be deleted"; the broad order ended in September 2025, but the company was required to produce a de-identified sample of 20 million consumer chat logs. [cite:de24_itg_nyt] The AI companies' contracts with the government, a separate matter, are in [scene:de26_ai_layer|Part Twenty-Six].

The PayPal offer

On 3 September 2025 PayPal offered its and Venmo's users in the United States and some other markets "a free 12-month trial of Perplexity Pro valued at $200" with early access to the Comet browser, for first-time users, until 31 December 2025. The announcement says nothing about sharing data between the two companies. [cite:de24_paypal_pplx] Perplexity's policy covers the category in general terms: partners that give free access may send it information, and it shares information with them when a user signs up through their offer, without listing which fields. [cite:de24_pplx_policy] That is the whole documented link. No document shows PayPal payment data going to Perplexity, or Perplexity's prompts going to PayPal. Nor does the offer connect the network this book follows to either company today. Peter Thiel and Elon Musk were among PayPal's founders, as [scene:de9_paypal_palantir_facebook|Part Nine] and [scene:de11_zip2_xcom_paypal|Part Eleven] describe, but eBay bought PayPal on 3 October 2002 and spun it off as an independent public company in July 2015, and neither man appears among the directors nominated in PayPal's 2026 proxy statement. [cite:de11_ebay_8k] [cite:de13_rtp_spin] [cite:de11_pypl_proxy]

What the court cases changed

Fines and settlements make headlines; the test used here is whether practice changed. In Brown v. Google, settled on 1 April 2024, Google agreed to delete old private-browsing records, rewrite its disclosures and limit collection for five years, with no money for the class. [cite:de24_winston_brown] In Rodriguez v. Google, a jury awarded about $425 million in September 2025 for collecting app activity from users who had switched tracking off, but the judge refused a permanent injunction, finding no "prospective, irreparable harm," so nothing had to change. [cite:de24_mlm_rodriguez] Texas obtained $1.375 billion from Google in May 2025, with no admission; Google said the claims concerned "product policies we have long since changed." [cite:de24_hunton_txgoogle] Texas's $1.4 billion from Meta in July 2024 concerned a face-tagging feature that had already ended in November 2021, after an Illinois settlement. [cite:de24_bbl_txmeta] TikTok changed owners rather than practices: a joint venture led by Oracle, Silver Lake and the Abu Dhabi fund MGX took over its American business on 22 January 2026, retraining a licensed algorithm on American data, with ByteDance keeping nearly 20 percent. [cite:de24_axios_tiktok] The Justice Department's children's-privacy case against TikTok ended on 21 August 2026 in a $400 million settlement with no injunction, no finding of a violation, and the 2019 order against the company to be vacated. [cite:de24_hunton_tiktok]

The shopping basket

Retailers know a great deal. Kroger's data-science arm, 84.51°, builds shoppers' loyalty-card profiles and enriches them with broker data that can include estimated race and ethnicity, finances and employment, and sells insights drawn from them to the brands on its shelves; its policy says what it shares is "anonymized and aggregated." [cite:de24_npr_kroger] Target's statistician Andrew Pole described a model for predicting customers' pregnancies in 2010, later made famous by The New York Times; a much-repeated story of a father learning of his daughter's pregnancy from Target's coupons has never been substantiated. [cite:de24_kdn_target] On 17 January 2025 the Federal Trade Commission's staff reported that eight firms, among them Mastercard, JPMorgan Chase, Accenture and McKinsey, helped about 250 retailers vary prices using shoppers' location, timing and behaviour; the incoming chairman, Andrew Ferguson, who had dissented, closed the public inquiry days later. [cite:de24_rb_ftcpricing] No record shows a federal agency buying shopping baskets or loyalty-card histories. The documented commercial pipe into immigration enforcement carries identity, address, utility and plate records. [cite:de24_globe_utility] [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] Payment controls, which can stop a purchase rather than record it, are in [scene:de26_money_rails|Part Twenty-Six] and [scene:de35_the_consequence_ladder|Part Thirty-Five].

The common thread is retention. Each of these companies keeps records it could, under subpoena or warrant, be made to hand over, and several keep more than before. That is a capability. The documented attempts to unmask government critics through technology companies, by administrative subpoena, are set out in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Twenty-Eight]; whether any of these records stays anonymous once handed over is the next section's question.
