Disengagement, 2003–2005: Leaving, and What Stayed

In 2005 Israel removed every settlement and every soldier from inside the Gaza Strip. It did so unilaterally, without an agreement with the Palestinian Authority. This section sets out how the decision was made, what the withdrawal involved, what Israel kept, and the argument, which the record states but does not settle, over whether the occupation of Gaza ended in September 2005.

The decision. Prime Minister Ariel Sharon, who as a general had led the campaign of 1971 in Gaza, first presented the plan to withdraw from Gaza at the Herzliya Conference on 18 December 2003, and set it out to President George W. Bush in a letter of 14 April 2004. His own party's members rejected it in a referendum on 2 May 2004, 65 percent against; the cabinet approved an amended version by 14 votes to 7 on 6 June; the Knesset gave preliminary approval by 67 to 45 on 26 October, and on 16 February 2005 passed the Disengagement Plan Implementation Law by 59 votes to 40. [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement]

The reason, in an adviser's words. What the withdrawal was for was disputed from the start. Sharon's senior adviser, Dov Weisglass, gave a different account in an interview with Haaretz on 6 October 2004: the disengagement, he said, supplied "the amount of formaldehyde" needed so that there would be no political process with the Palestinians, and he described it as freezing the peace process. [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement] Critics of the plan have read the interview as evidence that the withdrawal was meant to freeze the conflict rather than to open a path to a Palestinian state; others treat it as one adviser's view rather than the government's.

The withdrawal. The 21 settlements in the strip were evacuated between 15 and 22 August 2005, Netzarim last; about 8,000 settlers left, two-thirds of them accepting compensation for leaving voluntarily. Demolition crews razed about 2,800 homes and public buildings. The last Israeli soldier left on 12 September 2005, and Egypt was permitted to station 750 border guards along the Philadelphi corridor on the Egypt–Gaza frontier. The whole operation is estimated to have cost about $2 billion. [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement] [cite:gz_wiki_gushkatif] Kfar Darom, founded in 1946, abandoned in 1948 and re-established in 1970, was evacuated on 18 August. [cite:gz_wiki_kfardarom]

What stayed. The soldiers and settlers left; Israel's control of the strip's borders did not. After September 2005 Israel kept control of Gaza's airspace and territorial waters, of six of its seven land crossings (Egypt controlled the seventh, at Rafah), and of the population registry that determines who is a resident; it kept the right to re-enter militarily; and the strip remained dependent on Israel for water, electricity and telecommunications. [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement]

The argument is not academic. If Israel remained the occupying power, it remained bound by the law of occupation's duties toward Gaza's population; if it did not, those duties fell away, and Gaza became, in Israel's description, a hostile entity on its border. The International Court of Justice took up the question in 2024, as Part Seven sets out. What the record settles is the physical fact: from September 2005 there were no Israeli settlers or soldiers inside the strip, and every way in or out of it, except the crossing at Rafah, ran through Israel.
