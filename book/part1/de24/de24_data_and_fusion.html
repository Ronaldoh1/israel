The Data Layer: Brokers, Cameras and the Government's Own Files

Book Five puts the same five questions to every tool it examines: what the tool collects or does, who uses it and under what contract, where it has been misused or got things wrong, what the law and the courts allow, and what its worst use would look like. The last question belongs to the book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, in which immigration enforcement is the occasion for building machinery that could later be pointed at anyone. Throughout, capability is not intent: what a tool can do is a documented fact, and its use against a critic needs a document of its own. This section begins with the layer every other tool draws on, the records about people that already exist and can be bought, borrowed or joined. The platforms that join them, chiefly Palantir's, and the legal rule that lets an agency buy what it could not seize without a warrant are set out in [scene:ow9_ice|ICE, the sole-source contracts and the data they run on] and [scene:us10_us|the United States section of US vs Israel], and are not repeated here.

What a data broker holds

California, which keeps a public register, defines a data broker as "a business that gathers and sells consumer information that a consumer didn't give them directly." More than 500 were registered there by the end of 2025. [cite:de24_ca_drop] Their raw material is ordinary paperwork: property and court records, vehicle registrations, marketing lists, and the identifying top lines of credit files, known as "credit header" data (name, addresses, date of birth and Social Security number, without the account details). Brokers match these records on shared keys such as a name and birth date, and sell either searchable access or bulk files. The intelligence agencies buy too. A report for the Director of National Intelligence, dated January 2022 and declassified on 12 June 2023, found that the intelligence community acquires a "significant amount" of commercially available information and that such data "can reveal sensitive and intimate information about individuals." [cite:de24_lawfare_odni]

One episode shows how far an ordinary record can travel. The National Consumer Telecom and Utilities Exchange, a group of utility and telephone companies that shares customers' payment histories, licensed the names, home addresses, Social Security numbers and other details of more than 170 million people to the credit bureau Equifax, which sold them on as "utility header data" into investigative databases including Thomson Reuters' CLEAR. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) paid tens of millions of dollars for access to it through CLEAR. After The Washington Post reported it in February 2021 and Senator Ron Wyden pressed the exchange, it told Equifax to stop selling new data in October 2021, and Equifax stopped supplying it to law enforcement on 23 October 2021; data sold before then remained in use. [cite:de24_globe_utility]

The two investigative databases

Two companies, better known for legal research, supply most of the commercial records ICE searches. LexisNexis Risk Solutions holds a contract signed in February 2021, under President Biden, worth about $24.5 million, that gives ICE its Accurint and LexID people-search tools; a 2021 change added jail-booking data. Records obtained by the legal group Just Futures Law show ICE users ran more than 1.2 million Accurint person searches between March and September 2021. The contract was due to end on 31 August 2026, and on 10 August 2026 ICE gave notice of a $6.7 million sole-source bridge whose work statement says the system "must connect through application programming interfaces" to ICE applications including "the Palantir platform". [cite:de24_bu_lexis] An application programming interface, or API, is a standard doorway through which one program asks another for data automatically, without a person typing each search.

Thomson Reuters' CLEAR was renewed for the Department of Homeland Security without competition from 31 March 2026, on the justification that "only CLEAR provides the range of law enforcement data the agency requires"; the data types listed include utility data, credit reporting bureaus and licence-plate records. A separate Thomson Reuters contract for plate data, LEIDS-5, ran from May 2021 to May 2026 at $22.8 million. The company told the legal-technology writer Bob Ambrogi that it does "not comment on specific customer contracts" and that "Palantir is not a customer of CLEAR." [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] Which ICE contracts these are, set beside the Palantir awards, is listed in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Twenty-Eight].

Fusion centres

A fusion centre is a state or city intelligence office where police, federal agents and analysts share reports and records so that, in principle, a clue held by one agency reaches another. The model spread after the attacks of 11 September 2001 with federal money. A two-year bipartisan inquiry by the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, begun by Senator Tom Coburn, a Republican, and released with Senator Carl Levin, a Democrat, on 2 October 2012, counted more than 70 centres and found that the Department of Homeland Security could estimate its spending on them since 2003 only as somewhere between $289 million and $1.4 billion. The intelligence its officers produced there was of "uneven quality – oftentimes shoddy, rarely timely," the subcommittee found, and the department could not show that the centres had uniquely helped to stop a terrorist plot; one review of spending found flat-screen televisions, two sport-utility vehicles and mobile-phone tracking devices. [cite:de24_psi_fusion] The report also said the reporting was "sometimes endangering citizens' civil liberties and Privacy Act protections." [cite:de24_govexec_fusion] The money began under a Republican president and continued under a Democratic one; the problems the Senate found were of quality and oversight, not of a single administration.

Licence-plate readers

An automated licence-plate reader (ALPR) is a camera that photographs every passing number plate, reads it by software and stores it with a time and a place. One reading says little. Millions, searchable together, become a record of where a car has been. In February 2014 Homeland Security Secretary Jeh Johnson cancelled an ICE request for access to a nationwide plate-tracking database, saying it had been posted without the knowledge of ICE's leaders, and ordered a review. [cite:de24_physorg_2014] In January 2018 ICE gained that access through a contract with West Publishing, part of Thomson Reuters, working with Vigilant Solutions; ICE said the data was "one tool in support of its investigations" and that a privacy assessment written after 2014 governed its use. [cite:de24_upi_vigilant]

The largest network today belongs to Flock Safety, whose cameras were in more than 4,000 communities by August 2025. On 25 August 2025 Flock paused pilot programmes with Customs and Border Protection and its investigative arm after Illinois' Secretary of State, Alexi Giannoulias, said an audit had found CBP searching Illinois plate data in breach of state law. "We clearly communicated poorly," said Flock's chief executive, Garrett Langley; federal searches would be labelled as such and limited to one police agency at a time. [cite:de24_ap_flock] In Woodburn, Oregon, a city audit found that the Department of Homeland Security and the Border Patrol ran 6,089 searches through a Flock trial between January and November 2025, although the police department's own policy barred immigration use; the city ended its contract at the end of April 2026. [cite:de24_katu_woodburn] The cameras also misread. In August 2020 police in Aurora, Colorado, held Brittney Gilliam's family at gunpoint after a reader matched her car to a stolen motorcycle from another state, and the city settled for $1.9 million; one study cited by the Electronic Frontier Foundation found readers got the state wrong on one plate in ten. [cite:de24_eff_alpr]

The government's own files, reused

The newest change since 2025 is not a purchase but a reuse: records people gave the government for one purpose opened to another. The Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) system, long used to check benefit applicants' immigration status, was linked to Social Security data in May 2025 and from 15 August 2025 could check voters in bulk with only the last four digits of a Social Security number; election officials had run more than 33 million voters through it by September 2025. Queries are kept for ten years, the state agreements let DHS use the data "for any purpose permitted by law," and the agency had published no accuracy testing. [cite:de24_npr_save] In April 2025 the Internal Revenue Service agreed to check names and addresses for ICE; a federal judge later found the IRS had broken the law "approximately 42,695 times," and an appeals panel upheld her block on 8 September 2026, while another panel had declined to block the sharing in February. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] For Medicaid, Judge Vince Chhabria ruled on 29 December 2025 that, for the twenty states that sued, health officials could pass ICE only five fields about people not lawfully present (status, address, phone, date of birth and Medicaid number), and no health records. [cite:de24_wt_medicaid] How each of these walls was built and breached is the subject of [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|Part Thirty]; the ICE tool reported to draw on Medicaid data is described in [scene:ow9_ice|the One-Way Mirror]. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite]

The biometric store is older than all of these. The Homeland Advanced Recognition Technology system (HART), meant to replace a 1990s database of faces, fingerprints and irises, was contracted to Northrop Grumman for $95 million in February 2018, under President Trump's first term. [cite:de24_wt_hart2018] By September 2023 the Government Accountability Office reported that its start had slipped from December 2020 to at least September 2023, that costs had risen by $354 million, that the old system held over 290 million identities, and that the department had met only 5 of 12 federal privacy requirements; all nine of its recommendations were still open. [cite:de24_gao_hart] In August 2026 Kyle Schutt, a software engineer who had moved through agencies with the Department of Government Efficiency, became the department's acting chief information officer and head of the biometrics office, with HART due to reach initial operation "no later than September 2026." [cite:de24_bu_schutt] Whether that deadline was met could not be confirmed as of October 2026.

What this layer documents is supply: records on most adults, sold or shared, with doorways built to let one system ask another. What it does not document is a single master file on every American, or the use of any of these sources to find a critic for what he or she said. The question of what the joining platform does with them, and what its makers say it cannot do, belongs to [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Seven]; the next section turns to what people hand over themselves, through the apps on their phones.
