Mothers of the World: Pachamama, Yemayá, Brigid and the Dark Virgins

Tepeyac is not unique. Wherever Catholic missions met peoples who honoured a divine mother, of the earth, the mountain or the sea, a pattern followed that historians describe in two ways at once: the mother was pushed out of sight, and she came back inside a Virgin. This section follows four of those meetings, in the Andes, in Rome in 2019, in the Afro-Caribbean world shaped by slavery, and in Ireland, and closes with the Black Madonnas of Europe, about which more is claimed than documented. In each case it names who acted, including where the actor was not the Church. [cite:vt36_wiki_pachamama]

Pachamama and the Virgins of the Andes

Pachamama is the Andean mother of earth and time, worshipped across the Inca state alongside Inti, the sun, at sacred rocks and trees, with offerings of llamas and guinea pigs. After the Spanish conquest the Indigenous population was brought into the Catholic Church, and through the process scholars call syncretism many Andean people came to associate Pachamama with the Virgin Mary, especially with the Virgin of Candelaria. The campaigns waged against Andean religion in the colonial period, the bishops' "extirpation of idolatries" and its destruction of shrines and sacred objects, are set out in [scene:vt34_the_americas|Part Thirty-Three]; the devotion that survived them is the subject here. Her rites continue: the ch'alla, a libation poured to the earth before drinking, and the offerings made throughout August. John Paul II, in homilies in Peru in 1985 and Bolivia in 1988, spoke of homage to Pachamama as a way of recognising God's providence. [cite:vt36_wiki_pachamama]

The best-known Andean Virgin stands on a site the Inca held sacred. At Copacabana, on Lake Titicaca, near a site the Inca had held as a temple of the sun, the Indigenous sculptor Francisco Tito Yupanqui carved an image of the Virgin, which was brought to Copacabana and enthroned on 2 February 1583 (some accounts place the start of his work as early as about 1576); an adobe shrine was built for it around 1583; Augustinian friars took charge of it in 1589, and Pius XII made the church a minor basilica on 2 July 1940. The shrine remains a principal sacred place for Indigenous Andeans and Catholics alike. [cite:vt36_wiki_copacabana]

The most striking image of the fusion is a painting of 1720, the Virgin of the Mountain of Potosí, now in the Museo Nacional de Arte in La Paz. The Virgin's body is the Cerro Rico, the silver mountain that dominates the mining town; the Trinity crowns her from above, and the patrons who paid for the painting kneel below. Some scholars read the mountain-Virgin as Pachamama in Marian form, the earth goddess of the mountains visible inside the Mother of God. Others point out that Spain had its own mountain Virgins, Montserrat above all, so the image could draw on both worlds; the Smith College survey of colonial art concludes that it is transatlantic Catholic devotion made distinctly Andean in style and meaning. [cite:vt36_smith_cerro]

Rome, October 2019

Pachamama returned to the centre of Catholic controversy during the Synod of Bishops for the Pan-Amazon Region. On 4 October 2019 wooden female figures were part of a tree-planting ceremony in the Vatican Gardens attended by Pope Francis, and were later displayed in the Carmelite church of Santa Maria in Traspontina near St Peter's. On 21 October two men filmed themselves taking the figures from the church and throwing them from a bridge into the Tiber. The Italian Carabinieri recovered them undamaged. [cite:vt36_cwr_pacha2019]

On 25 October Francis told the synod that the figures had been in the church "without idolatrous intentions" and said: "I ask forgiveness from those who have been offended by this gesture." He called them "Pachamama"; Paolo Ruffini, prefect of the Vatican's Dicastery for Communication, had said that "fundamentally, it represents life". On 4 November an Austrian activist, Alexander Tschugguel, said in a video that he was one of the two men, and that the figures "do not belong in a Catholic church." [cite:vt36_cwr_pacha2019] [cite:vt36_cr_tschugguel]

The episode reverses the usual roles. The act of destruction was carried out by lay Catholic critics acting against their own pope; the pope apologised for it; and the Vatican's officials themselves did not settle what the figures represented, life, a mother, the earth or a goddess. The argument inside the Church about whether an Indigenous mother figure belongs in a Catholic church, in other words, is still live. [cite:vt36_cwr_pacha2019]

Yemayá, Oshún and the Virgins of the Caribbean

In Cuba and Brazil the meeting took place under slavery. A large share of the West Africans carried to Cuba between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries were Yoruba, and their religion, combined with Catholicism and later with Spiritism, became the religion known as Santería or Lucumí. The Spanish colonial government permitted only Catholicism. The orishas came to be paired with Catholic saints by shared attributes: Changó, who wears red, with Saint Barbara; Babalú Ayé with Saint Lazarus; Oshún with Our Lady of Charity of El Cobre, the patroness of Cuba. [cite:vt36_wiki_santeria]

Yemayá (Yemoja in Yoruba, Iemanjá in Brazil), the mother of waters, patron of the Ogun river in Nigeria and of the ocean in the Americas, is paired in Cuba with Our Lady of Regla, whose procession in Regla falls on 7 September, and in Brazil with Our Lady of the Navigators and of the Conception. Her largest festivals are Brazilian: offerings in the sea at Rio Vermelho in Salvador on 2 February and on beaches across the country on New Year's Eve. The Virgin of Charity, for her part, entered the Church's highest honours: Benedict XV declared her patroness of Cuba on 10 May 1916 at the request of veterans of the war of independence, and John Paul II crowned her image on 24 January 1998. [cite:vt36_wiki_yemoja] [cite:vt36_wiki_caridad]

Scholars disagree about what the pairing meant to the enslaved people who made it. One argument holds that they linked their deities to the saints to hide their worship from Spanish authorities, a mask over a continuing practice. Another holds that the linking also eased entry into colonial society by assimilating to Catholic norms. A third, from the ethnomusicologist María Teresa Vélez, sees the religion as something new, born from the encounter of diverse Yoruba peoples with one another, with other Africans and with Europeans. The pressure did not end with Spanish rule: after independence the Euro-Cuban Catholic establishment typically dismissed Santería as witchcraft, and in the United States the Supreme Court had to strike down a Florida city's ordinances aimed at its rites, in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993). [cite:vt36_wiki_santeria]

Brigid of Kildare

Ireland's case is the most uncertain. Saint Brigid is traditionally dated about 451 to 525 and said to have founded a monastery at Kildare around 480. A goddess Brigid appears in later Irish texts, including Cormac's Glossary, with the same name and the same domains of poetry, healing and smithcraft. In the late twelfth century Gerald of Wales described nineteen nuns at Kildare taking turns to tend a fire said to have burned since Brigid's time, inside a hedge no man could cross. Scholars divide: some see the saint as the goddess turned Christian, others as a real woman to whom the goddess's traits were later attached, and Dáithí Ó hÓgáin and others suggest she may have led a pagan sanctuary that became a monastery. Her earliest Lives are largely miracle stories and do not settle the question. What is documented is continuity, not a recorded suppression: since 2023 the Republic of Ireland has kept a public holiday in her name at her feast of 1 February. The losses that are recorded came later and from another actor: her relics at Down were desecrated and destroyed in 1538, in the Reformation, under the English lord deputy Lord Grey. [cite:vt36_wiki_brigid]

The Black Madonnas

Some 400 to 500 images of a dark-skinned Virgin survive in Europe, depending on how they are counted, with at least 180 in southern France alone; Montserrat in Catalonia and Częstochowa in Poland are the most famous. A widely repeated claim holds that they are Isis or an earth goddess in disguise. The record is thinner. In 1952 the anthropologist Leonard Moss sorted them into groups, and found that many were dark because of the wood used, or had darkened with age, candle smoke and the deterioration of lead-based pigments. Others may have been made dark deliberately, perhaps after the bride of the Song of Songs ("I am black, but comely"), and the writer Ean Begg argued for pagan origins; Monique Scheer argues the dark colour matters to worshippers because it signals age and authenticity. Research is limited and the origin theories remain debated. Some of the losses had nothing to do with the Church: the ebony Virgin that Louis IX gave to Le Puy in 1254 was destroyed during the French Revolution and replaced by a copy. [cite:vt36_wiki_blackmadonna]

Across these cases the record shows two forces working at once. Colonial states and Church officials did press against Indigenous and African mother figures, and some of that pressure was violent. Yet the figures most often survived by moving into Marian devotion, and the institution, from Pius XII at Copacabana to Benedict XV at El Cobre, honoured the Virgins in which they lived on. The oldest of these questions, whether Mary herself took the place of an older goddess, is the subject of the [scene:vt36_isis_and_mary|next section].
