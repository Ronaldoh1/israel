The Catalogue: What Was Exposed, and What Still Runs

Between June 2013 and 2017 the public learned more about American and British signals intelligence than in the forty years before. Most of it came from the archive that Edward Snowden, a contractor working for the National Security Agency (NSA), took and gave to journalists; some came from other sources, which matters for accuracy. This section lists the main programmes, says in plain words what each did, and gives its status as of October 2026. Three terms recur. Metadata is the record of a communication rather than its content: who called whom, when, for how long, from where. Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) is the 2008 law that lets agencies collect, without an individual warrant, the communications of foreigners abroad from American companies; Americans in contact with them are collected too. Executive Order 12333, signed by President Ronald Reagan in 1981, is the president's own rulebook for intelligence collected abroad; it was never passed by Congress, and Congress does not vote to renew it. How 702 works, its target counts and its 2026 lapse are set out in [scene:us10_us|the library's account] and are only summarised here.

The phone records: exposed, ruled unlawful, ended

The first disclosure, in June 2013, was an order of the surveillance court requiring Verizon to hand over its customers' call records in bulk under section 215 of the Patriot Act. In May 2015 the Second Circuit held that the programme exceeded what Congress had authorised, and in June 2015 the USA FREEDOM Act ended bulk collection, leaving the records with the phone companies for the NSA to query number by number. [cite:us10_wiki_clapper] The successor system failed on its own terms. In June 2018 the NSA announced it was deleting more than 685 million call records collected since 2015 because of "technical irregularities" in data from the carriers, which had sent it records it was not authorised to receive. [cite:de29_voa_685m] Section 215 itself expired on 15 March 2020, with an exception for investigations already open; no renewal had been reported as of October 2026. [cite:de29_eff_215] Of everything exposed in 2013, this is the one programme that was clearly stopped, and it was stopped by a leak, then a court, then Congress.

Section 702: PRISM and Upstream

On 6 June 2013 the Washington Post and the Guardian described PRISM, the part of 702 collection that draws on the large internet companies. The Post's account suggested nearly direct access to company servers; the companies named, among them Google, Apple, Facebook, Microsoft and Yahoo, denied it. Facebook said it did not provide "any government organization with direct access" to its servers, and Apple said it had never heard of PRISM. [cite:de29_tc_prism] The accurate description is the legal one: the companies hand over the accounts of designated foreign targets when the government serves them with directives under 702. The second part, Upstream, copies traffic as it crosses the internet's main cables inside the United States, with the help of the carriers that run them. Until 2017 Upstream also collected messages that merely mentioned a target, called "about" collection. The NSA ended it on 28 April 2017 after an internal review found compliance lapses that it had reported to Congress and the court, and it deleted most of the Upstream data it held. [cite:de29_nsa_about]

The statute behind both lapsed at midnight on 12 June 2026. Collection did not stop: the certifications approved by the surveillance court in March 2026 run, on the Brennan Center's reading, to 17 March 2027. [cite:us10_eff_702lapse] [cite:us10_bcj_702] No renewal had been reported as enacted as of October 2026. The structural effect of the lapse runs against intuition: until those certifications expire, there is no deadline at which Congress can attach conditions.

Collection abroad under the president's own order

Two of the most consequential programmes ran under Executive Order 12333 rather than FISA, outside the surveillance court. XKEYSCORE is software the NSA's analysts use to search the agency's stored collection, including bulk collection abroad; the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board (PCLOB) finished a study of it in December 2020 and released it, heavily redacted, on 12 February 2024, with a dissent from one member, Travis LeBlanc. [cite:de29_savage_xks] MUSCULAR, reported by the Washington Post on 30 October 2013, was a joint NSA and British GCHQ operation that copied whole data flows on the private links between Google's and Yahoo's data centres. In one 30-day period it sent back more than 180 million records. Google said it was "not aware of this activity", and Yahoo said it had given no agency access to its data centres. [cite:de29_ap_muscular] The companies' answer was technical: they encrypted the traffic between their own buildings, so that copying it yields scrambled data. The authority itself was untouched.

Britain's equivalent of bulk cable tapping, run by GCHQ under a programme the Guardian named Tempora in June 2013, reached a court. In Big Brother Watch v. United Kingdom, on 25 May 2021, the Grand Chamber of the European Court of Human Rights held that bulk interception is not in itself unlawful but that the British regime lacked the safeguards required, from independent authorisation to later review, and so violated the rights to privacy and to free expression, the second because journalists' material was unprotected. [cite:de29_asil_bbw]

The CIA has its own bulk holdings under the same order. On 10 February 2022 Senators Ron Wyden and Martin Heinrich, both Democrats, made public a letter of April 2021 saying a CIA programme operated "outside the statutory framework that Congress and the public believe govern this collection." Released papers showed that analysts searching it for Americans saw a reminder on screen but were not required to record why they searched, and that a programme collecting financial data on the Islamic State had swept in Americans' records. The CIA said it "may incidentally acquire information about Americans" and protects it under procedures approved by the attorney general. [cite:de29_ap_ciabulk] What the programme holds, and whether it continues, has not been disclosed.

The hacking tools, and a catalogue that was not Snowden's

The agencies also break into devices. An exploit is a piece of code that uses a flaw in software to take control of a phone, computer or television without the owner knowing. On 29 December 2013 Der Spiegel published an NSA catalogue of hardware and software implants for phones, computers and network equipment, known as the ANT catalogue. It is often credited to Snowden; it was never attributed to him, and both Bruce Schneier and Glenn Greenwald, who worked on the Snowden archive, said it came from a second, unidentified source. [cite:de29_es_ant]

The CIA's equivalent came out through WikiLeaks as "Vault 7", beginning on 7 March 2017 with 8,761 documents. One tool, Weeping Angel, reportedly built with Britain's MI5, put certain Samsung televisions into a false "off" mode and used their microphones to record; others targeted iPhones and Android phones and read messages from apps such as Signal and WhatsApp by taking over the handset rather than breaking the encryption. [cite:de29_sg_vault7] The source was Joshua Schulte, a CIA software engineer, who copied the archives in 2016; he was convicted of espionage in 2022 and, in a separate trial, of possessing child sexual abuse material, and was sentenced to 40 years. [cite:de29_ng_schulte] The CIA's own task force, in an October 2017 report released by Wyden in June 2020, found security in the unit that built the tools "woefully lax" and wrote that had the tools been stolen and not published, "we might still be unaware of the loss." [cite:de29_aje_lax] The leaked tools themselves were burned; the capability to build new ones was not.

From collecting to buying

The largest change since 2013 is not in the leaked programmes but beside them. Data that once required a court order can now be bought from brokers, the companies that gather location and browsing data from apps and advertising (explained in [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four] and [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Six]). A report written for the Director of National Intelligence in January 2022 and declassified in June 2023 said such information "historically could have been obtained, if at all, only through targeted (and predicated) collection" and "could be used to cause harm to an individual's reputation, emotional well-being, or physical safety." [cite:de29_fortune_cai] In 2023 the FBI's director, Christopher Wray, told senators the bureau had bought location data in the past but was not doing so. On 18 March 2026 his successor, Kash Patel, told Wyden: "We do purchase commercially available information that is consistent with the Constitution and the laws," declined to commit to stop, and the bureau maintains that no warrant is needed, a position no court has yet tested. [cite:de29_tc_patel] The legal doctrine of buying rather than asking a judge is examined in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Thirty].

Read together, the catalogue has a plain shape. What Congress wrote, it could end, and in one case did. What the president's own order governs has never been through a vote, and its rules can be changed by the executive that writes them. And buying what a warrant would have required sits outside both. [cite:de29_ap_ciabulk] [cite:de29_tc_patel]

If it were connected

The catalogue and the immigration ecosystem of [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty] run under different laws; the question is where they meet. Documented: in the market. The FBI buys commercially available information, and ICE buys people-search profiles and phone-location trails from the same kind of broker. [cite:de29_tc_patel] [cite:de28_bu_lexis] Documented too: intelligence holdings searched for domestic ends, when FBI agents ran improper 702 queries on people arrested at the 2020 racial-justice protests and on suspects of 6 January. [cite:de29_ap_fisc2023] Possible, with no document: 702 or Executive Order 12333 material reaching ICE's integration platforms beside a broker's address and a Medicaid record; that would need the agencies' sharing rules to allow it and a request through the FBI or DHS's intelligence office. Not supported: a standing feed from NSA or CIA collection into immigration enforcement; the record shows the two systems meeting in the market, not in a pipe.
