Hebron, 1929

Popular accounts often date the beginning of Arab-Jewish violence in Mandate Palestine to the organized Arab Revolt of 1936. That starting point is seven years too late. In August 1929, riots that began over disputed rights at the Western Wall spread to Hebron, one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the world. Sixty-seven Jews were killed [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The event has a specific death toll, a specific and lasting demographic consequence, and a specific political figure whose career it helped to make.

The immediate dispute is well documented. Through 1928 tensions built over Jewish prayer arrangements at the Western Wall, a site owned by Muslim religious authorities. Both sides were active. There was a documented Arab campaign asserting Muslim rights over the site, and documented Jewish assertions of access for prayer there. Each community viewed the dispute as symbolically central to its competing claims [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The record establishes that real tension existed on both sides before the riots; it does not settle which particular actions bear greater responsibility for the escalation.

In August 1929 the rioting, inflamed by rumors and organized propaganda, reached Hebron. Sixty-seven Jews were killed. The city's Jewish community, present there for centuries, was evacuated by the British authorities in the aftermath [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. Organized Jewish life in Hebron did not resume until 1968.

That consequence sets Hebron apart. Many episodes of violence in this period produced disruption followed by some form of return. Hebron produced an immediate, total and long-lasting change: a community present for centuries was removed within days and remained absent from organized communal life in the city for thirty-nine years [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The circumstances of the return in 1968, and what followed it, belong to a later part of the story.

The role of Amin al-Husseini, the Mufti of Jerusalem, needs careful statement because it is contested. Some things are documented. In 1928 he led a campaign asserting Muslim rights over the Wall, and his activists distributed inflammatory material before the riots [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. What historians disagree about is the degree of his responsibility for the violence itself: whether he directly organized the riots, or whether he capitalized on tensions that escalated beyond his control. The record supports the pre-riot campaign. It does not clearly settle the question of direct organization, and this book leaves that question open.

On the political consequence, historians are more specific. Historians, notably Oren Kessler in a 2024 account, describe 1929 as the event that made al-Husseini's political reputation among Palestinians [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. It was the platform on which his later leadership of the Arab Revolt of 1936 to 1939 was built. Accounts that begin his rise in 1936 miss its foundation. That does not make 1936 less important to his career; it means the career had begun earlier, at Hebron.

The violence also arrived in the same month that the enlarged Jewish Agency was ratified at Zurich, as described in [scene:us_s03|the enlarged Jewish Agency]. And it was followed by the British inquiries and the policy reversal traced in [scene:pf_s01|the Passfield White Paper]. Placed correctly in time, Hebron is not a footnote to the revolt of 1936. It is an earlier turning point, with consequences for the city, for Palestinian politics and for British policy that were already visible years before the revolt began.
