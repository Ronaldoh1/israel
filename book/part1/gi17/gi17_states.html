What States Did

Governments acted on their own account in five ways after October 2023: they recognised a Palestinian state, restricted arms, sanctioned Israeli ministers and settlers, cut or threatened trade, and set conditions on normal relations. A smaller number moved the other way. Each measure is given with the reason the government stated and Israel's response.

Recognising Palestine

On 22 May 2024 Ireland, Spain and Norway announced that they would recognise a Palestinian state; Norway's recognition took effect on 28 May, on the lines that existed before 4 June 1967. Spain's prime minister cited "peace, justice and democracy," Ireland's the "appalling" conditions in Gaza, and Norway's the Palestinians' "fundamental, independent right to self-determination." Israel recalled its envoys from Dublin and Oslo, and its foreign minister said Israel "will not back down against those who undermine its sovereignty and endanger its security." [cite:gi17_na_recog2024]

The larger wave came in September 2025, around the UN General Assembly. Britain announced its recognition on 21 September; Canada, Australia and Portugal followed, and France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Malta, Monaco and Andorra recognised Palestine at the Assembly. By Al Jazeera's count this brought the total to 157 of the 193 UN member states, with eleven new recognitions in 2025 and twenty since October 2023. [cite:gi17_aje_recog2025] Netanyahu called Britain's step a "prize" for Hamas and said a Palestinian state "will not happen"; Israel's ambassador to the UN called the statehood summit a "circus." [cite:gi17_aje_recog2025] When those plans were announced in August 2025, Smotrich presented final approval of the E1 settlement plan as an answer to them, saying it would "permanently bury the idea of a Palestinian state." [cite:wb_itv_e1] [scene:wb9_annex|The West Bank, Documented: since October 2023] Recognition creates no state on the ground; what it changes is the diplomatic standing of the territory Israel's annexation track would absorb. [scene:gi6_votes|Part Six: the annexation track]

Arms, and their limits

The arms measures were narrower than the recognitions. Britain suspended about 30 of 350 export licences in September 2024 but kept supplying F-35 parts, and its courts upheld the exception. The Netherlands stopped F-35 parts only under a court order. [cite:gi17_aje_f35uk] [cite:gi17_es_dutch] [scene:gi17_courts|The courts] Germany, which supplied 30 percent of Israel's major arms imports from 2019 to 2023 by SIPRI's count, restricted exports of weapons that could be used in Gaza in August 2025, after Israel's cabinet decided to seize Gaza City. It lifted the restriction from 24 November 2025; the chancellor's spokesman said that "since October 10, we have had a ceasefire in Gaza and it has fundamentally stabilised." Israel had said in August that such moves "reward terrorism"; in November its foreign minister, Gideon Saar, called on "other governments to adopt similar decisions, following Germany." [cite:gi17_aje_germany] The supplier that matters most, the United States, moved in the opposite direction: in March 2025 Secretary of State Marco Rubio used emergency authority to speed some $4 billion in military assistance, calling his predecessor's conditions a "partial arms embargo." [cite:ow1_state_rubio2025] [scene:ow8_pace|The One-Way Mirror: the pace of deliveries]

Sanctions on ministers and settlers

On 10 June 2025 Britain, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Norway froze the assets of, and barred entry to, Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich, two serving cabinet ministers whose parties lead the settlement and annexation agenda described in Parts Five and Six. Their joint statement said the two "have incited extremist violence and serious abuses of Palestinian human rights." Saar called the decision "outrageous"; the US State Department called it "extremely unhelpful"; Smotrich, at the inauguration of a new settlement in Hebron, expressed "contempt" for Britain and said Israel would keep building. [cite:gi17_aje_sanctions2025] Hours later he ordered the cancellation of the indemnity that lets Israeli banks deal with Palestinian ones, citing the Palestinian Authority's "delegitimisation campaign." [cite:wb_aj_bankwaiver2025] [scene:wb12_revenue|The West Bank, Documented: the revenue]

The American sanctions on violent settlers imposed in 2024 were revoked in January 2025. [cite:wb_ofac_20250124] The European Union, which needs unanimity for sanctions, was repeatedly blocked by Hungary. After Viktor Orbán lost Hungary's April 2026 election, EU foreign ministers agreed in principle on 11 May 2026 to sanction Hamas leaders and Israeli settler figures; Haaretz reported that the draft list named the organisations Amana, Nachala, Hashomer Yosh and Regavim and the Nachala leader Daniella Weiss, though no minister. The EU's foreign-policy chief, Kaja Kallas, said it was "high time we move from deadlock to delivery." Saar called the sanctions "arbitrary and political"; Weiss called them "ridiculous." [cite:gi17_mp_eusettlers]

Trade

The European Union's trade concessions to Israel run through its Association Agreement. In September 2025 the European Commission proposed suspending the agreement's trade provisions, which would affect about €5.8 billion of Israeli exports; suspension needs a qualified majority of member states, full suspension unanimity. In April 2026 Spain, Ireland and Belgium were pressing for action, while Germany, Denmark and the Netherlands were reluctant, and no majority existed. Israel called the proposal "morally and politically distorted." [cite:gi17_an_eutrade] In May 2026 ministers again failed to agree on suspending the agreement or banning settlement goods. [cite:gi17_mp_eusettlers] Turkey went furthest. It announced in May 2024 that it would stop all trade with Israel until aid flowed freely into Gaza, and on 29 August 2025 its foreign minister, Hakan Fidan, told parliament that Turkey had severed all economic and commercial ties, barred its ships from Israeli ports and closed its airspace, though flight trackers showed Israeli airliners still crossing it that day. Bilateral trade had been nearly $6.8 billion in 2023. [cite:gi17_tn_turkey]

The Arab states and the Abraham Accords

In the reporting checked for this part, none of the Arab states with a peace treaty or normalisation agreement with Israel broke it. The positions they took were conditions. Saudi Arabia told Washington in February 2024 that "there will be no diplomatic relations with Israel" unless an independent Palestinian state is recognised on the 1967 lines with East Jerusalem as its capital. [cite:gi8_reu_saudi2024] The United Arab Emirates, the Accords' principal Arab signatory, said in September 2025 that West Bank annexation would be a "red line," and that "the choice before Israel right now is annexation or integration." [cite:gi6_axios_uae2025] When Netanyahu said in August 2025 that he was attached to the vision of a "Greater Israel," the foreign ministers of 31 states, with the Arab League, the OIC and the Gulf Cooperation Council, called his remarks "a direct threat to Arab national security," and Jordan called them "a dangerous and provocative escalation." [cite:gi7a_oman] [cite:gi7_na_outrage] [scene:gi7_interview|Part Seven: the interview] Later that month President Trump said: "I will not allow Israel to annex the West Bank." [cite:gi6_ap_trump0925]

The Accords themselves held. The UAE and the other signatories kept their trade and security ties with Israel, and on 6 November 2025 Kazakhstan, which has had relations with Israel since 1992, said it would join them. [cite:gi17_aje_kazakh] One state moved toward Israel on the territorial question itself: on 10 August 2026 Colombia's new government recognised Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights, the second country after the United States to do so. [cite:gi8_aje_colombia] [scene:gi8_golan|Part Eight: the Golan]

The pattern is that symbolic and diplomatic measures spread widely while material ones stayed narrow or were reversed. The governments that acted named the war in Gaza, settler violence and annexation as their reasons; Israel's government answered that each measure rewarded Hamas or was biased.
