Bonn's Quiet Partnership, 1957–1962: Strauss, Adenauer and "Aktion Geschäftsfreund"

In the five years after the Sinai war, Israel built a relationship with a state that would not exchange ambassadors with it, and whose head of government many Israelis bitterly opposed meeting at all: the Federal Republic of Germany. The reparations agreement of 1952, told in [scene:rd6_financing|financing the new state], had opened a channel for money between the two governments. What followed between 1957 and 1962 was different in kind. It ran largely through the two defence establishments, it was kept secret by both sides, and part of it was booked in Bonn's accounts as something it was not. Whether any of that money paid for Israel's nuclear reactor at Dimona is still argued, and the evidence on both sides is set out below.

A common account treats Germany's support for Israel as the work of a lobby, on the model of the organizations that grew up in Washington and London. The record of these years points elsewhere. In Germany the relationship was made by a handful of officials at the top of two governments, acting on state policy and in secret, rather than through an organized lobby.

Bonn, 3 July 1957. The channel opened between two defence officials. According to the historian Uri Bar-Joseph, Shimon Peres met West Germany's Defence Minister, Franz Josef Strauss, in Bonn on 3 July 1957. Israel asked for two submarines. Strauss, in Bar-Joseph's account, was ready to "build bridges" and was interested in what the Israeli army had learned in the Sinai war. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] The submarines were not built in Germany. Bar-Joseph writes that they were bought in Britain with German money, for less than the cost of a single French Vautour aircraft. Trade ran the other way as well: Germany bought about $30 million worth of Israeli-made combat materiel. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph]

The contact did not lapse. The historian Michael Brenner notes that by 1960 Strauss and Peres, by then Israel's deputy defence minister, had been in touch for years, and that they spoke at the time of the meeting between their heads of government in March 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_ja_brenner]

The road to New York, February–March 1960. In March 1960 Ben-Gurion was to see two heads of government in the United States, the American President and the West German Chancellor. The American half of the arrangement survives in the State Department's record. On 1 February 1960 Israel's ambassador, Harman, asked Secretary of State Herter for a private meeting between Ben-Gurion and President Eisenhower. He suggested an honorary degree from Brandeis University as the occasion, so that Ben-Gurion could come "quietly." Herter told him an official invitation was out of the question. A handwritten note on the memorandum records the President's approval of an appointment at 11 a.m. on 10 March 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d119] The record fetched for this book shows how the appointment was arranged; it does not include what Ben-Gurion and Eisenhower said to each other on 10 March.

The Waldorf-Astoria, 14 March 1960. Four days later Ben-Gurion, then 73, met Chancellor Konrad Adenauer, 84, in the presidential suite on the 35th floor of the Waldorf-Astoria hotel in New York. No official transcript of the meeting exists. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] [cite:rs_cw4_ja_brenner] [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] What is known of its content comes from the participants' later statements and from what each government did afterwards. Michael Borchard, historian of the Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung, writes that Ben-Gurion proposed economic aid of $500 million as a loan for Israel's further development, and brought a wish list that included guided missiles and submarines. Adenauer agreed to help without naming a sum. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] Bar-Joseph gives the request in a different form, as $40–50 million a year for ten years, which comes to the same order of magnitude. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph]

Afterwards Ben-Gurion said: "Das Deutschland von heute ist nicht das Deutschland von gestern" ("The Germany of today is not the Germany of yesterday"). He said it against bitter opposition from many Israelis. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] [cite:rs_cw4_ja_brenner] The sentence was a political claim, and the Germany Ben-Gurion described was not free of yesterday's men. Hans Globke was one of several figures around the Chancellor with a Nazi-era past: he had written commentaries on the Nuremberg Laws, and was Adenauer's chief adviser. The minister Theodor Oberländer resigned a few months after the Waldorf meeting when his own past became public. [cite:rs_cw4_ja_brenner] [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] Ben-Gurion's sentence did not deny those facts. It can be read as a wager that the state Adenauer led could be judged by what it now did, and that Israel needed what that state could give.

"Aktion Geschäftsfreund." What it gave was a loan, disguised. According to Bar-Joseph, the arrangement agreed after the Waldorf meeting was code-named "Aktion Geschäftsfreund" ("Operation Business Friend"). It provided about $50 million a year for ten years, at 3.6% interest. The money did not go to Israel under Israel's name. It was routed through a government-owned development bank in Frankfurt and booked as transfers under agreements with unnamed developing countries. The first payment came in December 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] The sources do not record Bonn's own reasons for the disguise. The context suggests one: West Germany was withholding diplomatic relations from Israel for fear that Arab states would recognize East Germany in return, and an open loan on this scale would have been hard to reconcile with that policy. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard]

The German documents that Bar-Joseph cites give the transfers year by year: 82 million marks in 1961, 97.6 million in 1962, 150 million in 1963, 149.8 million in 1964 and 150 million in 1965, a total of 629.4 million marks to 1965. The Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung gives a close figure on a slightly different basis: almost 630 million DM up to 1966, not counting weapons. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] The two series agree on the size of the economic loan. Neither, by itself, says what Israel did with it.

The weapons. Alongside the economic loan ran a separate stream of military aid and arms financing, and here the sources disagree on the sum. German documents, under the heading "Frank/Kol," put military-related aid and arms financing from 1962 to early 1965 at 340 million marks. The historian Roni Stauber, writing in 2022, puts it at 500 million marks. Bar-Joseph reports both figures. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] The difference of 160 million marks is not explained in the material available here; it may reflect what each counted as military aid, but the sources do not say, and neither figure can be preferred on the evidence fetched for this book.

Did German money pay for Dimona? The strongest case on each side deserves to be stated in full.

The case that it did is circumstantial, and its proponents say so. Bar-Joseph argues that West Germany was the "major funder" of Dimona through the Negev loan. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] The points that support him are in the record set out above: a loan of about $50 million a year, agreed at a meeting with no transcript, booked in Bonn's accounts under other countries' names, and arranged while the Peres–Strauss defence channel was open. The title of Bar-Joseph's article speaks of "historical clues." The German newspaper Die Welt, as reported by The National Interest, went further and judged the German funding of the program "with near certainty." It cited a claim by Strauss that Ben-Gurion and Adenauer had discussed Israel's nuclear program when they met in Paris in 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_natinterest] Bar-Joseph adds a further thread from the Eichmann trial: he writes that during the trial Strauss stressed to Israeli officials that naming senior officials in Bonn, especially Globke, would put the arrangement at risk, and he quotes Peres as saying that Ben-Gurion linked the Eichmann trial to the Dimona reactor. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] That account rests on Bar-Joseph's reporting and Peres's recollection; how the West German concern played out inside the trial is told in [scene:cw4_eichmann|the Eichmann section].

The case that it is unproven rests on what is missing. Bar-Joseph himself concedes that the Israeli documentation on how the loan was used is missing, and that it is "impossible to assert unequivocally" that Bonn underwrote the reactor. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] No transcript of the Waldorf meeting exists, so no record shows the reactor being discussed there. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] Germany has been silent on the question, and Peres denied that German money funded Dimona. [cite:rs_cw4_natinterest] The Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung describes the money as development aid. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] Strauss's claim about Paris in 1961, on which Die Welt relied, is the claim of one participant, reported second-hand.

Peres's own accounts of who did pay complicate the picture further, because they do not agree with each other. Bar-Joseph reports Peres saying that about half the cost of the reactor and its complex, about $40 million, came from donations by wealthy Jews. The National Interest reports Peres saying that $40 million of Israeli government funds went to Dimona and that this was only half the cost. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] [cite:rs_cw4_natinterest] Both versions put a known payment at about $40 million and both say it covered only half. They disagree on whether that half came from private donors or from the state, and neither says where the other half came from. That unexplained half is the space into which the German hypothesis fits; it is also, on the evidence, only a space.

A state policy, not a lobby. The contrast with Britain in the same years is instructive. In 1957, the year Peres met Strauss in Bonn, Labour Friends of Israel was founded at Westminster. With the later Conservative Friends of Israel, founded in 1974, it is one of the cross-party parliamentary groups through which support for Israel has been organized in British politics. [cite:wikipedia_cfi_uk] The British pattern, in other words, ran through parliamentarians and party organizations, in public. The German pattern of 1957–1962 had nothing comparable. It was made by a defence minister, a chancellor and his staff, and a development bank, acting on a decision of state and in secret. German support for Israel has been described since as driven primarily by explicit government policy tied to historical responsibility, rather than by an organized lobby of the British or American kind; how later German leaders named that doctrine, as Germany's Staatsräson, is told in [scene:wr2_s01|Germany's reason of state].

The cost of secrecy. The arrangement depended on Bonn's refusal to recognize Israel. West Germany put off Israel's requests for diplomatic relations until 1965 for fear that Arab states would recognize East Germany in return. The secrecy did not last. The arms deliveries became public in 1964, military aid was temporarily halted, and the two states established diplomatic relations on 12 May 1965. [cite:rs_cw4_kas_borchard] [cite:rs_cw4_ja_balke] That end belongs to a later stage of this volume. In the years covered here, Israel received German money and German-financed arms from a government that would not exchange ambassadors with it, and Bonn obtained a relationship it could value without having to defend in public.

Some things the record leaves open. What Ben-Gurion and Adenauer said at the Waldorf-Astoria is known only from later accounts. What Israel did with the 629.4 million marks it received to 1965 is not shown by any Israeli document available here, and so the question whether German money paid for any part of Dimona cannot be settled on present evidence: it is a strong inference for one historian and a newspaper, and an unproven claim on the documents. The size of Germany's military aid in 1962–1965 is given as 340 million marks by German records and 500 million by one historian, without a reconciliation. Peres is reported in two incompatible versions on who paid for half the reactor.
