Girard and the Katechon

Of all the influences on Thiel, the one he has named most often is a French literary scholar who taught at Stanford. René Girard was a professor of French and Italian whose theory of imitation and violence Thiel encountered as an undergraduate in the late 1980s, and whose work he has funded since 2007. [cite:de10_gsb] [cite:de10_worldcrunch] By 2025 the same intellectual line had brought Thiel to an older and stranger idea, the "katechon," the force in Saint Paul's letters that holds back the end of the world, and to a series of lectures on the Antichrist delivered in four cities over six months. This section sets out what Girard taught, how Thiel has used it, what Thiel has said about the Antichrist and the katechon, and how much of that is known from his own words rather than from secondhand accounts of private talks.

What Girard taught

Girard's theory has two parts. The first is "mimetic desire": people do not want things on their own account but learn what to want by imitating others, so that characters in novels from Don Quixote to Madame Bovary shape their desires on a model. Because imitators come to want the same things, imitation breeds rivalry, and rivalry, unchecked, spreads through a community. [cite:de10_worldcrunch] The second part is the "scapegoat mechanism." Archaic societies, Girard argued, ended these spreading crises by turning together on a single victim, whose killing restored peace and was then remembered as sacred; religion and political order grew from that hidden founding act. For Girard the Gospels were unique because they told the story from the side of the innocent victim, exposing the mechanism, so that scapegoating could no longer work as well once people saw it for what it was. [cite:de10_worldcrunch] [cite:de10_straussian_es]

What Thiel took from him

Thiel has described meeting Girard "when I was a student at Stanford in the late 1980s," and said that reading Girard's Things Hidden since the Foundation of the World "felt like you understood the meaning of history." [cite:de10_worldcrunch] He turned that interest into money for the field. In 2007 he co-founded Imitatio, with Hamerton-Kelly, to support research on mimetic theory, and it became one of the Thiel Foundation's three programmes, according to a Stanford Graduate School of Business case study; some later accounts date the fund to 2008. [cite:de10_gsb] [cite:de10_worldcrunch]

He used Girard in two directions. In his 2004 essay he set Girard against Strauss: Girard held that the revelation of founding violence was irreversible and might make the myths that hold societies together unsustainable, opening the way to apocalyptic violence, whereas Strauss would have kept such knowledge within a small circle. [cite:de10_straussian_es] In business he used the first half of the theory, imitation leading to rivalry, as an argument against copying competitors and for building something without rivals, as the previous section showed. [cite:de10_worldcrunch] He also passed the theory on. J.D. Vance has written that a talk Thiel gave at Yale "remains the most significant moment of my time at Yale Law School," and that Girard's account of mimetic rivalry "spoke directly to some of the pressures I experienced at Yale"; that story belongs to [scene:de12_the_thiel_years|Part Twelve]. [cite:de10_worldcrunch] A 2026 essay in the Los Angeles Review of Books argued that Vance's Girard is Thiel's Girard, taken from the 2004 essay; that is one critic's reading of the texts, not a finding about Vance's sources beyond what he has said himself. [cite:de10_larb]

The katechon

"Katechon" is a Greek word from the Second Letter to the Thessalonians, where Paul tells his readers that the "man of lawlessness" will not be revealed until something, or someone, that now "restrains" him is taken out of the way. Paul never says what the restrainer is. [cite:de10_rns_christensen] Catholic tradition linked it to the Roman Empire and its emperor; other readings have proposed the Church or the Holy Spirit; and in the twentieth century Carl Schmitt, in The Nomos of the Earth, made it the idea through which a Rome-centred Christianity held off the Antichrist and the end of the age. [cite:de10_wiki_katechon] Thiel's 2004 essay already described the Straussian project as an effort to preserve the katechon. [cite:de10_straussian_es] In 2025 he made the idea the centre of his public thinking.

The Douthat interview, June 2025

The clearest record of what Thiel thinks about the Antichrist is an on-the-record interview, published on 26 June 2025 in Ross Douthat's New York Times series "Interesting Times." Thiel argued that the danger of the age is not runaway technology but its suppression, and that a would-be world ruler would come to power by exploiting the fear of catastrophe. "The slogan of the Antichrist is 'peace and safety,'" he said, citing Paul; the political form he feared was "the one world totalitarian state." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] [cite:de10_nr_douthat] Asked who might play that role today, he said: "In our world, it's far more likely to be Greta Thunberg." [cite:de10_nr_douthat]

Two exchanges in the interview travelled furthest. When Douthat said he assumed Thiel would prefer the human race to endure, Thiel hesitated. "This is a long hesitation," Douthat said; asked directly, "Should the human race survive?", Thiel answered, "Yes," and added that he also wanted humanity to "radically solve these problems," in an exchange about transhumanism and the limits of the body. [cite:de10_douthat_tx] [cite:de10_nr_douthat] The second exchange went to his own business. Douthat observed that Thiel was "deeply invested in Palantir, in military technology" and in surveillance, and suggested that an Antichrist might use the very tools he was building. Thiel replied: "I obviously don't think that that's what I'm doing." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] Both exchanges are his own words, on the record; the second is examined in [scene:de10_philosophy_vs_practice|the next section].

The lectures, September 2025 to March 2026

Between September and October 2025 Thiel gave four lectures, of about two hours each, titled "The Antichrist: A Four-Part Lecture Series," at the Commonwealth Club in San Francisco. They were organised by the Acts 17 Collective, a non-profit that seeks to build a Christian community in the technology industry, and announced as drawing on Girard, Francis Bacon and Schmitt. [cite:de10_fortune_series] [cite:de10_sfstandard] The first was on 15 September 2025; protesters outside carried signs about Palantir and immigration enforcement. [cite:de10_sfstandard] Attendees were told the talks were off the record. On 10 October the Washington Post reported that it had reviewed audio of all four, and that a digital-forensics expert at Berkeley, Hany Farid, judged a sample probably authentic; the Guardian also reported from recordings. [cite:de10_wapo] [cite:de10_varsity]

Because the lectures were private, their content is known only from these leaked recordings and the reporting on them. On that basis, Thiel called the American empire "the natural candidate for Katechon," and also "ground zero of the one-world state"; warned of a "caesaropapist one-world state"; listed tax treaties, financial surveillance and the sanctions system as features of the global order he feared; said that "in the 21st century, the Antichrist is a Luddite who wants to stop all science"; and named the AI-risk writers Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nick Bostrom, with Thunberg, as "legionnaires of the Antichrist." [cite:de10_reason] [cite:de10_wapo] He described himself in the same lectures as "a humble classical liberal." [cite:de10_reason]

He then took the series abroad. On 26 January 2026 he spoke at the Académie des sciences morales et politiques in Paris, to a working group on the future of democracy, saying "je m'inquiète de l'Antéchrist" ("I worry about the Antichrist"); protesters gathered outside. [cite:de10_lgc_paris] [cite:de10_varsity] Within the following week he gave closed "Antichrist Lectures" at St Catharine's College, Cambridge, by invitation of the divinity scholar James Orr, an adviser to the Reform UK party. [cite:de10_varsity] From 15 March 2026 he gave a closed, invitation-only series in Rome; Catholic universities denied hosting it, and the Pope's adviser on artificial intelligence, Father Paolo Benanti, called Thiel a "political theologian." [cite:de10_reuters_rome]

What the argument says, and what it does not

Taken from his own words, the argument is a theological case against global regulation of technology. The danger Thiel names is a single world authority that uses fear of catastrophe, nuclear, climatic or from artificial intelligence, to halt science; the restraining force he hopes for is, at least as a candidate, the United States. [cite:de10_reason] [cite:de10_douthat_tx] It is not an argument for a monarch or for ending elections, and in it the dangerous concentration of power is a global one, not the American executive. It does identify named critics of technology, by name, as servants of the Antichrist. That is a rhetorical attack on people, made in religious language, and it should be described as such; it is not a call for any action against them. [cite:de10_wapo] [cite:de10_reason]

Interpretation, not finding: the stones and the restrainer

The Sophia platform reads this material through two lenses. In the first, Palantir, named after the seeing stones of Tolkien's fiction, which show distant things to whoever holds them and can deceive a holder who thinks he is the one looking, is read as an instrument that could itself become the restrainer's opposite: the man worried about a world state of "peace and safety" owns a share in the software that makes total visibility possible. In the second, Thiel is read as having learned Girard's scapegoat mechanism and inverted it, building tools used on a population that can be blamed. These are readings, attributed to the platform, and they are developed in [scene:de39_seeing_stones|Part Thirty-Nine]. The strongest counter-reading comes from the record itself. Thiel has said in public that he does not think he is building the Antichrist's tools; his named fear is a global authority, not a national one; Girard's theory, as Thiel summarised it in 2004, treats the revelation of scapegoating as irreversible rather than as a technique to be used; and no document shows Thiel directing how Palantir's customers use its software. [cite:de10_douthat_tx] [cite:de10_straussian_es] Whether the readings illuminate anything is for the reader to judge; they are not evidence.
