A State Built on a Census

Lebanon became independent in 1943 on the strength of an agreement that was never written down. The National Pact, worked out that summer between President Bechara el-Khoury, a Maronite Christian, and Prime Minister Riad al-Solh, a Sunni Muslim, and made public on 7 October 1943, assigned the state's top offices by religious community: the presidency to a Maronite, the premiership to a Sunni, the speakership of parliament to a Shia, and the deputy posts to Greek Orthodox Christians. Seats in parliament were shared six to five in favour of Christians. [cite:hz1_wiki_pact] The ratio rested on the census of 1932, which counted Christians as a slim majority; no full census has been taken since. [cite:hz1_bpb_refugees]

The pact was also a bargain about borders and allegiance. Muslim leaders accepted the frontiers France had drawn in 1920 and gave up the goal of union with Syria; Christian leaders gave up seeking French protection and accepted that Lebanon had an "Arab face". [cite:hz1_loc_pact] The Library of Congress country study on Lebanon judges that the arrangement "merely perpetuated the power of the privileged", guaranteeing the position of each community's established leaders rather than of the communities themselves. [cite:hz1_loc_pact] The six-to-five ratio lasted until the Taif Agreement of 1989, which ended the civil war and moved parliament to an even split between Christians and Muslims while trimming the Maronite president's powers. [cite:hz1_wiki_pact]

The Shia Periphery

Of the large communities, the Shia gained least from this design. Most lived in two regions far from Beirut's banks and ports: the south, known historically as Jabal Amel, and the Bekaa valley in the east. Minority Rights Group International, in its profile of the community, writes that before the 1960s most Shia "remained on the political and economic periphery", represented in parliament by a few wealthy landowning families. [cite:hz1_mrg_shia] From the 1960s many moved to the capital in search of work. By 1975, by the same account, 40 per cent of the Shia of Jabal Amel had moved to Beirut, where the newcomers from the south and the Bekaa settled in the poor districts on the city's southern edge and became, in that profile's words, "acutely aware of their comparative poverty". [cite:hz1_mrg_shia] The same profile gives the community's present weight as almost a third of the population, up to 32 per cent, against 21 per cent of the seats in parliament; because no census has been taken, both the population share and the size of the gap are estimates. [cite:hz1_mrg_shia]

The south was also the region nearest Israel. That geography would matter more than any other fact in this book: from the late 1960s onward, the villages that were already the poorest in Lebanon became the ground on which Palestinian guerrillas, the Israeli army and, later, Hezbollah fought. The next section follows how that happened. [scene:hz1_plo|The PLO in Lebanon]

Musa al-Sadr and the Movement of the Deprived

The person who turned this community into a political force was a cleric born outside Lebanon. Musa al-Sadr was born in Qom, Iran, in 1928, the son of an ayatollah, and trained in Qom and in Najaf in Iraq, with a period studying law and economics at Tehran University. [cite:hz1_brit_sadr] He arrived in Tyre in late 1959 as an emissary of senior Shia clerics, and spent his first years in social work among a community that Encyclopaedia Britannica calls "largely disenfranchised". [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] [cite:hz1_brit_sadr] In 1969 he became the first head of the Supreme Islamic Shia Council, and Britannica dates the formation of this higher council to 1968–69. [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] [cite:hz1_brit_sadr]

In 1974 he founded, with Hussein el-Husseini, the Movement of the Deprived (Harakat al-Mahrumin), which pressed for better economic and social conditions for the Shia and built schools and clinics across the south. [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] When the civil war broke out in 1975, the Shia were the only major community without a militia. Al-Sadr's answer was Amal, formed in 1975 as the movement's armed wing to defend the community. [cite:hz1_brit_amal] [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] Amal was a Lebanese party with Lebanese aims: protection of the community and a larger share of the state. Its later relations with Syria were close, and Minority Rights Group describes it as a pragmatic party that cooperated with Damascus against a Palestinian armed revival in the 1980s. [cite:hz1_mrg_shia]

This matters for the question at the centre of Part Three: whether Hezbollah would exist without Israel's invasions. Lebanon's Shia had begun to organise politically, and to arm, years before 1982, through a movement that was not created by Iran's revolution (which came later) or by Israel. [cite:hz1_brit_amal] Part Two follows how Hezbollah's founding drew on, and split from, this existing movement. [scene:hz2_founding|The founding] That is a documented sequence of events; what weight to give it is an argument, taken up in Part Three. [scene:hz3_question|Would Hezbollah exist?]

The Disappearance

On 25 August 1978 al-Sadr flew to Libya at the invitation of its ruler, Muammar Gaddafi, with Sheikh Mohammad Yaacoub and the journalist Abbas Badreddine. The three were last seen on 31 August 1978 and were never heard from again. [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] [cite:hz1_olj_sadr] Libya's government said they had left Tripoli that evening on an Alitalia flight to Rome. Passengers and crew told Italian investigators they had not seen anyone resembling al-Sadr, and Italian authorities concluded in July 2015 that he never set foot in Italy. [cite:hz1_olj_sadr] Lebanon's government indicted Gaddafi over the disappearance on 27 August 2008; Libya under Gaddafi consistently denied any responsibility. [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr]

The case is still open. Gaddafi's son Hannibal was seized by armed men in December 2015, taken to Lebanon and then held by the Lebanese authorities on a charge of withholding information about the disappearance. He was never tried, and was released on bail of $900,000 in November 2025. [cite:hz1_newarab_hannibal] What happened to al-Sadr has not been established by any court. The public record does not show who killed him, or whether he was killed in Libya, and the accounts of former Libyan officials remain the claims of those officials.

The disappearance removed Amal's founder in the same year that Israel's 1978 invasion of the south drove more than a hundred thousand people from their homes in the south, and less than six months before a revolution in Iran brought clerics into power in the country of his birth. Some of his followers would later find a different patron in Tehran. [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] [cite:hz1_aawsat_arafat] [scene:hz1_1978|1978 and 1979]
