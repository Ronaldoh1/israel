The Split: Productivity and Pay after 1973

The promise of hard work rests on an economic claim that most Americans never hear stated in so many words: when workers produce more in an hour, they will be paid more for that hour. Economists call output per hour of work "productivity". The claim is not a slogan; it is the standard account of how wages are set in a competitive market, and the economist Michael Strain of the American Enterprise Institute has defended it against those who say the link between pay and productivity has been "effectively severed". [cite:ad4_aspen_strain] For a generation after the Second World War, the record and the promise moved together. Some time in the 1970s they began to separate. This section sets out how far they separated, by which measure, and why careful economists on different sides still disagree about the size of the gap while largely agreeing on part of its cause.

Why the part begins in 1973

Three dates recur in this story, and each has its own reason. The first is 1973. By the Pew Research Center's count, the purchasing power of the average hourly wage peaked in January 1973, when the average was $4.03 an hour, worth $23.68 in 2018 dollars. [cite:ad4_pew_wages2018] The second is 1979, the starting point the Economic Policy Institute (EPI), a labour-aligned research group, uses for its widely quoted productivity–pay chart. The third is 1986, when the second of two large tax laws set the top income tax rate on a path down to 28 per cent. [cite:us11_tpc_toprate] The period between the first and last of these dates is the hinge of this book: the point at which the promise as taught and the system as built started to diverge in several mechanisms at once. The wage split comes first because every later part depends on it.

The EPI series

The EPI's chart is the version most readers have seen. Its September 2026 edition, with data to the second quarter of 2026, measures productivity as "net" output per hour for the whole economy, meaning output after subtracting the wear and tear on equipment and buildings, called depreciation. It measures pay as the average hourly compensation, wages plus benefits, of production and nonsupervisory workers in the private sector, a group that is roughly four in five private employees and leaves out managers. Both are converted into constant dollars with the same consumer price index. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] Constant or "real" dollars are dollars adjusted for inflation, so that a 1979 hour of pay and a 2026 hour of pay buy comparable baskets of goods.

On that basis the two lines ran close together for a generation. Setting both equal to 100 in early 1948, the EPI's index reaches 214.8 for productivity and 193.8 for pay by early 1973: productivity had risen by 114.8 per cent and pay by 93.8 per cent. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] The lines were not identical, but most of each gain in output was matched by a gain in the typical worker's hourly pay. From the fourth quarter of 1979 to the second quarter of 2026, the record is different. The productivity index rose from 216.8 to 419.9, an increase of 93.7 per cent; the pay index rose from 190.7 to 253.0, an increase of 32.7 per cent. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] Spread across those 46 and a half years, that is roughly 1.4 per cent a year for productivity and 0.6 per cent a year for pay (the compound rate that turns 1 into 1.937 and 1.327 respectively over the period). The EPI's own summary is that productivity "has grown 2.9x as much as pay". [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026]

The critics: how much of the gap is measurement

The EPI chart has been challenged for more than fifteen years, and the challenges are serious. They turn on three choices, each defensible, each of which widens the gap. The first is which workers are counted. The EPI's line covers production and nonsupervisory workers; managers and professionals, whose pay rose fastest, are left out, while productivity covers everyone's output. The second is whether pay means wages or total compensation. Health insurance and retirement contributions grew faster than wages for decades, so a wage-only line looks worse than a compensation line. The EPI uses compensation; many popular charts do not. The third, and the largest, is the price index used to turn dollars into real dollars, called the deflator. Productivity is usually measured with the prices of what the economy produces. Pay is usually deflated with the consumer price index, the prices of what households buy. When consumer prices rise faster than output prices, using the consumer index for pay and the output index for productivity opens a gap even if workers' share of income never changed. [cite:ad4_heritage_sherk] [cite:ad4_aspen_strain]

The economist Martin Feldstein made the case most directly in a 2008 National Bureau of Economic Research paper. Measuring total compensation and deflating it with the same nonfarm business price index used for productivity, he found that from 1970 to 2006 productivity grew 1.9 per cent a year and real compensation per hour 1.7 per cent a year. Employee compensation was 66 per cent of national income in 1970 and 64 per cent in 2006. [cite:ad4_nber_feldstein] James Sherk of the Heritage Foundation, a conservative think tank, reached a similar conclusion in 2013: from 1973 to 2012, measured consistently, productivity rose 100 per cent and hourly compensation 77 per cent. He attributed 35 per cent of the apparent gap to counting only some workers and only wages, 44 per cent to the different price indexes and 21 per cent to other factors such as depreciation and import prices, and called most of the gap "a statistical illusion". [cite:ad4_heritage_sherk]

Strain's 2019 review for the Aspen Institute's Economic Strategy Group, summarising the economist Robert Lawrence, reported that switching to net output for the whole economy cut the gap by 20 per cent, using compensation instead of wages cut it by 8.2 per cent, including managers and professionals explained 30 per cent, and deflating wages with an output price index explained 35 per cent. [cite:ad4_aspen_strain] He also reported the work of Anna Stansbury and Lawrence Summers, who found that from 1975 to 2015 a one-point rise in productivity growth was associated with a 0.73-point rise in compensation growth for the median worker and 0.74 points for average compensation, but only 0.53 points for production and nonsupervisory workers. [cite:ad4_aspen_strain] Productivity, in other words, still pushes pay up; something else has been pushing the typical worker's pay down at the same time.

What survives the dispute

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the federal agency that produces both the productivity and the price data, tested the argument industry by industry. Its 2017 study of 183 industries from 1987 to 2015 found that productivity outpaced compensation in 83 per cent of them. In the median industry the output price index rose about 0.6 percentage points a year more slowly than consumer prices, and labour's share of income fell by about 0.6 per cent a year. The study's conclusion was that "the median effect of the change in labor share was the same as that of the difference in deflators." [cite:ad4_bls_btn2017] In plain terms, about half of the gap in a typical industry was a matter of which prices one uses, and about half was a real fall in the share of each industry's income that went to the people who worked in it.

That finding is close to what the careful critics themselves concede. Sherk acknowledged that median pay grew more slowly than average productivity, which he attributed to technology rewarding higher skills. [cite:ad4_heritage_sherk] Strain wrote that real wage growth of about half a per cent a year for production workers is not strong and that gains have been unequally shared. [cite:ad4_aspen_strain] Pew found that since 2000 real weekly wages rose 3 per cent for the lowest tenth of earners and 15.7 per cent for the top tenth. [cite:ad4_pew_wages2018] The dispute, then, is about size and cause, not about direction. Average compensation, counted fully, roughly kept up with average productivity, especially before 2000. The typical worker's pay did not; the difference went to higher earners and, in the BLS industry data, to capital.

Where it works as promised

The taught version holds in two specific respects. For the generation from 1948 to 1973 the EPI's own index shows typical pay rising almost in step with output, the period many Americans remember as the Dream's high point. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] And across the whole economy, total compensation per hour has kept growing with productivity on Feldstein's matched measure, so that a worker's average hour bought more in 2006 than in 1970. [cite:ad4_nber_feldstein] Where the promise fails is narrower and more precise: for the worker in the middle, an extra unit of output after 1979 has translated into well under one unit of extra pay, by every measure reviewed here.

The EPI's explanation and its limits

The EPI's own account of why the split happened is policy-based. It lists "policy choices": tolerance of excess unemployment, minimum-wage increases that were "smaller and rarer", labour law that "failed to keep pace", lower top tax rates, deregulation and weaker antitrust enforcement. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] Those are causal claims, and each needs its own evidence.
