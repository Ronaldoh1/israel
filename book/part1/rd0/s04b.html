Baron de Hirsch and the Argentine Alternative, 1889–1896

Edmond was not the only Jewish financier settling refugees on the land. In September 1891, six years before the First Zionist Congress met at Basel, a company was incorporated in London to move persecuted Jews out of the Russian Empire and Romania and settle them as farmers. Its founder was Baron Maurice de Hirsch, a German-Jewish financier and philanthropist. Its destination was Argentina. Its name was the Jewish Colonization Association, the JCA. [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] [cite:wiki_jewish_colonization_association]

A common account holds that Zionism was the first and only organized plan to resettle persecuted European Jews somewhere new. The record does not bear that out. The first large, organized and capitalized attempt to answer what the century called "the Jewish Question" through mass resettlement was the JCA, and it was not a plan for a state. It was a plan for refuge and for a change of livelihood, and the place it chose first lay on another continent. That precedent was in front of Theodor Herzl when he wrote his own pamphlet five years later.

The company and its capital. The JCA was incorporated under the English Companies Acts, a charitable body given the legal form of a company. Its authorized capital was £2,000,000, divided into 20,000 shares of £100 each that paid no dividend. Hirsch subscribed 19,993 of the 20,000 himself. [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] In practice, then, this was one man's fortune given the form of an institution. According to Hadassah Magazine's review of Matthias Lehmann's history of the association, the JCA's budget was described as "the greatest charitable trust in the world at that time." [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] One account in the research for this book adds that Hirsch put in a further £7,000,000 the following year. No separate source for that figure has been found, and it is given here only as that account's claim.

The association's purpose was written into its founding terms, and the Jewish Encyclopedia records it in these words: to "assist and promote the emigration of Jews...to any parts of the world, and to form and establish colonies in various parts of North and South America and other countries." [cite:wiki_jewish_colonization_association] The scope was deliberately open. "Any parts of the world" is not a Palestine project under another name. Argentina became the JCA's founding and primary field, but the charter did not confine it there.

The colonies. The Argentine work began before the company existed. Moisés Ville was founded in 1889 by Dr. Wilhelm Löwenthal with Hirsch's backing (one account calls the initiative Hirsch's, another Löwenthal's), and is described as the first large agricultural colony for Eastern European Jews in the JCA's network, although it predates the association's incorporation by two years. [cite:wiki_moises_ville] Further colonies followed: Mauricio, Clara (also called Villa Clara) and San Antonio. By 1896, the year Hirsch died, roughly 6,757 colonists lived across the Argentine settlements. By 1939 the JCA held about 1,519,000 acres in Argentina. [cite:wiki_moises_ville] [cite:wiki_hirsch_argentina_settlements]

The mechanism was simple to state. Money came from one fortune, held in a London company. The company bought land, brought families from the Russian Empire and Romania, and settled them on the land as farmers. The Argentine government was the sovereign; the JCA was a landowner and a sponsor inside it. Nothing in the arrangement gave the colonists, or the company, any political authority of their own.

The harder history. Popular memory has often treated Moisés Ville as a bright counterpoint to European persecution, a place free of prejudice. The documented record is more complicated. The settlers met real hardship, including a typhus epidemic and food shortages. There is also a documented history of violence within the community, examined directly in Javier Sinay's book The Murders of Moisés Ville. [cite:wiki_moises_ville] The numbers above are real and substantial. They do not show that the settlers' experience was a uniformly happy one, and an honest account keeps both.

The idea behind it: "productivization". What separated the JCA from the political movement that followed was not its scale but its theory. Its explicit goal was not sovereignty, and not autonomy. It was to "productivize" Jews: to move them out of urban trades, in which they were often persecuted, and into manual and agricultural labor, so that their social and economic position within existing nation-states would become normal. [cite:wiki_maurice_de_hirsch] It was assimilation through labor, not separation through statehood. Two answers were now on offer to the same nineteenth-century question of how persecuted European Jews could build a secure future. One said: a state of their own. The other, tried first and at real scale, said: a different livelihood inside the nations that already existed.

Whether productivization worked is a separate question, and the sources used here do not answer it. They establish that the theory was held and acted on in Argentina. They give no basis for saying that it was vindicated or refuted by what happened there, or that economic normalization reduced antisemitism.

Refuge, not a rival nation. Historians are explicit on the central point. The JCA aimed at agricultural resettlement and integration into Argentine society; there was no political program for Jewish national autonomy in Argentina. [cite:wiki_jewish_colonization_association] That is why Argentina could appear in Herzl's pamphlet of 1896 as a candidate territory rather than as a competing national project already under way. Had the JCA been building Jewish self-government on the Pampas, Argentina and Palestine would have been two national movements in competition. They were not. One offered refuge and integration; the other came to offer refuge and statehood. That difference let Herzl weigh Argentina as an option, a choice examined in [scene:s03|Dreyfus, Herzl and Der Judenstaat]. The distinction describes the institution's declared aim. It does not tell us how every colonist or organizer saw the two projects in private.

Nor were the two simply opponents. After Hirsch's death in 1896 the JCA also began to fund settlement in Palestine. [cite:wiki_jewish_colonization_association] In the same year, Baron Edmond de Rothschild instructed the JCA to begin lending to the colonists he supported there. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] By one account he later transferred the formal administration of his colonies to the association, in 1899. [cite:schama_two_rothschilds] That story belongs to [scene:bc_s04|Edmond de Rothschild's colonies]. The overlap matters here because it rules out a tidy picture of Argentina against Palestine. The same philanthropic body, built for any country, came to work in both.

Hirsch's death and its date. Hirsch died in 1896, a year before the Basel Congress of 1897, and never saw it. [cite:hadassah_mag_hirsch] One passage in the research for this book says he died "five years before" the Congress; that is an error, and the dates above are the ones the sources support. Because he died before Basel, his record cannot show what he would have made of political Zionism. It shows only that he had chosen a different instrument, and that his foundation did not treat the two causes as exclusive.

What the precedent shows, and what it does not. Organized, heavily funded planning for Jewish resettlement existed years before Herzl and decades before the Holocaust. When the Zionist movement later fixed on Palestine, it was choosing among alternatives, one of which had already been tried on the ground at real scale. The record does not show that Hirsch opposed the later Zionist project or saw himself as its competitor. Nor do the sources here say how the colonists themselves weighed their choice, how many left the colonies, or how the Argentine settlements compared in cost per family with the colonies in Palestine. Those questions remain open in the material this book draws on.
