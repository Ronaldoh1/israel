The Members at Zero

Every list in Part Four ends with a short group of names beside which the figure is nothing. In the Congress of 2026, on Track AIPAC's count of career pro-Israel money, four senators in the Democratic caucus stand at zero: Bernie Sanders, Chris Van Hollen, Jeff Merkley and Peter Welch. In the House, seven Democrats do, among them Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Rashida Tlaib, Ilhan Omar, Summer Lee, Pramila Jayapal, Greg Casar and Ayanna Pressley, and one Republican, Thomas Massie. [cite:trackaipac_congress] This section asks what is documented about the members who refuse the money: who they are, whether their number is growing, and what has happened to them.

A small group, growing slowly. For most of the period this volume covers, refusing pro-Israel money was unusual and largely confined to members already known as critics of Israeli policy. That began to change in 2025. During the August recess of that year three House Democrats who had taken AIPAC money said they would take no more: Deborah Ross of North Carolina, who had received $41,900 in the 2022 cycle and $97,876 in 2024; Valerie Foushee of North Carolina, who had received more than $800,000 in direct and bundled AIPAC giving and about $2 million in super PAC support in her first race; and Morgan McGarvey of Kentucky. [cite:pm_intercept_ross] [cite:pm_gazette_reject] They acted, the reporting found, after constituents raised the issue at town-hall meetings, in a year when Gallup found that only 8 percent of Democrats approved of Israel's military action in Gaza, down from 36 percent in November 2023. [cite:pm_gazette_reject] Three members is a small share of the 120 Democrats elected in 2024 with AIPAC's endorsement, and others said they would keep taking the money. [cite:pm_gazette_reject] The 2026 primaries, described in Part Five, showed the money itself becoming an issue in Democratic campaigns.

What happened to the members at zero. The record of the members who criticized the lobby includes both the largest spending against sitting members and formal rebukes. Two progressive members of the House in 2024, Jamaal Bowman and Cori Bush, were defeated in primaries in which AIPAC's super PAC spent record sums against them, in Bowman's case more than any interest group had spent on a single House race, as Part Five sets out. [cite:factcheck_udp_2024] The Republican at zero, Thomas Massie, lost in 2026 in the most expensive House primary on record, with the president also against him. [cite:lpm_massie_gallrein_funding] The case of Ilhan Omar shows the institutional side.

Ilhan Omar. In February 2019, asked who was paying members of Congress to support Israel, Omar replied on Twitter "AIPAC!" and "It's all about the Benjamins." Leaders of both parties said the words invoked the antisemitic trope of Jewish money, and she apologized. Days later she spoke of "the political influence in this country that says it is OK for people to push for allegiance to a foreign country"; the Anti-Defamation League's chief executive urged the Speaker to hold a vote, and the House passed a resolution, broadened to condemn many forms of bigotry, by 407 to 23, with Omar voting in favor. [cite:axios_omar_removal] [cite:nbc_omar_vote_2019] In February 2023 the House voted 218 to 211, on party lines, to remove her from the Foreign Affairs Committee, citing six of her statements, the "Benjamins" tweet among them; the Democratic leader, Hakeem Jeffries, called it "political revenge." [cite:cbs_omar_removal] In her primaries, a challenger raised nearly $500,000 from pro-Israel donors in 2020; in 2022 AIPAC's super PAC gave $350,000 to a separate committee backing her opponent, a transfer disclosed only after the vote she won by about 2,500 votes; in 2024 her opponents spent only about $110,000, AIPAC having reportedly judged the challenger too weak. [cite:strib_omar_2024] [cite:jewishinsider_udp_omar_2022] [cite:washex_omar_2024]

The comparison above is the one drawn in [scene:cm_s07|the asymmetry test], and it has two readings, both documented. On one, a member's public commitment to the defense of a foreign state drew no institutional response, while a member's criticism of the pressure to make such commitments drew a congressional vote. On the other, the response to Omar was a response to language that invoked a long-standing antisemitic trope about Jews and money, for which she herself apologized, and not to her criticism of policy. [cite:nbc_omar_resolution] Both readings rest on the same sequence of events. The next section turns to the one member in the record who left Congress altogether.
