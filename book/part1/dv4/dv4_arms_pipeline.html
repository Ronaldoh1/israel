The Arms Pipeline, and the Times Washington Pulled the Lever

Money becomes force only when it becomes weapons, and the path from an appropriation in Washington to a munition in Israel runs through a set of laws and practices that no other foreign military enjoys in the same combination. This section describes that pipeline: the legal promise of a military edge, the stockpile kept in Israel, the joint missile-defence programmes and the emergency transfers of the Gaza war. It then sets beside it the other half of the record, which the test in Part One requires: the documented occasions when Washington held weapons or money back, what it was trying to achieve, and whether it worked.

An edge written into law. The idea that Israel should keep a qualitative military edge over its neighbours dates from the Cold War and was applied to Israel after the 1973 war. In 2008 Congress wrote it into statute. The Naval Vessel Transfer Act of that year defined the edge, required an assessment of it every four years, and required the executive to determine, before selling weapons to other Middle Eastern states, that the sale would not undermine it. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] In effect, every American arms sale to an Arab government is now measured against Israel's advantage by law.

A stockpile on Israeli soil. The United States keeps a reserve of munitions in Israel, the War Reserve Stocks for Allies–Israel. In the first six months of the Gaza war it was drawn on to speed deliveries, and Congress has authorized additions to it of up to $500 million a year through 2027. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] Alongside the stockpile sit the jointly funded missile-defence programmes: the 2016 memorandum set aside $5 billion for missile defence over ten years, and the April 2024 supplemental added $4 billion more and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser system. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] The memorandum also sets a goal of producing a share of these systems in the United States. [cite:israeled_mou_text]

The war years. Brown University's Costs of War project counted at least $21.7 billion in American military aid to Israel in the two years after 7 October 2023, including at least $4.4 billion drawn from American stockpiles in the first year. [cite:ap_cow_2yr] In March 2025 the State Department used emergency authority to speed about $4 billion in arms sales past the usual period of congressional review. [cite:khaleej_rubio_4b] [cite:aca_rubio_bypass] On 28 February 2026 American and Israeli forces began joint strikes on Iran, killing its Supreme Leader on the first day; by 3 September 2026 the US Central Command estimated the American cost of that war at $43.6 billion, most of it munitions, and the Congressional Budget Office estimated about $3 billion a month after August. [cite:wiki_iran_war_2026] [cite:ap_iran_cost_436] [cite:cbo_iran_38b] By then the United States was no longer only supplying the force; it was using its own alongside Israel's.

The laws that could restrict. Three legal tools could in principle stop the flow. Section 620I of the Foreign Assistance Act bars assistance to any country that restricts the delivery of American humanitarian aid. [cite:aljazeera_620i_explainer] The Leahy laws bar assistance to foreign security units credibly implicated in gross violations of human rights. [cite:leahy_law_statedept] [cite:congress_leahy_law] And a national security memorandum issued in February 2024, NSM-20, required recipients to give written assurances that they would comply with international humanitarian law and not obstruct aid; it was later rescinded by the Trump administration. [cite:ajil_biden_memorandum_israel_compliance] [cite:jns_trump_rescinds_nsm20] Each of these works only when the executive formally finds a violation. In May 2024 a joint State and Defense report concluded that Israel was not blocking aid, although, according to reporting at the time, two agencies had found otherwise; a senior official resigned in protest. [cite:foreignpolicy_620i_scorecard] The finding was never made, and the laws were not applied.

The times Washington said no. The record nonetheless contains real uses of leverage, and they show the lever works when a President chooses to pull it.

  • In 1956 President Eisenhower forced Israel to withdraw from the Sinai Peninsula after the Suez war. [cite:dv4_jvl_special_alliance]
  • In 1987 Israel's cabinet cancelled its own fighter aircraft, the Lavi, partly financed with about $1.8 billion in American aid, after sustained American pressure over its cost and its competition with American aircraft. [cite:migflug_lavi_cancelled]
  • In 1991 President George H. W. Bush asked Congress to delay Israel's request for $10 billion in loan guarantees, to stop settlement building while he assembled the Madrid peace conference. He called himself "one lonely little guy" against "powerful political forces." After Yitzhak Rabin's government curtailed settlements in 1992, the guarantees were approved, reduced by the amount Israel spent on settlements. [cite:djn_loan_guarantee_1991] [cite:fmep_loan_guarantees_1992]
  • In 2000 Israel cancelled the sale of Phalcon early-warning aircraft to China a day before a scheduled House vote, after American objections and reports that aid was at risk; it later paid China $350 million in compensation. [cite:ynet_phalcon_2000] [cite:flightglobal_phalcon_350m]
  • In May 2024 the Biden administration held a shipment of 2,000-pound bombs over concern about their use in densely populated Rafah, the first restriction of its military support in the war. On 25 January 2025 the new administration released them. [cite:dv4_national_bombs_2025]

What the pipeline shows. In most of these cases the trigger was an American interest, such as competition with American industry, China and Taiwan, or a peace conference the President wanted, rather than the conduct of the war itself; the human-rights statutes, by contrast, were never applied. [cite:migflug_lavi_cancelled] [cite:ynet_phalcon_2000] [cite:foreignpolicy_620i_scorecard] That pattern matters for the verdict. It shows that Washington's support is not automatic or beyond its control. It is a policy, renewed by choice, that its government has bent when it wanted something and has not bent on the questions most of the world has asked it to.
