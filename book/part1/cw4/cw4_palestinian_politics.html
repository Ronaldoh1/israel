Palestinian Politics, 1957–1962: Fatah Abroad, Al-Ard at Home

The houses of Wadi Salib had belonged to Palestinians who became refugees in 1948, and the refugees' own politics did not stop in 1948 either. The diplomacy of these years was conducted almost entirely between governments: Israel's, the United States', France's, West Germany's, and those of the Arab states. In the American and Israeli records of these years, Palestinians appear mainly as refugees, a question to be settled between governments, rather than as political actors. That was not because they had stopped acting. Between 1957 and 1962 two movements took shape that would matter for decades, one among Palestinians abroad and one among the Palestinian Arabs who had remained inside Israel and become its citizens. Neither was large at the time. Both were defined by a choice to act as Palestinians in their own name: abroad, independently of the Arab governments; inside Israel, as a Palestinian national movement among the state's Arab citizens.

The ground both stood on was the war of 1948. About 700,000 Palestinians fled or were expelled from their homes in that war, the event Palestinians call the Nakba, or "catastrophe." The refugees settled in Gaza, then under Egyptian administration, in the West Bank, then under Jordanian administration, and in Lebanon, Syria and Jordan. That displacement was the political fact around which every later Palestinian movement organized. [cite:npr_hamashistory] A decade later, Palestinians were scattered across those places and beyond them.

Fatah in Kuwait. One of those places was Kuwait. According to the Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question, published by the Institute for Palestine Studies, Fatah's core group was "most likely founded in Kuwait in autumn 1957." Its founders were five or six Palestinian expatriates, most of them from Gaza, among them Yasir Arafat and Khalil al-Wazir; Salah Khalaf and Kamal Adwan joined soon after. [cite:rs_cw4_palquest_fatah] The date is disputed. The Encyclopedia of the Modern Middle East and North Africa reports that some of the founders themselves date the movement to 1959, when the Kuwait group took over a magazine. [cite:rs_cw4_emmena_fatah] The two dates may describe different things: the first the gathering of a small group, the second the moment it acquired a public voice. The sources used here do not resolve which the founders meant, and both positions are given.

The magazine was Filastinuna – nida' al-hayat, published in Beirut. In its first issue, in October 1959, the movement's name first became public. The name was a reversed acronym of Harakat al-tahrir al-watani al-Filastini, the Palestinian National Liberation Movement. [cite:rs_cw4_palquest_fatah] [cite:rs_cw4_emmena_fatah]

"Palestine first." What made the group distinct was not its size but its argument. Fatah took the prevailing slogan of the day, which made Arab unity the road to Palestine, and reversed it, holding that "the liberation of Palestine is the road to Arab unity." It sought to act independently of Arab governments and parties, and it asked its activists to give up their party affiliations. Its principles were armed struggle, "Palestine first," and non-interference in the internal affairs of Arab states. [cite:rs_cw4_palquest_fatah] [cite:rs_cw4_emmena_fatah] The last of these principles can be read as the practical condition of the second: a movement that wished to operate from Arab capitals without being captured by them had to promise not to meddle in their politics. Fatah was, in the later summary of NPR's history of the period, explicitly secular and nationalist, and it sought Palestinian statehood through a combination of armed struggle and political negotiation. [cite:npr_hamashistory]

In the years covered here Fatah was a magazine and a network, not an armed force. Its armed operations, and the Arab League's creation of the Palestine Liberation Organization in 1964 under Ahmad Shuqayri, which partly pre-empted Fatah, belong to Stage Six. Fatah's military wing began operations at the end of 1964, and Fatah became the dominant faction in the PLO by 1969. [cite:rs_cw4_emmena_fatah] [cite:npr_hamashistory] The later rivalry between the PLO and the Islamist movement that became Hamas is told in [scene:hma_src_s02|Palestinian political origins].

Al-Ard inside Israel. Inside Israel the setting was entirely different. Most of the Arab citizens of the state lived under the military government whose structure Volume II describes and whose Border Police unit killed 49 villagers at Kafr Qasim in 1956, as told in [scene:mg_s01|eighteen years of military rule]. That regime remained in force throughout this period. It was under it that Al-Ard was founded in 1959, as the first Palestinian national movement among Israel's Arab citizens after 1948. Its leaders included Sabri Jiryis, Habib Qahwaji, Saleh Baransi and Mansour Kardosh. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] According to Edo Konrad, writing in +972 Magazine, it emerged from the remains of the Popular Front, a grouping of nationalists and communists. [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972]

Al-Ard's demands were stated in its own documents. It called for equal rights for Arab citizens and for recognition of the refugees' right to return. Its bylaws argued: "If the Jews have a right to an independent state, the people of Palestine also have a right to an independent state." [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] That sentence can be read as the movement's own answer to the charge later brought against it: it argued from a Jewish right to statehood to the same right for Palestinians.

A newspaper one issue at a time. The state's response came first through licensing. Al-Ard was refused a licence for a newspaper. It found a way around the refusal in a rule surviving from the Mandate that allowed one issue of an unlicensed publication a year. Al-Ard published issue after issue under changing titles, each of which kept the words "al-Ard." After the thirteenth issue its leaders were convicted of publishing without a licence. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] The tactic tells something about both sides: the movement's leaders chose a legal device rather than an underground press, and the authorities answered with prosecution under the licensing law rather than with a charge about the content of what was printed.

The movement then tried to set up a printing company. The registrar refused to register it, on grounds of "public security and interest." The company was registered in early 1962, after the Supreme Court ruled that the registrar had no power to assess questions of national security. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] The ruling was a limit on an official's discretion, not a judgment on Al-Ard's aims; it held that a companies registrar was the wrong official to decide whether a company endangered the state.

"Two organizations which resent Israel." The government's view was given by the Prime Minister. Ben-Gurion told the Knesset that there were "two organizations which resent Israel," and named Al-Ard among them. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] The sources used here do not give the date of the statement or name the second organization.

The two readings. Al-Ard has been read in two ways, and the court that ruled on it in 1965 divided along the same line. In the first, it was a civil-rights and national movement, suppressed because it voiced dissent. That is the reading of Fida Jiryis, writing in the Palestine-Israel Journal in 2023, and of Konrad in +972 Magazine, whose article argues that dissent has always been off limits to Arab citizens in Israel's democracy. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] Its strongest evidence is the movement's own record: it used the courts and the licensing law, registered a company, and wrote into its bylaws a recognition of Jewish statehood alongside a claim to Palestinian statehood. In the second reading, Al-Ard was a movement aimed at the destruction of the state. That was the view of the Haifa district commissioner, of the security services, and, in 1965, of the majority of the Supreme Court. [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] Its strongest statement is the court majority's, through Justice Agranat: that the list aimed at "the elimination of the State of Israel." The sources used here give the conclusion of those officials and judges more fully than the evidence they relied on.

The end, 1964–1965. The movement's end falls after this stage and is told here in brief. Al-Ard was outlawed in 1964. Jiryis attributes the order to Prime Minister Eshkol; +972 Magazine attributes it to the Defence Minister. Eshkol held both posts, so the two accounts may describe the same act. In October 1965 the Supreme Court upheld, by two votes to one, the disqualification of Al-Ard's Socialist List. Justice Agranat held that the list aimed at "the elimination of the State of Israel." Justice Cohn dissented, finding no "real or clear or present danger." [cite:rs_cw4_jiryis_pij] [cite:rs_cw4_konrad_972] The court's division reproduces the two readings above, inside a single bench.

The two movements of these years made the same move in different settings. Fatah, abroad, refused to wait on the Arab states; Al-Ard, inside Israel, organized as a Palestinian national movement among the state's Arab citizens. One chose armed struggle as a principle from the start. The other chose the licensing law, the companies registry and the courts. They were answered differently: Fatah was partly pre-empted by the Arab League's creation of the PLO; Al-Ard was prosecuted, outlawed and, finally, kept off the ballot by the Supreme Court.

What the record leaves open. The founding date of Fatah remains disputed between autumn 1957 and 1959, and the sources do not settle it. The Arab Nationalist Movement, the other main current of Palestinian political organization in these years, is not documented in the sources used for this section, so its part in the story cannot be told here. The exact date of the Supreme Court's ruling on Al-Ard's printing company is not given; only its result, in early 1962, is. The date of Ben-Gurion's Knesset statement and the identity of the second organization he named are not recorded here. And the evidence on which the Haifa district commissioner and the security services based their view of Al-Ard is reported only in its conclusions, through the movement's sympathetic historians and the court's majority opinion.
