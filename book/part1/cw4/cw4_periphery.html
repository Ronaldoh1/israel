The Periphery and the Trident, 1957–1958

The French reactor answered one of Israel's weaknesses after Suez. Another was diplomatic: in 1957 Israel stood outside every regional alignment. Western regional defence had been organized without it, in the alliance told in [scene:cw2_baghdad_bandung|the Baghdad Pact and Bandung], and the Sinai war had then ended with American and Soviet pressure forcing Israel's withdrawal. The answer that Ben-Gurion's government worked out between early 1957 and the autumn of 1958 was to look past its Arab neighbours to the states beyond them: Turkey, Iran and Ethiopia, and in some versions Sudan. Israeli officials came to call this the periphery. Its most concrete product was a secret intelligence pact with Turkey and Iran, the Trident.

The word. "Periphery" was Israel's own policy language, and it describes the region as seen from Jerusalem: an inner ring of hostile Arab states and an outer ring of non-Arab states that might be friendly. The historian Noa Schonmann, who has worked through the Israeli archival record, notes that Israeli documents used another phrase for it, the "Outer Circle." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] Both terms carry a point of view. Turkey, Iran and Ethiopia were not peripheral to themselves. Each was a state with its own government and its own reasons for dealing with Israel, and, on Alpher's account below, each kept the relationship at the level it chose. This section uses the word because it was the policy's name, not as a description of the countries it named.

The roots, 1957. The first approach in the record came from Ethiopia, not from Israel. According to Schonmann, Emperor Haile Selassie initiated contact in February 1957 through Israel's defence ministry delegation in Paris. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] Ben-Gurion's thinking about Turkey can be followed in his private writing. On 15 September 1957 he wrote to his daughter that the Middle East had only two stable states, Turkey and Israel; on 16 October 1957 he recorded in his diary that Israel and Turkey were the only stable, free and democratic countries in the region. Both are cited by the scholar Alon Liel. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] Schonmann writes that the foundations of the policy were in place by the Syrian crisis of August 1957. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024]

The first meeting with Turkey's leadership took place in Paris. On 15 December 1957 Israel's ambassador to Italy, Eliyahu Sasson, met the Turkish Prime Minister, Adnan Menderes, and proposed linking the Mossad with Turkey's intelligence service. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] The proposal was for intelligence cooperation, not a public alliance, and that shape held for the whole of what followed.

The next meeting showed where the two governments' interests parted. At a follow-up in early April 1958, Menderes proposed a coup in Syria. Ben-Gurion rejected it. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] The Turkish Prime Minister brought to the relationship an aim of his own, the overthrow of the government of a neighbouring state, and the Israeli Prime Minister declined to join it. Whatever the periphery became, it was not, in its first year, an agreement in which one side simply adopted the other's plans.

July 1958: from contacts to policy. The turn came with the revolution in Baghdad on 14 July 1958, told in the next section, which killed Iraq's king and its prime minister. Liel writes that the Baghdad Pact thereby lost its main pillar, and that Turkey sought closer ties with Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] For Ankara, the Arab state on which its regional alliance had rested had fallen to a government it could not count on. For Jerusalem, the same event made an approach that had been tentative look necessary.

Ben-Gurion moved within a week. Schonmann writes that he presented the plan to his government on 20 July 1958, when it was adopted as state policy. Her sources are the Israel State Archives' minutes of the government meetings of 20 and 28 July 1958 and of the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Security Committee of 16 July and 19 August 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] The archival trail matters, because it shows that the periphery was not the private project of one man or one service. It went before the cabinet twice and the Knesset's security committee twice in five weeks.

The letter to Eisenhower, 24 July 1958. Four days after the cabinet decision, Ben-Gurion wrote to President Eisenhower seeking American support for a pact of Turkey, Iran, Ethiopia and Sudan with Israel. The letter's central image survives in two renderings. Schonmann translates it as "a strong dam against the Nasserist–Soviet torrent"; Liel's German version reads, in English, "a strong dam against Nasser's wild Sovietism." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] The two are close in sense and both present Nasser's Egypt and Soviet influence as a single flood. They differ in emphasis: one speaks of a "Nasserist–Soviet" current, the other of Nasser's own "Sovietism." The original Hebrew or English text is not among the sources gathered here, and this section does not choose between the translations. Eisenhower replied on 25 July, promising a fuller answer from Secretary of State Dulles. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017]

What did Ben-Gurion want from Washington? Schonmann's answer is that the plan aimed at "political friendship" with non-Arab states rather than military aid, and at creating "a phantom of a pact" that would persuade Washington of Israel's regional value. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] The phrase is hers, and it is an interpretation, but it fits the shape of the letter: a pact presented to the United States as a barrier against Nasser and Moscow, which would make Israel worth supporting. Isser Harel, the head of the Mossad, put the same idea in other words, describing Israel as possibly "the long arm" of the United States in Asia and Africa. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] Yossi Alpher, a former Israeli intelligence officer and the author of Periphery: Israel's Search for Middle East Allies (2015), summarizes Ben-Gurion's pitch to Washington as "you've got NATO, we have Trident." [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings]

The agreement with Turkey, August 1958. According to Schonmann, a secret, formal political agreement between the Israeli and Turkish prime ministers was signed in August 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] Beyond that, the sources diverge on two points: where it was made, and what it did to the level of relations.

On the first, Liel and the journalist Amotz Asa-El both describe a secret visit by Ben-Gurion to Ankara. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] [cite:rs_cw4_asael_aijac] Schonmann, in the work cited here, does not state where the agreement was signed. The Ankara visit therefore rests on two later accounts, one in Liel's study and one in the Australia/Israel Review, and is not confirmed by the archival scholarship.

On the second, the disagreement is direct. Asa-El says the two leaders agreed to upgrade relations to full embassies. [cite:rs_cw4_asael_aijac] Liel writes that relations reached ambassadorial level only in January 1992. [cite:rs_cw4_liel_bmlv] A Turkish research site, STRASAM, citing Israel's published foreign-policy documents, lists the points agreed: closer political, military, economic and cultural ties, cooperation between the two General Staffs, and the return of envoys to their posts. [cite:rs_cw4_strasam] The two positions can be read together. If the text provided for envoys to return, it restored representation without necessarily raising it, which would square with Liel's 1992 date; Asa-El's "full embassies" may describe an intention rather than an act. That reconciliation is offered here as a possibility, not a finding. The documents cited by STRASAM are the nearest thing to the text in these sources, and they speak of envoys, not ambassadors.

The Turkish side of this story is often summarized by a claim the record does not support: that Turkey has always been hostile to Israel. Turkey and Israel had established diplomatic relations in March 1949, the first Muslim-majority state to do so, a story that belongs to Volume II; and in 1958 Turkey entered a secret intelligence pact with Israel and Iran. [cite:w_isr_tur] The partnership of the 1990s, with its military agreements, lies well beyond this chapter. For the period here, the plain fact is that the Turkish government chose a secret alignment with Israel at the moment its own alliance system broke.

The Trident, autumn 1958. The intelligence pact followed. Schonmann writes that after October 1958 the Trident brought the heads of the Israeli, Turkish and Iranian secret services together in semi-annual conferences, with Ethiopia joining later. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] Alpher dates the first trilateral meeting to late September or early October 1958, and says Israel informed the Americans as soon as it was concluded. [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_relay] Another account traces the alliance's formation through meetings in Europe, Ankara and Tehran between 1956 and 1958, and names its drivers as Israel's 1956 Sinai campaign, concern over Nasser's Egypt, the Iraqi coup of 1958 and shared fears of Soviet influence. [cite:foreignaffairs_trident_legacy] [cite:wiki_alliance_periphery] The Iranian side of the relationship, which had begun with oil and with contacts between the Mossad and Iran's security service, is told in the section on Iran and Israel later in this chapter.

How it worked. The Trident was an arrangement among intelligence services, not among governments in public, and its machinery reflected that. According to Alpher, its "primary purpose" was intelligence on Syria, Iraq and Soviet penetration of the region, and on the Israeli side it was run by the Mossad directly under the prime minister. [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] Alpher says that Turkish scholars told him "no more than 20 people in Turkey" knew of it. [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] Israel's presence in Tehran was formally a trade delegation, and, in his words, Turkey, Iran and Ethiopia all "kept it at low level to keep their Arab friends happy." [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings]

That last phrase, on Alpher's account, is the key to the partners' side of the bargain: each had relations with Arab states that it valued, and kept its tie to Israel low to protect them. The structure that resulted was secret and asymmetric. Alpher's assessment, as a review of his book summarizes it, is that the Trident was lopsided, with Iran and Turkey exploiting Israeli intelligence. [cite:rs_cw4_apn_review] Another account puts the same point from Israel's standpoint: Israel provided substantially more intelligence and expertise than it received, and valued the arrangement chiefly as proof, aimed at the Americans, the Soviets and the Arab states, that it was not regionally isolated. [cite:foreignaffairs_trident_legacy] These are the judgments of a participant and of one later account, and they are labelled as such. They agree with Schonmann's archival reading in one respect: the value of the periphery to Israel lay as much in what it showed Washington as in what it delivered.

The money. The sources say little about who paid, with one exception. Alpher says that CIA money built a Trident headquarters near Tel Aviv, which the Mossad turned "into a training facility very quickly." [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] That is a participant's account and is not confirmed from American records in the sources gathered here. If accurate, it means that Washington, whatever its diplomats said about the pact, paid for part of its infrastructure.

Washington's answer. How the United States received the periphery is disputed, and the two accounts come from different kinds of evidence. Alpher, the participant, says Dulles gave a "lukewarm blessing." [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] Schonmann, working from the American archives, finds something cooler. The State Department's Policy Planning Staff wrote on 11 August 1958 that "the cornerstone of the suggested entente is not firm," and on 18 August Dulles approved "extreme caution in giving Ben-Gurion further encouragement or support." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] Just before them came the overflight crisis of 1–10 August, told in [scene:cw4_overflights|the overflight crisis], after which Israel's own ambassador spoke of the need to remove American "doubts about Israel's steadfastness." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d36]

The strongest form of each case can be stated. On Alpher's side: Israel told Washington of the Trident as soon as it was concluded, the CIA by his account helped pay for its headquarters, and nothing in the record shows the United States trying to stop it. On Schonmann's: the internal American papers of August 1958 show the planners doubting the pact's foundation and the Secretary of State ordering restraint in encouraging it. Both may be true at once. A government can decline to back a policy in its diplomacy while its intelligence service quietly funds the part it finds useful. The sources do not show whether that is what happened, only that the diplomatic record and the participant's memory point in different directions.

A later American telegram, reporting Ben-Gurion's account of a secret talk with Iran's General Teymur Bakhtiar, records the policy in the form Washington understood it: Israeli policy aimed in part to strengthen relations with Turkey, Iran, Ethiopia and Sudan, and possibly later Iraq. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] The telegram's own date is uncertain, a question taken up in the section on Iran, but its list matches Ben-Gurion's letter of 24 July closely.

What it was for. Alpher places the periphery among Ben-Gurion's grand strategies: "periphery doctrine is one, a nuclear deterrent is another, a great power, or super power ally, is a third," the fourth being mass immigration. [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] Set beside the French reactor of the same years, told in the opening section of this chapter, the list explains the policy's logic as its makers saw it. A state surrounded by hostile neighbours and never granted a written guarantee by the United States sought allies beyond the ring, a deterrent of its own, and a great-power patron, and used the first to win the third. That is the strongest Israeli case for the periphery, and it does not depend on any claim about the peoples of the partner states: it was a policy toward their governments, which had their own reasons for accepting it. Alpher's own verdict was mixed. He judged the periphery "on balance ... a strategic success," while calling the partners "fair-weather friends, to a large extent." [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings]

The common account holds that Iran's only relationship with Israel before 1979 was commercial, oil and arms, with no state-level intelligence alliance, and that Turkey was always hostile. The record of 1957–1958 shows both to be wrong. Turkey's Prime Minister signed a secret political agreement with Israel's, and both countries' intelligence chiefs, with Iran's, met twice a year from the autumn of 1958. The Shah's public part in the oil embargo of 1973, while the cooperation continued, and his acknowledgment of the ties in 1975 belong to later volumes and are told in [scene:ira_s00c|the Trident and the embargo].

What the record does not settle. The exact place of the August 1958 agreement is not settled: Ankara rests on Liel and Asa-El and is not stated by Schonmann. Whether that agreement raised relations to embassy level is disputed, and the agreed text as summarized speaks only of envoys returning. Who first conceived the doctrine is not established in these sources. The original wording of the "dam" sentence is known only in two translations. The CIA's funding of the Trident's headquarters rests on Alpher's account alone. And how much the pact actually delivered, beyond the conferences and the proof of Israel's regional value, cannot be measured from the record gathered here; the judgment that it was lopsided comes from the Israeli side and has not been tested against Turkish or Iranian sources.
