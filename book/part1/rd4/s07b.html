The White Paper and the Biltmore Program

In May 1939, with war in Europe imminent, the British government issued a White Paper on Palestine. It capped Jewish immigration at 75,000 people over five years. After that, any further immigration would require Arab consent. [cite:whitepaper1939] In practice the policy would have frozen the Jewish population of Palestine as a permanent minority. Zionist leaders read the document as a direct abandonment of the promise Britain had made in the Balfour Declaration of 1917. [cite:whitepaper1939] The source used here records that reading; Britain's own strategic and administrative reasons for the policy belong to a fuller account of the Mandate and are not set out in it.

The timing gave the cap its weight. The same number of places might have been read very differently in a calm decade. Issued in May 1939, months before a war that would leave Palestine one of the few remaining escape routes for European Jews, it arrived at the moment the need to leave was most urgent.

The story of Israel's founding is often told as the gradual, natural unfolding of the Balfour Declaration, step by step until 1948. The documents show a break. Balfour's 1917 language was deliberately ambiguous: it promised a "national home," not a state, and the phrase had never been legally or diplomatically defined as equivalent to statehood. That ambiguity held for 25 years. It ended in a hotel in New York.

The war made it impossible to convene a regular Zionist Congress. Instead, roughly 600 delegates from 18 countries met at the Biltmore Hotel in New York from May 6 to 11, 1942, in a gathering treated as functionally equivalent to one. They included Chaim Weizmann, president of the World Zionist Organization, and David Ben-Gurion, who led the Jewish Agency's leadership in Palestine. [cite:biltmore_wiki] The pressure behind the meeting was plain: the White Paper had capped immigration just as the Holocaust was accelerating, and reports of the scale of Nazi atrocities had been reaching American Jewish leaders through 1941 and early 1942.

The resolution the conference adopted urged that Palestine be established as a Jewish Commonwealth, integrated into the structure of the postwar democratic world, and that the Jewish Agency, rather than the British Mandatory administration, be given control over immigration. [cite:biltmore_wiki] It was the first time an authoritative Zionist body explicitly demanded a sovereign Jewish commonwealth, encompassing all of Mandatory Palestine. [cite:biltmore_wiki]

The word "commonwealth" can sound modest. The historian Rafael Medoff's research shows it was not meant to be. The delegates did not intend anything less than a sovereign state; they envisioned a status like that of Australia or Canada, fully sovereign in practice. [cite:biltmore_medoff] That is the scholarly reading of the term's intended weight, which does not mean that all 600 delegates understood it identically.

Nor was the outcome unanimous or inevitable. Weizmann and Ben-Gurion, present in the same room, represented two strategies. Weizmann favored continued diplomatic engagement with Britain, the approach that had defined the movement for 25 years. Ben-Gurion, speaking for the Jewish population of Palestine directly, argued that explicit, uncompromising statehood was the only viable path. Biltmore is recorded as the moment Ben-Gurion's approach prevailed. [cite:biltmore_medoff] It does not follow that Weizmann's approach was abandoned altogether or that he opposed the final resolution. The same sources also document real internal opposition to Biltmore's maximalism, both from those who preferred continued diplomacy and from those who wanted an explicitly binational state.

The program soon became more than a conference resolution. The Zionist General Council adopted it as official policy that October, and it remained the movement's operative goal until late 1946. [cite:biltmore_enc] Biltmore marks the point at which the demand for a state became explicit and dominant. For the primary documents of the period, see the White Paper itself. [cite:white_paper_1939]
