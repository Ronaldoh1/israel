The Scores, Area by Area

This section sets out every row of the new scorecard. Each table gives the measure, its goalposts, each country's value and the score that value earns, from 0 at the floor to 10 at the goal; the area score below each table is the plain average of the rows scored for both countries. Green marks the higher score where the two differ by at least a quarter of a point. Rows a country cannot be scored on are shown and left out. The arithmetic can be checked by hand: a value halfway between floor and goal scores 5. [scene:us22_method|How this scorecard grades]

Health

Health is the area with the widest gap and the firmest data, and it is where the first scorecard's verdict holds most clearly. Israel's law covers every citizen and permanent resident; America left 7.9 per cent of its residents uninsured all year in 2025. Israel records longer lives, 83.2 years against 78.9, and half America's infant mortality, and its people live more of those years in good health: the World Health Organization's healthy life expectancy was 70.9 years in Israel and 63.2 in the United States in 2021. [cite:us22_who_hale21] Israel scores 8.4 and the United States 4.0. Spending is reported, not scored, under this scorecard's rules, but it sharpens the result: America spent about 3.3 times as much per person in 2023, $13,473 against $4,033, and 16.7 per cent of GDP against 7.1. The one exclusion matters at the edges: Israel's universal coverage leaves out asylum seekers, undocumented migrants and foreign workers, who are not counted in its uninsured rate. [scene:us16_systems|Part Sixteen: two health systems] [cite:us16_wb_chepc] [cite:us16_wb_chegdp]

Work and wages

This is one of the areas the United States wins, 4.5 to 3.9, though narrowly: the lead rests on the average-wage row, and without it Israel would lead, 3.6 to 3.3. American average pay is far higher in purchasing-power terms and grew faster after 2000, and Americans work about 70 fewer hours a year. Israel's law guarantees twelve days of paid vacation, which American federal law does not, and a larger share of its employees are covered by collective agreements; Israel's coverage figure is the midpoint of an OECD range of 20 to 30 per cent from about 2015, set against an American figure for 2025. A row Part Thirteen describes, Israel's wage floor tied by law to the average wage against a federal floor frozen since 2009, is not scored, because this part could not confirm the two countries' minimum-to-median ratios on one OECD basis before publication; it would narrow the American lead further. [scene:us13_wages|Part Thirteen: minimum wage, hours and pay gaps] [scene:us14_wages|Part Fourteen: wages against prices]

Household debt and prices

American households carry more debt, 69.2 per cent of GDP against 42.3 per cent; American inflation in the year to September 2026 ran further from the 2 per cent both central banks aim for; and an American public university's published tuition is more than three times an Israeli one's. Israel scores 8.1 and the United States 4.3. Two cautions belong here. Israel's prices for food, dairy and cars are among the OECD's highest, which the wage row captures only in part, because purchasing-power figures already adjust for prices. And on net price after grants, about $2,300 at an American public four-year college, the tuition row would reverse in America's favour; no comparable Israeli net figure exists, so published prices are compared. [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the basket] [scene:us17_higher|Part Seventeen: universities]

The first scorecard's debt score, 1.5 for the United States and 4.5 for Israel, leaned in part on the claim that 66.5 per cent of American bankruptcies are medically driven. That figure comes from a 2019 survey in which filers named medical bills or illness-related lost income as a contributing cause. [cite:us22_himmelstein2019] A 2018 study in the New England Journal of Medicine, which followed hospitalised adults through their credit and bankruptcy records, estimated that hospitalisation causes about 4 per cent of bankruptcies among non-elderly adults. [cite:us22_dobkin2018] The two answer different questions, contribution and cause, and neither has an Israeli counterpart, so neither is scored. The household-debt row carries the comparison instead.

Housing

The only housing measure both countries publish in a form that can be scored is homelessness, and on it the American record is far worse: about 218 people per 100,000 on one January night in 2025, against an Israeli figure of about 46 per 100,000 in 2020, counting the 3,470 people local authorities treated and the roughly 800 street dwellers they reported outside treatment. The two are counted differently, so Israel is scored on the highest count available. Israel scores 8.5 and the United States 2.7. This reverses the first scorecard, which scored housing for the United States; that score presumably weighed house prices, which grew less affordable in both countries on measures this volume found cannot be set side by side. Israel's own gap is public housing, sold faster than it was bought between 2014 and 2019. [scene:us15_housing|Part Fifteen: homelessness and public housing] [scene:us14_housing|Part Fourteen: housing prices]

Education

Education is the United States' clearest win, 6.0 to 2.4. American fifteen-year-olds scored 490 in reading in the 2025 PISA round, Israel's 436, the lowest Israel has recorded. Both systems leave a wide gap between advantaged and weaker pupils: Israel's was 121 points in reading in 2018 against an OECD average of 89, and in 2025 the United States had the widest gap between its highest- and lowest-scoring readers of any country tested. America also spends almost twice as much per student across all levels, $19,973 against $10,279; that figure is reported, not scored, because this scorecard grades what spending delivers. The first scorecard gave Israel a narrow lead here; on the 2025 results it cannot. [scene:us17_schools|Part Seventeen: schools]

Family and safety net

Israel guarantees fifteen weeks of paid leave after a birth by national law; American federal law guarantees none. Israel spends 1.89 per cent of GDP on family benefits, more than three times America's 0.60 per cent; this is the one spending row scored, because the benefit is itself the money families receive, and the OECD measure leaves out tax breaks, among them the American Child Tax Credit, so it understates American support. A worker who loses a job on two-thirds of the average wage gets back about 65 per cent of previous net income in the first months in Israel and 55 per cent in the United States, on the OECD's 2025 calculation; for a worker on the average wage the figures are 59 and 38 per cent. Benefits last up to 26 weeks in most American states and from 50 to 175 days in Israel, depending on age and dependants. [cite:us22_oecd_nrr_isr67] [cite:us22_oecd_nrr_usa67] [cite:us22_oecd_nrr_isr100] [cite:us22_oecd_nrr_usa100] [cite:us22_cbpp_uiweeks] [cite:us22_nii_uiperiod] In old age, 22.9 per cent of Americans aged 66 and over were poor on the OECD's measure in 2023, against 15.3 per cent of Israelis in 2022. [cite:us22_oecd_idd_usa66] [cite:us22_oecd_idd_isr66] On poverty across all ages the order reverses, narrowly: on the OECD's common basis of half the median income, 18 per cent of Americans and 19.5 per cent of Israelis were poor in the latest year available. [cite:us22_statista_oecdpov] [cite:us18_nii_pov2023] Israel scores 5.3 on the area and the United States 2.8; like every area score, these average only the rows scored for both countries. Child poverty on the same basis is shown but not scored: Israel's rate, 23.4 per cent, is second-highest in the OECD, but this part could not confirm the American rate on that basis before publication. The final section shows that scoring it at any American value moves the area towards the United States without changing either total's order. [scene:us18_poverty|Part Eighteen: poverty] [scene:us18_family|Part Eighteen: family benefits]

Public safety

Israel's national homicide rate was 3.04 per 100,000 in 2023, the American 4.1 in 2025, America's lowest on record; Israel scores 7.3 and the United States 6.2. The national figure hides Israel's sharpest internal gap: Arab citizens were killed at 11.11 per 100,000 in 2023, about thirteen times the rate among Jewish citizens and above any American national rate in the series. Firearm deaths, 12.8 per 100,000 Americans in 2024, most of them suicides, are shown but not scored, because Israel publishes no comparable series. Imprisonment, a measure of the state's coercion as much as of safety, is scored in the next area. [scene:us5_homicide|Part Five: homicide] [scene:us5_guns|Part Five: guns]

Wellbeing

The World Happiness Report asks people to rate their lives from 0 to 10. Averaged over 2023 to 2025, Israelis rated theirs 7.187, eighth in the world, and Americans 6.816, twenty-third. [cite:us22_whr26_hli] [cite:us22_fox5_whr26] Israel scores 8.0 and the United States 7.0. Israel's rank held through two years of war, at eighth in both the 2025 and 2026 reports; the survey samples Israel's citizens and residents, not the Palestinians it governs. [cite:us22_jvl_whr]

Family budget and security: the American Dream test

This area asks the question the Sophia platform's American Dream module asks of the United States alone: once a family has paid what the system leaves it to pay, how much is left, and how secure is it? That module traced how American households came to carry costs that other rich countries carry in common, from health care and childcare to college, retirement and the car needed to reach work. Here both countries are measured on the same rows. The area's seven scored rows split four to three, and Israel scores 6.0 against America's 3.4, because the rows it wins it wins by the width of a law.

Israeli families pay less out of pocket for health care, about $827 a person in 2023 against $1,469 in America; Israelis pay a larger share of their health costs directly, about a fifth against a ninth, but of a bill less than a third the size. [cite:us16_wb_oop] [cite:us16_wb_chepc] Israeli law guarantees paid sick leave, accruing at a day and a half a month, and requires employers and employees together to pay 18.5 per cent of wages into a pension and severance fund; American federal law requires neither, although 81 per cent of private-sector workers get paid sick days from their employers and 52 per cent take part in an employer retirement plan. [cite:us22_kolzchut_sick] [cite:us22_dol_sick] [cite:us22_bls_ebs26_sick] [cite:us22_boi_dp202508] [cite:us22_bls_ebs26] And a low-wage worker keeps more of each paycheck in Israel: on the OECD's 2025 figures, income tax and social contributions took 19.4 per cent of the labour cost of a single worker on two-thirds of the average wage in Israel and 27.5 per cent in the United States. That measure leaves out taxes on spending, which fall harder on Israelis: Israel's value added tax rose to 18 per cent in January 2025, while American state and local sales taxes averaged 7.53 per cent in 2026 and five states levy none. [cite:us22_oecd_tw26_ch3] [cite:us22_pwc_il_vat] [cite:us22_tf_sales26]

The other three rows go to the United States. Food took 17.9 per cent of Israeli household spending in 2023 against 12.9 per cent in America in 2024, and housing 21.1 against 16.5 per cent on the OECD's common measure; among low-income renters, 54.1 per cent in Israel and 48.3 per cent in America spent more than 40 per cent of their income on rent, though the American figure is measured against gross income. [cite:us22_cbs_hes23_food] [cite:us22_bls_ce24_food] [cite:us22_oecd_hpt_usa] [cite:us22_oecd_hpt_isr] Part of the food gap is arithmetic, since poorer households spend more of their budgets on food, and part is price: Israel's food and drink cost about 52 per cent more than the OECD average. [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the basket]

Several of the costs the American Dream module names most often could not be scored, because only one country publishes them. When a parent stops paying court-ordered child support, Israel's National Insurance Institute pays the award itself, under a law of 1972, and collects from the parent: about 11,564 women a month received such payments in 2023, averaging NIS 2,204. In the United States the state enforces but does not pay: 65 per cent of current support due was collected in 2024, and in 2017 fewer than half of custodial parents, 45.9 per cent, received the full amount. [cite:us22_nii_mezonot23] [cite:us22_ocss_fy24] [cite:us22_census_p60269] American student borrowers owed an average of $40,467 each in 2026, 42.6 million of them; Israel has no national student-loan programme or debt series, and tuition at the Hebrew University is NIS 12,017 a year. [cite:us22_edi_sld] [cite:us22_fsa_q2fy26] [cite:us22_huji_tuition] An American couple with two young children, both on the average wage, spent about a fifth of their disposable income on childcare in 2022, a single parent 37 per cent; Israel subsidises daycare for working parents of children up to three by income, but no OECD figure for its net cost was found. [cite:us22_statista_ncc] [cite:us22_idi_daycare] Americans owed at least $220 billion in medical debt in 2021. [cite:us16_pkff_debt] No recent OECD household saving rate for Israel was found to set beside America's 4.9 per cent, and the transport share of spending cannot be compared, because Israel's survey combines transport with communications.

Two cautions keep this area honest. It measures what the laws and national averages provide, and averages hide who is inside them: an Israeli family in the centre of the country with two salaries lives a different budget from a Haredi or Arab family, just as an American family with employer coverage lives a different one from a family without it. And many of Israel's guarantees, from the pension to the daycare subsidy, are tied to employment, so they reach families in work more fully than those outside it.

Liberty and coercion

This is the area the first scorecard did not have, and the one where whom you count decides the result. For its own citizens, Israel scores 6.2 and the United States 5.8. Israel imprisons about two-fifths as many people per head, though its figure includes Palestinians held as security prisoners, and has executed no one since Eichmann in 1962, against 47 American executions in 2025; America scores higher on Freedom House's measure of political rights and civil liberties, on press freedom and on religious freedom, where Israel has no civil marriage. That lead is too narrow to call. Drop the prison row and the United States leads, 6.7 to 6.0; drop the executions row and it leads 5.8 to 5.4; and the executions row's floor of 3 per 10 million is a choice: set it at 10 and the United States leads the area. [scene:us4_prisons|Part Four: prisons]

For everyone each state governs, the United States scores 6.3 and Israel 5.5. Weighted by population, the Freedom House score of the people under Israel's control falls from 73 to about 53, because the West Bank scores 22 and Gaza 2; about 36 per cent of the people Israel governs cannot vote for its government, against about 1 per cent under American rule; and Israel holds about 29 people per 100,000 governed without criminal charge, against 20 in America's immigration detention at its 2026 peak. Measured over everyone it governs, Israel's prison rate falls to about 127 per 100,000, which scores in its favour. The order holds whichever single row is dropped except the vote row, the largest single difference, without which the two are level. Three choices in this view should be stated. Freedom House's West Bank and Gaza scores also reflect the Palestinian Authority's and Hamas's own rule; counting Gaza as governed by Israel is itself contested, though Part Nine sets out the control Israel exercises there; and the vote row counts Palestinians under Israeli control but not the foreign nationals living in the United States, who can vote in their own countries. The governed population used here is about 15.5 million: Israel's 10.3 million, plus 5.56 million Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, less about 362,000 East Jerusalem Palestinians counted in both. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] [scene:us4_detention|Part Four: detention without trial] [cite:us9_aje_ejvote] [cite:us4_wpb_il]

Economy and public finances

The United States scores 6.0 and Israel 5.6. America produces far more per person, $67,946 in constant 2015 dollars against $42,596; Israel's output per person grew slightly faster over the period, 1.86 per cent a year against 1.75 since 1980, and it carries less public debt, about 68 per cent of GDP against 99 per cent. The two debt figures are defined differently, and the American one, federal debt held by the public, is the narrower; counting all American government debt on Israel's basis would score the United States lower still. The American lead rests on output per person: without that row Israel would lead. Israel's research spending, 6.35 per cent of GDP in 2023 against America's 3.45 per cent, is reported, not scored. [scene:us11_fiscal|Part Eleven: taxes and debt] [scene:us11_growth|Part Eleven: growth] [scene:us12_tech|Part Twelve: technology] [cite:us12_wiki_rd]

Equity inside each system

Each system's internal gaps are of a similar order. Black American infants die at 2.44 times the rate of white infants, Arab Israeli infants at 2.43 times the rate of Jewish ones. The World Economic Forum ranks America 42nd and Israel 76th on its gender-gap index. The United States scores 4.0 and Israel 3.2, a lead that rests on the gender row; without it the two are level. Equity is the area with the fewest shared measures, and the poverty and homicide gaps in the areas above belong here too: Israel's Arab–Jewish homicide ratio of about thirteen to one in 2023 is wider than the American ratio between Black and white victims, about eight to one before the pandemic, while the American wealth gap between white and Black households, about nine to one in 2025, has no Israeli counterpart series.
