Admission to the United Nations and the Question of Jerusalem, 1949–1950

In the year after the war Israel sought two kinds of acceptance: recognition by other states, and membership of the United Nations. It won both within a few months. The terms on which it won the second, and the argument over Jerusalem that followed within the year, show how much of the 1947 plan the new state's admission left formally in place, and how little of it was carried out.

Recognition came from an unexpected direction in March 1949, when Turkey established diplomatic relations with Israel, the first Muslim-majority state to do so. [cite:w_isr_tur] The relationship that grew from it, including a secret intelligence pact with Israel and Iran in 1958 and military-cooperation agreements in 1996, belongs to later volumes and is followed in [scene:tk_s03|Turkey's alliance years]. In 1949 its significance was simpler: a Muslim-majority state had recognized Israel.

Membership was a harder question, because the United Nations had already set out terms for the territory in two resolutions that Israel had not fulfilled. Before voting, the General Assembly's Ad Hoc Political Committee met on 6 May 1949 to take up Israel's attitude to Resolution 181 of November 1947 and Resolution 194 of December 1948. It named three issues: the internationalization of Jerusalem, the repatriation of the refugees, and the investigation of the assassination of Count Bernadotte and Colonel Sérot, the UN mediator and the French officer serving as a UN observer who was killed with him in September 1948. Egypt had opposed inviting Israel's representative to explain. [cite:rs_rd6_ahpc_47]

The committee did not sit alone. Behind it, the Holy See ran a diplomatic campaign at the United Nations to make Israel's admission conditional on two commitments: allowing the return of the Palestinian refugees, and formal guarantees protecting Christian and Muslim holy sites, particularly in Jerusalem. These were specific negotiating positions, pressed through diplomatic channels. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] Ben-Gurion's response is recorded in a sentence often quoted: "There is a major religion in the world, which has to settle with us a historical reckoning." The Vatican's position had a long history. In January 1904 Theodor Herzl had sought Pope Pius X's support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine and been refused, on the ground that the Church could not support the restoration of the Jews to a land central to its own theology while they denied Christ. That refusal held as official Vatican policy for 89 years. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The campaign of 1949 was not silence or indifference at the founding of the state. It was active opposition to unconditional admission.

It did not succeed. On 11 May 1949 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 273 (III), admitting Israel to membership of the United Nations, by 37 votes to 12, with 9 abstentions. [cite:rs_rd6_ecf_273] The resolution's text records the conditions it did attach, which were declaratory. It noted Israel's declaration that it "unreservedly accepts the obligations of the United Nations Charter." It recalled the resolutions of 29 November 1947 and 11 December 1948, the partition resolution and Resolution 194. And it took note of "the declarations and explanations made by the representative of the Government of Israel" before the Ad Hoc Political Committee. [cite:rs_rd6_unga273_text] Israel was admitted without the guarantees the Vatican had sought, a documented diplomatic defeat for the Holy See at that moment. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition]

What those "declarations and explanations" were matters to a later argument. Palestinians and their supporters, who treat Resolution 194 as establishing a right of return, note that Israel's admission came with a pledge to honour it; the work this book draws on names Israel's representative, Abba Eban, as giving an assurance that Israel would honour Resolutions 181 and 194. [cite:wiki_res_194] The committee's summary record consulted for this book documents the agenda of 6 May, not the content of Israel's answers, and the resolution itself only "takes note" of them. [cite:rs_rd6_ahpc_47] [cite:rs_rd6_unga273_text] The wording of any assurance, and how binding it was meant to be, cannot be stated here from the primary record; the competing readings of Resolution 194 are set out in [scene:rr_s01|the section on Resolution 194].

Jerusalem was the issue on which the gap between the resolutions and the ground was widest. Resolution 181 had made Jerusalem and Bethlehem a corpus separatum, a separate entity under UN administration. After the war, according to Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education, Israel held West Jerusalem and Jordan held East Jerusalem, including the Old City, the Temple Mount and the Western Wall, and Jews were denied access to their holy sites there. [cite:rs_rd6_bpb_jerusalem] The armistice with Jordan had put free access to the holy places on the agenda of a special committee. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text]

On 9 December 1949 the General Assembly, in Resolution 303, again called for international status for Jerusalem. The response came in four weeks. Ben-Gurion spoke against the resolution in the Knesset on 13 December. On 26 December the Knesset moved from Tel Aviv to Jerusalem. On 23 January 1950 it declared Jerusalem the capital of Israel by 60 votes to 2; the two votes against were cast by Communists who supported international status. [cite:rs_rd6_bpb_jerusalem] The same source reports that the declaration was not internationally recognized, and that no state opened an embassy in West Jerusalem until 1967. [cite:rs_rd6_bpb_jerusalem]

The sequence can be read two ways. On one reading, Israel joined an organization whose resolutions on refugees and Jerusalem it then declined to carry out, and moved its parliament into the city the same organization had just asked to be placed under international control. On the other, the internationalization of Jerusalem had already been overtaken by a war that left the eastern city, and the holy sites in it, under Jordanian rule with Jewish access denied, and Israel's admission resolution had recorded the UN's resolutions without making membership conditional on carrying them out. Both readings rest on the same dated record.

The Vatican's own reckoning took decades. It maintained non-recognition for 45 years after Israel's founding. The precondition for change was doctrinal: the Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate of 1965 revised Catholic teaching on Judaism, stating that God "holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers." [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The Fundamental Agreement of 30 December 1993, signed in Jerusalem, established diplomatic relations, and ambassadors were exchanged in 1994; property rights, tax exemptions and the status of Jerusalem were left for continuing negotiation. [cite:wiki_fundamental_agreement] After the General Assembly's vote of November 2012, the Vatican recognized the State of Palestine in 2013 and in 2015 signed an accord governing the Church's activity in Palestinian-controlled territory, which drew a formal Israeli protest. [cite:gulfnews_vatican_palestine_accord] That history is told in [scene:vat_s01|the Vatican's long non-recognition].

The record leaves two matters open. The first is the content of Israel's statements to the Ad Hoc Political Committee, which this book has not been able to read in the primary record. The second is the Vatican campaign's reach: the sources used here document the campaign and its aims, but not which governments, if any, it moved among the twelve that voted against admission.
