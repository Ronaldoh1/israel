Part Thirty-Four established that the sun religions of the Americas were dismantled by named actors for different reasons. In Peru, conquistadors took the Coricancha's gold for Atahualpa's ransom in 1533, the Dominicans built their convent on its walls, Viceroy Toledo's government banned the Inca-cult ceremonies in 1572 and seized the Punchao when it took Vilcabamba, and clerical visitors punished the Taki Onqoy movement, whose scale historians dispute. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] [cite:vt35_infobae_inti] [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru] [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki] In Mexico the Spanish razed the Templo Mayor in 1521 and built a church from its stones, Archbishop Montúfar had the Sun Stone buried, and priests, Landa among them, burned the Maya books, of which four survive; yet the colonial cathedral displayed the Sun Stone from 1791 and the Vatican Library has held the Codex Borgia since 1902. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor] [cite:vt35_wiki_cathedral] [cite:vt35_wiki_sunstone] [cite:vt35_wiki_mayacodices] [cite:vt35_wiki_borgia] The Mexica sun cult that ended was also a cult of human sacrifice. [cite:vt35_smith_tzompantli] Elsewhere the actors were Roman emperors, the Portuguese crown in Goa, and, for the Sun Dance, the United States Department of the Interior, whose 1883 ban stood until 1978; in Japan the sun goddess was never suppressed and the Christians were. [cite:vt21_wiki_theodpagan] [cite:vt35_wiki_goainq] [cite:vt35_narf_civ] [cite:vt35_ebsco_japan] Andean, Maya and Totonac communities carried the sun into their Catholic practice, and the claim that Christ is only a sun myth does not survive the sources. [cite:vt35_unesco_qoyllur] [cite:vt35_everyculture_chamula] [cite:vt35_wiki_zeitgeist] Part Thirty-Five turns from the suns to the mothers, beginning on the hill of Tepeyac.
