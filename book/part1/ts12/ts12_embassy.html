Jerusalem, the Golan and the Settlements, 2017–2019

Three decisions of the first Trump administration are the ones most often offered as proof that Christian Zionism had reached the levers of American policy: the recognition of Jerusalem as Israel's capital and the move of the embassy there, the recognition of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights, and the declaration that settlements in the West Bank were not in themselves contrary to international law. This section sets out what was decided, what reasons the officials gave at the time, where Christian Zionist figures appear in the record, and what the record does not show about cause.

The Jerusalem statement

On 6 December 2017 President Donald Trump announced that the United States recognised Jerusalem as the capital of Israel and would move its embassy there. His stated reasons were legal and diplomatic. Congress had passed the Jerusalem Embassy Act in 1995, and "for over 20 years, every previous American president has exercised the law's waiver" in the belief that delay would help peace; after those decades, he said, the parties were "no closer to a lasting peace agreement." He called the decision "nothing more, or less, than a recognition of reality," said the United States was "not taking a position on any final status issues," including boundaries, and asked all parties to "maintain the status quo at Jerusalem's holy sites." The one religious reference in the statement described Jerusalem as "the heart of three great religions." [cite:ts12_toi_trump2017] The statement did not mention evangelicals, prophecy or the Bible.

The promise had already been made before an evangelical audience. Addressing the Christians United for Israel summit in Washington on 17 July 2017, a month after the President had signed the usual waiver keeping the embassy in Tel Aviv, Vice President Mike Pence spoke of "the hand of heaven" in Israel's history and said of the move: "It is not a question of 'if.' It is only 'when.'" [cite:ts12_ncr_pence2017]

The opening, 14 May 2018

The embassy opened in Jerusalem on 14 May 2018, timed to the seventieth anniversary of Israel's founding, with Ivanka Trump and Jared Kushner in the American delegation. [cite:ts12_pbs_gaza] Two Texas pastors bracketed the ceremony. Robert Jeffress, the Southern Baptist pastor of First Baptist Dallas, gave the opening prayer; John Hagee, founder of Christians United for Israel, gave the closing benediction. [cite:ts12_cnn_pastors] Jeffress thanked God "that you have given us a president who boldly stands on the right side of history." [cite:ts12_npr_religion] This corrects two errors in earlier material: the opening was in 2018, not 2017, and Jeffress gave a public prayer at the ceremony, not a private service.

The choice of speakers drew objections at the time. Mitt Romney wrote that Jeffress, who had called Mormonism a "cult" and Islam an "evil religion," was "a bigot" who should not be "giving the prayer" at the opening; Hagee's 1990s sermon describing Hitler as "a hunter" in a divine plan had led John McCain to reject his endorsement in 2008. [cite:ts12_cnn_pastors] A Republican congressman, Francis Rooney, said he would have chosen a more ecumenical group. [cite:ts12_npr_religion] Each pastor gave his own answer. Jeffress said that the Christian teaching that "salvation is through faith in Christ alone" was "neither bigoted nor newsworthy"; Hagee called the claim that he condoned the Holocaust "the biggest and ugliest of lies," and his spokesman said the sermon drew on the writings of Rabbi Yissachar Shlomo Teichtal. The White House said Jeffress had "a strong relationship with many people in the faith community" and its spokesman said he did not know who had selected him. [cite:ts12_cnn_pastors]

The same day, Palestinians protested at the Gaza border fence. The Gaza Health Ministry said at least 41 people, five of them minors, had been killed by mid-afternoon and at least 772 wounded; the Israeli military said its troops had come under fire and that protesters were trying to break through the fence. [cite:ts12_pbs_gaza] The two scenes, a prayer of thanksgiving in Jerusalem and the deaths at Gaza, were reported side by side and became, for critics, the defining image of the day.

Why the embassy moved: drivers set side by side

Earlier material in the library states that the move was made "in direct response" to evangelical pressure. That is a causal claim, and the record supports it only in part. The strongest evidence for it is Trump's own later remark, at a rally in Oshkosh, Wisconsin, on 17 August 2020: "That's for the evangelicals," adding that evangelicals were "more excited about that than Jewish people." [cite:ts9_almon_trump2020] That is a statement of his own account, made at a campaign rally three years later, and it is set out with the other drivers in Part Nine. [scene:ts9_us|Part Nine: what American actors gained] Against it stand his statement of the day, which grounded the decision in the 1995 Act and the failure of the waivers [cite:ts12_toi_trump2017]; a campaign promise his predecessors had also made and not kept; and the political giving of Sheldon and Miriam Adelson, who also gave CUFI $4.6 million from 2012, according to their foundation's filings. [cite:ts9_972_adelson] No document examined here allows the weight of each driver to be measured, and this book records the cause as contested.

The Golan and the settlements

On 25 March 2019, by Proclamation 9852, the United States recognised "that the Golan Heights are part of the State of Israel." The proclamation's reasons were security: Israel "took control of the Golan Heights in 1967 to safeguard its security from external threats," and faced "aggressive acts by Iran and terrorist groups, including Hizballah, in southern Syria." [cite:gi8_ucsb_procl9852] Syria called it a "blatant attack" on its sovereignty, the European Union and the Arab League rejected it, and the UN Secretary-General said the Golan's status had not changed; the recognition continued under President Biden. [cite:gi8_wiki_usrecog] [scene:gi8_golan|The Golan in the Greater Israel volume] A claim in earlier material that Trump framed the Golan as "fulfilling biblical prophecy" has no quotation behind it and is not carried here; the religious comparison of that week came from Secretary of State Pompeo, in an interview about Queen Esther discussed in the next section. [scene:ts12_officials|The officials and their stated reasons]

On 18 November 2019 Pompeo announced that the United States no longer regarded civilian settlements in the West Bank as "per se" inconsistent with international law, setting aside the State Department legal opinion of 1978. His stated reasons were practical and legal: President Reagan had "disagreed" with the 1978 conclusion; "calling the establishment of civilian settlements inconsistent with international law hasn't worked. It hasn't advanced the cause of peace"; Israeli courts reviewed settlement activity; and there "will never be a judicial resolution to the conflict." He said the United States expressed "no view on the legal status of any individual settlement" and was not "prejudging the ultimate status of the West Bank." [cite:ts12_toi_pompeo2019] [cite:wb_jvl_hansell] Secretary of State Antony Blinken reversed the position on 23 February 2024, calling settlements "inconsistent with international law." [cite:wb_axios_blinken] In his second term Trump said on 25 September 2025, "I will not allow Israel to annex the West Bank," while not moving to curb settlement. [cite:gi6_ap_trump0925] [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] The record examined here shows no formal restoration of the 2019 position by October 2026.

Abroad: the governments that followed, and one that did not

Guatemala opened its embassy in Jerusalem on 16 May 2018, two days after the United States, under President Jimmy Morales, whom NPR described as an evangelical; Morales called it "a step forward in favour of union and friendship." [cite:ts7_npr_guatemala] Analysts tied the move to Guatemala's arms relationship with Israel in the 1980s, to its dependence on US aid and to the president's evangelical base together. [cite:pm_pitt_guatemala] Paraguay moved its embassy in 2018 under Horacio Cartes; his successor Mario Abdo Benítez reversed it months later, and President Santiago Peña reopened it on 12 December 2024. [cite:ts7_abc_paraguay] Honduras dedicated its embassy in Jerusalem on 24 June 2021 under President Juan Orlando Hernández. The reasons reported were diplomatic and personal, not religious: Hernández had attended an Israeli leadership course in 1991 and kept close contact with Netanyahu, who asked him to follow the United States and Guatemala, and Israel agreed in return to reopen its embassy in Honduras. [cite:ts12_ih_honduras] In August 2022 the foreign minister of Hernández's successor, Xiomara Castro, said a return to Tel Aviv "has already been discussed with President (Castro)," citing international law and "a balanced relationship with the other Arab countries and Israel." [cite:ts12_na_honduras] The embassy was still listed in Jerusalem in December 2024. [cite:ts7_abc_paraguay]

Brazil was the counter-case. President Jair Bolsonaro, with a large evangelical constituency, suggested in January 2019 that Brazil would move its embassy; after warnings about some $5 billion in halal exports to Arab markets, Brazil opened a trade office in Jerusalem on 15 December 2019 instead, and the embassy stayed in Tel Aviv. [cite:ts7_ynet_brazil] [cite:ts7_pti_brazil] Part Seven sets out the church figures for all four countries. [scene:ts7_abroad|Part Seven: Latin America and Africa]

The documented finding is specific. Christian Zionist leaders were given a public place at the opening of the embassy, a vice president promised the move at their summit, and the President later called it a gift "for the evangelicals." The official texts of all three decisions gave legal, diplomatic and security reasons, not religious ones, and the record does not establish how much each driver weighed. Abroad, the governments that moved acted for mixed reasons, and the one with the largest evangelical population did not move at all.
