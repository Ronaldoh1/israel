The Cathars and the Crusade

The name Cathar comes from the Greek katharos, "pure". [cite:hr_brit_cathari] The Cathars held that there were two principles, one good and one evil, and that the material world was evil. Their faithful were divided into the "perfect", who had received an initiation ceremony called the consolamentum and lived under strict ascetic rules, and the "believers". [cite:hr_brit_cathari] From the 1140s they formed an organised church with a hierarchy, a liturgy and a system of doctrine, and by about 1200 they had eleven bishoprics. They "severely criticized the worldliness and corruption" of the Catholic Church. [cite:hr_brit_cathari]

That criticism found ready hearers. The nineteenth-century historian Henry Charles Lea, writing in 1887, argued that the Church of the time, "intent upon the acquisition of temporal power", had "well-nigh abandoned its spiritual duties", and that in Languedoc the clergy were so despised that priests hid their tonsures for shame. [cite:hr_lea_ch3] Lea also pointed to money: the Cathars rejected tithes and offerings, the payments that made salvation profitable for the Church. [cite:hr_lea_ch3] Lea wrote long ago and from a critical position, but the way the war was paid for and settled, below, gives his reading of its economic side real weight.

The war began with a killing. Raymond VI, count of Toulouse, tolerated the Cathars, and after he was implicated in the murder of a papal legate, Pope Innocent III in March 1208 called for "a Crusade against Raymond and the heretics of Languedoc". [cite:hr_brit_crusade] Those who fought earned an indulgence, "a remission from punishment in the afterlife for sin", without serving more than forty days. The war was very popular in northern France. [cite:hr_brit_crusade]

In 1209 the crusaders took the city of Béziers and massacred nearly all its people. [cite:hr_brit_crusade] A famous sentence is attached to it. The Cistercian writer Caesarius of Heisterbach reported that the abbot Arnaud Amalric, the papal legate, was asked how to tell Cathars from Catholics and answered, in Latin, "Kill them all, God will know his own". Caesarius did not hear it himself; he wrote that Amalric "is reported to have said" it, and the historian Malcolm Barber notes that the phrase is "usually (although not invariably) discounted by serious historians". [cite:hr_wiki_amalric] What Amalric did write, in a letter to the pope in August 1209, was that his men spared no one "irrespective of rank, sex, or age" and killed almost twenty thousand people, a figure historians think exaggerated, since Béziers had perhaps ten to fourteen and a half thousand inhabitants. [cite:hr_wiki_amalric]

The land changed hands as the war went on. Simon de Montfort, a northern baron, took command and had captured most of the region by 1215, when the Fourth Lateran Council gave him the conquered lands; he was killed at a siege of Toulouse. [cite:hr_brit_crusade] Raymond VI and his son Raymond VII won much of it back, until King Louis VIII intervened and restored northern control in 1226. In 1229 the younger Raymond accepted a peace under which his lands would pass to the Capetian royal house at his death, "making the French crown the ultimate victor". [cite:hr_brit_crusade]

The money trail is short and clear. The papacy paid the soldiers in spiritual currency; the barons were paid in the lands of the condemned; and the final beneficiary was a king whose realm reached the Mediterranean. The heretics were the occasion; the transfer of a rich and independent region to the northern crown was the outcome. [cite:hr_brit_crusade]
