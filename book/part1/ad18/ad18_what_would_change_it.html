What Would Change It: The Levers, the Reading and the Verdict

If the gaps in the ledger have dates and designers, they can in principle be redesigned. This section sets out the levers for which the record holds evidence of effect: policies that have been tried in the United States, measured by named researchers, and in several cases reversed. Each is given with the strongest evidence for it and the strongest evidence against it, and with its status as of October 2026. None is offered as a programme; the choice among them depends on what a reader values and what a reader is willing to pay, which no ledger can settle. The section then gives the Sophia platform's reading of the Dream as a whole, labelled as interpretation, with the readings that answer it, and closes with the volume's answer to its own question.

Cash for children: the 2021 credit and its expiry

The clearest natural experiment in the volume ran for one year. In 2021 Congress raised the child tax credit from $2,000 to $3,000 per child, $3,600 under age six, and paid much of it in monthly payments from July. [cite:ad18_cbs_ctc] Child poverty on the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts tax credits and benefits, fell to 5.2 per cent in 2021; when the credit returned to its old form, it rose to 12.4 per cent in 2022. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] The dispute is over work. A study by Elizabeth Ananat and colleagues, using Census survey data from April to September 2021, found "very small, inconsistently signed, and statistically insignificant" effects on parents' employment. [cite:ad18_nber_ananat] Kevin Corinth, Bruce Meyer and colleagues projected that a permanent version would lead about 1.5 million working parents, 2.6 per cent, to leave work, and that child poverty would then fall by at most 22 per cent rather than the 34 per cent a static count suggests. [cite:ad18_nber_corinth] The first is a measurement of six months; the second is a projection from estimated responses to work incentives. Neither settles what a permanent credit would do over a decade. As of October 2026 the credit is $2,200 per child, $1,700 of it refundable, and since the 2025 budget law it requires a Social Security number for the filer or a spouse. [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] [cite:ad13_usccb_obbba]

Saving by default

The retirement record holds one lever whose effect is not much disputed. In plans that enrol workers automatically, 94 per cent participated in 2025, against 64 per cent in plans where workers must sign up. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] The 2022 retirement law, SECURE 2.0, requires most new 401(k) and 403(b) plans set up after 29 December 2022 to enrol workers automatically from 2025, at a default of 3 to 10 per cent of pay rising each year, with exemptions for employers of ten or fewer and new businesses. [cite:ad10_asppa_auto] The limits are also in the data. Automatic enrolment raises participation only where a plan exists, and 14 per cent of private workers now have a pension available and 70 per cent a 401(k)-type plan; participation among workers earning under $15,000 was 43 per cent; and 6 per cent of participants took hardship withdrawals in 2025. [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] The median balance, $44,115, shows how far a default leaves a typical saver from the security a pension once promised. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26]

The wage floor

No lever in the volume has a longer or more divided literature. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in February 2021 that raising the federal minimum to $15 by 2025 would lift 900,000 people out of poverty and raise affected workers' pay by a net $333 billion, while about 1.4 million fewer workers would be employed. [cite:ad18_bt_cbo2021] A study of 138 state increases from 1979 to 2016 by Doruk Cengiz, Arindrajit Dube and colleagues found that the number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years after each increase. [cite:ad18_nber_cengiz] A review by David Neumark and Peter Shirley found that 79.2 per cent of the preferred estimates in the American literature were negative, with the evidence strongest for teenagers, young adults and less-educated workers. [cite:ad18_nber_neumark] The two bodies of work differ in method, which workers they count and how far above the old floor the new one sits; both are published in leading journals. The states have meanwhile acted on their own: 30 states and the District of Columbia set a higher floor, and only 1.0 per cent of hourly workers were paid at or below $7.25 in 2024. [cite:us13_bp_states2026] [cite:ad5_bls_mw2024]

Medicaid expansion

Under the 2010 health law states may extend Medicaid to low-income adults, and the states that did so form a comparison with those that did not. In 2025, 9.0 per cent of working-age adults in expansion states were uninsured against 18.1 per cent in the others. [cite:ad18_nchs_unins25] Sarah Miller, Norman Johnson, Laura Wherry and colleagues, linking a large Census survey to death records, found that expansion cut annual mortality among low-income adults aged 55 to 64 by about 9.4 per cent, and estimated that about 15,600 deaths would have been avoided had every state expanded. [cite:ad18_ucd_wherry] The strongest evidence on the other side comes from the one randomised trial. In Oregon's 2008 lottery for Medicaid, coverage produced no measurable change in blood pressure, cholesterol or blood sugar after about two years; it cut depression by 30 per cent, nearly eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket spending and cut borrowing or skipped bills to pay for care by more than half. [cite:ad18_ohp_nejm] A critic of the mortality study has argued that its effect barely differs between people who were uninsured beforehand and the whole sample, so the channel through which expansion saves lives is unclear. [cite:ad18_ohp_bjoerkheim] The financial protection is the best-documented effect. As of October 2026 the 2025 budget law adds work requirements for expansion adults from 1 January 2027, and the Congressional Budget Office expects about 10 million more uninsured by 2034. [cite:us16_kff_obbba]

Building more homes

Minneapolis ended citywide parking minimums, allowed taller apartment buildings along transit and commercial corridors, and allowed two- and three-unit buildings on every residential lot. Between 2017 and 2022 its housing stock grew 12 per cent against 4 per cent in the rest of Minnesota, and its rents rose 1 per cent against 14 per cent; 87 per cent of new units were in buildings of 20 or more apartments, and the duplex and triplex change had a modest effect. [cite:ad18_pew_mpls] The caveats are documented too. Researchers cited in 2026 attributed part of the slow rent growth to weaker demand after the unrest of 2020 and to low interest rates that fed construction, and reported that the period of slow rent growth had ended. [cite:ad18_axios_mpls] The lever acts on the price-to-income gap in the ledger; it does not by itself close the ownership gap between Black and white households, which predates the price rise. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:ad6_stateline_gap]

Debt relief and the bankruptcy door

The volume's clearest example of a door closed by law is student debt in bankruptcy, made harder to discharge in steps from 1976 to the private-loan change of 2005. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] A process introduced in November 2022 by the Justice and Education Departments shows what a change in practice can do: in 98 per cent of the cases courts had decided by March 2024, borrowers won full or partial discharge. [cite:ad7_doj_2024] Its limit is scale: 1,220 cases were filed in sixteen months, against 9.3 million borrowers in default in June 2026. [cite:ad7_doj_2024] [cite:us17_ci_defaults] For medical debt, the federal rule to keep it off credit reports was vacated in July 2025; fifteen states and the District of Columbia have their own limits, and the consumer bureau's position as of October 2026 is that federal law preempts them, a question it says is for the courts. [cite:ad8_nclc_states]

An account at birth

Connecticut's baby bonds programme invests $3,200 for each child born on or after 1 July 2023 whose birth was covered by the state's Medicaid programme. The money can be claimed between ages 18 and 30 to buy a home or start a business in the state, pay for education or training, or save for retirement, after a financial-literacy course; the state projects it could grow to $11,000 to $24,000. [cite:ad18_ct_babybonds] The case for the idea rests on simulation: one study found a universal national programme would cut the ratio of young white to young Black median wealth from about 15 to 1 to about 1.6 to 1. [cite:ad18_zewde] The case against its larger claims is arithmetic: the median gap is small enough for a modest programme to close, but the gap in average wealth, driven by the top, runs to trillions, and a programme of about $80 billion a year would not come near it. [cite:ad18_ppp_bruenig] No child in the Connecticut programme can claim before 2041, so there is no evidence of effect yet.

Read across, the levers share two features. Those with the firmest evidence act on a specific number: a default that lifts participation, coverage that ends catastrophic bills, a credit that moves a poverty rate. And the record of both parties runs through them in both directions. A Democratic Congress expanded the child credit in 2021 and let it lapse; a Republican Congress and president raised the permanent credit in 2025 and tied it to a Social Security number. The 2010 health law expanded coverage and the 2025 budget law is projected to reduce it. Federal limits on overdraft and late fees were written in 2024 and undone in 2025, while the 2022 retirement law made saving by default the rule for new plans. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] [cite:us16_kff_obbba] [cite:ad9_plaw_119_10] [cite:ad10_asppa_auto] Even well-studied levers can disappoint their advocates: a 2024 review of some 500 estimates found that, apart from the 1993 federal reform, expansions of the earned income tax credit show no clear employment effect, against a long-held consensus. [cite:ad11_kleven_eitc]

Interpretation, not finding: the Dream as the story that keeps an extraction system running

The Sophia platform reads the evidence of this volume as a single design. In this reading, the American Dream works as an illusion: a story taught in schools and repeated by presidents of both parties that keeps people working inside a system that extracts from them. The story is told as income and effort, so that a household measures itself by its paycheck and blames itself when the paycheck falls short, while the returns that build security flow through channels the story does not name: shares bought back with profits, homes priced beyond a year's wage, credit priced at a fifth of the balance a year, a degree financed by a debt that bankruptcy cannot reach, health coverage that ties a worker to a job. Read through this lens, each designed friction in the ledger is a turnstile, each fee a toll, and the belief that hard work suffices is the mechanism that keeps people walking through. The fall in absolute mobility, the parting of pay and productivity, and the record share of wealth at the top are, in this reading, the system working as built.

The strongest counter-readings are documented in the same ledger. First, a design implies a designer, and the record shows many, at different dates, with opposed aims: the Congress that created Social Security and the one that wrote the 401(k), the Congress that expanded the child credit and the one that let it lapse; levers pulled by each party were reversed by the other. A system that cut old-age poverty from 35.2 to 9.9 per cent and halved the uninsured rate is hard to read as built only to extract. [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:us16_census_2025] Second, belief has tracked the record rather than masking it: satisfaction with the chance to get ahead fell from 77 to 52 per cent, only about a third say the Dream still holds, and the claim that the disadvantaged defend the system most did not survive a large test. [cite:ad16_gallup_opp24] [cite:ad16_brookings_opp26] [cite:ad1_apnorc26] [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt] Third, the levels moved up: real median household income was the highest on record in 2025, and relative mobility, the odds of climbing, held steady while the rungs moved apart. [cite:us14_census_story2025] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] Fourth, the fall in absolute mobility happened in every rich country where it has been measured, which points to forces wider than an American story, even if the American fall was among the largest. [cite:ad3_berman] Fifth, some extraction channels shrank without a new law, as overdraft revenue halved through banks' own changes. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] The reading and its answers both rest on the same documents; what divides them is whether the pattern is read as intended.

The answer in three registers

The volume asked whether the American Dream is an illusion, a mechanism or a promise. The record answers in three registers, and keeps them apart.

Documented: the Dream is a promise that was kept broadly for the generation born around 1940 and has been kept unevenly since. About half of children born in the early 1980s out-earned their parents; the odds of climbing from the bottom stayed near 8 or 9 in 100 and were a quarter as high for Black children as for white; real incomes and coverage rose while the cost of a home, a degree and health care rose faster than wages; Social Security kept its promise and the private pension did not survive as the norm. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] Each gap runs through a mechanism with a date and a designer, most of them enacted in public by Congresses of both parties, a smaller number of them captured departures that were settled or struck down. On this book's disclosed scorecard the promises are about half kept, 4.7 out of 10, an editorial synthesis rather than a measured fact.

Probable: the measured gaps owe something to measurement and something to policy. The pay–productivity gap is partly a matter of price indexes and partly a real fall in labour's share; the decline in union power, a wage floor left unchanged since 2009 and the rules on arbitration and bankruptcy probably shifted bargaining toward employers and lenders, though by how much is disputed. [cite:ad4_bls_btn2017] [cite:ad4_qje_farber] [cite:ad15_epi_colvin] The levers with the firmest evidence, cash for children, saving by default, coverage that ends catastrophic bills, would probably move the specific numbers they were measured to move if they were restored or extended, at costs a reader must weigh. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] [cite:ad18_ohp_nejm]

Not supported: that the Dream was never real; that the whole system was designed as one plan to extract from those who believe in it; that homelessness is maintained on purpose; that medical bills are settled as the leading cause of bankruptcy; and, at the other pole, that those who stay at the bottom have only themselves to blame, a claim Lincoln made in 1859 and the record of who climbs and who does not cannot sustain. [cite:ad14_ucp_colburn] [cite:us22_dobkin2018] [cite:ad1_lincoln_1859] [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] The Dream is neither an illusion nor a guarantee. It is a promise carried by machinery, and the machinery, unlike the promise, can be read, measured and changed.
