Jerusalem Under the Sword: Allenby, Martial Law and Storrs's City, 1917–1926

The Balfour Declaration was a month old when British forces reached Jerusalem. The surrender of the city, on 9 December 1917, was a confused affair. According to an account published by the National Library of Israel, the mayor, Hussein al-Husayni, set out with a letter of surrender and tried to hand it first to two British cooks and then to two scout sergeants before a Colonel Watson accepted it. [cite:rs_al_nli] Two days later General Allenby arrived. He dismounted at the Jaffa Gate and entered the city on foot. The mayor, citing pneumonia, missed the official ceremony, and he died a few weeks later. [cite:rs_al_nli]

The legal meaning of that walk was set out in a proclamation dated 11 December 1917. It was addressed "To the Inhabitants of Jerusalem the Blessed and the People Dwelling in Its Vicinity," and it placed the city under martial law. [cite:rs_al_proclamation] It promised that "every person pursue his lawful business without fear of interruption," and that the holy places would be protected "according to the existing customs and beliefs of those to whose faith they are sacred." [cite:rs_al_proclamation] According to the Times of Israel's centenary account, the proclamation was read at the citadel inside the Jaffa Gate and issued in seven languages. [cite:rs_al_toi]

Read closely, the proclamation defines what the occupation was before any mandate existed. In the passages that the sources used here quote, its instrument is martial law, and its promises are two: that ordinary lawful life would go on, and that the holy places would be kept under the customs already governing them. Those passages say nothing of a national home or of any future political order. [cite:rs_al_proclamation] Authority in Jerusalem now rested with the British military. The international instrument that would later govern Palestine, the Mandate, was not approved until 1922.

The same event was received in sharply different ways, and it still is. According to the Times of Israel, Jewish residents, coming a month after the Balfour Declaration, saw the conquest as a "Hanukkah miracle," and parts of the Christian world hailed it as a "Christmas gift." [cite:rs_al_toi] For them it meant deliverance from Ottoman rule. A very different reading appears in Palestinian-perspective writing. In an opinion essay for Al Jazeera in 2014, Hatem Bazian argued that 11 December 1917 made Jerusalem occupied territory and began a colonial project that enabled Zionism. [cite:rs_al_aljazeera] Both readings rest on real features of the record. The conquest did end Ottoman rule in the city, and contemporaries did celebrate it. The proclamation did also impose martial law, and it arrived a month after a declaration about the country's future on which Palestine's population had never been consulted. Which of these is the truer description depends on whose experience a historian takes as the measure. The documents support both facts.

The governor. The man who ran Jerusalem through these years was Ronald Storrs. He was Military Governor of Jerusalem from 1917 to 1920 and then, when military rule gave way to a civil administration, Civil Governor of Jerusalem and Judea from 1920 to 1926. He went on to govern Cyprus from 1926 and Northern Rhodesia in 1933–34. His papers are held at Pembroke College, Cambridge. [cite:rs_al_openjerusalem]

Storrs's most lasting institution was the Pro-Jerusalem Society. Its own records, published in 1921 by C. R. Ashbee, state that it was "Founded September 1918. Incorporated October 1920." [cite:rs_al_ashbee1921] Its council included the Mayor, the Director of Antiquities and the Grand Mufti, with Storrs as President and Herbert Samuel as Honorary President. [cite:rs_al_ashbee1921] On paper, then, the society gathered the municipal, archaeological and Muslim religious authorities of the city under the governor's chairmanship.

The governor's own powers reached further. A public notice issued under Storrs required a written permit from the Military Governor before anyone could build, alter or repair anything within 2,500 metres of the Damascus Gate, and it forbade "the use of stucco and corrugated iron within the ancient city walls." [cite:rs_al_ashbee1921] In practice this made the look of the Old City and its surroundings a matter of military licence. The society also took on restoration work. It reopened the original tile kilns in the Haram al-Sharif area to make tiles for repairs to the Dome of the Rock, and it contracted the Armenian potter David Ohanessian for the work. [cite:rs_al_ashbee1921] According to the historian Mahon Murphy, it restored the Citadel using refugee labour and removed the clock tower that had stood over the Jaffa Gate. [cite:rs_al_murphy]

Here, too, the record supports two readings. One treats Storrs as a preservationist steward of a multi-faith city. An Israeli design curator, interviewed by The Media Line in 2018, credited him in those terms and praised his approach to preservation. [cite:rs_al_medialine] The other reading comes from historians. Murphy argues that the society's multi-faith council was in practice subordinate to Storrs's own biblical vision of the city. [cite:rs_al_murphy] A 2022 doctoral thesis at the University of Exeter, by C. Burnham, argues that Storrs governed Jerusalem in a deeply personal and Orientalist way, while being constrained by his superiors, by Palestinians and by the Zionist movement. [cite:rs_al_burnham] The two readings are not wholly opposed. The kilns, the tiles and the building rules are documented either way. What divides them is whether a council that included the Mufti and the Mayor but met under a military governor's presidency, with his permit regime behind it, represented the city or decorated his rule. Burnham's emphasis on the limits placed on Storrs, by his superiors, by Palestinians and by the Zionist movement, is a reminder that even a governor this personal did not hold the city alone.

What the occupation meant in practice, then, before any mandate existed, can be stated from these documents. Jerusalem was governed by a British officer under martial law. Its Old City was administered through permits issued by that officer. Its holy places had been promised protection on the terms of existing custom. Its leading Muslim, municipal and antiquities officials were drawn into a society that the governor chaired. None of the documents used here gives the Balfour Declaration any administrative form in these years. The national home entered law only later, through the instruments described in [scene:blg_s01|the mandate system] and [scene:s07|the Mandate text of 1922].

Much remains unclear. The surrender story rests on an account the National Library of Israel published in 2019, drawing on the work of Dov Gavish, and the details of the cooks and the sergeants are reported, not documented here from a primary source. The sources used here do not describe the structure of the military administration above Storrs, how far Jerusalem's own inhabitants were consulted about the building rules, or what the Mufti and the Mayor did on the society's council. Those gaps are why the dispute over Storrs's legacy remains open.
