From Inner Map to Land Deed

This section brings the two halves of the volume together. Parts One to Twelve documented how one Protestant system of reading prophecy became a political shield for a state's policies. The previous sections of this part documented an older way of reading the same name, as a description of what happens inside a person. The question here is how those two ways of reading relate. The section has two layers, kept apart. The first sets out, from the documents, how the literal reading and the inward reading each handle the name and the promise. The second, headed as an argument, gives the platform's reading of that contrast, with the strongest objections to it beside it.

Documented: the literal reading

Dispensationalism, the system that Darby built and Scofield's notes carried to millions of readers, is defined by its own exponents through one distinction. Keith Mathison of Ligonier Ministries, writing from the covenant-theology tradition that rejects it, summarises it: "at its heart is the distinction between Israel and the church". [cite:ts1_lig_dispens] The historian Timothy Weber describes the result as two plans running in history, one for an earthly people, Israel, and one for a heavenly people, the church. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] In that scheme, the promises made to Israel are not read as allegories of the soul. Scofield's 1917 note on Genesis 12 calls the covenant with Abraham "wholly gracious and unconditional"; his note on Deuteronomy 30 sets out a "Palestinian Covenant" whose stages run from dispersion through repentance to "restoration to the land"; and his note on Romans 11 rules out the spiritualising move directly: "That the Christian now inherits the distinctive Jewish promises is not taught in Scripture." [cite:ts1_sac_gen12] [cite:ts5_sac_deut30] [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] Parts Three and Five trace this system at length ([scene:ts3_darby|Darby], [scene:ts5_notes|Scofield's notes]).

The same reading appears in the statements of the movement's largest organisation. In a CUFI webcast on 6 January 2011 John Hagee said that Jerusalem "is the property of the people of Israel" and that God "made a covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob"; Jacob, the man of Genesis 32, appears here as a party to a title. [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] Hagee's stated policy is narrower than the theology: Israelis "and they alone" decide on land and peace, and CUFI asks Washington not to press Israel for concessions. [cite:gi11_jpost_hagee] Among American evangelicals, the LifeWay survey of 2,002 people with evangelical beliefs, fielded 20–28 September 2017, found that 63 per cent supported Israel because "God gave the land of Israel to the Jewish people". [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] Not every Christian Zionist reasons from the end-times scheme: the scholar Stephen Spector found that "most Christian Zionists don't actually base their support for Israel on a catastrophic end-times scenario", and that Genesis 12:3 is the reason most often given. [cite:ts6_tablet_spector] But in the literal reading, wherever it is held, the promise is a promise about a territory and the name is the name of a people and a state.

Documented: the inward reading

The inward reading handles the same materials differently. For Philo, Israel is "the man who sees God", a kind of soul as well as a nation. [cite:ts13_philo_mut] [cite:ts13_alesso_philo] For Origen, scripture has a body, a soul and a spirit, and there is an Israel "according to the Spirit" beside Israel "after the flesh", with Jerusalem read as the heavenly city. [cite:ts13_origen_dp] In Kabbalah Jacob is Tiferet, the balance between expansive kindness and restraint. [cite:ts13_chabad_luria] In modern rabbinic teaching, the night at the Jabbok is the night a man stopped trying to be his brother. [cite:ts13_sacks_be] Both religions also built the levels into their method: Judaism's fourfold PaRDeS and the medieval Christian standard of literal and allegorical senses, described in [scene:s00g|the opening chapter], keep the plain sense and add the inner ones above it.

The record shows two things about this inward reading that matter for what follows. It is not the property of one religion: it appears in a Jewish philosopher, a Christian theologian, Jewish mystics and a modern rabbi. And it does not, in most of its forms, deny the plain sense. Philo's Israel remains a people, and so does Sacks's. The one documented form in which the inward reading has been used against the people who bear the name is the Christian move, possible from Origen's method, that treats bodily Israel as a mere "type" replaced by the church. Christian Zionist organisations attack that move by name: the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem warned in 2012 against "Cloaked versions of Replacement theology". [cite:ts13_origen_dp] [cite:ts11_icej_catc]

Argument: the platform's reading of the contrast

What follows is argument, labelled as such. Read through the IS-RA-EL lens, the history this volume has documented is the history of a map being read as a deed. In the inner traditions, the name describes a state a person can reach, the integration of opposed forces under the divine, completed after a night of struggle. In the institutional literalism that runs from Darby through Scofield to the organisations of Part Eight, the same name and the same promise are read as a title to territory and a timetable for history, and that reading has been turned, as Parts Nine to Twelve showed, into a political instrument: votes, lobbying, embassy moves and a shield against criticism of a government's policy. On this argument, the shield described in this volume did not require the inner reading to be refuted; it required only that one level of meaning be taken as the whole of it.

The argument has limits, and the platform states them. It is a reading of the contrast, not a claim about any person's motive; Part Nine's account of who benefited rests on documents, not on this lens. It is not an argument about Jews or Judaism. Literal and inward readings exist inside Judaism and inside Christianity alike, and the literalism this volume has examined is a specific Protestant system, named and dated. Nothing in the lens says that any people has taken a name not its own: the name Israel has named a people since the oldest document in which it appears, and the inward traditions that read it as a state of consciousness were, first of all, that people's own.

The objections, stated fairly

Three objections to the argument deserve to be stated in their strongest form. First, Christian Zionists hold that the literal reading is the faithful one, and that turning promises to Israel into allegories of the soul is the road to replacement theology, which in Christian history did real harm to Jews; on that view the "map" reading is not neutral but dangerous, and Scofield's insistence that the church does not inherit Israel's promises was a correction of it. [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] [cite:ts11_icej_catc] Second, the Jewish inward readings documented in this part did not set the inner meaning against the people or the land: Philo and Sacks both kept Israel a people. [cite:ts13_alesso_philo] [cite:ts13_sacks_be] An argument that treats literal attachment to the land as a loss of inner meaning can therefore be heard as an outsider's judgment on Jewish practice, which is not what the platform intends and which the lens must not become. Third, Spector's finding that most Christian Zionists reason from a blessing in Genesis 12:3 rather than from an end-times scheme suggests that the political shield rests less on a literal theory of prophecy than on a simpler conviction about loyalty, which the map-and-deed contrast does not fully capture. [cite:ts6_tablet_spector]

The platform's response is that the lens is offered as a key, not a verdict. It asks a reader to notice that the same name has been read in two registers for two thousand years, that the inward register belongs to Jewish and Christian tradition alike, and that the institutional politics documented in this volume chose one register and treated it as the only one. Whether that observation clarifies or distorts is a judgment the reader is entitled to make, with the record on one side and the reading on the other. The settled and unsettled claims of the whole volume are sorted in [scene:ts14_myths|Part Fourteen].
