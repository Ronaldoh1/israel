Resolution 194: What It Says, How Each Side Reads It, and How Many Refugees There Were

Resolution 194 is usually remembered for one paragraph, the eleventh, on the return of the refugees. It is a longer document than that, and it was adopted as a plan for ending the war as a whole: a commission to conduct the peace, an international regime for Jerusalem, protection for the Holy Places, and a settlement of the refugee question. Each side has since read it selectively. Two popular versions dominate: that international law guarantees every refugee's descendant a return, or that the resolution means nothing. Neither matches the text, which is short enough to read as a whole.

The General Assembly adopted Resolution 194 (III) at its 186th plenary meeting on 11 December 1948, by 35 votes to 15, with 8 abstentions. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] [cite:rs_rd6_ecf_194] It followed the September 1948 report of the UN Mediator, told in [scene:rd6_bernadotte|Bernadotte's mission].

Paragraph 2 established a Conciliation Commission of three UN member states. France, Turkey and the United States were named to it. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] [cite:rs_rd6_ecf_194] Paragraph 5 urged the governments concerned to negotiate "the final settlement of all questions outstanding between them." Paragraph 7 called for the protection of, and free access to, the Holy Places, including Nazareth, under "effective United Nations supervision." [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] Paragraph 8 called for the Jerusalem area to be treated separately and placed under "effective United Nations control," asked the Security Council to ensure its demilitarization, and instructed the Commission to propose a permanent international regime for it. Paragraph 9 called for the "freest possible access to Jerusalem by road, rail or air." [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] What became of the Jerusalem paragraphs in 1949, when the Assembly returned to the city and Israel moved its parliament there, is told in [scene:rd6_un_admission|Israel's admission and the Jerusalem question].

Then came paragraph 11. It resolved:

that the refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date

and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return. [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:wiki_res_194] The paragraph did not stop at return. It instructed the Conciliation Commission to facilitate the "repatriation, resettlement and economic and social rehabilitation" of the refugees and the payment of compensation, working with the Director of United Nations Relief for Palestine Refugees. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194] Resettlement thus sat in the same sentence as repatriation. The resolution offered the Commission both tools and did not say how the choice between them was to be made, beyond the refugees' own wish to return.

The General Assembly has reaffirmed the resolution every year since 1949. [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:wiki_res_194] At the time of its adoption, however, the Arab states and Palestinian representatives rejected it, because it implied acceptance of Israel. Only later did Palestinians come to cite it as the foundation of the right of return. [cite:wiki_res_194]

Israel's reading was stated by Ben-Gurion to the UN conciliators. The words "live at peace with their neighbours," he argued, made return contingent on peace with the Arab states. [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:unwatch_194] Critics of the Palestinian reading add three further points. General Assembly resolutions are recommendations, not binding law. The text says refugees "should be permitted" to return, not that they have a right to. And the return of several million descendants would end Israel's Jewish majority. [cite:unwatch_194] In its strongest form, the Israeli case is that the resolution itself made return part of a peace, and that no peace was made.

The Palestinian reading treats return as a right, not a concession. Its supporters argue that the right is grounded in broader international law, not in this resolution alone, and they cite Article 13(2) of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted the day before. They also point to the Assembly's annual reaffirmation, and to the terms on which Israel was admitted to the United Nations in May 1949, which they say came with a pledge to honor the resolution. [cite:wiki_res_194] The exact assurances given on Israel's behalf at that time are not confirmed in the UN records consulted for this book, and they are not relied on here. In its strongest form, the Palestinian case is that the right does not depend on this resolution's wording at all, and that the Assembly's repetition of it every year since 1949 shows how the international community has understood it.

The two readings met at the conference the Conciliation Commission convened at Lausanne in 1949, and the talks broke down on this question; it remained unresolved at Camp David in 2000. [cite:wiki_res_194] The sequence of offers at Lausanne is told in [scene:lau_s01|the Lausanne conference].

Behind the dispute over the text lay a dispute over numbers, which began at once and has never been closed. Benny Morris's survey of the estimates gives the range. Israeli spokesmen publicly said "about 520,000," and Israel officially held to a figure of 520,000 to 530,000. Arab officials, from 1949, spoke of 900,000 or one million. The British estimated 810,000 in February 1949: 210,000 in Gaza, 320,000 in the West Bank, and 280,000 in Lebanon, Syria and the East Bank of the Jordan. The UN Economic Survey Mission and UNRWA counted 726,000. [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix] The UN Conciliation Commission's own estimate, given in the previous section, was about 711,000.

One private Israeli judgment stands out. According to Morris, in late 1950 Walter Eytan, director-general of Israel's Foreign Ministry, privately called UNRWA's registration of 726,000 "meticulous," but said that "the real number was close to 800,000." [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix] The official public figure and the Foreign Ministry's private view thus differed by more than a quarter of a million people.

The question of compensation also produced a number. The Conciliation Commission's progress report of November 1951 put the value of abandoned Arab immovable property at about P£100 million, P£100,383,000 exactly, on 16,324 square kilometres of land, of which 4,574 were cultivable. For its calculations the Commission took the refugee population as approximately 900,000. [cite:rs_rd6_unccp_1951] That figure was a working approximation for the property estimate, not a count. How the property itself passed to the state is told in the section on the Absentees' Property Law.

The count of places emptied in the war differs as much as the count of people, and here the difference lies mostly in definitions. Benny Morris, in 1987, identified 369 villages. Walid Khalidi, in 1992, counted 418, excluding towns and sites without permanent structures. Earlier lists gave 399 (al-Arif), 443 (Nijm and Muammer) and 472 (Saleh). The Palestine Land Society, counting towns, tribal lands and police stations as well, gives 531 localities. [cite:rs_rd6_plands_villages] The lower figures count villages in a narrow sense. The highest counts every kind of inhabited place. Each total is accurate on its own terms, and none can be compared with another without its definition.

Much here remains unsettled. The record does not establish a single agreed number of refugees: every estimate between 520,000 and one million had an official source behind it, and the private Israeli view, close to 800,000, lay nearer the UN and British figures than to Israel's public one. Whether paragraph 11 created a right or a recommendation is a question of law and interpretation, not of record, and both readings are documented. What the text itself settles is narrower and clearer: it tied return to the refugees' wish and to living at peace, it set compensation beside return, it named resettlement and rehabilitation as tasks for the Commission, and it placed Jerusalem under international control. Most of that program was never carried out.
