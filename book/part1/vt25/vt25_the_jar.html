The Jar: How the Nag Hammadi Codices Were Found, Kept and Dated

In December 1945 men digging for fertile soil at the foot of the Jabal al-Tarif, a cliff on the east bank of the Nile across from the town of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, found a sealed earthenware jar. One of them, Muhammad Ali al-Samman, broke it open and found leather-bound books of papyrus inside. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] What came out of the ground that winter is the largest single collection of early Christian writings outside the Bible, and most of it had been unread for about sixteen centuries. Part Fourteen set the find on its basic footing and showed that no document links its burial to an order from any bishop. [scene:vt14_canon|Part Fourteen: Who Chose the Bible] This part goes into the books themselves, text by text. Before the texts, though, the find has its own told version and its own record, and the two are not the same.

What was in the jar

The find is usually counted as thirteen codices. Strictly, scholars count twelve bound books and a set of loose leaves that someone in antiquity had cut out of a thirteenth book and tucked inside the cover of the sixth; those leaves are called Codex XIII, and what became of the rest of that thirteenth book is not known. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] Together the codices hold 52 separate writings, which scholars call tractates. Because some works appear more than once (the Apocryphon of John three times, the Gospel of Truth, On the Origin of the World and the Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit twice each, Eugnostos twice), the number of distinct works is 46. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi]

All of them are in Coptic, the last stage of the Egyptian language written in an alphabet adapted from Greek, mainly in the Sahidic and Subakhmimic dialects. All were translated from Greek originals, most of which are lost. As many as fourteen different scribes' hands can be told apart. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] The books themselves were made around 350 CE. That date comes from the cartonnage: scrap papyrus, including old letters and receipts, that bookbinders glued together to stiffen the leather covers. The latest dated scrap is from 348 CE, so the covers were made after that year. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] The copies are fourth-century; the works copied into them are older, many of them composed in Greek in the second or third century. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

The story of the discovery, and how much of it holds

The version most readers know comes from the American scholar James M. Robinson, who led the team that published the codices. In his telling, Muhammad Ali and his brothers were digging for sabakh, a nitrate-rich soil used as fertiliser; Muhammad Ali feared the jar held a jinn, smashed it anyway, carried the books home, and his mother burned some of the pages in her bread oven. Weeks later the brothers killed the man they held responsible for their father's murder, and fear of the police investigation is said to be why the books were handed off quickly. [cite:vt25_goodacre2013] The detail of the mother and the oven is the part most often repeated. It does appear in standard reference accounts, which state that "his mother used some of the leaves as fuel for an oven". [cite:vt25_enc_nh] Its source, though, is Robinson's interviews with the villagers, not a document from 1945.

Robinson built the story from interviews that began in late 1974, nearly thirty years after the event, and published versions in 1977, 1979 and 1981 that differ on how many people were present and who actually unearthed the jar. In 2013 the New Testament scholar Mark Goodacre examined those versions. He noted that the Coptic specialists Rodolphe Kasser and Martin Krause had in 1984 called much of the detailed history "stories and fables"; that Robinson himself questioned Muhammad Ali's reliability; that Robinson paid informants on his visits; and that in a 1987 film Muhammad Ali said he had smashed the pot alone. The French scholar Jean Doresse, who visited the area between 1947 and 1950, recorded a much shorter and vaguer account with no brothers, no jinn, no oven and no blood feud, and Goodacre judged Doresse's earlier testimony the more reliable of the two. [cite:vt25_goodacre2013] The burning of some pages by the finder's family is therefore a reported detail from one investigator's late interviews, not an established fact; it is also a domestic accident, not an act of suppression by any church.

The record from the books' arrival in Cairo onward is firmer. Most of the codices passed to a Cypriot antiques dealer in Cairo, Phokion Tanos. The Egyptian Department of Antiquities held them back for fear they would be sold abroad, and after the revolution of 1952 they were declared national property and placed in the Coptic Museum in Old Cairo, where they remain. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt25_enc_nh] One codex, the first, left Egypt: it was sold to a Belgian dealer and bought for the C. G. Jung Institute in Zurich through the Dutch scholar Gilles Quispel, which is why it is called the Jung Codex. After a dispute over ownership its pages returned to Cairo only in 1975. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] A photographic facsimile of all the codices came out over the 1970s, and the first complete English translation, edited by Robinson, was published in 1977, with a revised edition in 1988. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

Who buried the books, and why

Nothing found with the jar says who owned the books or why they were buried. Three main answers have been proposed, and all three remain hypotheses.

The first, Robinson's, is monastic. The cliff lies near the site of ancient Pbow, the headquarters of the Pachomian monasteries, the first large communal monastic federation in Egypt, and some cartonnage scraps refer to places near the burial site. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] In 367 Athanasius of Alexandria sent his 39th Festal Letter to the Egyptian churches, listing the 27 books of the New Testament and warning against books "called apocryphal", which he called "an invention of heretics". [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] In Robinson's reading, monks who owned such books hid them after that letter. The letter warns readers; it contains no order to burn or bury anything, and no source records monks acting on it in this way. [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] The fullest modern version of the monastic case is Hugo Lundhaug and Lance Jenott's The Monastic Origins of the Nag Hammadi Codices (2015), which argues from the cartonnage, the scribes' notes and colophons, and comparison with other Egyptian manuscripts that the books were "produced and read by Christian monks, most likely Pachomians". On their reading the codices are not a Gnostic library at all but books "completely at home in the monastic manuscript culture" of the period. [cite:vt25_lj2015]

The second answer is a private owner. In 2014 Nicola Denzey Lewis and Justine Ariel Blount argued that the papyrologists who studied the cartonnage found no single Pachomian source for it, and that the scrap looked more like a town rubbish heap. They added, following David Brakke, that Athanasius's letter was aimed at Arian and Melitian opponents rather than at Gnostic books. [cite:vt25_lewisblount2014] Their alternative is the third answer, a burial with the dead. The Jabal al-Tarif was a cemetery used for centuries, and other codices of this period were found in graves: Codex Tchacos, which holds the Gospel of Judas, came from a family tomb, and the Codex Panopolitanus from a grave at Akhmim. On this view a wealthy Greek-speaking Egyptian with esoteric interests commissioned the books and was buried with them, much as earlier Egyptians were buried with the Book of the Dead. [cite:vt25_lewisblount2014]

Each explanation answers a different question. The monastic case answers who read these books: on the best available evidence, fourth-century Egyptian Christians, quite possibly monks, read Valentinian and Sethian treatises side by side with Plato, Hermetic prayers and orthodox wisdom sayings. The grave-goods case answers why a library might end up in a jar at a cemetery. Neither one records an order to destroy them. What can be stated without hypothesis is narrower: the books were made after 348, they were buried, they survived, and the Coptic Museum has kept them since the 1950s. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

What kind of collection it is

Popular accounts call the find "the Gnostic Bible". The contents do not support that label. The jar held a short extract from Plato's Republic (588a–589b) in a loose Coptic version; three Hermetic texts in which the Egyptian sage Hermes Trismegistus teaches his disciples; the Sentences of Sextus, a collection of moral maxims that circulated widely among orthodox Christians in Latin; and the Teachings of Silvanus, which most scholars read as non-Gnostic Christian wisdom. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt25_wiki_sextus] [cite:vt25_wiki_silvanus] Alongside these are Valentinian works from the school of the second-century Roman teacher Valentinus and Sethian works from the circle of mythmakers who traced humanity's spiritual lineage to Adam's son Seth. The previous part described both schools. [scene:vt24_what_they_taught|Part Twenty-Four: What the Evangelical Gnostics Taught] Nothing in the codices sets out a canon, a list of approved books or a rule of faith, and some tractates argue against others: the Testimony of Truth attacks the Valentinians by name. [cite:vt25_wiki_testtruth] A scribe's note after the Prayer of Thanksgiving in Codex VI says the text was chosen from a larger collection, so whoever assembled these books was selecting from a wider library. [cite:vt25_wiki_prayerthanks]

The next three sections take the contents in three groups. First come the sayings gospels and the gospels that teach in Jesus's voice, where the "kingdom within" theme is strongest. Second are the secret cosmologies, the myths of Sophia, the ignorant creator and the spark of light trapped in humanity. Last is a full catalogue of every tractate in all thirteen codices.
