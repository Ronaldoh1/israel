The Glory of Kings: A Book Written to Prove a Dynasty

In 1270 a nobleman named Yekuno Amlak rebelled against the Zagwe dynasty that then ruled the Ethiopian highlands, took the throne, and reigned until 1285. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] The dynasty he founded called itself Solomonic, and it claimed something no European royal house ever dared: direct descent, father to son, from King Solomon of Israel. The claim needed proof. About forty years after Yekuno Amlak's death, a team of Ethiopian clerics produced it. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] [cite:et_wiki_kebra]

The book is the Kebra Nagast, "The Glory of the Kings". Its colophon dates its composition to the period between 1314 and 1321 or 1322, and among the clerics who produced it the colophon names the nebura'ed Yeshaq of Aksum. [cite:et_wiki_kebra] The colophon also gives the book a pedigree: it was first written in Coptic, it says, then translated into Arabic in 1225 and finally into Ge'ez. Many scholars doubt that a Coptic original ever existed. [cite:et_wiki_kebra] A local governor, Ya'ibika Igzi' of Enderta, may have commanded the Ge'ez version. [cite:et_wiki_kebra]

The story it tells is grand. The Queen of Sheba, whom the book calls Makeda, travels to Jerusalem to learn Solomon's wisdom, and returns home bearing his son. That son, Menelik, grows up, visits his father in Jerusalem and returns to Ethiopia with an escort of Israelite nobles, and with the Ark of the Covenant, which the book says was brought to Ethiopia and kept there. [cite:et_wiki_kebra] Israel's chosen status, in the book's telling, passed with the Ark to Ethiopia, and its kings were the true heirs of David. The queen herself lays down the rule of succession: "No one except the male seed of David, the son of Solomon the King" shall ever reign over Ethiopia. [cite:et_wh_solomonic]

Its purpose is not seriously in doubt. Modern scholars regard the stories as "largely created to legitimize the dynasty's rise to power in Ethiopia in the 13th century", and the Wikipedia summary of current opinion is that "there is no historical evidence supporting the legends" of Solomonic origin. [cite:et_wiki_kebra] Most historians, World History Encyclopedia notes, agree that the book was written to legitimise the new dynasty against supporters of the Zagwe. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] It drew on the Bible, on Jewish and Christian legend and on older Ethiopian tradition, and wove them into a single argument: the man on the throne was there by right of blood that ran back to Solomon.

The Kebra Nagast is not part of the eighty-one-book canon. Its power did not depend on canonical status. It worked as the Donation of Constantine worked in the West, described in The Canon: a document produced by clerics close to power, presented as ancient, used to found a ruler's claim on sacred history. [scene:cn5_forged_title|The Canon: the forged title] The difference is that the Donation was exposed as a forgery by a single scholar's proof in 1440. The Kebra Nagast made no claim that could be disproved in the same way, and it held its authority for six and a half centuries, until the dynasty it legitimised was overthrown.

The book also changed what the Bible meant in Ethiopia. Read through the Kebra Nagast, the Old Testament was not only the history of a distant people; it was the prehistory of the Ethiopian royal house and of the nation. The Ark, kept somewhere in the north, as the next part shows, was not a lost relic but a present possession. The scriptures that Ethiopia preserved and Europe lost were bound, in this way, to the claims of a throne.
