Britain Trained the Army That Would Fight It

The Jewish fighting force of 1948 is often remembered as self-made, built in defiance of Britain. Defiance is part of the record, and from 1945 it became the larger part. But the documents show something the familiar account leaves out. Between 1938 and 1945, much of that force's training, its fighting method and its future officers came through British channels. Britain opened those channels when it needed Jewish fighters, first against the Arab Revolt and then against the Axis, and closed them, sometimes with arrests, when the need passed. Three episodes show the pattern: Wingate's Special Night Squads in 1938, the Palmach in 1941 and the Jewish Brigade Group in 1944–45. Running beside them was a second story, of the money the Jewish community in Palestine raised to pay for its own defense.

The first channel opened in 1938, at the height of the Arab Revolt ([scene:s07a5|the revolt of 1936–1939]). Captain Orde Wingate was a British intelligence officer and a devout Christian restorationist who embraced the prophetic vision of a Jewish return to the land. He proposed a joint unit of British soldiers and Jewish supernumerary police drawn from the Haganah, the Jewish defense organization that Britain never officially recognized. The Jewish Agency eventually agreed. The Special Night Squads operated from Kibbutz Ein Harod from June to October 1938. About 75 Haganah volunteers served in them. Among them were Moshe Dayan and Yigal Allon, both future generals, and Pinchas Kopel, who later founded the Border Police. [cite:nli_wingate_hebrew] [cite:wiki_special_night_squads] [cite:israeled_wingate]

The arrangement shows how the Mandate's security system worked in that year. The Haganah had no legal standing. Its members could serve the government only by being enrolled in a form the government recognized, here as supernumerary police under British command. The initiative came from a British officer, the Jewish Agency's consent was needed to supply the men, and the unit existed for as long as the British authorities found it useful. It lasted five months.

What Wingate taught was a method: small units moving at night and raiding into Arab villages. Critics argue that the method outlasted him. The Israeli writer Uri Avnery traced a line of retaliation-raid doctrine from Wingate through the Palmach to the Israel Defense Forces. [cite:avnery_dayan_wingate] That is a critic's reading, not a settled finding, and the record gathered here does not test it against the army's own doctrine. It does show two things plainly. The men Wingate trained went on to command. And the squads were a British instrument against the Arab Revolt, staffed partly by Haganah volunteers, whose targets were Arab villages.

The same administration that sponsored the squads also prosecuted Haganah members caught training with arms. Dayan, one of Wingate's trainees, was imprisoned in 1939. [cite:archiveshub_wingate] [cite:avnery_dayan_wingate] The two facts fit one policy. Training under British command served British aims; training outside it led to arrest.

The second channel opened under the pressure of the world war. Facing a possible Axis breakthrough in North Africa, Britain cooperated with the Haganah in forming the Palmach in May 1941. After El Alamein, when the danger receded, so did British support. [cite:wiki_haganah_mirror] The pattern was the same as in 1938: cooperation while the threat was acute, distance once it passed.

The third channel took longest to open. After long negotiation, the British Army announced a Jewish Brigade Group, formed in 1944. It fought the Germans in northern Italy in March 1945. [cite:wiki_zvi_brenner] [cite:wiki_haganah_mirror] The record here says nothing more about the Brigade: not its strength, its casualties or the negotiations that produced it. Nor does it say how many Jews and Arabs from Palestine volunteered for the British forces during the war. Those numbers are not given here, and this book does not supply them from elsewhere.

British training was one input. The other was money raised inside the Jewish community. By 1939 the Jewish Agency's budget carried an £80,000 line for "security service (defense)" within a total of £720,000, about one-ninth of the whole. In 1938, the year of the Night Squads, the community created the Kofer HaYishuv, a levy on cigarettes, restaurant bills and luxuries, to fund the Haganah. These are the first two steps of a funding chain that later ran through American donors and charity channels, a story told in full in [scene:gf_s02|the account of the pre-state army's budgets].

The two streams fit together. The Agency's budget and the levy paid for an organization that Britain did not recognize. Britain trained parts of it when British interests required, and it jailed members who trained outside British control. The result by 1947 was a force the Mandate had never legalized: about 10,000 mobilized men and 40,000 reservists, under officers many of whom had learned their trade in British service. [cite:wiki_haganah_mirror] From 1945 those cadres would turn against Britain itself.

How much the British contribution mattered, compared with what the Haganah built for itself, is a question of weight that the record here does not settle. It documents the channels, the dates and the men, but not a measure of how far the 1948 army's effectiveness came from Ein Harod, the Palmach or Italy rather than from its own training. The money record has a matching gap. No audited total of the Haganah's budget is publicly available. The levy and the 1939 budget line are documented individually, and no full account of the army's funding before 1948 can yet be added up from them.
