The Department's Handling: What the Attorney General Did

The person responsible for carrying out the release, from February 2025 until April 2026, was Attorney General Pam Bondi. She is not named in the files. [cite:ep9_nbc_officials2026] What matters about her for this book is what she did with them, and the record of that is complete enough to set out in order and let it speak.

The hearing

On 11 February 2026 Bondi appeared before the House Judiciary Committee for more than five hours. In her opening statement she called Epstein a "monster" and said, "I am deeply sorry for what any victim — any victim — has been through." [cite:ep9_abc11_bondi2026] [cite:ep9_aj_bondi2026] A group of survivors sat in the rows behind her. Representative Pramila Jayapal asked those who had never been able to meet anyone from the Justice Department to stand, and they did. She then asked Bondi whether she would turn to them and apologise for what her department had put them through. Bondi did not turn around. She asked why Jayapal had not put the question to her predecessor, Merrick Garland, and said she was "not going to get in the gutter" with "theatrics". [cite:ep9_mj_bondi2026] [cite:ep9_abc11_bondi2026] Danielle Bensky, one of the survivors, said afterwards there had been "such a lack of empathy". [cite:ep9_aj_bondi2026] Representative Thomas Massie, a Republican and co-author of the Transparency Act, said Bondi had been "afraid to" look at the survivors. [cite:ep9_hill_massie2026]

The rest of the hearing ran the same way. She had brought a printout labelled with Jayapal's search history, part of what critics called a "burn book" of prepared attacks; Democrats later demanded that the department stop tracking which files members of Congress searched, and the department said it logs all searches to protect victims' information. [cite:ep9_abc_bondi_fired] When Massie pointed out that Les Wexner's name had been redacted, she said it had been unredacted within forty minutes of his noticing; he answered, "Forty minutes of me catching you red-handed." Asked whether the president had attended a party with underage girls, she called the question "ridiculous", and when Representative Ted Lieu said he believed she had just lied under oath, she replied, "Don't you ever accuse me of committing a crime." She said there was no evidence the president had committed a crime. [cite:ep9_aj_bondi2026] The reports of the hearing do not state whether she was formally sworn.

After the hearing

On 4 March the House Oversight Committee voted to subpoena her, five Republicans joining the Democrats. At a closed-door briefing later that month, Democrats walked out, saying she would not commit to complying; she said, "I will follow the law." [cite:ep9_pbs_subpoena] [cite:ep9_iheart_walkout] On 2 April the president removed her, thanking her for having "faithfully served", and named Blanche acting Attorney General. ABC's sources cited frustration over her handling of the Epstein files, among other things. [cite:ep9_abc_bondi_fired]

On 29 May she sat for a closed-door transcribed interview with the Oversight Committee, on terms the chairman agreed: not under oath and not videotaped. The ranking Democrat, Robert Garcia, said he was "incredibly disappointed". She acknowledged "redaction errors", apologised to the victims, said she had not led every aspect of the release and had delegated its oversight to Blanche, and declined to answer some questions about the president under "ground rules" agreed with the committee. Democrats said nearly every answer was "not to my recollection", "I don't know", or a referral to Blanche. [cite:ep9_nhpr_bondi2026]

What the sequence shows

The book does not ascribe a motive to her; no document states one. It sets the acts in order. A "client list" announced, then said not to exist. Binders of public documents presented as a release. A briefing to the president that his name was in the files, followed two months later by an announcement that nothing more would be released. An investigation of the president's opponents opened on his instruction, by the department that four months earlier had closed its review. A release that exposed victims and blacked out others. Survivors behind her whom she would not turn to face. And, when finally questioned at length, an appearance under no oath, with the president's role off the table. Her own account, that transparency was delivered at the president's direction, that the errors were errors, and that she had delegated the release to her deputy, sits beside it. Readers can weigh the two.
