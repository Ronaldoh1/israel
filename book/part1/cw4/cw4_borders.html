The Armistice Lines, 1960–1962: Tawafiq, Rotem and Lake Tiberias

Along the armistice lines, Arab and Israeli governments met not in politics but in fire. Between 1960 and 1962 the lines that reached the United Nations were chiefly the Syrian ones, where the armistice of 1949 had left demilitarized zones and a lake whose status Israel and Syria did not see alike. The long history of those zones, and the water war of 1964–67 that grew out of them, is told in [scene:sy_s02|the demilitarized zones and the water war]. Two flare-ups there, in 1960 and 1962, came before the United Nations, and between them came a crisis in the Sinai. For the Syrian episodes the record consists chiefly of what the UN and the United States government wrote down, and in each Israel and its neighbour gave differing accounts.

The cultivation line, January 1960. The dispute began with ploughing. The Yearbook of the United Nations for 1960 records that Israeli cultivation in the southern demilitarized zone, north of Lake Tiberias, had spread eastward since the founding of Kibbutz Beit Katzir, cutting Arab farmers off from the lake. On 20 January 1960 the UN Chief of Staff, acting as chairman of the Mixed Armistice Commission, proposed a practical line. Israel ignored the proposal without expressly rejecting it. [cite:rs_cw4_unyb1960] The UN's account matters for what followed: eleven days before the raid, the UN's own officer had addressed the expansion of cultivation as the problem and offered a line that one side did not take up.

Tawafiq, 31 January–1 February 1960. On the night of 31 January–1 February 1960 Israeli forces shelled Arab positions and occupied Tawafiq, a village about 1,300 metres east of Beit Katzir, and withdrew the same day. The Israel–Syria Mixed Armistice Commission condemned Israel for an attack that caused the village's "almost total destruction." [cite:rs_cw4_unyb1960] Israel's case, as the Yearbook records it, was that Syrian positions at Tawafiq had been harassing Beit Katzir since December 1959, and that Israel had cleared forces "illegally entrenched there." [cite:rs_cw4_unyb1960] The United Arab Republic, of which Syria was then a part, said that Israeli forces had advanced on Arab farmers. [cite:rs_cw4_unyb1960]

The two cases can be stated at their full strength from the same UN record. Israel's was that a kibbutz inside the zone was under fire from positions that had no right to be there, and that clearing them was defence. The UAR's was that Israeli forces had advanced on Arab farmers; and the UN record adds that Israeli cultivation had spread east, cutting Arab farmers off from the lake, that the UN had offered a line less than two weeks earlier which Israel had left unanswered, and that the armistice commission condemned an attack that almost destroyed the village. Neither account, as the Yearbook records them, denies the other's facts outright. They differ on which came first and on whether the response matched what provoked it.

A later account adds detail that the Yearbook does not give. Wikipedia's article on the episode, citing the historians Bar-Joseph and Gluska, says that the raid was called Operation Hargol, that it was carried out by the Golani Brigade, and that it followed Syrian shelling which killed an Israeli policeman on 31 January. It gives the dead as 9 Syrians and 3 Israelis. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_rotem] Those figures are reported, not established from a primary source used here, and the UN Yearbook excerpt gives no casualty count to set beside them.

Radio Cairo, 3 February. The raid was answered first on the air. On 3 February 1960 Radio Cairo declared: "Our forces, reinforced after the unification of Egypt and Syria, have taught the Israelis a lesson." It added: "In the south, our heroes are stationed on the border, awaiting the signal." [cite:rs_cw4_cojs_radio_cairo] The broadcast joined the Syrian front to the Egyptian one, as the union of the two countries now allowed Cairo to do, and it pointed south.

The Rotem crisis, February–March 1960. What followed in the south was, in the title of Uri Bar-Joseph's 1995 study, "Egypt's Sinai surprise of 1960," and Israel was "caught unaware." [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph_1995] The fullest account available here is Wikipedia's, which rests on Bar-Joseph's work, and it is given as reported. Between about 17 and 24 February 1960 Egypt moved its 2nd Infantry and 4th Armoured Divisions into Sinai under radio silence. An Israeli reconnaissance flight confirmed the move on 23 February. Rabin is quoted as saying: "We've been caught with our pants down." On 25 February the General Staff issued the orders called "Rotem." Ben-Gurion refused a full call-up and authorized 7,000 reservists. Egypt's withdrawal began on 9 March. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_rotem] [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph_1995]

The episode was short, but its mechanism is precise. Two divisions moved into Sinai over about a week before Israel's reconnaissance confirmed them. Ben-Gurion chose a limited call-up of 7,000 reservists over a full one, and Egypt began to withdraw on 9 March. Historians, as Wikipedia summarizes their view, describe Rotem as a "trial run" for May–June 1967, when Nasser again moved forces into Sinai, that time in public. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_rotem] [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph_1995] The phrase is an interpretation, made with hindsight. It holds that the 1960 episode taught both sides something that shaped 1967; the sources used here do not record what either side's planners in 1967 said they had learned from 1960.

Lake Tiberias, March 1962. Two years later the lake itself was the issue. Exchanges of fire between Syria and Israel began on 8 March 1962. On the night of 16–17 March, according to Syria's complaint to the Security Council, Israel made a raid in force from the demilitarized zone. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d251] [cite:rs_cw4_unsc171] Israel's counter-claim was that Syrians had fired on its fishermen and on police boats on Lake Tiberias. Israel also asserted sovereignty over the whole lake. The State Department's legal adviser judged that this claim was not finally established, though Israel held exclusive control of the lake. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d251]

Israel's case, at its strongest, was the one it had made at Tawafiq: its citizens, here fishermen and the police who protected them, were under fire from Syrian positions on the shore, on a lake that Israel controlled and regarded as its own. Syria's case was that the attack of 16–17 March was a raid in force out of a zone that was meant to be demilitarized. The American legal opinion did not choose between the narratives, but it did weigh in on one premise of Israel's: control of the lake was a fact, sovereignty over all of it was a claim.

Resolution 171, 9 April 1962. The Security Council answered on 9 April 1962. Resolution 171 deplored the hostile exchanges between Syria and Israel that had begun on 8 March 1962. It reaffirmed the Council's resolution 111 of 1956, and it determined that "the Israel attack of 16-17 March 1962 constitutes a flagrant violation of that resolution." It called on both parties to observe the demilitarized zone. It passed by 10 votes to none, with France abstaining. [cite:rs_cw4_unsc171] It had two parts: one that spread responsibility for the exchanges across both sides, and one that singled out Israel's attack of 16–17 March.

How the draft lost its "balance." The State Department's internal account of the drafting, sent by Battle to Bundy in April 1962, shows how the text came to take that shape. The draft that the United States and Britain prepared had been balanced, in the Department's word, by a paragraph on Syrian actions of 8 March. But the US mission concluded that the facts did not support blaming Syria alone for them, and the text became, in the Department's phrase, "less 'balanced' if more objective." A Syrian draft, which the Department called "completely one-sided," was not pressed to a vote. [cite:rs_cw4_frus62_d251] The document records an American government deciding on the evidence that a paragraph meant to share blame with Syria could not be sustained, and saying so internally in plain terms. It also records the Department's dismissal of Syria's own text as "completely one-sided."

The mechanism. Set side by side, the two Syrian episodes show the same structure. In the demilitarized zones neither side had sovereignty that the other accepted, and the armistice machinery, in this record, proposed lines and condemned attacks but did not enforce either. Israel acted on the ground, by cultivation, and answered fire with raids in force; Syria's positions fired on the kibbutz and, Israel said, on its boats. The Mixed Armistice Commission in 1960 condemned the Israeli raid, and the Security Council in 1962, while deploring exchanges on both sides, singled out the Israeli attack as a flagrant violation. Israel, for its part, ignored the cultivation line and asserted sovereignty over the whole lake. The Sinai crisis between them showed, in Bar-Joseph's title, an Israel "caught unaware."

What the record leaves open. Several things cannot be settled from the sources used for this section. The Rotem crisis is documented here only through Wikipedia's account of Bar-Joseph's work and the bibliographic record of his 1995 study, not through a primary document or the study's text; its details, including Rabin's remark and the figure of 7,000 reservists, are therefore reported, not verified. (Wikipedia cites Bar-Joseph's work as 1996; the study itself is dated 1995.) The casualties at Tawafiq rest on that same secondary account. The casualties of the March 1962 fighting on Lake Tiberias are not recorded in any source used here. And who fired first on 8 March 1962 is not resolved: Israel said Syria did, and the US mission concluded only that the facts did not support blaming Syria alone.
