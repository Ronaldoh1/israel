What Would Change It: The Levers, the Reading and the Verdict

If the gaps in the ledger have dates and designers, they can in principle be redesigned. This section sets out levers that have been tried in the United States and measured by named researchers, each with the strongest evidence for and against it and its status as of October 2026. None is offered as a programme: the choice depends on what a reader values and will pay, which no ledger settles. The section then gives the Sophia platform's reading of the Dream as a whole, with the readings that answer it, and the volume's answer to its own question.

Cash for children: the 2021 credit and its expiry

In 2021 Congress raised the child tax credit from $2,000 to $3,000 per child, $3,600 under age six, and paid much of it in monthly payments from July. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad18_nber_ananat] Child poverty on the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts tax credits, fell to 5.2 per cent in 2021; when the credit returned to its old form, it rose to 12.4 per cent in 2022. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] The dispute is over work. Elizabeth Ananat and colleagues, using Census survey data from April to September 2021, found "very small, inconsistently signed, and statistically insignificant" effects on parents' employment. [cite:ad18_nber_ananat] Kevin Corinth, Bruce Meyer and colleagues projected that a permanent version would lead about 1.5 million working parents, 2.6 per cent, to leave work, and that child poverty would then fall by at most 22 per cent rather than the 34 per cent a static count suggests. [cite:ad18_nber_corinth] The first measured six months; the second projects from estimated responses to work incentives, and its central employment assumption was called "really not credible" by the Stanford tax economist Jacob Goldin. [cite:ad18_cbs_ctc] As of October 2026 the credit is $2,200 per child, $1,700 of it refundable, and since the 2025 budget law it requires a Social Security number for the filer or a spouse. [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] [cite:ad13_usccb_obbba]

Saving by default

One retirement lever is not much disputed. In plans that enrol workers automatically, 94 per cent participated in 2025, against 64 per cent in plans where workers must sign up. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] The 2022 retirement law, SECURE 2.0, requires most new 401(k) and 403(b) plans set up after 29 December 2022 to enrol workers automatically from 2025, with exemptions for the smallest and newest employers; as of October 2026 the requirement applies, and its final regulations had gone to the White House budget office for review in June. [cite:ad10_asppa_auto] The limits are in the same data: a default works only where a plan exists, and 70 per cent of private workers have a 401(k)-type plan available; participation among workers earning under $15,000 was 43 per cent; and the median balance was $44,115. [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26]

The wage floor

The Congressional Budget Office estimated in February 2021 that raising the federal minimum to $15 by 2025 would lift 900,000 people out of poverty and raise affected workers' pay by a net $333 billion, while about 1.4 million fewer workers would be employed. [cite:ad18_bt_cbo2021] A study of 138 state increases from 1979 to 2016 by Doruk Cengiz, Arindrajit Dube and colleagues found that the number of low-wage jobs was essentially unchanged five years after each increase. [cite:ad18_nber_cengiz] A review by David Neumark and Peter Shirley found that 79.2 per cent of the preferred estimates in the American literature were negative, with the evidence strongest for teenagers, young adults and less-educated workers. [cite:ad18_nber_neumark] Thirty states and the District of Columbia already set a higher floor. [cite:us13_bp_states2026]

Medicaid expansion

Under the 2010 health law states may extend Medicaid to low-income adults. In 2025, 9.0 per cent of working-age adults in expansion states were uninsured against 18.1 per cent in the others. [cite:ad18_nchs_unins25] Sarah Miller, Laura Wherry and colleagues, linking Census survey data to death records, found that expansion cut annual mortality among low-income adults aged 55 to 64 by about 9.4 per cent, and estimated that about 15,600 deaths would have been avoided had every state expanded. [cite:ad18_ucd_wherry] The one randomised trial points the other way on health. In Oregon's 2008 Medicaid lottery, coverage produced no measurable change in blood pressure, cholesterol or blood sugar after about two years; it cut depression by 30 per cent, nearly eliminated catastrophic out-of-pocket spending and cut the share borrowing money or skipping other bills to pay for care by 14.2 percentage points. [cite:ad18_ohp_nejm] A critic of the mortality study argues that its channel is unclear, since the effect barely differs between the previously uninsured and the whole sample. [cite:ad18_ohp_bjoerkheim] As of October 2026 the 2025 budget law adds work requirements for expansion adults from 1 January 2027, and the Congressional Budget Office expects about 10 million more uninsured by 2034. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] [cite:us16_kff_obbba]

Building more homes

Minneapolis ended citywide parking minimums, allowed taller apartment buildings along transit and commercial corridors, and allowed two- and three-unit buildings on every residential lot. Between 2017 and 2022 its housing stock grew 12 per cent against 4 per cent in the rest of Minnesota, and its rents rose 1 per cent against 14 per cent; most new units were in large apartment buildings, and the duplex change had a modest effect. [cite:ad18_pew_mpls] Researchers cited in 2026 attributed part of it to weaker demand after the unrest of 2020 and to low interest rates that fed construction, and reported that the period of slow rent growth had ended. [cite:ad18_axios_mpls]

Debt relief and the bankruptcy door

Student debt was made harder to discharge in bankruptcy in steps from 1976 to 2005. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] Under a process the Justice and Education Departments introduced in November 2022, borrowers won full or partial discharge in 98 per cent of the cases courts had decided by March 2024. [cite:ad7_doj_2024] Its limit is scale: 1,220 cases were filed in sixteen months, against 9.3 million borrowers in default in June 2026. [cite:ad7_doj_2024] [cite:us17_ci_defaults] For medical debt, the federal rule to keep it off credit reports was vacated in July 2025; fifteen states and the District of Columbia have their own limits, which the consumer bureau said in October 2025 federal law preempts, leaving the question to the courts. [cite:ad8_nclc_states]

An account at birth

Connecticut's baby bonds programme invests $3,200 for each child born on or after 1 July 2023 whose birth was covered by the state's Medicaid programme. It can be claimed at 18 to 30 for a home, a business, education or retirement in the state, and is projected to grow to $11,000 to $24,000. [cite:ad18_ct_babybonds] The case for the idea rests on simulation: one study found a universal national programme would cut the ratio of young white to young Black median wealth from about 15 to 1 to about 1.6 to 1. [cite:ad18_zewde] The case against its larger claims is arithmetic: the gap in average wealth, driven by the top, runs to trillions, beyond reach of a programme of about $80 billion a year. [cite:ad18_ppp_bruenig] No child can claim before 2041, so there is no evidence of effect yet. The 2025 budget law created a federal cousin of the idea: "Trump Accounts", with a $1,000 federal deposit for each US-citizen child with a Social Security number born from 2025 to 2028, open to contributions from 4 July 2026; by March 2026 more than 4 million children had been signed up. [cite:ad18_audit_irs_trumpacct] It is universal within those birth years rather than targeted at low-income births, and it too has no record of effect yet.

The levers with the firmest evidence act on a specific number: a participation rate, catastrophic bills, a poverty rate. The record of both parties runs through them in both directions. A Democratic Congress expanded the child credit in 2021 and let it lapse; a Republican Congress and president raised the permanent credit in 2025 and tied it to a Social Security number. The 2010 health law expanded coverage and the 2025 budget law is projected to reduce it; federal fee limits were written in 2024 and undone in 2025. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad11_cftd_rp2532] [cite:us16_kff_obbba] [cite:ad9_plaw_119_10] Even well-studied levers can surprise: a 2024 review of some 500 estimates found that, apart from the 1993 reform, expansions of the earned income tax credit show no clear employment effect, against a long-held consensus. [cite:ad11_kleven_eitc]

Interpretation, not finding: the Dream as the story that keeps an extraction system running

The Sophia platform reads the evidence of this volume as a single design. In this reading, the American Dream works as an illusion: a story taught in schools and repeated by presidents of both parties that keeps people working inside a system that extracts from them. The story is told as income and effort, so that a household blames itself when its paycheck falls short, while the returns that build security flow through channels the story does not name: shares bought back with profits, homes priced beyond a year's wage, credit priced at a fifth of the balance a year, a degree financed by a debt that bankruptcy cannot reach, health coverage that ties a worker to a job. Read through this lens, each designed friction in the ledger is a turnstile, each fee a toll, and the belief that hard work suffices is the mechanism that keeps people walking through. The fall in absolute mobility, the parting of pay and productivity, and the record share of wealth at the top are, in this reading, the system working as built.

The strongest counter-readings are documented in the same ledger. First, a design implies a designer, and the record shows many, at different dates, with opposed aims: the Congress that created Social Security and the one that wrote the 401(k); a Congress that expanded the child credit for a year and did not renew it; consumer limits written under one party and undone under the other. A system that cut old-age poverty from 35.2 to 9.9 per cent and halved the uninsured rate is hard to read as built only to extract. [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:us16_census_2025] Second, belief has tracked the record rather than masking it: satisfaction with the chance to get ahead fell from 77 to 52 per cent, only about a third say the Dream still holds, and the claim that the disadvantaged defend the system most did not survive a large test. [cite:ad16_gallup_opp24] [cite:ad16_brookings_opp26] [cite:ad1_apnorc26] [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt] Third, the levels moved up: real median household income was the highest on record in 2025, and relative mobility, the odds of climbing, held steady while the rungs moved apart. [cite:us14_census_story2025] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] Fourth, absolute mobility fell in every rich country where it has been measured, though most in the United States. [cite:ad3_berman] Fifth, some extraction channels shrank without a new law, as overdraft revenue halved through banks' own changes. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] The reading and its answers rest on the same documents; what divides them is whether the pattern is read as intended.

The answer in three registers

The volume asked whether the American Dream is an illusion, a mechanism or a promise. Documented: the Dream is a promise that was kept broadly for the generation born around 1940 and has been kept unevenly since. About half of children born in the early 1980s out-earned their parents; the odds of climbing from the bottom stayed near 8 or 9 in 100 and were a quarter as high for Black children as for white; real incomes and coverage rose while a home came to cost 5.0 years of income against 3.2 and the largest college grant shrank from about three-quarters of the full cost of a public college year to a little over a quarter; Social Security kept its promise and the private pension did not survive as the norm. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:ad7_ace_pell] [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell] [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] Each gap runs through a mechanism with a date and a designer, most enacted in public by Congresses of both parties, a few captured departures documented in federal settlements and investigations. On this book's disclosed scorecard the promises are about half kept, 4.7 out of 10, an editorial synthesis rather than a measured fact.

Probable: the measured gaps owe something to measurement and something to policy. The pay–productivity gap is partly a matter of price indexes and partly a real fall in labour's share; the decline in union power, a wage floor left unchanged since 2009 and the rules on arbitration and bankruptcy probably shifted bargaining toward employers and lenders, though by how much is disputed. [cite:ad4_bls_btn2017] [cite:ad4_qje_farber] [cite:ad15_epi_colvin] The best-evidenced levers would probably move the specific numbers they were measured to move if restored or extended, at costs a reader must weigh. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] [cite:ad18_ohp_nejm]

Not supported: that the Dream was never real; that the whole system was designed as one plan to extract from those who believe in it; that homelessness is maintained on purpose; that medical bills are settled as the leading cause of bankruptcy; and, at the other pole, that staying at the bottom reflects the person and not the system, a view Lincoln stated in 1859, allowing only for "singular misfortune", and one the record of who climbs and who does not cannot sustain. [cite:ad14_ucp_colburn] [cite:us22_dobkin2018] [cite:ad1_lincoln_1859] [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] The Dream is neither an illusion nor a guarantee. It is a promise carried by machinery, and the machinery, unlike the promise, can be read, measured and changed.
