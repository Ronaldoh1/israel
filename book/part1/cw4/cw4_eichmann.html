Eichmann, 1960–1962: The Capture, the Security Council and the Trial as Politics

Less than two months after Ben-Gurion met Adenauer, Israel seized the man whose trial would test that partnership. The trial of Adolf Eichmann is remembered for its evidence: the documents bearing his signature, the survivors who testified, the record of the destruction of European Jewry set out in a Jerusalem courtroom. That evidence is told, with its numbers and its documents, in [scene:rd4_documents|the documents of the Holocaust]. Between May 1960 and June 1962 the case was also an act of state: an abduction from a sovereign country, a dispute before the United Nations Security Council, a set of decisions in Israel's cabinet about how a trial should be run and what it should and should not say, and West German concern over whose officials would be named. How Israel's government handled each of those questions shaped what the trial became.

The familiar account holds that a group of private volunteers found Eichmann and brought him to Israel, and that the world accepted the result. The government's own archive tells it differently. Israel's official position in 1960 was indeed that volunteers had acted. The Israel State Archives now publish the record showing that the Prime Minister authorized the operation and that the state's intelligence service carried it out, and they note that Israel's version was not accepted at the time.

Buenos Aires, 11–20 May 1960. According to History Today, Eichmann was living in Argentina as "Ricardo Klement." On 11 May 1960 a small team of Mossad agents seized him outside his house at 14 Garibaldi Street in Buenos Aires. On 20 May he was flown out on an El Al aircraft, by way of Dakar. [cite:rs_cw4_historytoday_eichmann] The Israel State Archives record the decision behind the operation: Ben-Gurion authorized the head of the Mossad, Isser Harel, to carry out the abduction despite the diplomatic risk. After the aircraft reached Israel, the Mossad handed Eichmann to the Israel Police. Eichmann gave his captors a letter saying that he was ready to stand trial in Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The letter was given while he was held by the people who had seized him, and as evidence of consent it carries that limitation.

The announcement, 23 May 1960. Ben-Gurion told his cabinet first. Asked at the meeting that day how it had been done, he answered: "That is why we have a security service." He refused to name the country. The same day he told a hastily summoned session of the Knesset that the security services had found Eichmann and that he would be tried in Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_kramer_mosaic] [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The phrasing to the Knesset placed the state's services at the center of the capture. The account the government gave abroad two weeks later did not.

The note to Argentina. Israel's ambassador presented a note to the Argentine government, reported on 8 June 1960 in the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung. It said that Eichmann had been taken by Israeli "volunteers" and had "traveled to Israel of his own accord," that the Israeli government had not known he was in Argentina, and it expressed regret if Argentine law or Argentine sovereignty had been violated. [cite:rs_cw4_ghdi_faz] Golda Meir defended the act in a speech in Milwaukee: "I am not claiming that this is a normal situation ... But we are also not dealing with a normal case." [cite:rs_cw4_ghdi_faz] Her sentence conceded the irregularity and argued that the case was exceptional. The note, by contrast, conceded no part for the state in the act.

Argentina goes to the Council. Argentina recalled its ambassador from Tel Aviv and took the matter to the Security Council. It demanded that the Council censure Israel and that Eichmann be returned. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_res138] Israel's reply, in its letter S/4342 of 21 June 1960 and in Meir's statement of 22 June, rested on three points: private volunteers had acted, the matter lay outside the Council's competence, and it should be settled between the two governments. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_res138] The Israel State Archives note simply that Israel's version was not accepted. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] Whether Argentina also expelled Israel's ambassador in this period has not been verified in the sources used for this book; only the recall of Argentina's own ambassador is documented here.

Resolution 138, 23 June 1960. The Council acted at its 868th meeting, on 23 June 1960. The resolution's operative language is precise. It declared that acts such as this one, "which affect the sovereignty of a Member State," may, if repeated, endanger international peace and security. It requested the Government of Israel "to make appropriate reparation in accordance with the Charter of the United Nations and the rules of international law." And it expressed the hope that friendly relations between the two countries would be advanced. [cite:rs_cw4_unsc138] It was adopted by 8 votes to none. Poland and the Soviet Union abstained; Argentina, a party to the dispute, did not vote. [cite:rs_cw4_unsc138]

What "appropriate reparation" meant was left open. Argentina's delegate, Mario Amadeo, declined to say whether it meant Eichmann's return. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_res138] On one reading, the ambiguity let the resolution pass without either government having to accept a defeat: Argentina had a finding that its sovereignty had been affected, and Israel had not been ordered to give up its prisoner.

Whether the Council condemned Israel is itself disputed, and the disagreement turns on what counts as condemnation. The Israel State Archives describe the resolution as condemning Israel, and in substance the case is strong: the Council found that an act affecting a member state's sovereignty had occurred, addressed its request for reparation to Israel alone, and acted despite Israel's argument that the matter lay outside its competence. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The case on the other side rests on the text. Resolution 138 condemns no state by name, speaks of such acts in general terms and of their possible future danger if repeated, and asks for a reparation that the complaining party itself would not define. Argentina had asked for censure and Eichmann's return; the text secured neither in so many words. [cite:rs_cw4_unsc138] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_res138] Both readings are fair to the document; they differ on whether a request for reparation is a condemnation in all but name.

The joint statement, 3 August 1960. The dispute ended where Israel had said it belonged, between the two governments, but on terms that included what Israel's note had avoided. A joint Israeli–Argentine statement of 3 August 1960, drafted with Shabtai Rosenne and containing Israel's apology, acknowledged the violation of Argentine sovereignty and declared the dispute closed. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_res138] [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] Eichmann stayed in Israel.

Preparing the case. Inside Israel the work of building a prosecution began at once. The police set up a special unit, Bureau 06, under Avraham Selinger, to interrogate Eichmann and gather evidence; the work took about nine months. The Attorney-General, Gideon Hausner, led the prosecution. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] On 29 May 1960, less than a week after the announcement, Ben-Gurion set out what the trial was for. It should encompass the whole story of the Holocaust, he said, and it "must be conducted according to all accepted procedures." The archives summarize his principles in four parts: the trial was to be held at all costs; its educational value was to be stressed; Israel was to gain political benefit from it while limiting the risk to its relations with West Germany; and it was to be fair. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] Those four aims did not always point the same way, and much of the trial's politics, as the archives record it, can be read as a trading of one against another.

The German question. The third principle touched the most sensitive question. West German officials hoped that Israel would not use the trial against Germany. Their concern had a name. Hans Globke, Adenauer's chief adviser, had, in the archives' words, helped draft the Nuremberg Laws; East Germany's lawyer, Friedrich Kaul, used him in propaganda against Bonn. Adenauer defended Globke. Ben-Gurion asked Hausner to say "Nazi Germany" rather than "Germany." [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The sources describe Globke's past in slightly different terms: the archives say he helped draft the Nuremberg Laws, while the historian Michael Brenner writes that he wrote commentaries on them. [cite:rs_cw4_ja_brenner]

There was more at stake than words. In the same months, as told in [scene:cw4_europe|Bonn's quiet partnership], West Germany was arranging a secret loan of about $50 million a year to Israel and financing arms. According to the historian Uri Bar-Joseph, Franz Josef Strauss, the West German defence minister, stressed to Israeli officials during the trial that naming senior Bonn officials, especially Globke, would put that arrangement at risk. Bar-Joseph also quotes Shimon Peres as saying that Ben-Gurion linked the Eichmann trial to the Dimona reactor. [cite:rs_cw4_barjoseph] These are a historian's reporting and a participant's recollection, not a document of the time, and they should be read as such. They are consistent with Ben-Gurion's own written principle of limiting the risk to relations with Bonn, and with his instruction to Hausner. What they do not show is that any specific evidence was withheld from the court.

Fairness, decided by vote. The fourth principle was tested in the cabinet. The government voted 6–5 to let two Germans with Nazi pasts testify for the defence with immunity from prosecution. It agreed to pay $20,000 toward the cost of the defence. And it passed a special law allowing a foreign lawyer, Robert Servatius, to appear for Eichmann. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The narrow vote shows how contested the question was: almost half the cabinet would have refused immunity to defence witnesses with Nazi pasts. Concern about how the trial would be seen abroad was recorded as well: in a document on American opinion, Michael Arnon warned against a "show trial." [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann]

Trial, sentence, appeal. The trial opened on 11 April 1961 before a panel headed by Judge Moshe Landau. Sentence of death was passed on 15 December 1961. The Supreme Court rejected the appeal on 29 May 1962. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] What the court heard, and the dates on which the sources differ for the verdict itself, are told in [scene:rd4_documents|the documents of the Holocaust].

Clemency. The last decisions were political as well as legal. The archives record that the letters reaching the authorities showed near-complete public support for the death sentence. A group around Martin Buber and Hugo Bergman dissented: they asked President Ben-Zvi to annul the sentence. Inside the cabinet, the Finance Minister, Levi Eshkol, proposed commuting it to life imprisonment; the government rejected his proposal. Ben-Zvi denied clemency, and Eichmann was executed on the night of 31 May–1 June 1962. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] Buber and Bergman's appeal was a minority position against the near-complete support recorded in the letters, but it was not isolated: a minister in the government that decided the question had proposed sparing Eichmann's life as well.

What the trial changed. Ben-Gurion's second principle, the trial's educational purpose, is the one the archives judge to have been achieved. They record that the trial shifted Israeli attitudes toward the Holocaust. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] The archives' publication does not set out in detail what the earlier attitudes were or how the change was measured, and this book does not supply them. The effect abroad is recorded in a survey: the archives' survey recorded a sharp rise in arrests, trials and extraditions of Nazi criminals in the year after the capture. [cite:rs_cw4_isa_eichmann] An abduction that the Security Council had found to affect a state's sovereignty was followed by an increase in the ordinary legal pursuit of the same class of criminal. The survey as summarized does not break the rise down by country.

The case for Israel's conduct, in its strongest form, is the one Meir gave in Milwaukee: the act was irregular, but the case was not a normal one, and the crimes in question justified an exception. The case against it, as Argentina put it to the Council, is that the act violated Argentine sovereignty; and Israel's account to Argentina of who had acted differs from what its own archive now shows. The Council's resolution and the joint statement of 3 August accepted parts of both: Israel apologized and acknowledged the violation, and Eichmann was tried in Jerusalem.

Several things remain open. Whether Argentina expelled Israel's ambassador, as well as recalling its own, has not been confirmed. Whether West German pressure, or the arrangement with Bonn, affected what the prosecution said about officials of the Federal Republic rests on later recollection and a historian's reconstruction rather than on a document of the time. How Israeli attitudes toward the Holocaust changed, beyond the archives' general statement, is not detailed in the sources used here. And the most famous argument about the trial, Hannah Arendt's and her critics', lies outside the sources of this volume, so it is noted here and not summarized.
