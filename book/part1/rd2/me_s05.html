Kings by Appointment: Cairo, 1921, and the Sharif Who Would Not Sign

By 1921 Britain had to decide who would rule the Arab lands it now held. The wartime promises had been made: to Sharif Hussein of Mecca, to France and to the Zionist movement, as set out in [scene:s06|the three wartime promises]. The peace conference had handed out the mandates. What remained was to choose the men who would sit on the new thrones. The common picture of the region's monarchies is of dynasties that grew from deep local roots and popular choice. The record of 1921 shows something different. Two of the new rulers were chosen at a single British conference in Cairo. The third ruler in the story, Hussein, the father of both, refused the settlement that his sons accepted. By one account, the refusal cost him British support when his kingdom was invaded.

The decision was taken in March 1921. Winston Churchill, the Colonial Secretary, met British officials at Cairo. There they agreed to install Hussein's son Faisal as king of Iraq and another son, Abdullah, as emir of Transjordan. [cite:w_cairo1921] Faisal came to Iraq after losing a kingdom elsewhere. His Arab Kingdom of Syria, proclaimed in Damascus in March 1920, had been defeated by French forces at Maysalun on 24 July 1920. Iraq itself had risen in revolt against Britain in 1920. [cite:w_maysalun] [cite:w_iraq_revolt_1920] Abdullah's emirate, and the way Transjordan was separated from the rest of the Palestine Mandate, are the subject of [scene:s06a2|the separation of Transjordan].

The instrument was simple. A British minister and his officials agreed on names in Cairo, and the names became kings. The populations of Iraq and Transjordan did not elect them. When Iraq gained formal independence in 1932, it was under a treaty that preserved British bases. Across the region, rulers whom Britain had chosen or backed now held most of the thrones.

The same pattern reached beyond the Arab lands. In February 1921, a month before the Cairo meeting, an officer named Reza Khan led the Persian Cossack Brigade into Tehran in a coup that had the support of British officers, among them General Edmund Ironside. He became prime minister, in 1925 was made Shah and founded the Pahlavi dynasty. Iran's parliament survived, but with sharply reduced power. [cite:w_1921_persian_coup] Iran's later history belongs to another volume. Set beside Cairo, the coup can be read as part of the same pattern: much of the region's leadership in these years was installed, not elected.

Faisal's reign in Iraq also opened a chapter in the history of Iraq's Jews that later volumes follow. The mandate-era state founded in 1921 gave Iraq's Jews full citizenship, with the right to vote and to hold office. Jews became prominent in commerce, law, journalism and government service. [cite:ushmm_farhud] [cite:unpacked_farhud] Iraqi Jewish historians remember Faisal's reign as tolerant and call it a "Golden Age." [cite:unpacked_farhud] Iraq's Jewish community was the oldest and, at the time, one of the largest Jewish communities in the Middle East outside Palestine. By the 1940s it numbered roughly 135,000, about 3 percent of Iraq's population, with 90,000 in Baghdad alone. [cite:ushmm_farhud]

Both of the usual summaries flatten that history. One says that Jews and Muslims in Iraq always lived in harmony. The other says the community was persecuted from the start. The record shows a real period of civic inclusion that ended at a datable point. After independence in 1932, Iraqi nationalist sentiment increasingly cast the country's Jews as pro-British and pro-Zionist, running together Jews, Zionism and British influence. That shift laid the ground for what followed, which later volumes take up. [cite:ushmm_farhud] [cite:unpacked_farhud]

The father of the two new rulers would not accept the settlement. In 1919 Sharif Hussein declined to ratify the Treaty of Versailles. He did so in protest at the mandates imposed on Syria, Palestine and Iraq. Faisal had represented him in Paris. [cite:rs_hu_britannica] According to the PBS account of his life, he refused to recognize the Paris settlements at all. [cite:rs_hu_pbs]

Britain then tried to bind him by a separate treaty. The British India Office file that records the effort, IOR/L/PS/10/881, runs from 25 May 1921 to 25 August 1925. It covers two linked questions: the subsidy Britain paid Hussein, and the negotiation of an Anglo-Hejazi treaty. [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f426] T. E. Lawrence negotiated for Britain. On 28 November 1921 he telegraphed the Colonial Secretary with a proposal to revise draft Article 4 of the treaty. The new wording would "clear away all agreements" that Hussein cited but could not produce. [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f230] In December 1921 Lawrence and Emir Abdullah signed a treaty text. On 27 February 1922 Hussein threatened to abdicate. [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f111] [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f230] The Foreign Office considered making two demands of him before he could be invited to Britain: that he ratify the Treaty of Versailles, and that he accept the British treaty. [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f230]

The negotiation dragged on into 1923. On 19 April 1923 Curzon sent Jeddah a revised draft, initialled by himself and by Hussein's agent, Dr Naji al-Asil. It differed from the earlier text chiefly in a new Article 2 and in Articles 3 to 5, which dealt with "the disputed land frontiers of the Hedjaz." [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f111] Britain also changed the money. Instead of a continuing subsidy, it offered Hussein a single final payment of £50,000. [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f111]

Why did Hussein refuse? The British file shows that he refused and what was on the table. It does not, in the material here, give his reason in his own words. Jordan's official Arab Revolt centennial site gives one. It states that he refused the 1921 treaty because it required him to recognize the mandate over Palestine, and with it a Jewish national home. [cite:rs_hu_arabrevolt] That account comes from the Hashemite royal house's own heritage site. No British document in these sources names Palestine as his reason, so the explanation rests on the Jordanian account alone.

The refusal had a cost. According to PBS, his rejection of the Paris settlements and of the Anglo-Hashemite treaty cost him British support when Ibn Saud invaded. [cite:rs_hu_pbs] In March 1924 Hussein proclaimed himself caliph. In September 1924 an Ikhwan attack on Taif caught him unprepared. He abdicated on 5 October 1924. [cite:rs_hu_britannica] After his abdication Britain declined to resume treaty talks with his son King Ali, "so long as present unsettled conditions in the Hejaz continue." [cite:rs_hu_qdl_f426] Hussein lived in exile in Cyprus until 1930 and died in Amman in 1931. The Jordanian site gives the date as 3 June 1931 and says he was buried at the al-Aqsa compound. [cite:rs_hu_britannica] [cite:rs_hu_arabrevolt]

Hussein's refusal bears directly on the old question of whether Britain kept its wartime promises to him. Three positions are on the record, and each has documents behind it.

The first is that the promises were kept. Its strongest witness is Lawrence himself. As Rick Richman has set out in the Jewish Review of Books, Lawrence wrote that the Cairo settlement was "fulfilling (I think) our promises in letter and spirit." In January 1921 he told Churchill that Faisal had agreed to "abandon all claims of his father to Palestine." [cite:rs_hu_jrb_richman_2020] On this reading Britain gave the thrones of Iraq and Transjordan to two of Hussein's sons, and, on Lawrence's account, a Hashemite prince had given up the claim to Palestine. Faisal had also dealt directly with the Zionist movement in 1919, as told in [scene:rd2_faisal|the Faisal–Weizmann Agreement].

The second is that the promises were broken. The Hashemite account says plainly that "the Allies did not keep their promises," and it presents Hussein's refusal to sign a treaty recognizing the Palestine mandate as the expression of that view. [cite:rs_hu_arabrevolt] The Palestine Arab Delegation made the same case on the text of the correspondence. In its reply to the British government in 1922, it argued that McMahon had written "districts," not "vilayet." It argued that the area he reserved was Lebanon, and that "Palestine thus comes within the scope of the promise." [cite:rs_hu_cmd1700]

The third is the British government's official position, stated in the same 1922 parliamentary paper. It held that "the whole of Palestine west of the Jordan was thus excluded from Sir H. McMahon's pledge." [cite:rs_hu_cmd1700] That paper, the Churchill White Paper, is the subject of its own section.

Each position is strongest on its own ground. Lawrence's case rests on what the Hashemites received. Hussein's sons ruled two states, and one of them had, on Lawrence's account, agreed to give up the Palestine claim. The Arab case rests on the wording of the letters and on what the Sharif himself refused to sign. The British case rests on a reading of the wartime correspondence that the Arab Delegation disputed word by word in the same year. Hussein's refusal does not settle what McMahon meant. It does show that the man to whom the promise was made refused to recognize the Paris settlements and never ratified the treaty Britain offered him.

The record leaves several gaps. No British document in this material states Hussein's own reason for refusing, so the Palestine explanation rests on the Jordanian account. The sources here do not give the final fate of Curzon's 1923 draft beyond Hussein's abdication the following year and Britain's refusal to negotiate with his son. Lawrence's report that Faisal agreed to give up his father's claim is Lawrence's own account. Nothing here records Faisal saying so himself. And the meaning of McMahon's "districts" was argued in 1922 by the two sides that had most at stake, and it remains disputed.
