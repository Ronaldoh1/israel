Zip2, X.com and the PayPal Coup

Musk's first fortune came from a company that made online maps and directories for newspapers. His second came from a merger with Peter Thiel's company, which ended with Musk removed as chief executive while he was on a plane. Both episodes matter to this book for the same reason: they set the pattern of the money that later reached politics, and they are the documented origin of the tie between Musk and Thiel that is often described as an alliance. The record of 1995 to 2002 shows a business partnership, a boardroom defeat and a profitable sale. It does not show a shared political project.

Zip2

Musk founded the company in 1995 with his brother Kimbal and Greg Kouri, under the name Global Link Information Network; it became Zip2 in 1996. Its product was city-guide software licensed to newspapers, which let them publish their own online directories and maps. [cite:de11_wiki_zip2] It was this work, by his own 2009 deposition, that he began in August or September 1995 instead of enrolling at Stanford. [cite:de11_wapo_visa]

The venture firm Mohr Davidow invested $3 million in 1996. [cite:de11_wiki_zip2] The investment changed the company's control. Rich Sorkin became chief executive and Musk became chief technology officer, and the board blocked his later attempts to become chief executive. [cite:de11_wiki_zip2] [cite:de11_wiki_musk] The same funding agreement gave the founders 45 days to obtain legal work status, the clause discussed in [scene:de11_pretoria_and_haldeman|the previous section]. [cite:de11_wapo_visa] In April 1998 Zip2 tried to merge with its main competitor, CitySearch; Musk at first supported the deal and then persuaded the board not to proceed, and the companies cited incompatible cultures and technology. [cite:de11_wiki_zip2]

In February 1999 the computer maker Compaq bought Zip2 for cash. Accounts of the price differ slightly, between $305 million and $307 million. Musk received about $22 million for his share of roughly seven per cent. [cite:de11_wiki_zip2] [cite:de11_wiki_musk] He was twenty-seven. The lesson most accounts draw, and the one Musk's later behaviour bears out, is that he had founded a company and lost control of it to investors.

X.com and Confinity

He put much of the money into a new company in 1999, an online bank and payments firm called X.com. By the end of that year its investors had replaced him as chief executive with Bill Harris, the former head of the software company Intuit. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Down the street in Palo Alto, a rival start-up, Confinity, founded by Peter Thiel and Max Levchin, was building a way to send money by email, called PayPal. In March 2000 the two companies merged. The combined company kept the name X.com, and Musk returned as chief executive. [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] [cite:de11_wiki_musk]

The merger did not settle the arguments between the two camps. One was over the name. Musk kept championing X.com, the biographer Ashlee Vance wrote, "while most everyone else favored PayPal." [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] Others were over technology and strategy. In September 2000 the conflict was resolved without him. Musk had married Justine Wilson in January 2000 and delayed their honeymoon; as the couple boarded a flight for a combined fundraising trip and honeymoon, X.com executives delivered letters of no confidence to the board, which removed Musk and installed Thiel as chief executive. [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] The company was renamed PayPal in 2001. [cite:de11_snopes_ouster]

What Musk said about the ouster

He did not fight it. "It's not a good idea to leave the office when there are a lot of major things underway," he said later, and he chose not to contest the decision "rather than fight it at this critical time." [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] In 2007 he told Inc. magazine that he had made peace with the men who removed him: "I buried their hatchet," he said, adding, "Life is too short for long-term grudges." [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] He remained the company's largest shareholder. [cite:de11_wiki_musk]

A recurring question is whether Musk "founded PayPal." Both halves of the usual dispute are true. He founded X.com, the company that legally survived the 2000 merger and later took the PayPal name; the PayPal product, and the name, came from Confinity. [cite:de11_snopes_ouster] [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Calling him a PayPal co-founder is accurate in law and incomplete as history.

The sale to eBay

PayPal went public in 2002, and eBay bought it later that year for about $1.5 billion in stock. eBay's filing records the merger as completed on 3 October 2002, with each PayPal share exchanged for 0.39 of an eBay share. [cite:de11_ebay_8k] Musk, still the largest shareholder at 11.72 per cent, received about $175.8 million. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Thiel's share was smaller; his part of the story is told in [scene:de9_paypal_palantir_facebook|Part Nine].

The PayPal money then went in different directions. Musk founded SpaceX in May 2002 with about $100 million of his own early fortune, and led Tesla's first funding round in 2004. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Several of PayPal's early employees and executives went on to found or fund other companies and, later, to hold political roles; who they are, and where they sit in 2026, is the subject of [scene:de13_who_they_are|Part Thirteen]. PayPal itself followed its own path: owned by eBay from 2002, it "became an independent public company in July 2015," and neither Musk nor Thiel sits on its board in 2026; no current stake in it by either man is documented. [cite:de11_pypl_proxy] In July 2017 he bought back only the x.com web address, saying it had great sentimental value. [cite:de11_domain]

The tie to Thiel, by type

The relationship between Musk and Thiel is often described as a partnership that runs from PayPal to the White House. The documented record supports something narrower, and it can be stated by type of connection. In 2000 the two were merger partners and then boardroom adversaries, one replacing the other. From 2002 they were former co-shareholders of a sold company. In August 2008, after SpaceX's third consecutive rocket failure, Founders Fund, the venture firm Thiel co-founded, invested $20 million in SpaceX, and its managing partner Luke Nosek, another PayPal co-founder, joined the SpaceX board; Musk said he had accepted the money "as a precautionary measure" in case the next flight also failed. [cite:de11_space_ff] That is a fund-level investment, made by a firm in which Thiel was a partner; it is not documented as Thiel's personal money.

What does not appear in that record is a shared politics. In the years of PayPal and its sale, nothing documents a political project between the two men; their later political money, which differs sharply in scale and direction, is set out separately for each, in [scene:de9_money_and_influence|Part Nine] for Thiel and in [scene:de11_money_and_influence|this part's fifth section] for Musk. The ouster of 2000 is better evidence of rivalry than of alliance, and the reconciliation Musk described in 2007 is his own account of a business relationship.
