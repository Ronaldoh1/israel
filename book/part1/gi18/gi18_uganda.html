The Uganda Test, Revisited

The Road to 1948 volume ran a thought experiment it called the Uganda Test: suppose the movement had settled in East Africa or Argentina, and ask which parts of the later structure would have survived the move. Its answer was that the movement's financial and political institutions could have worked anywhere, while the theological layer, the land promise of Genesis and the prophetic reading built on it, could not. [scene:s04fa|The Uganda Test] This section runs the same test on a narrower question, this volume's own: what justifications for a Jewish state would have remained without the biblical claim, and which "Greater" claim could have existed at all. Everything below that goes beyond the documented alternatives is argument, and is marked as such. The documented alternatives come first.

The alternatives on record

The first was in the founding text. In Der Judenstaat, published in February 1896, Theodor Herzl left the territory open: "Shall we choose Palestine or Argentine?" he asked, and answered, "We shall take what is given us." [cite:gi18_pbs_herzl] [cite:fw_gutenberg_judenstaat] His pamphlet insisted on legal sanction from the ruling power rather than gradual settlement without it. [cite:fw_gutenberg_judenstaat] Over the next eight years he sought a charter from the Ottoman sultan, proposed El-Arish in Sinai to the British, and in 1903 received from the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, an offer of land in British East Africa, in what is now Kenya. [cite:gi2_wiki_uganda] [scene:gi2_herzl|Part Two: Herzl's diaries]

The offer came four months after the Kishinev pogrom of 6 and 7 April 1903, in which 49 Jews were killed and more than 500 injured. [cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev] Herzl put it to the Sixth Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1903 as a measure of refuge, telling the delegates that "Zion this certainly is not and can never become." The Congress voted 295 to 178, with 98 abstentions, to send a commission to look at the land, and members of the Russian delegation walked out, arguing that the plan broke the Basel Program's commitment to Palestine. [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_c] [scene:s04e|The offer for East Africa] Herzl died in 1904. The Seventh Congress, in 1905, rejected any national home outside Palestine and its vicinity, and the British offer lapsed. [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_c] [cite:fw_jvl_uganda]

The minority that wanted any safe land did not disappear. Israel Zangwill founded the Jewish Territorial Organization in Basel in July 1905, and it looked for land in Africa, Asia and Australia for twenty years before dissolving in 1925 without founding a lasting settlement. [cite:gi1_jvl_territorialism] [scene:s04f|East Africa rejected] A fourth alternative came from outside the movement altogether. In March 1928 the Soviet government designated Birobidzhan, in the Russian Far East, for Jewish settlement, and on 7 May 1934 made it the Jewish Autonomous Region, with Yiddish as an official language. [cite:gi18_ency_birobidzhan] It never acquired a Jewish majority. In 1939 Jews numbered either 13,291, 18.6 per cent of the population, or "just less than 18,000" of about 109,000, depending on the source; the peak, about 20,000, came at the end of 1948, and by the early 1990s only several thousand remained among more than 200,000 inhabitants. Nikita Khrushchev called the settlement "a failure" in 1958. [cite:gi18_ency_birobidzhan] A fifth alternative was no Jewish state at all: Brit Shalom from 1925, and in 1948 Hannah Arendt and Judah Magnes, argued for a binational arrangement, Arendt for a federation under UN trusteeship. [scene:rd3_brit_shalom|Brit Shalom] [cite:rs_rd5_manne_2026]

Two points of attribution follow from this record. The rejection of East Africa was the act of a named body, the Zionist Congress, led by a named delegation, the Russian Zionists, after a vote in which a majority had first agreed to look; it is not evidence of what Jews as a whole wanted, and the Road to 1948 volume's own account that rests it on "the Jewish masses" goes further than the vote does. [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] [scene:s04fa|The Uganda Test] And the alternatives show that the movement's own leaders treated the territory as a choice for a decade before they closed it.

What would remain without the biblical claim (argument)

Israel's founding declaration of 14 May 1948 grounded the state on four things: "our natural and historic right," the Balfour Declaration, the League of Nations Mandate, and the General Assembly's resolution of 29 November 1947. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] Three later lines of justification are also on record: refuge from persecution, national self-determination, and legal grant by an international authority. The argument that follows sorts them by whether they would have survived a move to East Africa or Argentina.

Refuge would have travelled intact. It was the stated ground of the East Africa offer, made after Kishinev, and in 1945 the Harrison report found most of the Jewish displaced persons in Europe's camps wanting to go to Palestine. [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_harrison] Nothing in the case for refuge names a place. A legal grant would also have travelled: the East Africa offer was a grant by the imperial power, as the Mandate of 1922 was. [cite:gi2_wiki_uganda] [cite:rs_md_avalon] So, on this argument, would its weakness. The League's Covenant said that for the former Ottoman lands "the wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration", and Palestinian Arab representatives rejected the Mandate as incompatible with self-determination. [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] [cite:rs_a22_deblois] Any territory offered by an empire in 1903 had inhabitants who had not been asked, and the consent question argued over in the next section would have followed the grant to Africa. Self-determination travels as a principle but not as a fact. It presupposes a people present in sufficient number to determine anything, and Birobidzhan, a territory designated for Jews that never held a Jewish majority, is the documented case of designation without that presence. [cite:gi18_ency_birobidzhan] The historic and religious claim alone would not have travelled. Herzl said as much of East Africa in 1903: "Zion this certainly is not." [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth]

Which "Greater" claim could have existed (argument)

The whole-land claim, as its own documents state it, is a claim to a named land. The 1977 Likud platform spoke of "the right of the Jewish people to the land of Israel" and of Judea and Samaria; the 2022 guidelines listed the Galilee, the Negev, the Golan and Judea and Samaria. [cite:gi5_jvl_likud1977] [cite:gi5_jl_guidelines] A state in British East Africa or in Argentina would have had no such texts to cite, and on this argument a programme of the West Bank kind, a claim to a heartland by right, could not have arisen there.

A second kind of expansion could have. The Zionist Organization's map of 1919 asked for Mount Hermon because "The Hermon is Palestine's real 'Father of Waters'", a reason of water, not scripture. [cite:gi2_bu_zo1919] The zones Israel has held in Syria and Lebanon since 2024 are justified by its government as security measures: in December 2024 Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar called the move into the Syrian buffer zone "a limited and temporary step we took for security reasons", and in April 2026 he said "Israel has no territorial ambitions in Lebanon." [cite:gi12_olj_saar] [cite:gi9_nahar_saar] [scene:gi9_syria|Part Nine: the Syrian buffer zone] Claims of that kind need no sacred map, and any state at war with its neighbours can make them. Critics answer that a security rationale can outlast its occasion and become a claim in practice; the Israeli government answers that the zones are temporary and tied to threats. Part Nine sets out both. [scene:gi9_lebanon|Part Nine: south Lebanon] The narrow conclusion of the test is that without the biblical claim there would be no whole-land programme, while buffer-zone expansion would remain possible anywhere. The commissioning argument holds for the first kind of "Greater" claim and not for the second.
