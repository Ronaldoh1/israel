The Sinai Test, 1956–1957

Only once between 1949 and 1967 did Israel hold a large area beyond the armistice lines. Its forces took the Gaza Strip and the whole Sinai peninsula in the first days of November 1956 and stayed until March 1957. The full history of the war, including the collusion with Britain and France, the killings in Gaza and the diplomacy, is told in the Cold War volume, starting from [scene:cw3_sevres|the Protocol of Sèvres]. This section asks a narrower question. What did Israel's leaders say they wanted to keep, and what did they keep? The answer matters for this volume because the war produced the phrase most often quoted as proof of a territorial programme, Ben-Gurion's "Third Kingdom of Israel." Where it was actually said needs checking.

The aims at Sèvres

At the secret conference at Sèvres, outside Paris, on 22 October 1956, Ben-Gurion set out a reorganization of the region that he himself called "fantastic," according to the historian Avi Shlaim. Jordan would be divided, Israel extended to the Litani, the Suez Canal internationalized and Nasser removed. At his request an eighth point was added to the draft, seeking "permanent annexation of the entire area east of the El Arish-Abu Ageila, Nakhl-Sharm el-Sheikh" line. Britain and France never backed it, and it is not in the signed protocol. On 24 October he named the interest he did secure: "The Straits of Tiran are the State of Israel's Suez Canal." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] According to the Gush Katif Heritage Center's history, which draws on the journalist Hagai Huberman, the cabinet meeting that approved the operation listed control of the Gaza Strip among its aims; Ben-Gurion had proposed retaking Gaza in March 1955, and the government rejected it. [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza]

"Third Kingdom": where the words were said

The phrase "Third Kingdom of Israel" is often attributed to Ben-Gurion's Knesset speech of 7 November 1956. The English text published by Israel's Foreign Ministry, reproduced by the Jewish Virtual Library, does not contain the words "Third Kingdom," "kingdom" or "Yotvat." It mentions Tiran only as an island that "for centuries" had been "desolate" until Egypt garrisoned it "for the purpose of interfering with Israeli shipping in the Gulf." [cite:gi3_jvl_bg7nov] Its strongest lines were about the armistice: "The armistice agreement with Egypt is dead and buried and cannot be restored to life," and the armistice lines "have no more validity." It said Israeli forces had orders not to cross the Canal "or to attack Egyptian territory proper, and remain entirely within the limits of the Sinai Peninsula," a phrase that set Sinai apart from Egypt proper without saying what Sinai was. It offered Egypt peace "under conditions of direct negotiations." It did not say whether Israel would keep the peninsula or leave it. [cite:gi3_jvl_bg7nov]

The phrase belongs to an earlier text. At a ceremony at Sharm el-Sheikh around 6 November, Moshe Dayan read the troops a letter from Ben-Gurion. According to a 2023 report by the Israeli news site Walla, which quotes the Hebrew, the letter said that Yotvat, the ancient name it gave Tiran, "will return to be part of the Third Kingdom of Israel." [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] Uri Avnery, a journalist and opponent of Ben-Gurion, later recalled the "victory announcement … proclaiming the 'Third Kingdom of Israel'," followed within about a day by a speech announcing that he had agreed to withdraw. [cite:rs_cw3_avnery] No archival copy of the letter was found. The wording is therefore reported, by two sources, and attributed to a letter to soldiers, not to the Knesset speech. It should be quoted that way.

Within a day

Eisenhower wrote to Ben-Gurion on 7 November. He warned that refusing to withdraw "could not but bring about the condemnation of Israel as a violator" and could "impair the friendly cooperation between our two countries." [cite:rs_cw3_app_ike_7nov] Ben-Gurion replied on 8 November that Israel would "willingly withdraw our forces" once satisfactory arrangements had been made with the UN for an international force, and he wrote: "We have never planned to annex the Sinai desert." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_ike_bg] The government announced the same day that "it was finally agreed to accept the United Nations Resolution and withdraw Israel's forces." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] The opposition leader did not accept the turn. On 16 November Begin praised the government's refusal to leave Sinai before Egypt made peace and, in the Begin Center's summary of a press report, said parts of the Land of Israel still needed to be liberated. [cite:rs_cw3_begin_center] The hour-by-hour record is in [scene:cw3_third_kingdom|the Third Kingdom and the retreat].

Gaza: the attempt to stay

The government's acceptance of withdrawal in principle did not settle Gaza. According to Benny Morris, Ben-Gurion told the Knesset on 19 December 1956 that Israel would never let Egypt resume control of the Strip, and argued on 23 January 1957 that "This Strip was never Egyptian." [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] The Gush Katif Heritage Center records a government resolution of 13 January 1957 that "Israel will hold the Gaza Strip" and Knesset approval on 23 January of the decision not to withdraw. [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] Of everything taken in 1956, Gaza, which had been part of Mandate Palestine, was the area Israel's government tried hardest to keep. Sinai, which had not been, it had already agreed in principle to leave. The occupation is told in [scene:cw3_gaza_occupation|Israel's first occupation of Gaza].

Eisenhower's pressure and the withdrawal

On 2 February 1957 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 1124 by 74 votes to 2. It "Deplores the non-compliance of Israel to complete its withdrawal behind the armistice demarcation line." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_1124] On 11 February Washington gave Israel an aide-mémoire. It said withdrawal from Gaza "should be prompt and unconditional," that the Gulf of Aqaba "comprehends international waters," and that the United States was "prepared to exercise the right of free and innocent passage." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_aidememoire] On 20 February Dulles named German reparations, the sale of Israel Bonds and US aid to congressional leaders as possible levers, and several of the leaders were reluctant. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] That evening Eisenhower asked on radio and television: "Should a nation which attacks and occupies foreign territory in the face of United Nations disapproval be allowed to impose conditions on its own withdrawal?" [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57]

On 1 March Golda Meir announced at the UN that "Israel will withdraw from the Gaza Strip and Sharm el-Sheikh." She stated Israel's assumptions, among them that only the UN Emergency Force would take over, and warned that interference with Israeli shipping would be treated as an attack. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] Israel left Gaza on 8 March and Sinai the same month. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] UNEF stayed on Egyptian soil until Nasser ordered it out in May 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Ben-Gurion told the Knesset on 5 March that Israel had gained free passage through Tiran and UN forces at Sharm el-Sheikh and in Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] The historian Uri Bar-Joseph's view is that Israel left without formal security gains, under "brutal" American pressure. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo] Both readings are set out in [scene:cw3_withdrawal|the withdrawal fight].

What the test shows

The documented facts point both ways. On one side are the Sèvres eighth point, the "fantastic" plan reaching to the Litani and a divided Jordan, the "Third Kingdom" letter as reported, the Knesset speech's dismissal of the armistice lines, and four months of effort to keep Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] [cite:gi3_jvl_bg7nov] [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] On the other are the written disavowal of 8 November, the government's war aims of security and navigation, and a full withdrawal by March 1957. It is the first withdrawal in this volume's counter-record, a quarter-century before Israel left Sinai again in 1982. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_ike_bg] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza]

Two readings follow, and both are arguments. On the first, the leader of a Mapai-led government held territorial aims beyond the armistice lines, in Sinai, Gaza and on paper as far as the Litani, and gave them up only under American, Soviet and UN pressure. On the second, the aims that decided the war were Tiran and the fedayeen bases, the letter was victory rhetoric for soldiers, and the speed of the concession shows that annexation was not a programme. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] The test supports a narrower finding than either reading. In 1956 Israel's leaders stated wider claims in private and in victory, and the government did not legislate or keep any of them. The claim to both banks of the Jordan stayed with Herut, which opposed the withdrawal. [cite:rs_cw3_begin_center] The memory of 1957 weighed on Israel's cabinet in June 1967, according to Bar-Joseph. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo] What came after that war is the subject of [scene:gi4_conquest|Part Four], and the 1957 withdrawal returns as the first entry in [scene:gi12_withdrawals|the counter-record].
