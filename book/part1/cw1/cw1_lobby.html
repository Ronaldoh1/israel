The Lobby in Its Cold War Frame, 1950–1954

The Senate fight over Israel's first grant had an organizer behind it, and his path to Washington explains a good deal about the lobby he built. The institutional line of descent, from the wartime American Zionist Emergency Council through the American Zionist Council to the committee later called AIPAC, and the federal foreign-agent order of 1962, is told in Volume II, in [scene:s07g|From AZEC to AIPAC]. This section does not retell it. It follows the man who opened the Washington office, the argument he brought, the campaign in which he first used it, and the three different dates the sources give for what he founded. It also sets beside him two American Jewish positions that did not share his aims, because the lobby was born into a community that was arguing with itself about what it owed the new state.

I. L. Kenen came to Washington from inside the movement and, for a time, from inside the Israeli state. According to his obituary in the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, he directed the lobbying of the American Emergency Committee on Zionist Affairs from 1943; he was the Jewish Agency's information director at the United Nations in 1947–48; and in 1949 he was a member of Israel's first delegation to the UN. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit] Those were three different kinds of employer: an American Zionist organization, the Jewish Agency, and the government of Israel. The distinction mattered later. A Washington lobby run by a man who had recently served on a foreign government's delegation was exactly the arrangement that the foreign-agent questions of the following decade would turn on, as Volume II's account of 1962 shows.

The move to Washington was, by one account, suggested from the Israeli side. According to Israel Hayom's report of research by Kobby Barda, it was Abba Eban, then Israel's ambassador to the UN, who suggested that Kenen go to Washington. [cite:rs_cw1_israelhayom_barda] Kenen brought an argument with him. In the same account, he held that the best route to American aid was to extend the approach of the Marshall Plan to the Middle East, inside a broader framework of aid for refugees. [cite:rs_cw1_israelhayom_barda] The logic was political. A grant for Israel alone would stand as a favour to one state; a grant for refugees, Jewish immigrants in Israel and Arab refugees in the Arab states alike, would stand as a regional programme on the Marshall Plan's model. Both the suggestion and the argument rest on a single reported account, and they should be read as that.

The aid that followed did take something like that shape. The fiscal 1953 request described Israel's money as aid for the relief and resettlement of Jewish immigrants and set it beside money for Arab refugees, as told in [scene:cw1_grant_aid|the first grant]. The record cited here does not show that Kenen's argument produced that design. The State Department had its own reasons for a regional programme, and its papers show it resisting the higher figures Israel's supporters pressed. What can be said is that the lobbyist's argument and the eventual form of the aid pointed the same way.

The first test was the Senate, in August 1951. Kenen's letter from Washington to Abba Hillel Silver, the leader who had built the wartime council, reports the floor work on the aid amendment and names those involved: Senators Douglas, Taft and Brewster, and the lobbyist Julius Klein. [cite:rs_cw1_kenen_silver_letter] The amendment raised Israel's figure, and Kenen reminded Silver that "all this is still an authorization." [cite:rs_cw1_kenen_silver_letter] The language in which the bill had been introduced that spring placed the effort in the Cold War. Its Senate sponsors, a Democrat and a Republican, called Israel "a bulwark in that area of world democracy" against Communist threats, "particularly in Iran." [cite:rs_cw1_jta_douglas_taft_1951] The lobby's first campaign thus ran on two arguments at once: a refugee argument about immigrants and absorption, and a strategic argument about the Soviet Union. The letter names Klein, but the record cited here does not describe what he did, or for whom.

When the lobby itself was founded, and under what name, is a question the sources answer in three ways. Kenen's JTA obituary says he moved to Washington in 1951 and set up the American Zionist Committee "to lobby Congress in support of Israel", and that the committee became AIPAC in 1954. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit] The Jewish Virtual Library dates AIPAC's beginnings to 1951, but inside the American Zionist Council. It says the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs was set up as a separate lobbying body in 1954, because the Council's non-profit status limited how much lobbying it could do, and it dates the name American Israel Public Affairs Committee to 1959. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_aipac] Volume II, drawing on Kenen's papers and other references, dates the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs to 1953–54, describes it as a new body that was not tax-exempt, and also gives 1959 for the change of name. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:wiki_american_zionist_council] [cite:britannica_aipac]

Read closely, the three accounts disagree less than they first appear to, because they date different things. All agree that Kenen's lobbying in Washington began in 1951. They differ on whether, in that year, it was his own committee (the obituary) or work carried on within the American Zionist Council (the Jewish Virtual Library). All three place the creation of a separate lobbying committee in 1953 or 1954. The real conflict is over the name: the obituary says the committee became AIPAC in 1954, the other two that it took that name in 1959. One reading, which is this book's and not a finding of the sources, is that the obituary applied the later name to the committee created in 1954. The sources also give two reasons for the separation, and these do not conflict either. The Jewish Virtual Library gives the limit that non-profit status placed on lobbying. Volume II's sources give a fear that the Eisenhower administration suspected Israel of funding the American Zionist Council and might force it to register as a foreign agent. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_aipac] [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:wiki_american_zionist_council] Both reasons may have been at work. The record cited here does not say which weighed more.

The sources say little about the committee's size or budget. One detail of scale survives: the obituary records that in 1957 Kenen used a $700 speaking honorarium to start a weekly, the Near East Report. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit]

American Jewish opinion was not of one mind about any of this, and two positions from these years show the range. The first was a settlement of terms between Israel's government and an American Jewish organization that did not speak for it. On 23 August 1950, at a luncheon in Jerusalem, Ben-Gurion and Jacob Blaustein of the American Jewish Committee exchanged statements. Israel recognized that American Jews owed their allegiance to the United States, and each side undertook not to speak on behalf of the other. [cite:rs_cw1_jmm_blaustein] The statement drew a line through the middle of the question that a Washington lobby for Israel would always face: whose interest it represented. Blaustein himself was no bystander to the aid effort. The following year he asked the State Department for $75 million each for Israel and the Arab states, and was turned down. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_feinberg_memo] Support for aid to Israel and a public insistence that American Jews' allegiance lay with the United States were, for him, compatible positions.

The second position was opposition. The American Council for Judaism, whose first president was Lessing Rosenwald and whose chief spokesman was Elmer Berger, had opposed the establishment of Israel, and after 1948 it remained critical of what it called "the Israel-Zionist domination of American Jewish life." It claimed 20,000 members, and by the end of 1955 it had set up ten schools teaching the positions of classical Reform Judaism. [cite:rs_cw1_ej_acj] Its case, in its own terms, was that American Jewish life should not be organized around the state of Israel or the Zionist movement. It is a reminder that the lobby Kenen built spoke for a movement within American Jewry, not for American Jews as a whole, and that some of its critics in these years were themselves Jewish organizations.

One more date needs care. The committee's founding in 1953–54 falls in the same year as the Anglo-American coup in Iran, and elsewhere in this library the two are paired in one account, [scene:s11|the coup of 1953]. That account states its own limit plainly: the shared year is a coincidence of timing, and no documented operational connection links the founding of the lobby to the overthrow of Mossadeq. The sources cited in this section add nothing that would change that. The only Iranian thread in them is rhetorical: the Senate sponsors' phrase of 1951 about Communist threats "particularly in Iran." [cite:rs_cw1_jta_douglas_taft_1951]

The gaps are several, and they are worth stating because the founding of this lobby is so often told with more confidence than the sources allow. The record cited here does not give the founding document of either the 1951 operation or the separate committee. It does not say who paid Kenen's salary or the committee's costs between 1951 and 1954, which is the question the foreign-agent inquiry of 1962 would later put to the American Zionist Council. Eban's suggestion that Kenen go to Washington, and the refugee argument Kenen carried there, rest on one reported account of one scholar's research. Klein is named but not described. And the sources disagree on the year the name AIPAC was first used, a disagreement this book has set out rather than resolved.
