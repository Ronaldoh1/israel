The Programme and the State

This part tests an argument put to the volume by its commissioning editor: that "Greater Israel" as a state programme presupposes 1948, and that had the state not been founded as it was, there would be no programme to document. The argument is about cause, and no document can prove a cause in a history that did not happen. What the record can do is test its parts. Three are taken in turn here: whether the programmes documented in Parts Four to Six were in fact carried by the institutions of a state; whether the idea of a larger land existed before the state, and so cannot have been produced by it; and how far the founding of 1948 itself was settled in advance or turned on votes and battles that could have gone otherwise. The first two are matters of record. The third mixes record with argument, and the argument is labelled where it begins.

Every documented programme ran through the state

The territorial programmes this volume has documented were each enacted, adopted or carried out by a state body. The conquest of June 1967, which put the West Bank, Gaza, Sinai and the Golan in Israel's hands, was the work of its army, and the government's first decisions on what to do with them were cabinet votes: on 19 June 1967, by ten votes to nine, to offer Egypt and Syria peace on the international border, and not to decide on the West Bank. [cite:gi4_cie_june19] The Movement for the Whole Land of Israel, which published its manifesto on 22 September 1967, was a private body, but its premise was a fact the state had made: "The whole of Eretz Yisrael is now in the hands of the Jewish people." [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:gi4_972_twoads] Gush Emunim, founded in 1974, was extra-parliamentary by design, yet the permanent incorporation it sought could be granted only by a government, and from 1977 it was one, Menachem Begin's, that made settlement in the hill country policy. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:gi5_jvl_likud1977] [scene:gi5_begin|Part Five: Begin and "Judea and Samaria"]

The later steps are all acts of the Knesset, the cabinet or a minister. The Golan Heights Law of 14 December 1981 passed the Knesset 63 to 21. [cite:gi5_wiki_golanlaw] The Nation-State Law of 19 July 2018, which named Jewish settlement a national value, passed 62 to 55. [cite:gi5_toi_nslaw] The guidelines of the government sworn in on 29 December 2022 claimed "an exclusive and inalienable right to all parts of the Land of Israel." [cite:gi5_jl_guidelines] On 23 July 2025 the Knesset voted 71 to 13 for a non-binding motion to apply sovereignty to the West Bank, and on 3 September 2025 Bezalel Smotrich, as finance minister and minister in the Defence Ministry, presented a map for annexing 82 per cent of it. [cite:wb_toi_71_13] [cite:gi6_toi_82pct] [scene:gi6_plans|Part Six: the annexation track] On this first test the argument holds without qualification: there is no documented Greater Israel programme in this volume that was not carried by a state's legislature, army or ministries, because only a state has those instruments.

The idea came first

The second test cuts the other way. The idea of a land larger than any partition existed long before 1948. On 3 February 1919 the Zionist Organization, the movement's mainstream body, asked the Paris Peace Conference for a territory running from south of Sidon over Mount Hermon to a line west of the Hejaz Railway, east of the Jordan, justified chiefly by water. [cite:gi2_bu_zo1919] [scene:gi2_map1919|Part Two: the 1919 map] In 1925 Ze'ev Jabotinsky's Revisionists made a Jewish majority "on both sides of the Jordan" their programme. [cite:gi2_ej_revisionists] [scene:gi2_bothbanks|Part Two: both banks] The idea outlived the founding unchanged: Herut, the Revisionists' party, held until 1965 that the state "should contain both banks of the River Jordan", and won 14 of 120 seats in the first Knesset. [cite:gi3_jvl_herut] The record therefore separates two things the argument joins. The idea did not depend on the state; it was older. What depended on the state was the programme, meaning a claim with an army, a budget and a legislature behind it.

Even the state was not enough by itself. Between 1949 and 1967 no Israeli government adopted the claim to both banks or to the West Bank; it remained an opposition party's platform, and the armistice lines were treated by successive governments as frontiers to be held, not moved. [cite:gi3_jvl_herut] [scene:gi3_borders|Part Three: the armistice lines] The documented whole-land programme begins after the conquest of 1967. A precise form of the argument would therefore say that the programme presupposes 1948 and also 1967: a state, and then a war that put the land in that state's hands.

How contingent was 1948?

The founding rested on a recommendation of the UN General Assembly, and that recommendation needed a two-thirds majority of the states voting yes or no; abstentions and absentees did not count. [cite:gi18_shindler_1947] In the Assembly's Ad Hoc Committee on 25 November 1947 the partition plan passed 25 to 13 with 17 abstentions, a majority short of two-thirds. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] [cite:gi18_shindler_1947] The plenary vote was put off by three days, which the plan's supporters used to lobby the abstainers, and on 29 November it passed 33 to 13 with 10 abstentions. [cite:gi18_shindler_1947] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] On those figures, two-thirds of the 46 states voting was 31 votes; had three of the 33 voted no instead, the result would have been 30 to 16, short of two-thirds, and the plan would have failed. Had they abstained instead, it would still have passed. Both superpowers voted in favour, the United States and the Soviet Union alike, and the Soviet bloc's votes were among the 33. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] [scene:so_s01|Moscow said yes first] On 24 November, the day before the committee's vote on the plan, a proposal to ask the International Court of Justice whether the UN could enforce or recommend a partition adopted without the consent of Palestine's inhabitants had failed in committee by a single vote, 20 to 21, with 13 abstentions. [cite:gi18_un_gapal85] [cite:gi18_wiki_adhoc] The historian Colin Shindler called the result "a close-run thing." [cite:gi18_shindler_1947]

The vote did not settle the matter. On 19 March 1948 the United States told the Security Council that partition "cannot now be implemented by peaceful means" and proposed a temporary trusteeship instead. [cite:rs_rd5_avalon_austin] [scene:rd5_trusteeship|Washington retreats, March 1948] The state was declared on 14 May 1948, and on 15 May the Arab League told the UN that its members were intervening in Palestine; the armies of Egypt, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq invaded, and Transjordan's forces entered the areas allotted to the Arab state and Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd5_un_s745] [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] The borders Israel held after the armistices of 1949 were set by that war, not by the resolution. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] [scene:rd6_armistices|The armistices of 1949]

From here the reasoning is argument, and there are two readings. On the first, the closeness of the vote, the American retreat and the uncertainty of the war show that the state, and with it any later programme, could easily not have existed; nothing in the 1919 map or the Revisionist platform could have become law without it. On the second, the vote mattered less than it seems. The Jewish Agency's leadership had already refused trusteeship outright: on 20 March 1948 Ben-Gurion said "We shall not agree to any kind of trusteeship, whether permanent or temporary." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_trusteeship] On that reading a state would probably have been declared and fought for whatever the Assembly did, and its borders would still have been set by war. Neither reading can be proved. What both accept is the narrower finding of this section: the idea of a larger land predates the state, and the programme does not.
