Pollard, Kadish and the AIPAC Case, 1985–2020

Three FBI cases that reached American courts after 1980 form the core of the public record of Israeli intelligence collection inside the United States, and each ended differently: a life sentence, a fine, and dropped charges. A fourth, often counted with them, was not an Israeli operation at all. This section sets out each case as the court record and the agencies' own documents describe it, beside the explanation the Israeli government or the defendants gave.

Pollard. Jonathan Pollard, a civilian analyst working for the US Navy, first offered to supply classified documents at a meeting with an Israeli Air Force officer, Aviem Sella, in late May 1984, and spied for Israel through 1984 and 1985. [cite:ow5_nsa_pollard] FBI agents arrested him on 21 November 1985 outside the Israeli embassy in Washington, where he had tried to seek asylum, and he was arraigned six days later on espionage charges as a paid agent of Israel. [cite:ow5_merip] The operation was run not by the Mossad but by LAKAM, a scientific-liaison bureau of the Israeli defence establishment then headed by Rafi Eitan. [cite:ow5_merip] [cite:ow5_nsa_pollard] Pollard pleaded guilty and was sentenced to life imprisonment in 1987. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp]

Israel's first explanation was that the operation had been unauthorized, run by a small group of officials without the government's knowledge; some officials in the Reagan administration doubted this, citing court documents that showed an organized, well-financed ring involving Israeli officers and embassy staff. [cite:ow5_csm1986] In 1987 Israel formally apologized and pledged that it would not spy on American soil. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The CIA's damage assessment of October 1987 found that Pollard had passed about 800 documents and some 1,500 intelligence summaries, and that his Israeli taskings concerned Arab states, Pakistan and the Soviet Union; the Israelis "never expressed interest in US military activities, plans, capabilities, or equipment." [cite:ow5_nsa_pollard] It also recorded that his handler told him to ignore a request from a superior for "dirt" on senior Israeli officials. [cite:ow5_jp_pollard] The American objection, in the assessment and among officials, was to the act itself: an ally running a human penetration of the US government. [cite:ow5_jp_pollard]

Pollard was paroled in 2015, under conditions that included a ban on travel abroad, and was released from parole in November 2020. [cite:ow5_nsa_pollard] He then moved to Israel, where Prime Minister Netanyahu welcomed and personally thanked him. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The arc, from an "unauthorized" operation and a formal apology in 1987 to a prime minister's welcome in 2020, is the clearest documented case in which Israel's public position on an espionage case changed over time.

Kadish. The second case concerned the same years but surfaced two decades later. Ben-Ami Kadish, an engineer at the Army's Picatinny Arsenal in New Jersey, admitted passing classified material to an Israeli agent between 1979 and 1985. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] In 2009, at the age of 85, he was fined under a plea agreement and given no prison sentence. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] Two points about the case are commonly misstated. The disclosures took place in the 1980s, before Pollard's arrest, not in the years after Israel's 1987 pledge. And the two men were not convicted of the same offence: Pollard pleaded guilty to espionage, while Kadish's case ended in a plea agreement whose result was a fine. The public record gives the outcomes, not the prosecutors' reasons for the difference.

The AIPAC case. The third case reached the most prominent pro-Israel organization in Washington. In 2004 the FBI's investigation of Lawrence Franklin, an analyst in the Defense Department, became public. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] Franklin pleaded guilty in 2005 to passing classified information, some of it to two senior staff of the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, Steven Rosen and Keith Weissman, and was sentenced to about 12 years, later reduced. [cite:pm_wiki_franklin] [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] AIPAC dismissed Rosen and Weissman in April 2005; they were indicted under the Espionage Act for receiving and passing on national defence information. [cite:pm_wiki_franklin] In 2009 the government dropped all charges against them, citing court rulings that made conviction unlikely and its unwillingness to expose classified information at trial. [cite:pm_wiki_franklin] Rosen and Weissman were never convicted of anything, and the case is not evidence of an espionage network; the questions it raised about AIPAC's legal status are taken up in [scene:ow2_lobby_law|the lobby and the law].

The case that was not Israeli. The arrest in October 2009 of Stewart Nozette, a former government scientist who gave classified information to a man he believed was an Israeli intelligence officer, is sometimes listed among Israeli cases. The man was an undercover FBI agent, and the Justice Department's charging documents state that they do not allege that the government of Israel or anyone acting on its behalf violated American law. [cite:csmonitor_nozette] [cite:justice_nozette] Nozette pleaded guilty to attempted espionage and was sentenced to 13 years. [cite:justice_nozette] The case documents the FBI's counterintelligence methods, not an Israeli operation.

What the cases show about the mechanism. In each case that reached court, the FBI's tools were the ordinary ones of criminal law: surveillance, arrest and prosecution of an American citizen. None of them reached an Israeli official. The scientific-liaison bureau that ran Pollard was a foreign government body, and the American courts had no jurisdiction over its officers; the remedy lay in diplomacy, which produced the apology of 1987. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] Where the person receiving information was not a government employee, as in the AIPAC case, the Espionage Act proved hard to apply. [cite:pm_wiki_franklin]

What the record does not settle. Who in the Israeli government authorized the Pollard operation has not been established in any public American or Israeli document. Kadish's handler, and how long the contact continued, are described in reporting that this book has not been able to recheck. And the full FBI files on all three cases remain unpublished.
