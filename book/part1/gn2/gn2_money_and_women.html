Money, Property and Women: What the Opponents Said, and Why

The opponents of the Gnostics wrote a great deal about two subjects: money and women. Their accusations are the most colourful parts of the heresy-hunting literature, and they are the least reliable. They are worth reading closely all the same, because they show what the bishops feared most, and because one Gnostic text on property survives in its author's own words.

Irenaeus's longest attack is on a Valentinian teacher named Marcus. Marcus, he says, gave women a direct role in worship: he had them consecrate cups of mixed wine and told a woman, "Open your mouth, and you shall prophesy". [cite:vt24_ah1_13] Then comes the charge. Marcus devoted himself especially to women "of great wealth", Irenaeus writes, who gave him their possessions, so that he "collected a very large fortune"; some later confessed that they had been "defiled by him". [cite:vt24_ah1_13] Irenaeus was Marcus's competitor for the loyalty of Christians, and nothing in the record corroborates the charges. They are an opponent's allegations and should be read as such. They also reveal a structure. In the churches Irenaeus led, offerings went to the bishop to distribute; a wealthy woman who gave her property to an independent teacher was taking her money, and her influence, out of the bishop's hands. [cite:vt24_justin67]

The prophesying is the other half of the fear. Several Gnostic groups allowed women to prophesy, teach or preside, as Marcus did, and some of their texts give Mary Magdalene a place among the disciples that the developing church denied her. [scene:vt26_gospel_of_mary|The Gospel of Mary] The bishops' churches were moving the other way, toward an all-male office. The accusations of sexual misconduct that run through the heresy-hunters' books attached themselves most readily to exactly those groups where women held roles the church was closing.

The one Gnostic text on property that survives in its author's words points in a different direction from the charge of greed. Clement of Alexandria, in the third book of his Miscellanies, quotes from a work called On Righteousness by Epiphanes, who he says was the son of the teacher Carpocrates and died at seventeen. It opens with a definition: "The righteousness of God is a kind of sharing along with equality." [cite:gn_wiki_epiphanes] The text promotes communal living, with property held in common, and some scholars link the idea to Plato's Republic. It extended the sharing to spouses as well as goods, and he and later writers made that the centre of their attack. [cite:gn_wiki_epiphanes] Some scholars doubt the story of the author: G. R. S. Mead thought Epiphanes might be legendary, and Roel van den Broek argues that the text is not Gnostic at all, since it treats the material world as good. [cite:gn_wiki_epiphanes]

The Carpocratians also bring the story to Rome. Irenaeus reports that they called themselves Gnostics, that a woman of their school named Marcellina came to Rome in the time of Bishop Anicetus, and that they kept painted images of Christ and crowned them together with images of Pythagoras, Plato and Aristotle. [cite:vt24_ah1_25] The detail places a woman teacher at the centre of the Roman church's first recorded contest with a Gnostic school, and it shows a Christianity that treated Jesus as one of the great teachers of humanity, honoured alongside the philosophers.

Put together, these fragments describe a conflict over resources as well as doctrine. The bishops' churches gathered offerings into a common fund under the bishop, from which, as Justin wrote, the orphans, widows and strangers were helped. [cite:vt24_justin67] The Gnostic schools drew on the support of individual patrons, some of them wealthy women, and at least one text of theirs preached property held in common. Each model put money and influence in different hands. The bishops' model won, and the accounts of the losing model that survive are almost all written by the winners.
