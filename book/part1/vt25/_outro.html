Part Twenty-Five established four things. First, the Nag Hammadi codices were bound after 348, buried by unknown hands, found in December 1945 and kept in the Coptic Museum in Cairo. The burning of some leaves by the finder's mother rests on James Robinson's interviews of the 1970s, which Mark Goodacre has shown to be unstable, and the monastic and grave-goods explanations of the burial both remain hypotheses. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_goodacre2013] [cite:vt25_lj2015] [cite:vt25_lewisblount2014] Second, the texts do teach what the "kingdom within" reading says they teach, in places: Thomas puts the kingdom "inside of you" and rejects fasting and prayer, and the Apocalypse of Peter calls self-appointed bishops "dry canals". But the library is mixed. Philip and the Valentinian Exposition are sacramental, Thomas the Contender and the Testimony of Truth are rigorist, and Plato, Hermes and Sextus sit beside Sethian myth. [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] [cite:vt25_wiki_gapocpeter] [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] [cite:vt25_wiki_testtruth] Third, more than half the tractates conflicted with the creed of 325 or with Irenaeus's four-gospel rule. [cite:vt25_nicaea_creed] [cite:vt25_iren_311] Fourth, the recorded condemnations came from bishops and heresy writers from Lyon to Jerusalem, and in two cases from pagan philosophers, and took the form of warnings, exclusions and anathemas, not burning orders. [cite:vt25_iren_129] [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] [cite:vt25_decretum] [cite:vt25_bmcr_plotinus] Part Twenty-Six goes outside the jar to three texts found in other manuscripts: the Gospel of Mary, the Gospel of Judas and the Pistis Sophia.
