The Electricity Monopoly: Rutenberg's Concession

On 12 September 1921 the Mandate government, the British administration of Palestine, signed the Auja, or Yarkon, Concession. It granted Pinhas Rutenberg's Jaffa Electric Company the exclusive right, for seventy years, to generate, distribute and sell electricity in the Jaffa district, using hydroelectric turbines on the Auja river [cite:wiki_israel_electric]. The Mandate itself contained a related provision: Article 11 allowed the administration to arrange with the Jewish Agency to build or operate public works [cite:zaoerv_mandate_article11]. Development under the Mandate is often presented as neutral modernization that benefited everyone. The story of the electricity supply suggests otherwise. Infrastructure, and not only land and arms, helped build the pre-state economy, and the grid that grew from this concession followed the map of Jewish settlement.

The concession had powerful backers and determined opponents. The High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel, and the Colonial Secretary, Winston Churchill, supported Rutenberg. In 1922 the House of Lords passed an amendment to stop the concession. The House of Commons overturned the Lords by a large majority, in a vote led by Churchill [cite:jc_turnberg_rutenberg]. The challenge did not come only from Westminster. A rival Greek concessionaire, Euripides Mavromatis, contested the monopoly before the Permanent Court of International Justice.

What was promised was not what was built. The hydroelectric station on the Auja never materialized. The grid that switched on in 1923 ran on diesel generators in Tel Aviv. And the irrigation rights Rutenberg had obtained in the Auja valley ended up benefiting only Jewish settlers [cite:jstor_electrifying_conflict] [cite:military_wiki_rutenberg]. A concession justified by a river had become a diesel network and a water right for one community.

From there Rutenberg's enterprise grew. In 1923 he founded the Palestine Electric Corporation. It built the country's only hydroelectric plant, at Naharayim on the Jordan, and in 1961 it was renamed the Israel Electric Corporation [cite:wiki_israel_electric]. The company that had held the Mandate's electricity monopoly now carried the new state's name.

Historians have looked closely at whom the grid served. Fredrik Meiton's prize-winning study, Electrical Palestine, finds that the centralized grid knit Jewish settlements together while marginalizing and bypassing most Palestinian Arab communities [cite:mesa_meiton_award]. Another historian writes that the economic separatism of the Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine, "was given a tremendous push" by the 1921 monopoly. These are historians' judgments about the grid's effects, drawn from the record; they stand alongside the documented facts of the concession itself.

Set beside the other institutions of the period, the electricity concession fills in a layer that is easy to overlook. The Histadrut, described in [scene:hl_s01|the Histadrut and Hebrew labor], organized a separate labor market with its own enterprises. The concession supplied something more basic: power, carried along lines that linked Jewish settlements to one another. A monopoly granted by the Mandatory power, and defended in Parliament by Churchill, produced a grid that followed Jewish settlement. It was economic separation built in copper and diesel, decades before any partition line was drawn.
