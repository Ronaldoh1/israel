From Redlining to the Suburbs: How the Federal Government Built the Owner's Dream

The home is the part of the American Dream that the federal government built most directly. Before the 1930s, owning a house was something a household did largely on its own terms and with private lenders, and fewer than half of American households managed it. Within a generation the share had risen past three in five, and the change was not an accident of prosperity: it followed a set of federal laws, agencies and lending rules written between 1933 and 1956. The same rules decided who could use them. This section follows both halves of that record, the design that worked and the exclusions written into it, because the taught version of the postwar suburb usually tells only the first.

What a mortgage looked like before the New Deal

A mortgage is a loan secured on a home: if the borrower stops paying, the lender can take the house. Before the Great Depression, American home loans usually ran for five to ten years, carried interest rates that could change, and were rarely larger than half the value of the house. Most did not amortise, meaning the monthly payments covered interest but not the principal; the whole balance fell due at the end, and the borrower had to pay it off or find a lender willing to roll it over. Homeowners typically renegotiated every year. [cite:ad6_greenwachter] When the banking system seized up after 1929, lenders stopped rolling loans over. Foreclosures ran at about 250,000 a year from 1931 to 1935, and at the worst point of the Depression nearly one home in ten was in foreclosure. [cite:ad6_greenwachter]

Congress answered with two agencies. The Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC), created in 1933, bought defaulted mortgages from lenders and rewrote about one million of them as fixed-rate, 20-year loans that paid themselves off in equal monthly instalments. The Federal Housing Administration (FHA), created by the National Housing Act of 1934, did not lend at all; it insured private lenders against loss on loans that met its standards, at first up to 20 years and 80 per cent of the home's value. [cite:ad6_greenwachter] [cite:ad6_prrac_timeline] The limits were then stretched in steps. In 1948 the maximum FHA term rose to 30 years, and in 1956 the maximum loan rose to 95 per cent of value for new homes and 90 per cent for existing ones. [cite:ad6_greenwachter] The Housing Act of 1954 had already cut FHA down payments, which had been at least 20 per cent from 1947, to between 5 and 16 per cent depending on the size of the loan. [cite:ad6_fed_frydman]

This is the part of the record where the promise as taught holds. The long, fixed-rate, self-amortising loan turned a house from something bought with savings into something bought with income, paid down over a working life. Homeownership rose from 43.6 per cent of households in 1940 to 64 per cent by 1980. [cite:ad6_greenwachter] Among non-farm households the rate went from 41 per cent in 1940 to 61 per cent in 1960, with about 60 per cent of that rise completed by 1947; veterans' loans, available from 1944, are estimated to have raised the national rate by about 7 per cent on their own. [cite:ad6_fed_frydman] The 30-year mortgage was a deliberate public design, it was meant to spread ownership, and it did. It remains the standard American home loan today.

Who the design left out

The same agencies that built the new mortgage also decided where it could go. Between 1935 and 1940 HOLC surveyors drew "residential security" maps of American cities, grading neighbourhoods from A, coloured green, to D, coloured red. [cite:ad6_mapping_ineq] These maps are the most reproduced image of "redlining". The FHA wrote the rules down in its own manual. Its 1938 Underwriting Manual, the guide its appraisers used to decide which loans to insure, listed among the protections it recommended for a neighbourhood the "prohibition of the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are intended", and said local schools "should not be attended in large numbers by inharmonious racial groups". [cite:ad6_fha_manual] A racially restrictive covenant is a clause in a deed or lease barring sale or occupancy to people of a named race; the FHA treated such covenants as a feature that made a loan safer.

The economic literature now disputes which agency did the most damage. A team led by economists Daniel Aaronson, Daniel Hartley and Bhashkar Mazumder at the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago compared blocks on either side of HOLC grade boundaries and found that the lower-graded side had lower homeownership, house values and rents in later decades, and became more heavily Black; the gaps grew until about 1970 or 1980 and then narrowed, and along the boundary between C and D areas the homeownership gap fell after 1990. [cite:ad6_ahm_holc] A second team, Price Fishback, Jonathan Rose, Kenneth Snowden and Thomas Storrs, points out that HOLC had made 90 per cent of its loans before its map project began in 1935, judges it "very unlikely" that the maps guided lending, and concludes that the red lines drawn by the FHA "were likely far more impactful". The FHA, they find, redlined core urban neighbourhoods "from day one"; in Baltimore from 1935 to 1940 it insured loans for only about 25 Black households, while HOLC had lent to hundreds. [cite:ad6_chifed_rose] Both findings describe exclusion by federal agencies; they differ on which agency's lines did the work. The difference matters for anyone using the HOLC maps as a stand-in for the whole system, because the maps may not have been its main instrument.

The GI Bill and Levittown

The Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944, the GI Bill, offered returning veterans guaranteed loans for homes, farms and businesses, and paid for college or training. Its text did not mention race. Its administration was local: banks made the loans, and state and local offices handled much of the rest. The political scientist Ira Katznelson argues that Southern members of Congress, who chaired key committees, shaped the law so that local control could preserve the racial order of the region. His best-known figure comes from a 1947 survey by Ebony magazine of thirteen Mississippi cities: of 3,229 home, business and farm loans guaranteed under the GI Bill there that year, two went to Black veterans. [cite:ad6_katznelson] [cite:ad6_snopes_gi] The education record is more mixed, and the strongest study of it supports Katznelson for the South only. Economists Sarah Turner and John Bound found that the GI Bill raised college attainment for Black men born outside the South by about 0.4 years, comparable to the gain for white veterans, but produced few gains for Black men from the South, where the bill widened the gap with white veterans. [cite:ad6_turnerbound]

Levittown, on Long Island, is the postwar suburb the textbooks use as the picture of the Dream, and its record shows both halves of the design at once. Levitt & Sons began building in 1947 and had about 17,447 homes in the area by 1951; houses sold from about $8,000, and with federal backing many buyers needed about $400 up front. [cite:ad6_wiki_levittown] The standard lease, clause 25, required that no home "be used or occupied by any person other than members of the Caucasian race". [cite:ad6_nmaahc_levitt] [cite:ad6_wiki_levittown] In 1948 the Supreme Court ruled in Shelley v. Kraemer that courts could not enforce such covenants, but the ruling did not stop private sellers and agents from refusing to sell, and the area remained almost entirely white for decades. [cite:ad6_prrac_timeline] [cite:ad6_wiki_levittown]

Federal design also shaped who gained within white America. Economists at the Federal Reserve Board, looking at home equity in the mid-century, found that white homeownership in the bottom third of the income distribution was flat from 1947 to 1960 while it rose substantially in the upper two thirds, and that FHA loans were held by 13.4 per cent of bottom-third homeowners against 20.5 per cent of the top third. [cite:ad6_fed_frydman] The programmes reached the middle class and above most easily; lower-income families often could not raise even the reduced down payment.

The gap the design left behind

The Fair Housing Act of 1968 made discrimination in sale and rental illegal, and the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 gave it an enforcement mechanism. [cite:ad6_prrac_timeline] The ownership gap those laws were meant to close has not closed. In 1960, 65 per cent of white households and 38 per cent of Black households owned their homes, a gap of 27 percentage points; in 2022 the figures were 74.6 and 45.3 per cent, a gap of about 29. [cite:ad6_stateline_gap] In the second quarter of 2026 the Census Bureau put non-Hispanic white homeownership at 74.5 per cent and Black homeownership at 45.4 per cent: 74.5 minus 45.4 is a gap of 29.1 points. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] Both rates rose over the six decades; the distance between them did not shrink. Part Thirteen sets out the documented causes of the gap and the disputes over their relative weight. [scene:ad13_black_americans|Part Thirteen: Black Americans and the Dream]

Zoning: the friction that stayed

Zoning is the local rule that says what may be built where. Its legal foundation is Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty (1926), in which the Supreme Court held that a town could use its police power to keep apartment buildings out of neighbourhoods of houses; Justice George Sutherland's opinion called the apartment house "very often" "a mere parasite". [cite:us15_wiki_sfzoning] Zoning that permits only one detached house per lot is not racial on its face. Its effect on supply is the subject of a large literature. Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko argue that where house prices sit well above the cost of building, the gap works as a "regulatory tax" imposed by local rules, and that in many areas the tax appears larger than any reasonable estimate of the harm new building would cause neighbours. [cite:ad6_glaeser_gyourko] A 2019 New York Times analysis found that in many American cities about three quarters of residential land is zoned for single-family homes only. [cite:us15_wiki_sfzoning] States and cities have started to change it: Minneapolis ended single-family-only zoning in 2018, Oregon passed House Bill 2001 in 2019, California SB 9 was signed in 2021 and Washington HB 1110 in 2023. [cite:us15_wiki_sfzoning] Part Fifteen of the US System vs Israel System volume compares these land rules with Israel's. [scene:us15_land|US System vs Israel System: land and planning]

Two kinds of friction therefore sit in the housing record from the start. The long amortising mortgage, federal insurance and the later fair-housing laws are designed friction that did roughly what they were built to do: they made ownership reachable on a wage. The racial underwriting rules, the covenants and the local administration of a nominally race-blind veterans' law were designed too, and documented in the agencies' own manuals; they were later outlawed, and the gap they helped open has persisted.
