Old Age, as Lived

The taught version of retirement ends in a picture: a paid-off house, a pension, a Social Security cheque and savings, enough to stop working and live without fear of running out. The two previous sections took the parts of that picture one at a time. This section asks what they add up to for the people now old, using the government's two poverty measures, an independent measure of what old age actually costs, and the record of older Americans who are still at work.

Two poverty rates for the same people

The official poverty measure, set in the 1960s, compares a family's cash income before taxes with a threshold based on the cost of food, updated only for inflation. On it, 9.9 per cent of Americans aged 65 and over were poor in 2024, close to the level of the past quarter-century and far below the 35.2 per cent of 1959. [cite:ad10_census_a3] The Census Bureau's supplemental poverty measure counts things the official one leaves out: taxes, benefits paid in kind, and necessary spending such as housing and out-of-pocket medical care. On that measure 15.4 per cent of people 65 and over were poor in 2025, more than 10 million people, up from 12.7 per cent in 2019 and a low of 9.4 per cent in 2020; the rate was 13.7 per cent for older men and 16.8 per cent for older women. [cite:ad10_aarp_spm65] [cite:ad10_leadingage] The difference between 9.9 and 15.4 per cent is not a contradiction. It reflects what the supplemental measure subtracts before treating income as available to live on, including housing costs and out-of-pocket medical spending. [scene:ad8_costs_and_coverage|Part Eight: health costs]

A third measure compares countries. The OECD counts a person as poor in relative terms if household income, after taxes and benefits and adjusted for household size, falls below half the national median. By that measure 22.9 per cent of Americans aged 66 and over were poor in 2023, a higher rate than for Americans as a whole. [cite:us22_oecd_idd_usa66] The figure describes distance from the middle, not hardship as such: an older person can be above every American poverty threshold and still below half the median income of a high-income country.

The Elder Index: what old age costs

Poverty lines measure deprivation, not security. Researchers at the University of Massachusetts Boston built the Elder Index to measure the second: the income an older person needs, county by county, to pay for housing, food, transport, health care and other necessities without help. In 2019 the national average was $25,416 a year for a single older adult who rented and $36,204 for an older couple. By that standard half of older Americans living alone, 50 per cent, and 23 per cent of older couples had incomes too low to cover their basic costs. Among single older adults, 18.2 per cent were below the federal poverty line, and another 32.1 per cent were above it but below the Elder Index, a group the researchers describe as living "in the gap." [cite:ad10_umb_2019] More recent state reports find the same pattern where housing is dear: in Massachusetts in 2025, 60 per cent of older adults living alone had incomes below the index, the second-highest share of any state. [cite:ad10_umb_ma25]

The arithmetic against Social Security is direct. The average benefit in 2026 is about $2,088 a month, or about $25,000 a year (12 × $2,088). [cite:ad10_napa_cola27] That is already below the 2019 national Elder Index for a single renter, $25,416, before allowing for seven years of rising prices. [cite:ad10_umb_2019] For an older person living alone and renting, with no other income, the average benefit does not cover the basic budget the index describes. A pension, savings or work has to fill the gap.

The savings that are supposed to fill it

For many households they do. In 2025, among families whose head was 55 to 64 and who held an IRA or 401(k)-type account, the mean balance was $670,200; across all families, about one in four held a defined-benefit pension. [cite:ad10_scf26] But that mean is pulled up by large accounts, the same effect that puts Vanguard's average balance at almost four times its median of $44,115. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] And 45.1 per cent of all families held no retirement account at all (100 minus the 54.9 per cent who did). [cite:ad10_scf26] For those families, old age rests on Social Security, home equity if they own a home, and whatever work they can still do. How wealth in general divides by age, income and race is set out in [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|Part Twelve].

Working longer

More older Americans are working. Pew Research Center, using federal survey data, found 19 per cent of adults aged 65 and over in work in 2023, against 11 per cent in 1987, and 9 per cent of those 75 and over, against 4 per cent; the older workforce, about 11 million people, has nearly quadrupled since the mid-1980s. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects the participation rate of people 65 and over will rise from 19 to 21 per cent by 2032. [cite:ad10_pew_older] The rise is partly a return: about 18 per cent of people 65 and over worked in the early 1960s, before the large increases in Social Security, so the 1980s low is as much the exception as today's level. [cite:ad10_pew_older]

Whether older Americans work because they want to or because they must is not settled by these figures. Pew points to several causes: more education and better health, both linked to working longer; jobs that have become less physically demanding; the shift from pensions, which encouraged retiring at a set age, to 401(k)-type accounts, which do not; and the later full retirement age set in 1983, which rewards staying on. In a Federal Reserve survey cited by Pew, 45 per cent of employed older adults described themselves as retired, which suggests that much later-life work is part-time work alongside a pension or benefit rather than a career that never ended. [cite:ad10_pew_older] [cite:ad10_ssa_1983] Others work because the arithmetic above leaves them no choice. Neither reading accounts for all of the change, and no national survey found here divides older workers cleanly between the two.

Where it works as promised

The largest change in this part of the record is good news, and it should be stated without qualification. An American who reached 65 in 1959 had about a one-in-three chance of being poor on the official measure; one who reached 65 in 2024 had about a one-in-ten chance. [cite:ad10_census_a3] The principal reason in the current data is a public programme that pays an inflation-protected benefit earned by work, and that in 2025 kept 28.8 million people of all ages above the supplemental poverty line. [cite:us18_census_p60290] Where the record falls short of the promise, it falls short in specific places: older people who live alone, who rent in high-cost areas, who carry heavy medical costs, or who reached retirement without a pension or an account. Those are the households the supplemental measure and the Elder Index pick out, and they are where the shift from pension to 401(k) and the gap between the average benefit and the cost of living meet.
