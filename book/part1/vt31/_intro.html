Part Thirty-One follows the money and the rules. The thesis Book Two tests holds that institutional religion taxed and obligated believers where the inner traditions asked for nothing, and this is the part of the thesis that the institution's own documents can test most directly, because every step left a canon, a capitulary, a bull or a statute behind it. The first section traces the tithe from a voluntary gift to a church law in sixth-century Gaul, a royal tax under Charlemagne and a thousand-year revenue, with the papacy's own streams beside it: annates, indulgences and Peter's Pence. It ends with what is asked today, from the Code of Canon Law to Germany's church tax. The second traces Sunday from a custom to a duty under pain of "grave sin", the yearly confession and Easter communion fixed in 1215, and the penalties for leaving, and sets beside them the Gnostic texts that tell their readers to lay down no rule, including the passages that complicate that picture. The third puts the two economies side by side across eight questions, names the exceptions on both sides, and closes with the Sophia platform's reading of the contrast, labelled as interpretation, and the strongest arguments against it.
