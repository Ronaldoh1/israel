The Poor Knights of the Temple

The order began in 1119, when two French knights, Hugh of Payns and Godfrey of Saint-Omer, undertook to guard pilgrims on the road from Jaffa to Jerusalem. King Baldwin II and the Patriarch of Jerusalem gave them quarters on the Temple platform, in the building the Latins called the "Temple of Solomon", the al-Aqsa mosque, and from it they took their name. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1] In January 1129 a council at Troyes, encouraged by Bernard of Clairvaux, gave them a Latin Rule of seventy-one clauses, and Bernard wrote a treatise praising this new kind of knighthood: monks who fought. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

On 29 March 1139 Pope Innocent II issued the bull Omne datum optimum. It called the Templars defenders of the Church, placed their possessions under papal protection and freed them from much local church authority. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1] This is the document behind the common statement that the Templars "answered only to the pope". As a description of their legal standing it is accurate, and it explains a later fact: only the pope could lawfully dissolve them.

Gifts of land across Europe, and the need to move money from estates in the West to castles in the East, made the order a financial institution as well as a military one. Pilgrims deposited valuables with one Templar house and drew on them at another by letters of credit; kings and nobles borrowed from the order and stored their treasure with it. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar] In France the Paris Temple became, in the words of a 2016 Madrid doctoral study of its accounts, "banker to kings": the royal treasury's accounts were kept there through much of the thirteenth century, the study covering the years 1221 to 1295. [cite:vt29_ucm_temple]

Popular accounts call this "Europe's first international banking network". The safer description is one of the earliest cross-border deposit and transfer systems in Latin Europe; the Italian banking houses grew alongside it, not out of it. The point that matters for what came next is simpler. By 1300 the French king owed the order money, and the order held his accounts. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar]
