The Jewish Lineages: Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Mizrahi

No one seriously disputes that Ashkenazi Jews are Jewish. What is disputed, often loudly and usually online, is where they come from. Two opposite errors circulate. The first holds that Ashkenazi Jews descend from Central Asian Khazar converts and carry no real Middle Eastern ancestry, and therefore no ancestral connection to the land. The second, less often stated but just as often assumed, treats all Jewish communities as genetically and historically identical, as if two thousand years spent in different countries had left no trace. The genetic record supports neither. What it shows is a set of distinct communities, each with its own documented history, sharing a detectable common origin and each carrying the marks of the places where it lived.

The word "Semitic" already names a family larger than any one people. The genealogy of Genesis 10, 11 and 25 runs from Shem through many generations to Abraham, and then divides: Ishmael, traditionally associated with Arab peoples, and, through Isaac, both Esau, traditionally associated with Edom, and Jacob, later renamed Israel.[cite:genesis_10_11_bible] [cite:genesis_25_esau_ishmael] By the text's own family tree, Jacob's line is one branch of Shem's descendants, not the whole of it, a point taken up with the history of the word itself in [scene:s00b|the section on "Semitic"]. The text establishes scope, not rank: it presents all three lines as branches of the same root. The same structure, a shared category holding real internal variety, applies to the Jewish communities themselves.

Three broad lineages make up most of world Jewry. Mizrahi Jews, the Jewish communities of Iraq, Yemen, Egypt, Iran, Syria and elsewhere, never left the Middle East. Their presence there goes back millennia; in Iraq it is traced to the Babylonian Exile of 586 BCE, and as late as 1945 Baghdad alone held roughly 140,000 Jews, about a third of the city. The Jews of Iran, one of the oldest of these communities, are treated in their own chapter of this library, [scene:ira_src_s16|the Jews of Iran]. Sephardi Jews trace to Iberia, Spain and Portugal, where their roots predate the Muslim conquest. Ferdinand and Isabella expelled them in 1492, and most resettled across the Ottoman Empire and North Africa, regions that already held related Middle Eastern Jewish populations; Salonika became a majority-Sephardic city for centuries under Ottoman rule. Ashkenazi communities formed in the Rhineland, in Central Europe, in the early medieval period, and later moved east into Poland, Lithuania, Russia and Ukraine. By the nineteenth century Ashkenazi Jews were the demographic majority of world Jewry, and they produced most of the Jewish immigration to the United States.

These are histories, documented in the ordinary way. The question of what each community carries in its genes is a separate one, and it was answered most thoroughly by a study published in Nature in 2010. Behar, Yunusbayev, Metspalu and eighteen co-authors compared genome-wide data from 14 Jewish communities spanning Europe, the Middle East, North Africa and Central Asia against 69 non-Jewish populations.[cite:behar_2010_nature] This was a large genome-wide analysis, not a study of a single marker or a handful of anecdotes. Its core finding was that most of the Jewish populations examined, Ashkenazi, Sephardi and North African among them, cluster together genetically and share their closest non-Jewish relationships with Middle Eastern populations, more so than with the European or other populations among whom they lived. The authors' own framing was that the data support common origins in the Middle East, followed by migrations into the diaspora.[cite:behar_2010_nature]

The same study found real structure inside that shared origin. The Jewish samples divided into three groups: an Ashkenazi, North African and Sephardi cluster; a Caucasus and Middle Eastern cluster; and a distinct Yemenite cluster.[cite:behar_2010_nature] The result is therefore not that every Jewish community is genetically identical. It is that every community examined carries a detectable shared ancestral signal, with real regional differences layered on top. The study does not by itself settle the size or the timing of particular episodes of admixture, and genetic work of this kind is continually refined by newer data. It is cited here because it remains the most comprehensive genome-wide study of its kind and because its core finding has held up under later attempts to replicate it, not because any single study is beyond revision.

The Khazar hypothesis is a different claim, and it deserves to be stated precisely before it is tested. The Khazars were a Turkic people of the Caucasus and southern Russia whose ruling elite, according to the historical record, converted to Judaism around the eighth or ninth century CE. As a fact about one medieval kingdom's ruling class, that conversion is a real and separately documented event. The hypothesis takes it much further: it proposes that the Ashkenazi population as a whole descends substantially from the Khazars rather than from the ancient Levant. In its modern scientific form the claim was argued in a 2012 paper by the geneticist Eran Elhaik in Genome Biology and Evolution, which proposed a Khazar component in European Jewish ancestry.[cite:adl_khazar] [cite:aap_khazar_factcheck] Critics faulted the paper for its method: Elhaik used modern Armenians and Georgians as proxies for the Khazars.[cite:aap_khazar_factcheck]

The test came in 2013. Behar and colleagues, more than twenty co-authors in all, published a study in Human Biology that analyzed 1,774 samples from 106 Jewish and non-Jewish populations, including populations native to the region of the former Khazar Khaganate.[cite:nli_behar_khazar] [cite:behar_2013_khazar] They found no particular similarity between Ashkenazi Jews and the populations of the Caucasus, and in their own words "no indication" of a Khazar genetic contribution. Ashkenazi ancestry, they concluded, derives primarily from the Middle East and Europe.[cite:behar_2013_khazar] [cite:aap_khazar_factcheck] Genome-wide and ancient-DNA studies since then have modeled Ashkenazi Jews as a mixture of Levantine and European ancestry. How large each share is remains open; one researcher, cited in a published fact-check, puts the Middle Eastern share at between 15 and 50 percent.[cite:nli_behar_khazar] [cite:aap_khazar_factcheck]

What is European in Ashkenazi ancestry is real, documented and partial. The European contribution is substantial, and it shows most clearly on the maternal line, reflecting some two thousand years of intermarriage and conversion within European societies. The same studies document a shared ancient Levantine origin on the paternal line, and the two findings sit side by side: the European layer is part of the history, not a replacement for the older one. There is no contradiction between them, only a contradiction between the data and either of the two errors with which this section began.

The Khazar theory is addressed directly here because of what it has been used to do. Since the nineteenth century it has served antisemitic arguments that portray Jews as alien impostors with no genuine connection to the Land of Israel, and so as not "really" Jewish or Semitic at all.[cite:jewishpress_khazar] [cite:aap_khazar_factcheck] Viral claims built on it continue to appear and continue to fail. One widely shared post asserted that a study had proved 97.5 percent of Israeli Jews have no ties to the land; fact-checkers rated it false, noting that the study in question included no Israeli Jews.[cite:aap_khazar_factcheck] The finding that the Ashkenazi population as a whole is not primarily of Khazar descent does not deny that the Khazar elite converted, and it does not settle every question about European admixture. What it settles is the specific claim, which does not hold when tested.

That claim returned to a large audience in February 2026. On 19 and 20 February, Tucker Carlson interviewed the United States Ambassador to Israel, Mike Huckabee, and asked: "Why don't we do genetic testing on everybody in the land and find out who Abram's descendants are? It's really simple. We've cracked the human genome. We can do that." He went on to single out Netanyahu, citing his family's Polish origin.[cite:jta_carlson_khazar_2026] [cite:toi_carlson_khazar_2026] Huckabee, a committed Christian Zionist rather than a reflexive critic, pushed back in the room, calling the idea a "dangerous conspiracy theory" from "some of the darkest realms of the internet." Reporters at JTA, the Times of Israel, the Jerusalem Post and the Forward identified the underlying claim as the Khazar theory: the premise that Eastern European ancestry among some Israeli Jews would undo their connection to the land.[cite:jta_carlson_khazar_2026] [cite:toi_carlson_khazar_2026] That identification was the reporters' own, made independently across outlets.

The exchange also raised a separate and more general question, which should be kept apart from the discredited one. What share of ancestry would make any identity claim, or any claim to land, "legitimate"? No geneticist, historian, court or international convention has ever defined such a threshold, for this question or for any other claim based on ancestry anywhere in the world. That is not a gap in the research. Ancestry percentages have never been the legal or historical basis on which such claims rest: treaties, borders and citizenship laws do not run on DNA, for Israel or for any other state. Collapsing the open philosophical question into the tested genetic claim is one of the ways the discredited version gets carried back into argument.

An analogy, offered as an analogy and not as a genetic argument, may help with the teaching gap underneath all this. Many people are taught that "Jewish" and "Semitic" are simple synonyms, and few are taught that Jewish identity itself contains several lineages with different histories. A Salvadoran, a Mexican and a Peruvian are all Latino, while each carries a different mix of Indigenous American, Spanish colonial and other ancestry, varying by country and family. No one is less Latino for having a different mix. In the same structural sense, Mizrahi Jews carry concentrated, continuous Levantine ancestry, and Ashkenazi Jews carry that same documented root together with substantial European admixture. The comparison borrows one idea, that a shared category can hold real internal variety, and nothing more; Jewish and Latino identities have entirely different histories, religious and genetic.

The Beta Israel of Ethiopia show that the question of who counts as a Jew has also been settled, in practice, by institutions rather than by genes. The Beta Israel, the "House of Israel," lived for centuries in the Gondar and Tigray regions of northern Ethiopia and practiced a Judaism distinct from rabbinic tradition.[cite:w_beta_israel] The familiar account holds that Israel always welcomed them as Jews. The record is slower and more conditional. Their status was disputed by Israel's religious authorities until 1973, when the Sephardi Chief Rabbi, Ovadia Yosef, ruled that they were Jews. The government applied the Law of Return to them in 1975, which made them eligible to immigrate.[cite:w_ethiopian_jews_israel] Even then, in 1985, the chief rabbinate required a symbolic conversion ritual before it accepted them fully.[cite:moment_failing] By the end of 2019 about 155,300 Ethiopian Jews lived in Israel.[cite:w_beta_israel]

The sequence shows where the power to decide lay. A religious ruling by one chief rabbi opened the door; a government decision under the Law of Return turned that ruling into a right to immigrate; and the chief rabbinate still imposed a further condition of its own twelve years after the ruling. The terms of the Law of Return itself are set out in [scene:ch01_citizenship|the section on citizenship]. The record available here does not explain why recognition took until 1973, or how the 1985 requirement was received within the community and how long it lasted; those questions remain open in this account.

Two points of discipline follow from all of this, and they apply in every direction. Genetics does not determine anyone's political rights, Jewish, Palestinian or any other. And the case against any state policy examined in this library never depends on the ancestry of the people who carry it out. A documented argument about policy stands on documents. Claims about the ancestry of Jews or of Palestinians add nothing to it, and in the Khazar case they are also false.

What the record leaves unsettled is real but narrow. The size and timing of particular admixture events in Ashkenazi ancestry are still being refined, and estimates of the Middle Eastern share vary widely. The genetic studies measure ancestry; they do not decide identity, which in the case of the Beta Israel was decided by a rabbinic ruling and a government decision. What is settled is that the Jewish lineages share a detectable Middle Eastern origin, that each also carries its own regional history, and that the claim of wholesale Khazar descent was tested and did not hold.
