Loops: Documented and Candidate

A loop, in a money map, is a chain of documented transfers that returns to where it started: A pays B, B does something for C, C pays A. Loops matter because they are where influence becomes return, and they are the strongest kind of finding a network map can produce. They are also the easiest kind to invent. Any two people who know each other and any two transfers in the same year can be joined by an arrow, and the eye completes the circle. This section therefore holds each possible loop to one rule: a loop is documented only if every arrow in it is documented. If even one arrow is inferred from timing, from motive or from the logic of the situation, the loop is a candidate, and it is written with the inferred arrow marked. Timing is not causation: a donation that comes before a benefit is a sequence, not a proof that one caused the other.

Five chains are tested below. One closes on documents, and it is a commercial loop, not a political one. The other four are candidates, each with a single missing arrow, and for each the missing arrow is named.

The one closed loop: Valar and Epstein

The only chain in this book's material that closes on documents runs through Valar Ventures, a financial-technology fund co-founded by Thiel and led by Andrew McCormack and James Fitzgerald. In 2015 and 2016 Jeffrey Epstein put $40 million into two Valar funds; by 2025 the stake was worth nearly $170 million and was the largest asset left in his estate, and Valar said it hoped the eventual distribution could help his victims. [cite:de14_nyt_valar] The money went in, the fund invested it, and the gains are owed back to the estate: an ordinary limited partnership, with every arrow in the record. It is a loop of investment and return, not of political influence, and it shows nothing about government. What it does show, and does not show, about Thiel and Epstein is the subject of [scene:de14_epstein_valar|the last section].

Candidate loop 1: candidate, office, contract, portfolio

The loop most often drawn runs from Thiel to Vance to the government to Palantir and back to Thiel. Its documented arrows are strong. Thiel employed Vance, reportedly backed his venture firm, and gave $15 million to the super PAC that carried his 2022 campaign. [cite:de14_cbs_klaidman] [cite:de14_forbesau] Vance became Vice President in January 2025. In April 2025 ICE agreed to pay Palantir $29.9 million for "ImmigrationOS," and in February 2026 DHS signed a purchase agreement with Palantir worth up to $1 billion over five years; Thiel is Palantir's co-founder and chairman. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:ow9_sa_dhsbpa] [cite:de9_companies]

The missing arrow is the one in the middle: from Vance's office to Palantir's contracts. No document shows the Vice President, or anyone acting for him, involved in any Palantir award. The contracts ran through ICE's and DHS's own procurement, and ICE justified the 2025 award on the grounds that "Palantir is the only source that can provide the required capabilities." [cite:ow9_reg_immos] Palantir's ICE business also began in 2014, under the Obama administration, and was renewed in 2019, years before Vance held office. [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] The loop is a candidate: three arrows documented, one inferred. The inference is not unreasonable, since an administration whose vice president owes his career partly to Palantir's chairman is not a neutral buyer of Palantir's software; but a reasonable inference is still an inference, and the record has a simpler explanation for the contracts, which is that ICE had used Palantir for a decade.

Candidate loop 2: the czar's portfolio

The second runs through David Sacks. Its documented arrows: Sacks's financial disclosures listed 708 technology investments, 449 of them in AI companies, according to the New York Times; he served from January 2025 to March 2026 as the White House's adviser on AI and crypto, with two ethics waivers that let him keep certain stakes while shaping policy in both fields; and his public filings did not show the remaining value of the assets he said he would sell, or when he sold them. [cite:de13_tc_nyt_sacks] [cite:de13_tc_sacks_exit] The missing arrow: a specific policy he shaped that raised the value of a specific holding he kept. The Times report argued that his position could benefit his portfolio; Sacks called it a "nothing burger," and no document published so far ties a named decision to a named gain. [cite:de13_tc_nyt_sacks] The waivers are a documented conflict of interest in the legal sense, a situation in which private interest and public duty overlap, which the government itself acknowledged by issuing them; a loop of benefit is a candidate.

Candidate loop 3: the campaign, DOGE and the contractor

The third runs through Musk. Documented: more than $290 million spent to elect Trump and other Republicans in 2024; a post as a special government employee leading DOGE from January to May 2025; and companies, chiefly SpaceX, that the Washington Post counted as having received at least $38 billion in federal contracts, loans, subsidies and tax credits over twenty years. [cite:de14_cnn_musk290] [cite:de13_abc_musk_exit] [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] The missing arrow is from DOGE's work to a new benefit for Musk's companies. Most of SpaceX's government business predates 2025. Whether DOGE's access to agencies that regulate or contract with his companies produced any specific gain is examined in [scene:de21_conflicts_and_contracts|Part Twenty-One]; on this part's material the loop is a candidate.

Candidate loop 4: the donor network and the ticket

The fourth runs through the Rockbridge Network. Documented: Thiel and Vance convened the 2019 Ohio summit from which it grew; Sacks and Andreessen are described as members; its affiliated super PAC, Turnout for America, spent $34.5 million in the 2024 cycle; and many of its members, the Washington Post reported, hope Vance will be the 2028 nominee. [cite:de14_wapo_rockbridge] The missing arrow is any documented return to the members: a contract, a regulation or an appointment traced to membership. The Post describes access, conferences with Cabinet-level speakers and a $500,000-a-head private club launched by Rockbridge's leader, but not a specific benefit. [cite:de14_wapo_rockbridge] Candidate.

Candidate loop 5: the shared intermediary

The fifth is not a loop at all, though it is often drawn as one. The Thiel Foundation has made grants to DonorsTrust, a donor-advised fund, and DonorsTrust also funds a long list of conservative and libertarian groups, from the Federalist Society to Heritage and Cato. [cite:de14_kindora_thiel] [cite:de14_dg_donorstrust] Because DonorsTrust's grants appear under its own name and not the donor's, a shared intermediary does not show a flow from any particular donor to any particular recipient. The pipe is shared; what passes through it from Thiel is unknown. No arrow from Thiel's grants to any specific end recipient is documented, so no loop can be drawn, and the [scene:de14_foundations_and_opacity|next section] treats it as an opacity point, not a finding.

The pattern across the five is consistent. The arrows that carry money into politics are well documented, because federal law requires super PACs to report their donors; the arrows that would carry policy back to private gain are not, because no law requires anyone to document them, and because none has surfaced in litigation, audit or leak. A reader can take that absence two ways: as a sign that the returns do not exist, or as a sign that they are not the kind of thing that gets written down. The record does not decide between those readings, and this book does not pretend that it does. It grades each loop on what is written down, and on that standard none of the four political loops closes.
