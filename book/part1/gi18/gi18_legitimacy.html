Two Questions of Legitimacy

The previous sections asked whether a Greater Israel programme could exist without the state of 1948. This one sets out the arguments about the legitimacy of the state itself, and of the land it has held since 1967, as they are made by the governments, courts and bodies that make them. Two rules govern it. No people's right is affirmed or denied here as a collective matter; each position is attributed to a named state, organisation, court or document, with its date. And two questions that public argument often runs together are kept apart: whether Israel is legitimate within the lines it held before June 1967, and whether it may hold, settle or annex land beyond them. The record gives very different answers to the two.

Israel's case

The declaration of 14 May 1948 rested the state on "our natural and historic right," on the Balfour Declaration of 1917, on the League of Nations Mandate and on the General Assembly's resolution of 29 November 1947, and promised equal rights "irrespective of religion, race or sex." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] Each of the legal steps it names is documented. The Mandate of 24 July 1922 made Britain responsible for securing the Jewish national home, while "safeguarding the civil and religious rights of all the inhabitants of Palestine"; the League's own study of 1945 called it a mandate "of a very special character" because it carried out the Balfour Declaration. [cite:rs_md_avalon] [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] The legal scholar Matthijs de Blois argues that it was unique in naming the Jewish people, wherever they lived, as its beneficiaries. [cite:rs_a22_deblois] The partition resolution passed 33 to 13. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] The United States recognised the provisional government de facto on 14 May 1948 and de jure on 31 January 1949; Britain followed de facto on 29 January 1949 and de jure on 27 April 1950. [cite:ow1_nara_recog] [cite:dv2_fathom_aridan] On 11 May 1949 the General Assembly admitted Israel to the United Nations by 37 votes to 12, with 9 abstentions, noting its declaration that it "unreservedly accepts the obligations of the United Nations Charter." [cite:rs_rd6_ecf_273] [cite:rs_rd6_unga273_text] [scene:rd6_un_admission|Admission to the UN]

Two further arguments complete the case. The first is self-determination, which Israel's Basic Law of 2018 states as "unique to the Jewish people" in the State of Israel. [cite:gi5_toi_nslaw] The second is defence. On 15 May 1948 the Arab League informed the UN that its members were intervening in Palestine, and the armies of Egypt, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq crossed into it; Israel's position has been that the lines of 1949 were the result of a war it did not begin. [cite:rs_rd5_un_s745] [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b]

The Palestinian and Arab case, and the critics'

The opposing case begins with consent. The League Covenant had said of the former Ottoman lands that "the wishes of these communities must be a principal consideration," and Palestinian Arab representatives rejected the Mandate as incompatible with self-determination. [cite:rs_a22_lon1945] [cite:rs_a22_deblois] In 1947 the Arab Higher Committee refused to cooperate with the UN Special Committee on Palestine, and at the Assembly Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Iraq, Syria and Yemen denounced partition as contrary to the Charter and said they would not be bound by it. [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] Their objection was concrete: by the Palestinian historian Muhsin Yusuf's figures the proposed Jewish state held about 498,000 Jews and about 494,000 Arabs. [cite:rs_rd5_yusuf_2002] The proposal to ask the International Court whether partition could be enforced without the inhabitants' consent failed by one vote. [cite:gi18_un_gapal85]

The second ground is the Nakba, the flight and expulsion of 1948. The UN Conciliation Commission's technical committee put the refugees from Israeli-held territory at about 711,000 in 1949; Israel officially held to 520,000 to 530,000, and Arab officials spoke of 900,000 or a million. [cite:rs_rd5_unccp_1949] [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix] [scene:rd5_refugees|The refugees of 1948] Resolution 194 of 11 December 1948 resolved that refugees "wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date." [cite:gi18_jvl_res194] Palestinians read it as a right of return; Israeli governments have read the condition of living at peace as making return depend on a peace that was never made. [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194 and its two readings] In July 1968 the Palestinian National Charter declared partition and the establishment of Israel "entirely illegal," "contrary to the will of the Palestinian people" and to "the right to self-determination." [cite:gi18_avalon_plocov]

The question narrows to the 1967 lines

From 1988 the main Palestinian and Arab bodies moved the argument from the existence of the state to its borders. The Palestinian declaration of independence of 15 November 1988 called partition a "historical injustice" yet held that Resolution 181 "continues to attach conditions to international legitimacy" that guarantee Palestinian sovereignty. [cite:gi18_mx_pdoi1988] On 9 September 1993 Yasser Arafat wrote that "The PLO recognizes the right of the State of Israel to exist in peace and security." [cite:gi12_jvl_letters1993] Egypt in 1979 and Jordan in 1994 signed treaties recognising Israel's sovereignty and borders. [cite:gi8_avalon_egtreaty] [cite:gi8_incore_jotreaty] In March 2002 the Arab League's Beirut summit offered to "consider the Arab-Israeli conflict ended" and "establish normal relations with Israel" in return for "full Israeli withdrawal from all the territories occupied since 1967," a Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital and a refugee solution based on Resolution 194. [cite:gi18_albab_api] As of November 2023, 30 of the 193 UN member states did not recognise Israel, among them Iran, Iraq, Saudi Arabia and Syria. [cite:ir13_vc_recog] As of 23 September 2025, 157 recognised the State of Palestine, which has been a non-member observer state at the UN since 2012. [cite:gi18_aje_recog2025] The legitimacy of Israel within its pre-1967 lines is, on this record, the position of most states and of the main Palestinian and Arab bodies; Iran's government and Hamas, among others, reject it. [scene:hm11_claims|The Hamas Architecture: claims]

Beyond the lines

The second question has drawn a consistent answer from the UN's organs. Resolution 242 of 22 November 1967 stressed "the inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war" and called for withdrawal "from territories occupied in the recent conflict," a phrase whose English text lacks the definite article that its French text has, a difference Israel has relied on. [cite:wb_wiki_242] The Security Council declared the Golan law "null and void" unanimously in 1981 and said in 2016 that settlements have "no legal validity." [cite:gi8_rw_res497] [cite:wb_wiki_2334] On 19 July 2024 the International Court of Justice advised that Israel's "assertion of sovereignty and its annexation of certain parts of the territory" "constitute a violation of the prohibition of the acquisition of territory by force," that Israel "is not entitled to sovereignty over" any part of the territory occupied in 1967, that its "security concerns" cannot override that prohibition, and that the Palestinian people "has been deprived of its right to self-determination over a long period." [cite:gi18_icj_pr2024] The judges divided 11 to 4 on the unlawfulness of the continued presence, 14 to 1 on settlements and 12 to 3 on states' duty of non-recognition; the opinion is advisory and not binding. [cite:gi18_idi_icj2024] The General Assembly then demanded, 124 to 14 with 43 abstentions, that Israel end its presence within twelve months. [cite:wb_ahram_unga2024] [scene:wb10_rulings|The West Bank, Documented: the rulings] [scene:gi17_courts|Part Sixteen: the courts]

Israel's government rejects that answer. Netanyahu responded to the opinion: "The Jewish people are not occupiers in their own land, including in our eternal capital Jerusalem, nor in Judea and Samaria, our historical homeland." [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] The 2022 guidelines claim "an exclusive and inalienable right to all parts of the Land of Israel." [cite:gi5_jl_guidelines] Israel had asked the Court not to give an opinion at all, and the Israel Democracy Institute's analysts criticised the opinion for giving Israel's security concerns little weight, a criticism some judges also made. [cite:gi18_idi_icj2024] The United States, the one state to recognise Israeli sovereignty in occupied territory before 2026, did so for the Golan in March 2019, and Colombia's government followed in August 2026; on the West Bank President Trump said on 25 September 2025, "I will not allow Israel to annex the West Bank." [cite:gi8_ucsb_procl9852] [cite:gi8_aje_colombia] [cite:gi6_ap_trump0925]

The two questions therefore have documented answers of different strength. The legitimacy of the state within its pre-1967 lines rests on a vote, an admission, treaties and recognition by most of the world, and the main bodies that once denied it, the PLO and the Arab League, moved by 1993 and 2002 to accepting it on conditions. The holding of land beyond those lines has been rejected by the Security Council, the General Assembly and, in advisory form, the International Court, and accepted as sovereign only in the Golan by two states. This is the line along which the volume has found its documented programmes: the West Bank and the Golan. [scene:gi13_record|Part Eighteen: settled, contested and open]
