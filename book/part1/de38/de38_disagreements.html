Where the Network Splits

The worst-case reading needs a bloc: people who share an end and move toward it together. The record of the people in this book shows something else as often as it shows that. They have quarrelled in public, over money, over tariffs, over who should hold power, and, most tellingly, over immigration itself, the occasion the hypothesis names. Some of the quarrels healed and some did not. This section sets them out by date, with each side's own words, because a reader cannot weigh the case for coordination without seeing where the supposed partners pulled apart.

Over immigration itself

The first open split of the second Trump era came before the inauguration, and it was about visas for skilled foreign workers, the H-1B programme. In the last week of December 2024 Elon Musk defended it, saying that bringing elite engineers from abroad was "essential for America to keep winning," warning of a "MAGA civil war" and declaring, "I will go to war on this issue." Steve Bannon, the president's former strategist, called H-1B workers essentially "indentured servants" and Musk a "toddler"; the activist Laura Loomer looked forward to "the inevitable divorce between President Trump and Big Tech." The president-elect sided with Musk on 28 December: "I've always liked the (H1-B) visas." [cite:de38_afp_h1b] The episode matters to this book more than its brevity suggests. The coalition the hypothesis treats as one machine divided, in public and before taking office, over the very subject it reads as the occasion, and the technology wing argued for more foreign labour, not less.

Musk and the president

The second split was over trade. In April 2025, after the president's tariffs were announced, Musk called the trade adviser Peter Navarro "a moron" and "dumber than a sack of bricks"; Navarro had called Musk a "car assembler," and the White House press secretary answered, "boys will be boys." [cite:de38_abc_navarro] The third was the largest. On 5 June 2025, days after Musk left the government, the president said the easiest way to save billions was to "terminate Elon's governmental subsidies and contracts," and Musk answered that Trump would have lost without him and alleged, without evidence, that Trump was in the Epstein files. [cite:de11_npr_feud] On 11 June he wrote, "I regret some of my posts," having deleted the Epstein post. [cite:de11_audit_pbs_regret] On 5 July he announced an "America Party" after the tax-and-spending bill passed; no registration with the Federal Election Commission was confirmed, and the president called it "ridiculous." [cite:de11_ap_party]

That split did not last. The two men shook hands at Charlie Kirk's memorial in September 2025, and on 18 November Musk attended the president's White House dinner for Saudi Arabia's crown prince. [cite:de38_almon_dinner] By October 2026 Musk's political committee had put more than $440 million into the 2026 cycle on the same side as before. [cite:de11_ibt_fec] The record therefore supports two readings at once, and both belong here. The feud shows that the executive and its largest donor could turn on each other, threaten each other's interests and part, which is not how a single bloc behaves. The reconciliation shows that partners who quarrel over means can still pull in the same direction when their interests meet again. Musk's own public claims across the episode are graded in [scene:de11_claims_vs_record|Part Eleven].

Thiel and Musk

The tie between Peter Thiel and Musk is often drawn as an alliance running from PayPal to the White House. Its first documented event was a boardroom defeat: in September 2000, while Musk was away on a fundraising trip and a delayed honeymoon, executives of the merged company delivered letters of no confidence and the board replaced him with Thiel. [cite:de9_snopes_paypal] In 2024 their politics diverged in money. Thiel said in June, "If you hold a gun to my head, I'll vote for Trump," but that he would not give to the president's super PAC, and in April he refused Trump's request for $10 million; Musk spent more than $290 million to elect Trump and his party. [cite:de9_nbc_aspen] [cite:de12_cbs_klaidman] [cite:de11_cnn_290m] In June 2025 Thiel described 2024 as "the year where Elon stopped believing in Mars." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] None of this is a feud. It is two men with a shared history and separate politics, which is what the documented record of their connection, set out by type in [scene:de11_zip2_xcom_paypal|Part Eleven], has shown since 2000.

The PayPal alumnus on the other side

One member of the 2007 photograph of PayPal's founders funds the opposition. Reid Hoffman, a supporter of Democrats and progressive causes, has clashed in public with Thiel and David Sacks over their support for Trump, and gave at least $7 million to Kamala Harris's campaign and nearly $9 million to Wisconsin's Democratic Party since 2020. [cite:de13_fortune_2024] [cite:de13_badger_hoffman] The "PayPal Mafia" is a shared career, not a shared politics, as [scene:de13_where_they_sit_now|Part Thirteen] records.

The theorist against the practitioners

The ideology's best-known author has been one of the administration's sharper critics from the right. Curtis Yarvin complained that DOGE had too much libertarian DNA, as Ava Kofman reported, and said the administration was "barely scratching the surface" of the change he wanted. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] [cite:de6_cnn_2025] Earlier in 2025 he wrote that it would "wither and eventually dissipate" unless it grew more extreme, and in October he wrote that he must start thinking about how to "flee the country," later edited to a plan for 2029. [cite:de5_sfstandard] The man whose program the hypothesis names as the destination believes, in his own words, that the government is not travelling there. The judgment runs the other way too. Thiel, whose fund invested in Yarvin's company, has been reported as saying Yarvin's ideas would not "work." [cite:de6_cnn_2025] J.D. Vance, who named Yarvin in 2021, had called Trump "cultural heroin" in 2016 before changing his mind, a reversal he explained in 2024 and that is graded in [scene:de12_claims_vs_record|Part Twelve]. [cite:de12_axios_trump2016]

The theorists against each other

The texts themselves do not agree. Nick Land, who named the Dark Enlightenment, wrote that "Neoreaction is Accelerationism with a flat tire," a judgment on Yarvin's political route from the side of the faster, more impersonal process he preferred. [cite:de4_haider] Land's serial expects no change "short of an existential civilizational cataclysm" and looks to China, where he lives; Yarvin's program is an American chief executive reached through an election. [cite:de4_land_de] [cite:de4_spectator] ([scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]) Hans-Hermann Hoppe, whose argument from ownership Yarvin built on, wrote in 2025 that he did not follow Yarvin closely ("too much rambling for my taste"), and separated his ideal of many small principalities from the "Silicon valley crowd" model of running the state like a business. [cite:de3_hoppe_reply]

The splits do not settle the question of direction, and the book does not claim they do. A coalition can quarrel over visas, tariffs and personalities and still move power the same way, and the money of 2026 shows that the largest quarrel was mended. What the splits do settle is the question of organisation. On every documented occasion where the people in this book could have acted as one bloc, on immigration, on trade, on money and on the government's direction, at least one of them went another way in public, and the theorist at the centre of the ideology said the government was not becoming what he wanted. That is the pattern [scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One] calls convergence, not coordination.
