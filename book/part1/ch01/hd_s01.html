How Historians Argue About 1948

Historians do not agree on what happened in 1948, though it is often assumed that they do. Their names will recur in later chapters, and a reader is better served by knowing in advance where each one stands. This short section maps the camps. It is a guide to the interpreters, not a verdict on them.

Until the 1980s, the dominant Israeli account held that Palestinians largely left on their own or at the urging of Arab leaders. That account changed as Israel declassified its state and military archives. A group of scholars who came to be called the "new historians," Benny Morris and Ilan Pappé among them, used those archives to document expulsions, massacres and the deliberate prevention of Palestinian return. They did not agree with one another about what the documents meant. Morris and Pappé reached sharply different conclusions about how far the expulsions were planned. Walid Khalidi argued that the Haganah, a Jewish armed force of the period, was involved in Deir Yassin at every stage. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] Some material in Israeli military archives, including files on Deir Yassin, remains classified, so parts of the record are still closed to everyone.

The new historians have their own critics. Historians such as Efraim Karsh reject the expulsion-centered account and stress instead the responsibility of Arab leaders and the Arab rejection of partition. [cite:dailyalert_194_karsh] The disagreement is not over whether the archives exist. It is over what they show about intent and responsibility.

A second debate concerns framing rather than events. Scholars such as Joseph Massad describe Zionism as a settler-colonial project. They point to Zionist leaders' own use of the word "colonization," including Vladimir Jabotinsky's 1923 essay "The Iron Wall." [cite:massad_settler_colonial] Others describe Zionism as the national liberation movement of a refugee people with ancient roots in the land, seeking safety after centuries of persecution. Both frames have their advocates, and the choice between them shapes how every later event is read.

The practical rule that follows is simple: read the document first and the interpreter second. Facts drawn from archives are shared ground. Judgments about intent, and the choice of frame, are where scholars divide. The chapters that follow cite the documents and say whose interpretation is whose, so that a reader can tell the two apart. For primary documents and further reading on these debates, see Shlaim's The Iron Wall and Masalha's Palestine: A Four Thousand Year History, among other works.
