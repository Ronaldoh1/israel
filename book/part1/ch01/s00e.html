Jews and Arabs: A Thousand Years of Coexistence

For more than a thousand years, Jewish communities lived across the Muslim-majority Middle East and North Africa: in Baghdad, Cairo, Damascus, Aleppo, Sana'a, Fez, Tunis and Casablanca. In some cities they formed a plurality or even a majority. One of the most durable claims about this history holds that Jews and Arabs never lived together in peace, and that an ancient, irreconcilable hatred is the natural and permanent state between them. The documented record does not support it. That coexistence was never utopian, and real violence occurred. But it was neither marginal nor brief.

The best-known chapter is al-Andalus, as Muslim Iberia was called, home to what is known as the Jewish Golden Age of the tenth to twelfth centuries. Its figures included Maimonides (Cordoba, 1135–1204), Judah Halevi, Solomon ibn Gabirol and Samuel HaNagid. Jewish scholars wrote philosophy in Arabic, served as viziers and produced foundational works of rabbinic literature. The period was not free of violence; Granada saw a pogrom in 1066. Yet historians including María Rosa Menocal, David Nirenberg and Mark Cohen document a sustained period of Jewish-Muslim intellectual and civic collaboration without parallel in Christian Europe of the same era.[cite:menocal_ornament]

When Ferdinand and Isabella expelled the Jews of Spain in 1492, Sultan Bayezid II opened Ottoman territory to receive them. Salonika became a majority-Sephardic city for centuries. Under the Ottoman millet system, each religious community governed itself under its own leaders, with legal, educational and religious autonomy; the Chief Rabbi of Istanbul was responsible for all Ottoman Jews. Jewish communities lived in Istanbul, İzmir, Damascus, Aleppo, Baghdad, Cairo and Jerusalem. This was not equality with Muslims. As dhimmi, non-Muslim subjects of a Muslim state, Jews paid the jizya tax and lived under restrictions. But the system sustained four centuries of daily civic life. It produced Sephardic and Mizrahi literature, and the mystical school of Safed associated with Isaac Luria and Joseph Caro. Norman Stillman's documentary sourcebook The Jews of Arab Lands collects the primary evidence.[cite:stillman_jews_arab_lands]

Baghdad offers the clearest single case. Jews had lived in Mesopotamia continuously since the Babylonian Exile of 586 BCE, more than 2,500 years. This community compiled the Babylonian Talmud between roughly the third and sixth centuries CE. It is one of the two foundational texts of rabbinic Judaism, and it was produced in what became Iraq, not in the land of Israel. By the mid-twentieth century, Baghdad's Jewish population is documented at roughly 90,000 to 140,000, depending on the year and the source, close to a third of the city. Families such as the Sassoons, Kadoories and Basris built commercial networks across the British Empire. Iraq's first finance minister, Sassoon Heskel, was Jewish, and chief rabbis advised the government. In 1917 the community welcomed British forces. Sasson Somekh, a Baghdad-born Jew who later became a professor of Arabic literature at Tel Aviv University, recorded this world in his memoir Baghdad, Yesterday.[cite:somekh_baghdad_yesterday]

That world ended within a single decade. The Farhud of June 1941, which took place during a short-lived pro-Axis coup, marked a serious rupture. After 1948, legal restrictions, property confiscations and further violence sped up departures, while organized emigration efforts by Israel and the Jewish Agency actively encouraged them. Both forces are documented, and neither explains the exodus without the other. Ella Shohat has documented the second-class treatment many Iraqi Jews then met in Israel: one displacement followed by another.[cite:shohat_sephardim_israel] Today Baghdad's community is functionally gone. The events of 1948 to 1972 are told in full in [scene:s00e2|the account of the exodus and its aftermath].

None of this shows that persecution never happened. It shows that persecution was not the whole story. A community that endured twenty-five centuries in Mesopotamia, and Jewish communities that lived under Muslim rule from Iberia to Iraq for a millennium, do not fit the frame of ancient and permanent hatred. Nor does the record support the opposite simplification, of unbroken harmony. Centuries of integration and specific, dated ruptures belong in the same account. What broke that coexistence, and when, is a twentieth-century story, and it is the subject of what follows.
