Plan Dalet: The Document and the Argument

For decades a familiar account held that Palestinians left their homes in 1948 voluntarily, at the urging of Arab leaders, before any Jewish forces acted. One document stands squarely in the way of that account, and it is not in dispute. What historians dispute is what the document was for.

Plan Dalet, in Hebrew Tochnit Dalet, or Plan D, was issued by the Haganah general staff on 10 March 1948. It was the operational plan for the period before and after the British withdrawal. Its village provisions directed brigades, in operations against villages inside and near the territory to be held, to encircle and search them. Where resistance was met, the brigades were to destroy the village, "setting fire to, blowing up, and planting mines in the debris," and to expel the population beyond the borders of the state. Villages were to be garrisoned or demolished according to their strategic position. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] Put simply, the plan told commanders to hold villages that submitted and to destroy those that resisted and expel their inhabitants.

The text is a primary document. Historians agree on what it says. They disagree, working from the same archives, about its purpose, and the disagreement falls broadly into two readings with others in between.

The first reading treats the plan as a master plan. Walid Khalidi set it out in a 1961 essay titled "Plan Dalet: Master Plan for the Conquest of Palestine," and Ilan Pappé developed it in his 2006 book The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] In this view the document was the blueprint for deliberately removing the Arab population from the territory of the Jewish state.

The second reading belongs to Benny Morris, whose archival work documented the expulsions of 1948 in detail. Morris reads Plan Dalet as a military plan to secure territory and lines of communication before the Arab states invaded. Its village provisions, in his account, gave local commanders discretion, which they used in some places and not in others. Expulsions were decided locally rather than ordered from the center, and the outcome was produced by war and by decisions made at brigade level rather than by a single master plan. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] Other historians place themselves between these two positions.

The disagreement is narrower than it may first appear. Neither reading supports the older account of a purely voluntary departure that preceded any Jewish military action. Morris, the historian whose reading is less sweeping, is also the one whose research documented the expulsions in detail. The dispute is about design and causation: whether the text caused the depopulation by intention, or supplied the frame within which local commanders made it happen.

The plan's implementation followed in April and May 1948. Operation Nachshon, the fall of Haifa, Jaffa and Safed, and the depopulation of several hundred villages belong to that period and are taken up in the sections that follow ([scene:s10|independence and the Nakba]). The reader can judge there how closely the events fit the document.

This book records the text and the competing readings and does not resolve an argument that the record itself has not resolved. What is settled is the document's language, its date and its authorship by the Haganah general staff. What remains open, among serious historians, is the intent behind it.
