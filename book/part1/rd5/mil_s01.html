Three Militias Become One Army

It is often assumed that Israel's army was a single national force from the day of independence. It was not. On May 14, 1948 the new state inherited three armed organizations, the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi. Turning them into one army took four months, and at two points it took force.

The legal step came first. On 26 May 1948, twelve days after the declaration, David Ben-Gurion's provisional government issued an order establishing the Israel Defense Forces. The order outlawed any other armed force and required the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi to merge into the new army. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] On paper, that settled the question. In practice, it set up a test.

The test arrived by sea in June 1948. The Irgun brought an arms ship, the Altalena, to the coast, carrying weapons it intended partly for its own units. Ben-Gurion refused to let the cargo bypass the new army. On 22 June, IDF units under the command of Yitzhak Rabin shelled the ship off the Tel Aviv beach. Sixteen Irgun members and three IDF soldiers were killed. The Irgun's separate units were then disbanded. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] The episode settled, within a month of the army's creation, that weapons arriving for the state would pass through the state's own forces. It did not end the political career of the Irgun's commander. Menachem Begin became prime minister in 1977.

The second confrontation came in Jerusalem. On 17 September 1948, members of Lehi assassinated the United Nations mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte. The government declared Lehi a terrorist organization and arrested its members. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] The arrests were not the end of the matter either. In February 1949 the detained members were released under a general amnesty. One of Lehi's three leaders, Yitzhak Shamir, became prime minister in 1983. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination]

These events complete a line that runs through this book's account of the road to 1948, from the paramilitary organizations of the Mandate years to the army of a sovereign state. The pre-state undergrounds had carried out operations that earlier sections describe, among them the attack on Deir Yassin, carried out by the Irgun and Lehi with Haganah and Palmach support ([scene:ma_s01|the massacres of 1948]). After independence, the question was no longer what these groups would do against the Mandate or against Arab forces, but whether the new state would tolerate armed organizations it did not control.

The answer, by September 1948, was that it would not. The state established its monopoly on armed force within four months of its founding, by order, by shelling and by dissolution. Yet the sequel ran in another direction. Within thirty-five years, the leaders of both absorbed undergrounds had held the office of prime minister. The record of these months is therefore one of absorption rather than simple suppression: the state brought the militias under its control by force where necessary, and then, over the following decades, brought their leaders into its highest office.
