Beyond the Catholic Church: The Same Questions Asked of Others

The abuse record is part of this volume because the Holy See's sovereignty, secrecy rules and central control of canonical trials make it a distinct case. It is not part of the volume because abuse or its concealment is peculiar to Catholicism. Inquiries that looked at other churches and institutions found the same failures: priority given to reputation, survivors disbelieved, offenders moved or left in place. This section sets out five of those inquiries from their own reports, so that the Catholic record can be read against them. As in the previous section, each has its own method and scope, and no figure is comparable with another.

England and Wales: the Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse

The Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse (IICSA) was set up in 2015 and chaired, after its first chair resigned, by Professor Alexis Jay. Its fifteen investigations included the Anglican Church, the Roman Catholic Church and other religious organisations and settings. [cite:vt12_ct_iicsa2022] Its report on the Anglican Church, published on 6 October 2020, found that between the 1940s and 2018, 390 people who were clergy or held positions of trust in the Church of England had been convicted of sexual offences against children. It found that alleged abusers often received more support than victims, that record-keeping was poor, and that the Church had neglected "the well-being of children in favour of protecting its own reputation," creating "a culture where abusers were able to hide." Archbishop Justin Welby called the findings "profoundly and deeply shocking." [cite:vt12_iicsa_anglican2020]

The final report, published on 20 October 2022, drew on more than 7,300 victims and survivors. Its central recommendation was a legal duty on people in regulated activities to report known or suspected child sexual abuse; Jay said that "mandatory reporting should be an absolute obligation." It also recommended a national redress scheme and a Child Protection Authority. The Church of England said it would include mandatory reporting in its own work and that a working party was examining the seal of confession, which only its General Synod could change. [cite:vt12_ct_iicsa2022]

The Church of England: the Makin review and Welby's resignation, 2024

John Smyth QC abused boys and young men in England, in settings linked to the Iwerne holiday camps and Winchester College, and continued after moving to Africa in 1984; he died in Cape Town in 2018. The independent review by Keith Makin and Sarah Lawrence, published on 7 November 2024, put the number of his victims at between 115 and 130 and found an "active cover-up" after a report on him by the Reverend Mark Ruston in 1982. [cite:vt12_wiki_makin] Welby, who was told of the allegations in 2013 and was assured that the police had been informed, announced his resignation as Archbishop of Canterbury on 12 November 2024. He wrote that he had "believed wrongly that an appropriate resolution would follow" and accepted "personal and institutional responsibility." [cite:vt12_vn_welby2024] [cite:vt12_wiki_makin] The nearest Catholic parallel in this part is the Chilean bishops' collective offer to resign in 2018. [scene:vt12_accountability|Accountability: Chile, 2018]

The Southern Baptist Convention: the Guidepost report, 2022

The Southern Baptist Convention has no pope, no canon law and no central court; each congregation is autonomous. Delegates at its 2021 annual meeting voted for an outside investigation of the Convention's Executive Committee, which hired the firm Guidepost Solutions. Its report, released on 22 May 2022 after seven months, found that for about two decades a few senior Executive Committee leaders, working with outside lawyers, had "largely controlled" the response to abuse reports and were "singularly focused on avoiding liability." Survivors were ignored or disbelieved and some were told that congregational autonomy meant nothing could be done. A staff member had kept a private list of hundreds of accused ministers, and the investigators found no sign that anyone acted to keep them out of ministry. [cite:vt12_ap_guidepost2022] The finding matters for this volume because it shows the same conduct in a body with none of the Holy See's structures: no secrecy rule, no central tribunal, no sovereign immunity.

Australia: the Royal Commission across institutions

Australia's Royal Commission examined institutions of every kind, religious and secular. Its final report, published on 15 December 2017, drew on almost 8,000 private sessions. Of the survivors heard, 58.1 per cent had been abused in an institution run by a religious organisation; of those, 61.4 per cent were in Catholic institutions, 14.8 per cent in Anglican institutions and 7.2 per cent in Salvation Army institutions. [cite:vt12_guardian_rc2017] These are shares of the people who came to the Commission, not rates of abuse, and they do not account for how many children each body had in its care. The Commission's 189 new recommendations included two addressed to Catholic rules: that there be no exemption from mandatory reporting for abuse disclosed in confession, and that the Australian bishops ask the Holy See to consider voluntary celibacy for diocesan clergy. [cite:vt12_guardian_rc2017]

Germany: the Protestant ForuM study, 2024

The Evangelical Church in Germany (EKD), a federation of twenty regional Protestant churches, commissioned an independent research consortium that reported on 25 January 2024, four years and about €3 million later, on abuse in the churches and in their welfare organisation, Diakonie. In the files the researchers could examine they found 2,225 survivors and 1,259 alleged perpetrators, pastors being the largest group; extrapolating, they estimated about 9,300 survivors and 3,500 perpetrators since the war, and called the confirmed figures "the tip of the tip of the iceberg." Only one of the twenty churches cooperated fully, and the planned systematic review of personnel files did not take place. The EKD's president, Bishop Annette Kurschus, said the Church accepted the findings "in humility." [cite:vt12_it_forum2024] The comparison with the Catholic MHG study of 2018 is direct: both were commissioned by the churches themselves, both depended on files the churches chose to supply, and both described their figures as minimums. [cite:vt12_ba_mhg2018]

What the comparison shows, and what it does not

Placed side by side, the inquiries show that the core failures documented in Catholic dioceses (protecting the institution's reputation, disbelieving survivors, leaving offenders in post) also occurred in a national Anglican church, a decentralised Baptist convention and a federation of German Protestant churches. [cite:vt12_iicsa_anglican2020] [cite:vt12_ap_guidepost2022] [cite:vt12_it_forum2024] The comparison does not cancel what is particular to the Catholic case: a single legal system run from Rome that, until December 2019, placed abuse trials under the pontifical secret; a central office in Rome to which every credible case of child abuse had to be sent from 2001; and a sovereign at the top that can invoke immunity in foreign courts. [cite:vt12_va_ddg2001] [cite:vt12_vn_rescript2019] [scene:vt5_recognition|Part Five: immunity] It also does not settle relative rates: the Australian shares measure who came forward, not how often abuse occurred in each church. [cite:vt12_guardian_rc2017] What the comparison supports is a narrower reading: the failures documented in Catholic dioceses also appear in institutions without the Holy See's structures, and those structures bear on how cases were handled in the Catholic Church and who could be held to account, which is the subject of this part.
