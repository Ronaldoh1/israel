The Shield: When the Word Is Used to Stop a Question

The line that matters can be stated simply. Hatred of Jewish people is antisemitism, and nothing in this book says otherwise. A question about what a government does is a question about a government, whether the government is Canada's, Mexico's or Israel's. The popular shorthand that questioning Israel is itself antisemitic collapses that line. The record from 2010 to 2026 shows how the collapse happens, what it costs the person asking, and what is also true on the other side.

The starting point is the text of the definition most governments and universities cite, the working definition of the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance. It contains a sentence of its own on exactly this point: criticism of Israel similar to that leveled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic. [cite:ihra_text_safeguard] That is the standard the definition sets for itself. What its attached examples add, and what remains disputed, is where criticism of Israel crosses into hostility to Jews.

The definition's lead drafter has said publicly that it is being misused. Kenneth Stern was the American Jewish Committee's antisemitism expert. He has testified that the definition "was not drafted, and was never intended, as a tool to target or chill speech on a college campus"; it was written to help governments collect data on antisemitism. In a December 2019 Guardian op-ed headlined "I drafted the definition of antisemitism. Rightwing Jews are weaponizing it," he called the executive order applying it to campuses an attack on academic freedom that would harm pro-Palestinian advocates and Jewish students alike. [cite:stern_guardian_2019] In 2024 he told NPR that weaponizing it makes students less safe. [cite:npr_stern_2024] [cite:politifact_aaa_2024]

The guideline has since moved toward law. A December 2019 executive order directed federal agencies, including the Education Department's civil rights office, to consider the definition when enforcing Title VI. By August 2024, 35 states and the District of Columbia had adopted it in some form. On May 1, 2024, the House of Representatives passed the Antisemitism Awareness Act, which would write the definition into federal civil rights enforcement, by 320 votes to 91, with members of both parties voting no. The ACLU urged a no vote, warning that the bill would likely chill student speech by wrongly equating criticism of the Israeli government with antisemitism. As of October 2026 the bill is not law; the Senate version stalled in committee in 2025. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] [cite:legiscan_s558]

A label works by raising the price of a question, and the documented prices are concrete. Columbia University lost $400 million in federal funding within four days of a federal task-force notice in 2025, and about 60 more universities received warning letters. Five days after that notice, Mahmoud Khalil, a recent Columbia graduate who had negotiated on behalf of the protesters, was detained for deportation. In July 2024 Meta began treating "Zionist" as a stand-in for Jews in narrowly defined hate-speech cases after an advocacy campaign. Each of these cases has its own record, including real failures at Columbia that federal investigators documented. Together they show why many people choose not to ask.

The effect follows from the seriousness of the charge. Being called antisemitic is grave because antisemitism is a grave hatred. That gravity is exactly what gives the label force when it is aimed at a question about a government. A person who asks risks a job, a place at school, a visa or an online account, and so many stop asking, leaving the version they learned in school unexamined. This is what the ACLU and the definition's own drafter warned about. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] [cite:stern_guardian_2019] It is documented as an institutional effect, working through rules and penalties. This book does not claim to measure what happens inside any individual's mind.

The other half of the record matters as much. The charge is sometimes misused, and antisemitism is also real and rising. The ADL counted 9,354 antisemitic incidents in the United States in 2024, the most since it began counting in 1979. Federal investigators found that Columbia had no reporting system until the summer of 2024 and left swastika vandalism uninvestigated. Some speech that calls itself anti-Zionist does cross into hatred of Jews, and even the Jerusalem Declaration, written to defend criticism of Israel, lists examples of it. Many of the strongest critics of Israeli policy are themselves Jewish. Misusing the charge and ignoring real antisemitism both do damage, to Jews and to everyone asking honest questions.

Two questions sort almost every case. The first is whether a statement is aimed at Jewish people or at what a government does. The second is the definition's own standard: would this criticism be fair if it were aimed at any other country? If the subject is a government's actions, and the same thing would be said of Canada or Mexico, it is a political question, and asking it is not hatred. If the subject is Jews as a people, or holds them responsible for a state, or draws on old tropes, it is antisemitism, whatever it calls itself. [cite:ihra_text_safeguard]
