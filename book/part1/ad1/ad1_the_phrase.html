The Phrase: What James Truslow Adams Meant

The American Dream has a birth year. In 1931, in one of the worst years of the Great Depression, the historian James Truslow Adams published a one-volume history of the United States called The Epic of America, and made "the American dream" its theme. He had wanted to call the book The American Dream; his publishers would not let him. The book became a bestseller anyway, and the phrase outlived it. [cite:ad1_brandt_ah] Adams was not a romantic outsider. He had been a partner in a Wall Street firm from 1907 to 1913 before retiring from business in his mid-thirties to write history, and his The Founding of New England won the Pulitzer Prize for History in 1922. [cite:ad1_audit_adams_ebsco] The man who named the Dream knew both the counting-house and the archive.

What he meant by it matters, because most of this book measures the Dream in dollars, and Adams warned against exactly that. He described "that dream of a land in which life should be better and richer and fuller for every man," and then, in the next breath, said what it was not: "It is not a dream of motor cars and high wages merely." It was, he wrote, a dream of a social order in which each person could reach the fullest stature they were capable of, and be recognised for what they are "regardless of the fortuitous circumstances of birth or position." [cite:ad1_taylor_adams] Elsewhere he called it "the dominant motif in the American epic" and "a moving force as truly as wheat or gold," and wrote of "a better, richer, and happier life for all our citizens of every rank." [cite:ad1_taylor_adams] [cite:ad1_brandt_ah]

Two features of that definition carry through the rest of this book. First, the original Dream was about a social order, not a shopping list: what a country lets its people become, not what each of them owns. Second, Adams did not say material security was irrelevant. "Merely" is the hinge word. Cars and wages were part of the Dream, but not the whole of it, and the later shorthand that equates the Dream with a house, a pension and a rising paycheck is a narrowing of what he wrote, not a betrayal of it.

Older than the phrase

Adams named something he believed was already there, and the record bears him out. The Declaration of Independence of 1776 lists "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" among the unalienable rights governments exist to secure. [cite:ad1_decl] It promises the pursuit, not the happiness, and it says nothing about income. But it set the frame every later version works inside: the government's job is to keep the path open, and what each person finds at the end of it is their own.

Abraham Lincoln gave the economic version its first clear form. Speaking to the Wisconsin State Agricultural Society in Milwaukee on 30 September 1859, he described what he called the free-labour system: "The prudent, penniless beginner in the world, labors for wages awhile," saves a surplus to buy tools or land, works on his own account for a while, and at length hires another beginner to help him. Then he added the sentence that turns a description into a promise: "If any continue through life in the condition of the hired laborer, it is not the fault of the system," but of a dependent nature, improvidence, folly or singular misfortune. [cite:ad1_lincoln_1859] Two years later, in his message to Congress of 4 July 1861, he defined the purpose of the government the Union was fighting for: "to afford all an unfettered start and a fair chance in the race of life." [cite:ad1_lincoln_1861]

Those two Lincoln passages contain, in compressed form, both halves of what this book tests. The first is a claim about how the system works: wages lead to savings, savings to ownership, ownership to independence. The second is a claim about fault: when the ladder does not lead anywhere, the cause lies in the person, not the structure. The book's later parts take each mechanism in turn and ask which of the two halves the documented record supports, for whom, and when.

The popular fiction of the decade after the Civil War gave the promise a face. Horatio Alger wrote more than a hundred books for boys, which sold over 20 million copies; Ragged Dick, serialised in 1867 and published as a book in 1868, set the pattern of an honest, hardworking poor boy who is rewarded, though usually through luck. [cite:ad1_brit_alger] The story is remembered as "rags to riches," but that is not what happens in it. Dick saves, studies and refuses to steal; his break comes when he rescues a drowning child, and the grateful father gives him a job in his firm. He ends the book as a respectable clerk, not a rich man, which is why the critics Carl Bode and Gary Scharnhorst describe Alger's real theme as "rags to respectability." [cite:ad1_wiki_ragged] The best-known parable of American self-making turns, on the page, on a stroke of luck and a wealthy patron.

Adams was also not the first to use the words. The historian Sarah Churchwell has traced "the American dream" back to the 1910s and 1920s, when it stood for a broad vision of democratic and economic equality and was argued over in the same years as the slogan "America First." [cite:ad1_churchwell] The phrase was contested before it was popular, and it has stayed contested.

From a social order to a house

A dream about human development became, in common speech, a dream about a house with a yard, and part of the reason is that the house was sold, deliberately and early. Toward the end of the First World War, the National Association of Real Estate Boards launched an "Own Your Own Home" campaign, and the Department of Labor took it up in 1917–18, making it the first federal programme aimed explicitly at encouraging home ownership; it was purely promotional, with no money for buyers or builders. [cite:ad1_cannato] [cite:ad1_retsinas] In the early 1920s the Commerce Secretary, Herbert Hoover, led the Better Homes in America movement, backed by Presidents Harding and Coolidge. [cite:ad1_retsinas] Hoover wrote in 1925 that "Maintaining a high percentage of individual home owners is one of the searching tests" facing the nation. [cite:ad1_cannato]

Promotion alone did not move the numbers. The share of households owning their homes was 45.6 per cent in 1920, 47.8 per cent in 1930 and 43.6 per cent in 1940, after the Depression foreclosures. [cite:ad1_cannato] What changed it was finance. Before the New Deal, a typical mortgage ran five to ten years and covered about half the price of the house; the Federal Housing Administration's mortgage insurance made the fully amortised 20- to 30-year loan covering 80 per cent of the price the national norm. ("Fully amortised" means each monthly payment covers both interest and part of the principal, so the loan is paid off by the end rather than falling due in one large lump.) After the Second World War, the Veterans Administration guaranteed mortgages for returning veterans. [cite:ad1_cannato] [cite:ad1_retsinas]

Where it worked as promised

The postwar record is the strongest evidence that the promise, in its material form, could be kept on a national scale. Between 1940 and 1960 the home ownership rate rose from 43.6 to 61.9 per cent, an increase of 18.3 percentage points in twenty years, after three censuses (1920, 1930 and 1940) in which it had stayed between 43.6 and 47.8 per cent. [cite:ad1_cannato] For the generation that bought those homes, the chain Lincoln described (wages, savings, ownership) worked very close to the way it was later taught. The rate has never fallen back to its prewar level; in the second quarter of 2026 it stood at 65.2 per cent on the Census Bureau's seasonally adjusted measure, 65.0 per cent before adjustment. [cite:ad1_fred_hor] [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226]

The same record also shows who was left out. The developments that defined the new suburbs were sold with federal backing and private restrictions: Levittown on Long Island, advertised at $7,990 a house as "the housing buy of the century," used a 1947 rental contract that limited occupancy to "members of the Caucasian race," and in 1949 a salesman told a Black veteran that the builders "have not at this time decided to sell to Negroes." [cite:ad1_ah_levitt] How federal underwriting rules and private covenants shaped who could buy, and what that did to family wealth across generations, is the subject of [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|Part Six] and [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|Part Twelve]. For now the point is narrower: the Dream as a house became real for tens of millions of families at the same moment and by the same means that it was closed to others.

That is the inheritance the modern phrase carries. It began as a claim about what a free society lets people become, borrowed its economic logic from Lincoln's ladder, took its popular image from Alger's lucky clerk, and acquired its most concrete form, the owned home, through real estate promotion and federal mortgage finance.
