Afula, 1994

Hamas carried out its first attack in 1989. [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] The turn to killing civilians inside Israel came in April 1994. On 25 February 1994 Baruch Goldstein, an Israeli settler, opened fire on Muslim worshippers at the Ibrahimi Mosque in Hebron and killed 29 of them. On 6 April a Hamas suicide bomber blew up a car beside a bus in Afula, in northern Israel, and killed eight Israeli civilians. Hamas said it was revenge for Hebron. It was the first Palestinian suicide attack on civilians inside Israel; Matti Steinberg, an adviser to the Shin Bet, said that until then Hamas had refrained from attacking civilians inside Israel. [cite:hm_wiki_afula] The West Bank, Documented sets out the Hebron massacre and its aftermath. [scene:wb5_hebron|Hebron]

The bombings came in the years of the Oslo Accords, which Hamas opposed, and they continued, with pauses, for more than a decade. [scene:gz3_oslo|Oslo and Gaza] In October 1994 a Hamas bomber killed 22 people on a bus on Dizengoff Street in Tel Aviv. In the second intifada, from 2000, the campaign peaked: a Hamas bomber killed 21 people outside the Dolphinarium nightclub in Tel Aviv in June 2001, and another killed 16 at the Sbarro pizzeria in Jerusalem that August, in an attack claimed by both Hamas and Islamic Jihad. [cite:hm_wiki_suicidelist] On the first night of Passover in 2002, a Hamas bomber killed 30 people at the Park Hotel in Netanya; in March 2002 alone, Palestinian attacks killed 135 Israelis. [cite:hm_wiki_suicide]

In 2002 Human Rights Watch found that those who planned and carried out suicide bombings against civilians, naming Hamas, Islamic Jihad, the al-Aqsa Martyrs' Brigades and the PFLP, were guilty of crimes against humanity: "The people who carry out suicide bombings are not martyrs, they're war criminals," said its director, Kenneth Roth. [cite:hm_hrw_erased] Human Rights Watch later reached a similar finding on the rocket fire that replaced the bombings, which Part Six sets out. [scene:hm6_rockets|The rockets]

The Leaders Targeted

Israel's answer included killing Hamas's leaders. On 25 September 1997, two months after a Hamas suicide bombing in Jerusalem's Mahane Yehuda market, two Mossad agents tried to poison Khaled Mashaal, a Hamas leader living in Jordan, on a street in Amman; one sprayed him, and Jordanian police caught both. King Hussein demanded the antidote; Israel supplied it and Mashaal recovered. To recover its agents and repair relations with Jordan, Israel released Ahmed Yassin from prison, and he went back to Gaza to lead Hamas. [cite:hm_toi_mashaal]

On 22 March 2004 Israeli helicopters fired missiles at Yassin as he left dawn prayers in Gaza City, killing him and nine others nearby. Israel's UN ambassador, Dan Gillerman, said: "When you kill our citizens, you will not be spared." Britain's foreign secretary, Jack Straw, called the killing "unacceptable, unjustified". Yassin's successor in Gaza, Abdel Aziz al-Rantisi, was killed weeks later. [cite:hm_wiki_yassin]
