Midnight Hammer

On 21 June 2025 the United States joined the war. Seven B-2 bombers took off from Whiteman Air Force Base in Missouri around midnight and flew east for about eighteen hours with almost no radio contact. They dropped fourteen GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrators, bombs built to reach deeply buried targets, on the enrichment plants at Fordow and Natanz. A submarine fired more than two dozen Tomahawk cruise missiles at the nuclear site at Isfahan. In all, more than 125 American aircraft took part. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Dan Caine, said initial assessments showed "extremely severe damage and destruction" at all three sites, but that "final battle damage will take some time." The Defense Secretary, Pete Hegseth, said the mission "was not, has not, been about regime change." [cite:ir10_bd_hammer]

Trump spoke from the White House at 10:02 that evening. He said: "Our objective was the destruction of Iran's nuclear enrichment capacity." He said that "Iran's key nuclear enrichment facilities have been completely and totally obliterated." He thanked and congratulated Netanyahu, and warned: "There will be either peace or there will be tragedy for Iran... Remember, there are many targets left." [cite:ir10_gov_address] Congress had not authorised the strike. On 27 June the Senate voted 47 to 53, rejecting a resolution by Senator Tim Kaine that would have required congressional approval for further hostilities against Iran. [cite:hz12b_ap_jun2025] Costs of War at Brown University later estimated the cost of the June operations at $2.04 to $2.25 billion. Most of that, $1.8 to $1.95 billion, was THAAD interceptors fired in Israel's defence. The bombers, the GBU-57s and the Tomahawks cost far less. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] Part Fourteen sets out the wider bill. [scene:ir14_bill|The American bill]

Iran replied on 23 June with missiles fired at Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar, where US forces are based. It warned in advance. According to Axios, Qatar and the Trump administration were both told beforehand. Qatari air defences intercepted the missiles, and one struck near the base without causing casualties. Iran's Supreme National Security Council said the number of missiles matched the number of bombs the United States had dropped. Axios read this as a signal that Iran wanted to de-escalate. Qatar condemned the attack and called for a return to negotiations. [cite:ir10_ax_udeid] Trump said that "NO Americans were harmed, and hardly any damage was done." [cite:ir10_gov_udeid] He thanked Iran for the "early notice." [cite:ir10_ax_udeid] Iran's choices that day are documented: it struck a US base, it warned beforehand, and it fired a matching, symbolic number. They bear directly on whether Iran wanted war with the United States, which Part Twelve examines. [scene:ir12_question|Does Iran want war?]

The Ceasefire

That evening Trump announced a "complete and total" ceasefire. It took effect early on 24 June. A White House official said Vice President JD Vance, Rubio and Witkoff had talked to Iran "through direct and indirect channels." Qatar played what the report called "an important role," and Trump thanked its emir, Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad al-Thani. [cite:ir10_en_ceasefire] Some accounts credit Oman with brokering the ceasefire. This book did not find a primary source for that, so it states only the announcement and Qatar's role. Netanyahu said that Israel "will respond forcefully to any violation." Strikes continued up to the deadline: local authorities reported five killed in Beersheba, and nine were reported killed in Iran. [cite:ir10_en_ceasefire] That morning Trump said Iran had violated the truce "but Israel violated it too." He wrote: "ISRAEL. DO NOT DROP THOSE BOMBS." [cite:ir10_kt_violations] The same day Netanyahu declared a "historic victory" and said: "For decades, I have promised you that Iran would not have nuclear weapons." [cite:ir5_pmo_jun2025] The ceasefire held for eight months. On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel attacked Iran together, and the Supreme Leader was killed. [cite:ow7_npr_epicfury] Part Eleven covers that war. [scene:ir11_war|The 2026 war]

Obliterated, or Set Back?

How much damage the strikes did became a dispute within days. On 24 June CNN and other outlets reported a preliminary Defense Intelligence Agency (DIA) assessment that the programme had been set back by only months. The assessment was of low confidence and was made soon after the strikes. Fuller analyses could reach different conclusions. On 25 June the CIA Director, John Ratcliffe, said that "a body of credible intelligence indicates Iran's Nuclear Program has been severely damaged." He said several key facilities "would have to be rebuilt over the course of years." The CIA did not release its evidence. Gabbard said the facilities had been "destroyed." The Financial Times, citing European officials, reported that Iran's highly enriched uranium was largely intact. At the NATO summit Trump described the DIA report himself: "They said it could be limited or it could be very severe." [cite:ir10_ng_cia] On 2 July the Pentagon spokesman, Sean Parnell, said the programme had been degraded by "probably closer to two years." He did not explain how that estimate was reached. Grossi said Iran could resume enrichment within "a matter of months," calling the damage "severe" but "not total." [cite:ir10_ss_parnell]

In August 2025 Hegseth removed the DIA's director, Lieutenant General Jeffrey Kruse. Anonymous sources gave the reason as "loss of confidence." The Pentagon did not comment. Reporting placed the firing after the leaked assessment, but no official linked the two. [cite:ir10_adn_kruse] The connection is a sequence of events, not a documented cause.

Later evidence supports serious damage while leaving the most important material unaccounted for. An analysis of the IAEA's June 2026 reports by the Institute for Science and International Security, a Washington research group, concluded that "Iran has no identifiable route to produce weapon-grade uranium" in its centrifuge plants. This was the Institute's own assessment, not an IAEA finding. [cite:ir10_isis_jun26] In June 2026 AP reported that, before the wars, Iran had been "months or years away" from a usable weapon, with no evidence it had decided to build one. Netanyahu said that without the two operations Iran "would have had atomic bombs." [cite:ir5_ap_jun2026]

The Inspectors and the Missing Uranium

Iran then cut back the IAEA's access. On 25 June the Majlis voted 221 to 0, with one abstention, to suspend cooperation with the agency. The speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said the IAEA had "put its international credibility up for auction." [cite:ir10_dp_majlis] On 2 July Iranian state television reported that President Masoud Pezeshkian had signed the law. It requires approval from the Supreme National Security Council before inspectors can visit nuclear sites. Iranian officials described the Board's 12 June resolution as one of the "excuses" for the attacks. The US State Department called the suspension "unacceptable," and Germany called it a "disastrous signal." [cite:ir10_aj_iaealaw] Iran and the IAEA reached the Cairo agreement on 9 September 2025 to resume inspections. Later that month UN sanctions were reimposed under the snapback mechanism that Britain, France and Germany had triggered in August, and on 20 November Iran ended the agreement. [cite:ir7_crs_snapback] [cite:ir10_isis_jun26] By November the agency had inspected only undamaged sites such as Bushehr. Iran said that "any cooperation with the Agency is conditional on the decision" of the Supreme National Security Council. [cite:ir10_ap_nov25]

The question that mattered most was what had happened to the uranium. The IAEA's last estimate, for 13 June 2025, the day the attacks began, was 440.9 kilograms enriched up to 60 per cent; earlier figures had been verified, this one was not. [cite:ir10_isis_jun26] Grossi said it could be enough for as many as ten bombs if Iran chose to build them, but that this did not mean Iran had a weapon. [cite:ir10_ap_nov25] Soon after the strikes he said: "We do not have information of the whereabouts of this material." [cite:ir10_fox_grossi] A year later the position had not changed. In its June 2026 report the IAEA said it "cannot provide any information on the current size, composition or whereabouts of the stockpile." It reported regular vehicle activity around the entrance to the tunnel complex at Isfahan, where some of the material had been stored, in February 2026, and asked Iran for access. The agency had stopped verification in Iran after 28 February 2026. [cite:ir10_isis_jun26] As of October 2026 the public record does not show the stockpile accounted for.

The twelve days can be summarised in three parts. The documented facts are these. Israel struck during active US–Iran talks and the day after an IAEA non-compliance finding. The United States knew the attack was coming, publicly stood aside, and then bombed the three main nuclear sites. Iran's retaliation against the United States was warned in advance and caused no casualties. [cite:ir10_wh_rubio] [cite:ir10_bd_hammer] [cite:ir10_ax_udeid] The pattern that can be shown is that each government's claims ran ahead of its evidence. The "obliterated" claim was not supported by the first intelligence assessment. Israel's claim that Iran was moving to weaponise was not matched by any public US or IAEA finding. Iran's insistence that its programme was peaceful sat beside a 60 per cent stockpile it would not let anyone verify. [cite:ir10_ng_cia] [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] [cite:ir10_ap_nov25] The question of motive remains an argument. On one reading, Israel's attack drew the United States into a war it had been trying to avoid by negotiation. On the other, Trump chose to bomb on his own stated aim of ending Iran's enrichment, a demand his envoy had already made at the table. [cite:ir10_ap_round4] Part Thirteen weighs both readings.
