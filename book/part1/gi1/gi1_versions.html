Three Versions and Who Held Them

This volume sorts the claims made under the name "Greater Israel" into three versions: a maximal version drawn from the broad biblical texts, a Revisionist version claiming both banks of the Jordan, and a post-1967 version claiming the land between the Jordan and the Mediterranean, with the Golan Heights and, for a time, Gaza and Sinai. The three-part division is an organising device used by this library, not a category any scholar or party uses for itself. Each version is tied below to the named movements and documents that held it, and set beside the currents that rejected it. The political scientist Nadav Shelef, who traced how religious nationalist groups defined the homeland between 1925 and 2005, found that the claims changed over that period and judged that the change "is not yet finished, nor is it irreversible." [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael]

The maximal version

The maximal version takes the Genesis and Deuteronomy promises as a map: from the river of Egypt, read as the Nile, to the Euphrates. [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] [cite:gi1_bg_deut1124] Its documented carriers have been few. According to Shelef, in 1925 a minority of Religious Zionist groups backed a homeland on that scale; by 2005 the mainstream of those groups had narrowed their claim to Israel, the Golan, Gaza and the West Bank, while the hardline Hardal wing of Religious Zionism later revived the larger claim. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] The one founder-era trace is Herzl's 1898 diary note of a plan, never carried out, to ask the Ottoman sultan for land from the Egyptian frontier to the Euphrates. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] No Israeli government has adopted the maximal version as policy, and the laws, platforms and coalition agreements reviewed for this volume claim no territory in Egypt, Iraq or Syria beyond the Golan. Statements by individual ministers that invoke wider biblical horizons are examined, with their wording checked, in Parts Five, Seven and Eleven. [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five: Likud in government] [scene:gi11_religious|Part Eleven: the religious engine]

The Revisionist version: both banks of the Jordan

The second version belonged to the Revisionist Zionist movement led by Ze'ev Jabotinsky. In 1929 Jabotinsky wrote the poem known as "The East Bank of the Jordan", first given to a student union in Kaunas on 18 November 1929 and published in Jerusalem in April 1930; its refrain declares that both banks of the Jordan belong to the Jewish people, and it became one of the best-known songs of Betar, the Revisionist youth movement. [cite:gi1_wiki_eastbank] Betar and the Irgun, the Revisionist underground, regarded the whole territory of the British Mandate, including Transjordan, as the land to be claimed. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] Part Two follows this current, its maps and the emblem of the Irgun. [scene:gi2_bothbanks|Part Two: both banks of the Jordan]

The Revisionists were a minority within the Zionist movement on exactly this point. The Labor Zionist leadership accepted partition, and in 1947 the Jewish Agency accepted the United Nations plan that divided western Palestine alone between a Jewish and an Arab state. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_myths_partition] [scene:wb1_partition|The West Bank, Documented: the partition plan] The claim to the east bank did not survive in Likud's founding document: the 1977 platform opposed any plan that "relinquishes parts of western Eretz Israel", a formula that confines the claim to the land west of the river. [cite:gi1_jvl_likud1977] Part Eight takes up Jordan and the later slogan that "Jordan is Palestine". [scene:gi8_jordan|Part Eight: Jordan]

The post-1967 version: between the sea and the Jordan

The third version was born in the weeks after the war of June 1967, in which Israel captured the West Bank, East Jerusalem, Gaza, Sinai and the Golan Heights. In July 1967 a group of writers, soldiers and public figures founded the Movement for Greater Israel, in Hebrew HaTnu'a Lema'an Eretz Yisrael HaShlema, which called on the government to keep the captured areas and settle them. [cite:gi1_wiki_mgi] Its founders did not come from the Revisionist right alone. They included leading figures of the Labor movement, among them Yitzhak Tabenkin, Nathan Alterman, Haim Gouri, Rachel Yanait Ben-Zvi, Zivia Lubetkin and Yitzhak Zuckerman, alongside Uri Zvi Greenberg, Israel Eldad, Shmuel Katz, S. Y. Agnon, Isser Harel and Avraham Yoffe. [cite:gi1_wiki_mgi] Its electoral support was small. Its list won 7,561 votes, 0.6 per cent, in the 1969 election and no seat; it then joined Likud in 1973 and was absorbed into it. [cite:gi1_wiki_mgi] Part Four examines the movement, its manifesto and the Gush Emunim settler movement that followed it in 1974. [cite:wb_ej_gush] [scene:gi4_movement|Part Four: the Land of Israel Movement]

This version entered government with Menachem Begin. The Likud platform on which he won the 1977 election stated that "the right of the Jewish people to the land of Israel is eternal and indisputable", that "Judea and Samaria will not be handed to any foreign administration" and that "between the Sea and the Jordan there will only be Israeli sovereignty." [cite:gi1_jvl_likud1977] Begin did not annex the West Bank or Gaza once in office. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] The guidelines of Israel's 37th government, sworn in at the end of December 2022, opened with a sentence in the same line of descent: "The Jewish people has an exclusive and inalienable right to all parts of the Land of Israel." [cite:gi1_jns_guidelines] Parts Five and Six follow what was legislated and done under it, and what was not. [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five: Likud in government] [scene:gi6_plans|Part Six: the annexation track]

The currents that said no

Each version has had opponents inside the Zionist movement and inside Israel, and their record belongs in the account. After the Zionist Congress turned down the British offer of territory in East Africa, Israel Zangwill founded the Territorialist Jewish Organization in Basle in July 1905 to look for land for Jewish settlement in Africa, Asia and Australia; it dissolved in 1925. [cite:gi1_jvl_territorialism] Labor's own post-1967 plan, put forward by Yigal Allon on 27 July 1967, proposed keeping the Jordan Valley, East Jerusalem and the Etzion area while offering the populated hill country to Jordan; it was never formally adopted, and King Hussein rejected it. [cite:wb_wiki_allon] [scene:gi4_allon|Part Four: the Allon Plan] Israeli governments withdrew from captured land: from Sinai in April 1982 under the Camp David agreements with Egypt, from south Lebanon in May 2000 and from Gaza in 2005, removing its settlements. [cite:gi1_csm_sinai1982] [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [cite:gz_wiki_disengagement] In 2008 Olmert declared the idea of Greater Israel finished. [cite:gi1_ynet_olmert] [scene:gi12_withdrawals|Part Twelve: the withdrawals]

The three versions are not stages through which a single movement passed. They were held by different people, at different times, with different texts, and each met opposition from other Israelis and other Zionists. What links them is a phrase. The parts that follow test which of its versions became the documented policy of a named actor, and which remained a poem, a slogan, a sermon or a rumour.
