This volume is about Christian Zionism: the belief, held by some Protestants, that God's promises to Abraham still bind Christians to support the Jewish people's return to the land and, since 1948, the State of Israel. It was written to answer direct questions. Where did the belief come from, and is it true that Cyrus Scofield invented it? What did Darby's system and Scofield's notes actually teach about Israel, and who paid for the notes? How did a Protestant reading of prophecy become an organised political force, from Blackstone's petition of 1891 to Christians United for Israel, and what did Israeli governments and American politicians gain from it? How does what this volume calls the theology shield work, the move that turns a dispute about a state's policies into a dispute with God, and where has it failed? And what do the churches, Palestinian Christians and a younger generation of evangelicals say against it?

The record answers some of these questions and not others. It shows Christian belief in a Jewish return in print from 1609, a Foreign Secretary asking the sultan in 1840 to admit Jewish settlers as "a check" on Egypt's ruler, and a creed adopted at Niagara in 1878 that Israel "shall be restored to their own land," all before the Scofield Bible of 1909. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] [cite:ts2_cojs_palmerston1840] [cite:ts3_ak_niagara] It shows Scofield jailed on forgery charges in 1878 and released when they were dropped on 6 November 1879, his Bible funded by Brethren-linked businessmen, and no document behind the story that Samuel Untermyer or the Rothschilds paid for it. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_sfiles] [cite:ts4_cyf] It shows the International Fellowship of Christians and Jews reporting $318 million in revenue for 2024, a vice president promising the embassy move at CUFI's summit in 2017, two Texas pastors praying at its opening on 14 May 2018, and a president saying in 2020 that it was "for the evangelicals." [cite:ts8_pp_ifcj] [cite:ts12_ncr_pence2017] [cite:ts12_cnn_pastors] [cite:ts9_almon_trump2020] It shows white evangelical Protestants in May 2026 the only American religious group with a majority favourable to the Israeli government, 57 per cent, down from 71 per cent in 2024, with Republicans aged 18 to 29 at 28 per cent. [cite:ts11_pew_2026] [cite:ts11_crosswalk_pew] It does not show that end-times belief caused any American decision on its own, that Scofield's notes called for a Jewish state, or that the millions who tell pollsters they support Israel reason the way the shield's most vocal users do. The book is in fourteen parts. The first twelve follow the belief from the Puritans to the officials of 2017 to 2026. [scene:ts13_israel_lens|The inner reading] Part Fourteen keeps a ledger of the claims made about Christian Zionism and sorts the volume's findings into what is settled, contested and open as of October 2026. [scene:ts14_myths|The ledger] [scene:ts14_record|What the record settles] Throughout, beliefs and acts are attributed to named churches, organisations and individuals, never to Christians, evangelicals, Jews or Israelis as a whole; survey figures carry their survey, date and population; myths are named as myths, with the evidence, including the antisemitic accusation behind the story of a Jewish financier steering Christian belief; and the stated position of each person or organisation is set beside the criticism of it. Several stories often told about the Scofield Bible, a forgery conviction, a second divorce, an Untermyer subsidy, a 1917 reference to the Balfour Declaration and a framework virtually absent before 1909, find no support in the record.
