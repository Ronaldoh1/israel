Two Real Histories, Not One Winner

Arguments over who belongs to this land usually rest on a single assumption: that only one population has a real, ancient claim, and that the other's national identity is a recent invention without roots. Whichever side it favors, the argument makes the same error. It folds three different questions into one: whether a population was present, whether its culture was continuous, and when it formed a national identity in the modern political sense. The documented record, from the Ottoman period through 1948, treats these as separate questions with separate answers.

The first answer concerns Jewish presence, and it is not in doubt. Ancient Jewish kingdoms existed in parts of the land. Jewish religious, cultural and historical ties to the region are documented across archaeological, textual and religious sources spanning more than three thousand years. Mainstream historians of every political orientation accept this continuity. [cite:substack_historical_record_balfour] Ancient presence alone, however, does not settle modern questions of sovereignty or statehood. Historical presence and modern political legitimacy are related, but they are not the same thing.

The second answer concerns the Arab population, and it is just as well documented. The Ottoman census of 1871–72 counted the population of Palestine, excluding Bedouin, at 381,954: roughly 85 percent Muslim, 11 percent Christian and 4 percent Jewish. The presence of this Arabic-speaking population under Ottoman rule is documented continuously for centuries, and modern genetic and archaeological studies find strong Levantine continuity consistent with long-term local ancestry. [cite:cjpme_historical_demography] [cite:factually_archaeological_evidence] The land was not empty, and its people were not newcomers. Still, a counted and continuous population is not yet a formal national identity.

The third answer concerns national identity, and here the leading Palestinian historian has documented the timeline himself. Rashid Khalidi is Edward Said Professor of Arab Studies at Columbia and a past president of the Middle East Studies Association. In his foundational study Palestinian Identity: The Construction of Modern National Consciousness, he argues that formal Palestinian national identity is modern. In his account it grew out of nationalist currents emerging among Ottoman peoples in the late nineteenth century and sharpened in what he calls the critical years of 1917 to 1923. [cite:wiki_palestinian_nationalism] [cite:cup_khalidi_palestinian_identity] Khalidi is explicit that this modern consciousness drew on a much older regional history and population. He does not treat its late emergence as proof that the people or their culture were invented, and his finding should not be read that way.

Zionism follows the same pattern. Formal Zionist national identity was organized at the First Zionist Congress in Basel in 1897: a modern political movement built on older religious and cultural material, as earlier sections of this book document. Formal Palestinian national identity, emerging in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, has the same structure. A rule that says "this national identity is recent, therefore illegitimate," applied to one side and not the other, is an inconsistent standard, not a historical finding. The parallel concerns how national consciousness formed. It does not mean the two movements' histories run alike in every other respect.

Taken together, the record supports three facts at once. There is an ancient and continuous Jewish connection to the land. There is a substantial, continuously documented Arab population under Ottoman rule. And there are modern national identities on both sides, formed through comparable political processes in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. None of these cancels the others. The question "who is truly indigenous" casts history as a single contest with one legitimate winner, and the documents support that frame for neither side. Nor do they resolve any present-day question of sovereignty or statehood. What they show is the shape of the history on which those questions rest.
