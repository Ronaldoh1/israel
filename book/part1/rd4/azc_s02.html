The Ground Game, From the Organizers' Own Files

It is often said that American political support for a Jewish state grew spontaneously out of public sympathy. The sympathy was real. So were the letters, telegrams and visits that expressed it. What the organizers' own papers show is that much of this expression was also organized, timed and aimed. The internal correspondence of the American Zionist Emergency Council, preserved in the Abba Hillel Silver Papers at the American Jewish Archives, documents how the work was done from day to day.

The structure was national. From headquarters at 342 Madison Avenue in New York, memos went out to "Chairmen of Local Emergency Committees" in cities across the country. When the Wagner–Taft Palestine resolution was introduced in January 1944, one memo told local chairs that they would receive sample letters, so that in a concentrated period letters from all over the country would pour into Washington. The same instructions told local committees to interview their senators and representatives and to place letters to the editor in their local newspapers [cite:aja_azec_local_1944] [cite:aja_azec_local_1945]. The mail was, in part, a campaign scheduled from New York.

The council also worked on Congress as a body. Every member of Congress received its book America and Palestine, which printed the pro-Zionist statements of nearly 400 members of the 78th Congress [cite:aja_azec_silver_resignation]. The design was simple. Each legislator could see, in print, the public commitments his own colleagues had already made.

Pressure was not reserved for opponents. Telegram campaigns were directed at named members, including Representative Sol Bloom, chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee and himself a supporter of the cause [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]. In 1945 local councils mobilized thousands of telegrams to President Truman; in Detroit alone, 150 Zionist and community leaders organized the appeal. The discipline extended to friends who strayed. In 1948, during the American retreat toward trusteeship, Bloom remarked that Truman was "on the right track." An AZEC staff memo called the statement "horrible" and urged that people in Bloom's own district raise it with him [cite:aja_bloom_1948].

One further claim comes from a critical source and should be read as such. A critic's history of the period reports that mail addressed to Senator Robert Wagner, in his capacity as chairman of the American Palestine Committee, was opened and kept at Zionist headquarters, with Wagner receiving a copy [cite:hnn_clark_truman]. This account comes from a critical history rather than from the council's own files, and it has not been confirmed here from those papers.

Taken together, the documents describe a method more than a budget. The letters were sincere, and the sympathy behind them was genuine. Both were also organized, timed and directed at particular members of Congress and at the White House. That method, rather than money, is what the movement's own files document most fully.
