Three Cities Side by Side

The three cities in this volume are usually described together, as if they were the same kind of place. Set side by side, they turn out to have almost nothing in common except an unusual relationship with the country around them. One is a medieval corporation with fewer residents than a small town and one of the largest financial markets in the world inside its boundary. One is the smallest state on earth, the seat of the Catholic Church's central government. One is a federal district of nearly seven hundred thousand people, governed in the last resort by a legislature its residents do not vote for. This section compares them on the points that matter for the question of power: what each one is in law, how large it is, who governs it, who polices it, who votes, and who speaks for it abroad.

The City of London. The City covers 1.12 square miles and had 8,583 residents at the 2021 census. It is surrounded by Greater London but remains separate from the London boroughs, and as a ceremonial county it is unique in one small way: its Commission of Lieutenancy is headed by the Lord Mayor rather than by a Lord-Lieutenant appointed by the Crown. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] Inside its boundary lie the Inner Temple and the Middle Temple, two of the few surviving "liberties," enclaves treated as independent and outside any parish. [cite:dv1_wiki_city] The City elects its own council and its own Lord Mayor, and it is one of very few places in Britain where businesses as well as residents vote; Part Two sets out how that franchise works and how large it is. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation]

The City also has its own police force, separate from London's Metropolitan Police. The Police Act 1996 lists "the City of London police area" as one of the police areas of England and Wales and provides that the Common Council "is to continue to be the police authority" for it. The Common Council must issue a policing plan before each financial year, and the Commissioner of Police for the City of London must report to it. [cite:dv1_police_act_1996] This is the clearest single example of how the City differs from an ordinary London borough. It is also a reminder of where the difference comes from: the force exists and answers to the Common Council because an Act of Parliament says so, and Parliament can change the arrangement. [cite:dv1_police_act_1996]

Vatican City. Vatican City is the smallest state in the world, and even its size is recorded two ways. Some references give 49 hectares (0.49 square kilometers) and others 44 hectares (109 acres); the difference has been traced to an atlas of 1930 that listed 44 hectares and was corrected to 49 in 1945 and 1946. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] Both figures are in circulation, and this volume records both. In 2024 the city had 882 residents of any citizenship, and a further 372 Vatican citizens, mostly diplomats and cardinals, lived elsewhere. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican]

The city is an elective absolute monarchy. The pope holds all executive, legislative and judicial power, and is chosen for life by the cardinals in conclave; the present pope, Leo XIV, was elected on 8 May 2025. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] Its security is kept by two bodies. The Corps of Gendarmerie, founded by Pope Pius VII in 1816, numbered 122 sworn members in 2022 and handles policing, border and traffic control and criminal investigation under the Governorate of the Vatican City State; it also hosts Interpol's national bureau for the city. [cite:dv1_wiki_gendarmerie] The Pontifical Swiss Guard, which dates its founding to 22 January 1506, was enlarged from 110 to 135 members in 2019, and recruits only unmarried Catholic Swiss men who have completed Swiss military training. [cite:dv1_wiki_swissguard] Between them the two bodies number about one member for every three or four of the city's residents,, a ratio no ordinary city approaches.

The city's foreign relations are not its own. They are conducted by the Holy See, the authority the pope holds over the Catholic Church, which is a separate subject of international law from the territory over which it has direct sovereignty. [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] The Holy See has full diplomatic relations with 184 states, as well as the European Union and the Order of Malta. It has been a permanent observer state at the United Nations since 6 April 1964 and has chosen not to seek membership, while belonging in its own right to several of the UN's specialized agencies, among them the World Intellectual Property Organization, the International Telecommunication Union and the Universal Postal Union. [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] [cite:dv1_wiki_holysee_un] When this volume says "Rome" or "the Vatican" acted toward Palestine or Israel, it nearly always means the Holy See, not the city, and nearly always means acts that predate the city's existence or reach far beyond it.

The District of Columbia. The district had an estimated 693,645 residents on 1 July 2025, on 61.13 square miles of land. [cite:dv1_census_dc] Its legal basis is a single clause of the Constitution, which gives Congress power "to exercise exclusive Legislation in all Cases whatsoever" over a district "not exceeding ten Miles square" ceded by particular states to become the seat of government. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] Over that district Congress holds the powers of both a local and a national legislature. It is not required to give the district an elected government, and it is not forbidden to delegate its powers to one. [cite:dv1_conan_dc]

The district's own government has risen and fallen with Congress's choices. An elected mayor governed from 1820; elected government ended after financial scandals in 1874; a commission appointed by the President ruled from 1878, and an appointed mayor-commissioner and council from 1967. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] The present arrangement rests on the Home Rule Act, approved on 24 December 1973, which provided for an elected mayor and council. [cite:dv1_crs_dc_homerule] It came with conditions that no state government faces. Most of the council's laws take effect only after lying before Congress for thirty days in session, and laws on crime and criminal procedure for sixty. All of the district's spending must pass through the federal appropriations process. [cite:dv1_crs_dc_homerule] Congress has used its formal veto sparingly, enacting resolutions to disapprove district laws three times, in 1979, 1981 and 1990, and has more often shaped the district's affairs through riders attached to appropriations bills. [cite:dv1_crs_dc_homerule] District residents vote for President under the Twenty-third Amendment and elect a delegate to Congress who cannot vote on the floor; an amendment to give them full representation failed to win ratification. [cite:dv1_conan_dc]

What the comparison shows. The three cities are unusual in three opposite directions. The City of London has more self-government than its size would suggest, kept by privilege and statute rather than by sovereignty. Vatican City has full sovereignty over almost no territory, and its significance lies in the church authority it houses rather than the ground it covers. The District of Columbia has less self-government than any comparable American city, because it was designed to belong to the federal government rather than to any state. The popular story that groups them as three sovereign city-states treats these three different kinds of strangeness as one. They are not one, and the power that each name stands for, the London market and the British state, the Holy See, and the government of the United States, has to be traced separately. That is the plan of the three parts that follow.
