Three Thousand Years in Brief: Judah, Rome, Palaestina, Islam, Crusaders, Ottomans

Both peoples in this history invoke a deep past, and both are often told a version of it that erases the other. One version holds that Jews were absent from the land for two thousand years. The opposite version holds that Palestinians appeared only in the twentieth century. The record supports neither.

Jewish kingdoms of Israel and Judah existed in the first millennium BCE. Rome conquered the region in 63 BCE and in 6 CE created a predominantly Jewish province, Judaea. Two great revolts against Roman rule failed. The first ended with the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE. The second, the Bar Kokhba revolt of 132 to 135 or 136, ended in catastrophe for Judea's Jews. Rome killed, enslaved or expelled much of the Jewish population, rebuilt Jerusalem as a pagan colony called Aelia Capitolina, and barred Jews from entering it. It also renamed the province Syria Palaestina.[cite:wiki_syria_palaestina] Most scholars see that renaming as an attempt to sever the Jewish connection to the land.[cite:wiki_palestine_region]

The erasure was not complete. A smaller Jewish presence continued without interruption, with Galilee as its religious center, and much classical Jewish literature was produced there.[cite:encyclo_israel_history] The land itself passed through a long succession of rulers: Byzantine Christian rule; Muslim rule from the seventh century; the Crusader kingdoms; the Mamluks in the thirteenth century; and from 1516 the Ottoman Empire, which governed for four centuries.[cite:wiki_palestine_region]

In the sixteenth century, Jewish communities took root in four cities regarded as holy: Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed. These communities formed what is known as the "Old Yishuv," the Jewish population of the land before the Zionist movement. It remained a minority, and its size rose and fell over time.[cite:encyclo_israel_history]

Over the same centuries, the majority population became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] These are the ancestors of today's Palestinians. Their presence is documented across those centuries; they did not appear only in the twentieth century.

Placed together, the two histories do not cancel each other. The record documents an ancient Jewish connection to the land, a continuous if reduced Jewish presence after Rome's suppression of the revolts, and centuries of residence by the Arabic-speaking majority. Each of the popular simplifications works by removing one of these strands. The Jewish tie to the land is in the record; so is the long residence of the people who became the Palestinians.

That is why the modern conflict cannot be understood as a contest between a true claim and a false one. It concerns how two documented claims to the same ground came to be handled after 1897. The chapters that follow trace that handling. This section establishes only the starting condition: by the end of Ottoman rule, the land held a majority of Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians and a Jewish minority whose ties to the land reached back to antiquity.
