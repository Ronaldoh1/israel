The Story as Told, and the Record

Two stories about the Bible's making circulate widely, and they contradict each other. The first, taught in many churches, holds that the early Christians recognised the inspired books more or less naturally, that the Holy Spirit guided the process, and that councils merely confirmed what believers already knew. The second, popular in documentaries and online, holds that the Emperor Constantine and the Council of Nicaea chose the Bible in 325, burned the gospels they did not like and imposed a version of Christianity designed to serve the empire. Each story contains something true. Neither survives the documents intact.

Against the first story, the record shows that recognition was neither natural nor quick. Christians argued for three centuries over books such as Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, Jude and Revelation; the historian Eusebius, writing around 325, sorted the books of his day into those accepted, those disputed and those rejected, and placed several of today's New Testament books among the disputed. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] The Syriac churches long used a New Testament of 22 books, and the Armenian church added Revelation only around 1200. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon] [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] The Catholic list became binding only on 8 April 1546, when the Council of Trent voted it an article of faith under anathema by 24 votes to 15, with 16 abstentions. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] [cite:vt14_trent_s4] A vote with fifteen against is not the record of a settled consensus.

Against the second story, the record is just as firm. The Council of Nicaea did not discuss the canon. Its documented decisions concern the relation of the Son to the Father, the date of Easter and twenty disciplinary canons; none of its texts lists books of scripture. [cite:vt22_canons] [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea] The tale that the council's bishops piled up the candidate books and kept those that leapt onto an altar comes from a much later Greek compilation, the Synodicon Vetus, and was popularised by Voltaire. [cite:vt22_wiki_synodicon] [cite:vt22_voltaire] Constantine did order books burned after Nicaea, but they were the treatises of Arius, a priest of Alexandria whose teaching the council condemned, and the penalty for hiding one was death. [cite:vt21_socrates19] [scene:vt22_myths_tested|Myths about Nicaea, tested]

What both stories leave out is the subject of this book. The canon was formed and enforced by people who held offices, and the offices carried power, privilege and money that grew as the canon closed. That is not a claim about hidden motives. It is a matter of record: the exemptions Constantine granted the clergy in 313, the estates the Roman see accumulated, the forged grant the popes cited for six centuries, the loan the Fuggers made against the sale of indulgences and the list of banned books that the Roman Inquisition kept until 1966. [cite:vt21_hist_x7] [cite:cn_wiki_donation] [cite:cn_brit_albert] [cite:cn_va_index1966]

The parts that follow move in order of time. Part Two begins with the man who forced the question: Marcion, a wealthy shipowner whose list of scripture came first and whose money the church of Rome gave back. Parts Three and Four follow the emperors and the bishops of the fourth century, when imperial patronage and the closing of the lists happened in the same decades. Part Five turns to the papacy as a prize contested by Roman families, and to the forgeries that underwrote its claims. Parts Six to Eight trace how the laity lost access to the Bible in their own languages, how the sale of indulgences broke the Western canon in two, and how the Index of Prohibited Books policed what Catholics could read. Part Nine sets out the pattern that repeats across all of it.
