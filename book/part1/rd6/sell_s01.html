How the Case Was Made to Americans: Refuge, Bible and Democracy

A familiar explanation for American support of Israel runs backward from the present. Because the two countries later became close strategic partners, it is assumed that Americans backed the new state in 1948 for the same reason: that Israel was already a proven ally whose value to Washington was plain. The record of 1948 does not bear this out. The case put to the American public in the founding years rested on three different arguments, none of them strategic, and the men responsible for American strategy in that period made the opposite case in writing.

The first and central argument of 1945–48 was refuge. In Europe after the war some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons were still waiting in camps, and the destruction of European Jewry in the Holocaust was recent. The argument was humanitarian before it was political: the survivors needed somewhere to go. America's own door was part of the same argument. When Truman signed the Displaced Persons Act on 25 June 1948 he did so "with very great reluctance," saying that it "discriminates in callous fashion against displaced persons of the Jewish faith," and he pressed Congress for two years until it was amended in 1950; that story belongs to [scene:tr_s02|Truman's decisions after 1948]. [cite:truman_institute_dpact]

The second argument was religious. Christian restorationist belief, the conviction that the Jews would be restored to the land of the Bible, had a history going back to 1839. For Christian supporters the new state could be read as a biblical fulfillment. The third argument was political in the plainest sense: the new state was presented to Americans as a democracy.

Against all three stood the American national-security establishment. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Secretary of Defense and the Central Intelligence Agency each warned that American backing for a Jewish state would harm American interests. The warnings were consistent and on paper. Declassified CIA documents from 1948 predicted that recognition would produce armed conflict and damage American access to oil. Far from calling the future state a strategic asset, the government's own strategists treated it as a strategic liability. Truman overruled them. He recognized Israel eleven minutes after its declaration of independence on 14 May 1948, so quickly that his own State Department was caught unaware. The White House aides who argued the domestic political case, Clark Clifford and David Niles among them, and the personal channel that reached him through his friend Eddie Jacobson, carried the day. [cite:wiki_david_niles]

The map above lays out that contest as the documents record it: each formal warning against partition and recognition from 1946 to 1948, set against the political advice and the personal channel that prevailed. Its point for this section is simple. Whatever moved American policy in 1948, it was not a consensus inside the government that Israel served American strategy. The government's strategic voices said the reverse and lost.

The publicity of the period also had a hostile side, aimed at the Arab opponents of partition. Here the record requires care in both directions. Pro-Zionist publicity drew heavily on the wartime collaboration of the Grand Mufti of Jerusalem with Hitler. That collaboration was real and is documented; it was not invented for propaganda. What the publicity did with it was another matter. A single leader's alliance with the Axis was used as a broad brush to color Arab opposition as a whole, which the documented episode does not support. The episode should be stated plainly, as fact, and the generalization drawn from it should be named for what it was.

It is tempting to read later developments back into this period, and two of them in particular. The first is the organized anti-Islam network in the United States. The organized, well-funded anti-Islam network is documented mainly from the 2000s, building on currents that formed after 1967 and after 1979. [cite:cap_fear_inc] It was not part of the 1948 argument. The anti-Arab framing of 1948 leaned on a specific wartime episode, not on an industry.

The second is the argument most often assumed to have been there from the beginning. The claim that Israel was a strategic asset to the United States grew after Israel's victory in 1967, a generation after the founding, and was formalized under President Reagan, who called Israel a strategic asset and signed a strategic-cooperation agreement with it in 1981. Until then American aid was cautious: from 1948 into the early 1960s it consisted of economic aid and loans, with Washington avoiding direct arms sales for fear of alienating Arab oil-producing states, a pattern taken up in [scene:rd6_financing|how the new state was paid for]. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990]

Set side by side, the sequence corrects the familiar account. In 1948 Americans were asked to support Israel as a refuge for survivors, as the fulfillment of a promise that some Christians read in scripture, and as a new democracy. The strategic argument was then being made by the other side of the debate, inside the American government, and it pointed against support. The alliance framing came later and was built on later events.

What the record does not settle is how much each argument weighed with the public at the time. The documents establish which arguments were made and which officials opposed the policy; they do not measure how many Americans were moved by the survivors, how many by scripture and how many by the language of democracy, or how far the hostile portrait of Arab opposition shaped opinion. No survey or source in this chapter's research answers that, and this book does not guess at it. Nor does the record here trace the precise path by which the 1967 argument became the 1981 doctrine; that belongs to a later volume.
