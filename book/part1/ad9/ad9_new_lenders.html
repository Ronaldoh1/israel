The New Lenders: Payday, Buy Now Pay Later, Wage Apps and Medical Cards

For a household with no savings cushion, the gap between a bill and the next paycheck has to be bridged somehow. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey found that 63 per cent of adults would cover an unexpected $400 expense with cash or its equivalent, down from 68 per cent in 2021; the rest would borrow, sell something or could not pay. [cite:ad9_shed2025] A set of lenders has grown up to serve that gap: storefront payday lenders, buy-now-pay-later plans at the online checkout, apps that advance part of a paycheck, and credit cards offered in the dentist's or doctor's office. Each presents itself as a cheaper, simpler or kinder alternative to the bank. This section sets out how each one charges, what the regulators and courts have found, the industry's case, and where each stood in October 2026.

Payday loans: the arithmetic of a two-week loan

A payday loan is a small loan, usually a few hundred dollars, due in full on the borrower's next payday, typically secured by a post-dated check or permission to debit the borrower's bank account. Its price is quoted as a fee, commonly $15 for each $100 borrowed. The CFPB shows how that becomes an annual rate: $15 over 14 days is about $1.07 a day, and $1.07 × 365 is about $391, so the APR is about 391 per cent. [cite:ad9_cfpb_apr] The quoted fee looks small because the loan is meant to last two weeks. The CFPB's 2014 study of millions of loans found that it usually did not: more than 80 per cent of payday loans were rolled over or followed by another loan within 14 days, about half of all loans were made in sequences of ten or more, and one borrower in five paid monthly stayed in debt for the whole year. [cite:ad9_cfpb_rollover]

The Pew Charitable Trusts' 2012 survey gave the borrower's side. About 12 million Americans a year took payday loans, spending more than $7 billion. The average borrower took eight loans of about $375 a year, paid about $520 in interest and was in debt about five months of the year. Sixty-nine per cent of first-time borrowers said they used the money for recurring bills such as rent, utilities or food, and 16 per cent for an unexpected emergency. [cite:ad9_pew2012] That is the core of the dispute: a product sold as an emergency bridge is used mostly to cover a shortfall that recurs, and each recurrence costs another fee.

Payday lending is regulated mainly by the states, and the states disagree. By the Center for Responsible Lending's count, Rhode Island in July 2025 became the 21st state, alongside the District of Columbia, to cap payday rates at about 36 per cent, a level that in practice ends the two-week product; its law takes effect on 1 January 2027. [cite:ad9_crl_ri] Pew found that 2.9 per cent of adults used payday loans in the most restrictive states against 6.6 per cent in the least, and that few would-be borrowers in states without storefronts turned to online lenders instead. [cite:ad9_pew2012] Congress set a national cap for one group only: the Military Lending Act of 2006 limits the cost of most consumer credit to active-duty service members and their families to a 36 per cent military APR, and a 2015 Defense Department rule extended it to credit cards and most other loans. [cite:ad9_fdic_mla] The CFPB's 2017 payday rule would have required lenders to check that borrowers could repay; the bureau rescinded that requirement on 7 July 2020, before it took effect, and kept only the payment provisions, which bar a lender from trying again to debit an account after two failed attempts and require advance notices. [cite:ad9_crs_payday2020] Those took effect on 30 March 2025, but two days earlier the bureau said it would not prioritise enforcing them, and its regulatory agenda of July 2026 announced a proposal to reconsider them; status as of October 2026: in force on paper, not a supervisory priority, under reconsideration. [cite:ad9_sheppard_payday]

The industry's case was put by Dennis Shaul of the Community Financial Services Association, the payday trade group, to Congress in 2016: demand for the loans reflects unstable incomes; for many customers a payday fee is cheaper than a bounced check, a late fee or a cut-off utility; states already license the business; and the CFPB's underwriting rule would "effectively end payday lending as it currently exists". [cite:ad9_cfsa_2016] There is evidence that access can help in a crisis. Adair Morse found that after natural disasters in California, foreclosures rose by about 4.5 per 1,000 homes and that the presence of payday lenders offset about 1.0 to 1.3 of them, with no such effect where homeowners' insurance covered the disaster. [cite:ad9_morse2011] Both findings can hold at once: a payday loan can be the least bad option for a one-time shock and an expensive trap for a recurring shortfall, and the CFPB data show the second use is the more common.

Buy now, pay later

Buy now, pay later (BNPL) plans split a purchase, usually into four payments over six weeks with a quarter paid at checkout, and generally charge the shopper no interest. The CFPB's January 2025 study of six large lenders counted about 277 million such loans worth $34 billion in 2022, up from barely 100,000 applications a day in 2019 to more than a million. More than 60 per cent of loans in 2021–2022 went to borrowers with subprime or deep-subprime scores, about 63 per cent of borrowers held more than one loan at the same time, and BNPL users carried card utilisation of 60 to 66 per cent of their limits against 34 per cent for non-users; the bureau said this showed association, not that BNPL caused the other debt. [cite:ad9_cfpb_bnpl2025] In 2025, 16 per cent of adults used BNPL, and 11 per cent of users said a BNPL payment had triggered an overdraft or NSF fee at their bank. [cite:ad9_shed2025] In May 2024 the CFPB issued an interpretive rule classing BNPL providers as credit-card issuers under the Truth in Lending Act; on 6 May 2025 it said it would not prioritise enforcing it, and on 12 May 2025 it withdrew it. [cite:ad9_orrick_bnpl] [cite:ad9_cfpb_bnpl_page] As of October 2026 no federal rule specific to BNPL applies.

Wage advances and cash-advance apps

Earned-wage access (EWA) products let a worker draw part of the pay already earned before payday, repaid automatically from the next paycheck. Some are offered through employers; others are phone apps that advance cash against expected pay and are repaid by debiting the user's bank account. Most do not charge interest. They charge fees for instant delivery, monthly memberships, or ask for "tips". California's financial regulator collected data from these companies for 2021; on the National Consumer Law Center's summary of it, users took about 36 advances a year, mostly between $40 and $100 for about ten days, and the average cost worked out to an APR of 334 per cent at tip-based companies and 331 per cent at those charging fees. Tips were paid 73 per cent of the time, and 97 per cent of advances were repaid. [cite:ad9_nclc_ewa] On 23 December 2025 the CFPB issued an advisory opinion that EWA repaid by payroll deduction, limited to wages already earned, with no recourse against the worker and no credit check, is not credit under the Truth in Lending Act, and that its delivery fees and tips are not finance charges; it rescinded a 2024 interpretation that had treated such products as loans. States are taking their own course, with California regulating EWA under its own consumer-finance law. [cite:ad9_mofo_ewa]

Federal enforcement has focused on how the apps were marketed. In November 2023 the Federal Trade Commission obtained $18 million in refunds from Brigit, which had advertised "instant" advances of up to $250 that few users received, charged a 99-cent fee for transfers it called free, and made its $9.99 monthly membership hard to cancel. [cite:ad9_ftc_brigit] The FTC sued Dave, Inc. in November 2024, alleging misleading advance amounts, undisclosed fees and tips taken without consent; the Justice Department added the chief executive as a defendant in December. [cite:ad9_ftc_dave] Dave called the suit "government overreach" and replaced tips and express fees with a new fee structure for new members from December 2024. [cite:ad9_pd_dave] A federal judge denied its motion to dismiss on 12 September 2025; as of the company's March 2026 annual report no trial date or settlement had been disclosed, and the city of Baltimore had filed its own suit. [cite:ad9_dave_10k] None of these allegations has been decided at trial.

Medical and dental credit cards

Medical credit cards are offered at the point of care, often by staff in doctors' and dentists' offices, typically with a deferred-interest promotion: no interest if the balance is paid in full within, say, six to eighteen months. If any balance remains on the last day, interest is charged on the full original amount back to the purchase date. The CFPB's May 2023 report found that the typical rate on these cards was 26.99 per cent, against about 16 per cent on general cards at the time; that about one promotion in five was not paid off in time; and that patients paid about $1 billion in deferred interest between 2018 and 2020. [cite:ad9_cfpb_medcc] [cite:ad9_cfpb_carecredit] On the bureau's own example of a $2,400 procedure on a six-month promotion, a patient who has paid down most of the balance but not all of it is charged interest on the whole $2,400 for six months, about $324 at 26.99 per cent (2,400 × 0.2699 × 6 ÷ 12; book arithmetic, simple interest). In 2013 the bureau ordered GE Capital Retail Bank and its CareCredit unit to refund up to $34.1 million to patients who had been enrolled in providers' offices believing the cards were interest-free. [cite:ad9_cfpb_carecredit] Part Eight follows what happens when such balances go unpaid. [scene:ad8_medical_debt|Part Eight]

Interpretation, not finding: the poverty premium as a business model

The Sophia platform reads the products in this part as one system: the less money a household has, the more it pays to use money, through overdraft fees, check-cashing charges, payday rollovers and deferred interest, and the revenue from that premium gives the businesses that collect it a stake in the shortfall continuing. In this reading, the Dream's promise that credit is a ladder works in reverse for people at the bottom, for whom credit is the cost of being short.

The strongest counter-readings are these. First, small, short, unsecured loans to people with weak credit are costly to make, and losses and fixed costs explain part of the price; the industry argues that caps remove the product rather than lowering its price, and the Morse study shows access can prevent worse outcomes such as foreclosure. [cite:ad9_cfsa_2016] [cite:ad9_morse2011] Second, the record shows the market and the law moving in both directions: large banks cut overdraft revenue in half on their own, the unbanked share halved, twenty-one states capped payday rates, and Congress protected service members. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] [cite:ad9_fdic2023] [cite:ad9_crl_ri] [cite:ad9_fdic_mla] Third, the most clearly deceptive conduct has drawn enforcement: the CareCredit order (2013), the Brigit refunds (2023) and the Dave suit, filed in 2024 and still being litigated by the Justice Department under the following administration. [cite:ad9_ftc_brigit] [cite:ad9_ftc_dave] [cite:ad9_cfpb_carecredit] What the record establishes is narrower than either reading: each product here charges the most to borrowers who use it repeatedly, those borrowers are disproportionately people with the least cushion, and as of October 2026 most of the federal limits proposed in 2024 have been withdrawn, vacated or repealed.
