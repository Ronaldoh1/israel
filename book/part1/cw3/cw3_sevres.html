The Protocol of Sèvres, 14–25 October 1956

Eden denied for the rest of his life that there had been any collusion with Israel. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The document that proves otherwise was signed on the evening of 24 October 1956 in a villa at Sèvres by representatives of Britain, France and Israel, and the British prime minister ordered every copy destroyed. One survived in Ben-Gurion's waistcoat pocket. What follows is drawn from Avi Shlaim's reconstruction of the conference, published in International Affairs in 1997, which draws on the participants' own accounts, among them Pineau's record and Peres's writing, and from the accounts of how the Israeli copy came to light. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres]

Chequers, 14 October. The plan reached Eden from Paris. On 14 October 1956, at Chequers, General Maurice Challe, accompanied by France's acting foreign minister Albert Gazier, put to him what became known as the "Challe scenario": Israel would attack Egypt in Sinai, and Britain and France would then have a pretext to occupy the Canal Zone. Shlaim quotes Nutting that Eden "could scarcely contain his glee," and gives Eden's reply: "So that lets us off the hook." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] Since the nationalization, as [scene:cw3_road_to_war|the previous section] showed, Britain had hinted at force without finding a ground for it that the other powers would accept. The scenario offered one. Britain and France would not appear as the attackers of Egypt; they would appear as the powers separating two combatants and protecting the Canal.

The villa, 22–24 October. The conference opened at 4 p.m. on Monday 22 October 1956. Britain was represented by Selwyn Lloyd, who came incognito, with Donald Logan. Israel sent Ben-Gurion, Moshe Dayan and Shimon Peres, with Mordechai Bar-On as secretary. The French side was the largest: Mollet, Christian Pineau, Bourgès-Maunoury, Challe, Mangin and Abel Thomas. The British were absent on 23 October. Patrick Dean and Logan arrived at 4:30 p.m. on 24 October, and the protocol was signed that evening. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres]

The composition of the delegations tells part of the story. France sent six men and Israel four, Ben-Gurion among them. Britain sent Lloyd under cover on the first day, stayed away on the second, and on the third sent Dean and Logan to sign. The government that would most need to deny the meeting kept its own presence at it as slight and as hidden as it could.

Ben-Gurion's fears. Ben-Gurion came to Sèvres with two fears, and the protocol was shaped by both. The first was of Egyptian bombing. He feared that "Tel Aviv could be wiped out" unless the Royal Air Force first destroyed Egypt's air force. The second was of British betrayal. Shlaim records that it was Ben-Gurion who took the initiative in proposing that the agreement be written down, as "a guarantee against betrayal by Perfidious Albion." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The phrase marks how little he trusted the partner whose bombers he needed. An Israeli attack that Britain then failed to follow would have left Israel alone at war with Egypt, and condemned by the powers that had invited it.

A "real act of war." The British required something more than a skirmish. If they were to intervene as protectors of the Canal, the Israeli attack had to threaten it. Dayan's revised plan answered that need. It called for "a real act of war": a paratroop drop on the Mitla Pass, about thirty miles from the Canal, with a mechanized brigade thrusting westward, so that Britain would have a credible pretext to intervene about 36 hours later. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The design was political as much as military. A drop deep in Sinai, close to the Canal, would look like a threat to it; and the interval of a day and a half would let the ultimatum follow without seeming prearranged.

The fantastic plan, and the eighth point. Ben-Gurion's ambitions at Sèvres went beyond the operation. On 22 October he laid out a reorganization of the region that he himself called "fantastic": Jordan divided, Israel extended to the Litani, the Canal internationalized, and Nasser removed. At his request an Israeli eighth point was added to the draft, seeking "permanent annexation of the entire area east of the El Arish-Abu Ageila, Nakhl-Sharm el-Sheikh" line. Pineau recorded it. Britain and France never backed it, and it is not in the signed text. On 24 October Ben-Gurion put Israel's central interest in one sentence: "The Straits of Tiran are the State of Israel's Suez Canal." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The distinction between what Ben-Gurion proposed and what was signed is the heart of the argument over his motives, and both halves of it belong in the account. He sought territory; his partners declined to underwrite it; and what he secured in writing was the Straits.

The seven articles. The protocol itself is short. Paraphrased from Shlaim, its seven articles set out a timetable and a division of roles.

Read as a mechanism, the articles turn on the second. The "appeal" to both sides on 30 October was written before either side had fired, and its terms were fixed in advance. Israel, whose forces would be in Sinai, would be asked to stand ten miles from the Canal; Egypt, on its own soil, would be asked to withdraw from its own waterway and accept Anglo-French occupation of key positions, within twelve hours. A demand drafted to be refused was the trigger for Article 3. Article 4 gave Israel its own aim, Tiran and the western shore of the Gulf, on terms of studied neutrality from its partners. Article 5 kept Jordan out of the war on both sides: Israel would not attack it, and Britain would not come to its aid if Jordan attacked Israel. Article 6 kept the whole secret.

Signed, then hidden. The protocol was signed by Patrick Dean, ad referendum, that is, subject to his government's approval; by Christian Pineau; and by David Ben-Gurion. Eden, by Shlaim's account, "was clearly surprised and very put out" that it had been committed to paper at all, and ordered all copies "rounded up and delivered to No. 10 for destruction." The British copy was destroyed. On 25 October Dean and Logan tried to persuade the French and the Israelis to destroy theirs, and failed. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] Ben-Gurion's copy, "thrust deep into the pocket of his waistcoat," went into the Ben-Gurion Archives. It became public when photocopying was permitted for a BBC documentary in 1996. A contemporary account of the BBC's search reports that the British copy had been "burned almost as soon as it was signed," and that the French said theirs had been "misplaced." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] [cite:rs_cw3_jj_sevres] A photocopy of the three French pages was found by Pineau's widow in 2002, according to the Wikipedia account, which is used here for reference only. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_sevres]

The reactor. Sèvres carried a second agreement that appears nowhere in the protocol. Peres wrote that during a break before the signing on 24 October he "finalized with these two leaders," Mollet and Bourgès-Maunoury, "an agreement for the building of a nuclear reactor at Dimona." An earlier reactor agreement dated from 21 September 1956. By Shlaim's account Pineau opposed and Mollet was undecided; in September 1957 France delivered a reactor of twice the capacity previously promised. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The war plan and the reactor were negotiated by the same men in the same rooms, and the larger reactor followed the war. How the reactor was built and how Washington discovered it belong to Stage Four and to [scene:s11b|Dimona].

Washington outside. The protocol's sixth article bound its signatories to strict secrecy, and the secrecy held against Israel's and Britain's principal ally. Peter Hahn writes that American officials did not anticipate the collusion before hostilities began. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The consequence shaped everything that followed. The United States would meet the war not as a party to a plan but as a government that had been deceived by its two closest European allies, and its response, told later in this stage, was framed by that.

The dispute over motives. On what Sèvres was, the record is now settled: it was a written agreement to stage a war. On why each party joined it, historians differ, and the strongest form of each case deserves a hearing. Shlaim's reading is that France was the matchmaker, Eden duplicitous, and Ben-Gurion's demand for a written guarantee the product of a mistrust of Britain verging on paranoia; Israel's wider aims, the "fantastic" plan and the annexation point, show to him something more than self-defence. His verdict is plainly an interpretation: "The Protocol of Sèvres was thus a monument to French opportunism, Eden's duplicity, and Ben-Gurion's paranoia." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres]

Israel's own account frames its part differently. Ben-Gurion described the campaign afterwards as self-defence under Article 51 of the United Nations Charter, against Egypt's raids and blockade, and France was the one arms supplier willing to help. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] On that view, a small state facing a rearming enemy took the only alliance on offer, and the written protocol was ordinary prudence toward a partner with every reason to deny it later, as Eden in fact did. The historian Michael Oren argues that the war partly completed what the 1949 armistices had left open, and that the 1956 campaign established that Israel was there to stay. [cite:rs_cw3_oren_hnn] Ben-Gurion's fear of betrayal, on this reading, was not paranoia but foresight: the British copy was destroyed, reportedly almost as soon as it was signed.

The two readings can be weighed against the same evidence. Ben-Gurion's eighth point and his talk of the Litani support Shlaim's view that his aims at Sèvres reached past defence; his own description of the plan as "fantastic," and the fact that he signed without it, show a leader who knew the difference between what he wanted and what he could get. The signed text gave Israel Tiran and nothing more. Whether that was the limit of Israel's purpose or only the limit of its partners' consent is the question on which the two readings divide.

What the record leaves open. The protocol's articles are known here through Shlaim's paraphrase; the sources used for this section do not reproduce the text itself. The French copy's history between 1956 and 2002 rests on a single reference account. What was said on 23 October, when the British were absent, is not recorded in these sources. Nor do they show how far the British cabinet as a whole knew of the signed document, or what exactly Mollet and Bourgès-Maunoury committed to on the reactor during the break on 24 October beyond Peres's own words. What is not open is that three governments agreed in writing, five days before the war, on who would attack whom, when, and on what pretext, and that one of them spent the rest of its prime minister's life denying it.
