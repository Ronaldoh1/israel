The Other Fact

The recognition described in the previous section sits beside a second record, kept by the same state. [scene:ir3_community|Two documented facts] It begins three months after the revolution. On 9 May 1979 a revolutionary court had Habib Elghanian, an industrialist and the most prominent figure in Iran's Jewish community, shot by firing squad. He was charged with "friendship with Israel, Zionists, and the enemies of God, and sowing corruption on earth." [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian] Khomeini's assurance to the Jewish delegation in Qom came in the days that followed. [cite:ir3_wiki_history] Another Jewish businessman, Albert Danialpour, was executed on 5 June 1980. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] By one estimate about 30,000 Jews left within months of the revolution; another holds that more than 80 percent of those who remained in 1979 had left by 2006, most for Israel and the United States, many crossing illegally into Pakistan in the 1980s and 1990s. [cite:ir3_wiki_exodus] Israel's own officials later cited the protection of this community as one of their reasons for selling arms to Iran in the 1980s. [cite:ir2_djn_fallguy86] [scene:ir2_arms|The secret arms trade]

Recognised, not equal

The constitution that recognises Jews also sets limits. The president must be chosen from among "religious and political personalities" and must believe in the official Shia school of law, a requirement no Jew can meet; Article 14's protection extends only to non-Muslims who do not conspire against Islam or the Islamic Republic. [cite:ir3_const_en] Freedom House reports that non-Shia religious minorities are "rarely awarded senior government posts." [cite:ir3_fh_2026] The State Department's 2014 report found that the government "discriminated against all religious minority groups in employment, education, and housing." [cite:ir3_usip_irf2014] Rights activists note that Iranian law usually requires death when a non-Muslim kills a Muslim, while a Muslim who kills a non-Muslim usually owes financial compensation. [cite:ir3_wiki_ghahremani] The limits are not peculiar to Jews: Freedom House records that Bahá'ís, whose faith the constitution does not recognise, are "systematically persecuted," jailed and barred from higher education. [cite:ir3_fh_2026]

The Shiraz trial, 1999–2000

In January and March 1999 thirteen Jews, most of them from Shiraz, were detained and accused of spying for Israel. [cite:ir3_jta_freed2003] In July 2000 a revolutionary court in Shiraz convicted ten of them and two Muslim co-defendants and acquitted three Jews and two Muslims. The charges included "cooperating with a hostile government, membership in an illegal (spy) ring and recruitment of new agents"; the Jewish defendants received four to thirteen years. Eight defendants had confessed, and state television broadcast the confessions of two before their lawyers could consult them. [cite:ir3_ap_shiraz2000] Israel denied that any of the men had worked for it; Prime Minister Ehud Barak asked other governments to press for their release, and President Bill Clinton said he was "deeply disappointed" that Iran had again failed to act under the rule of law. Maurice Motamed, then the Jewish member of parliament, said the verdicts had "seriously affected the community." [cite:ir3_ap_shiraz2000] On 21 September 2000 an appeals court overturned the spy-ring and recruitment convictions, upheld the conviction for cooperating with Israel and cut the sentences to two to nine years; the provincial judiciary chief called them "the lowest possible sentences," reflecting "the ultimate of Islamic kindness and generosity." [cite:ir3_ap_appeal2000] The last five prisoners were freed in February 2003. [cite:ir3_jta_freed2003]

The execution of Arvin Ghahremani, 2024

On 4 November 2024 Iran hanged Arvin Ghahremani, a Jewish man, in Kermanshah prison. He had been convicted of murdering Amir Shokri, a Muslim man who owed him money, in a street fight in November 2022. [cite:ir3_olj_ghahremani] [cite:ir3_wiki_ghahremani] His family said Shokri attacked him with a knife and he acted in self-defence; the provincial prosecutor said the killing was intentional. Under Iran's law of retribution, the victim's family could have accepted blood money instead; the judiciary's news site said the family "refused to give consent." Iran Human Rights, a Norway-based group, said the case had "significant flaws" and that "institutionalized anti-Semitism in the Islamic republic undoubtedly played a crucial role" in the sentence being carried out; Iran's judiciary treated it as an ordinary murder case. [cite:ir3_olj_ghahremani] Israel's foreign ministry and the US antisemitism envoy condemned the execution, while some Iranian Jews told Voice of America it was an ordinary murder case. [cite:ir3_wiki_ghahremani] The case is contested: the record shows a homicide conviction, a disputed account of the killing and what one compilation describes as the first execution of an Iranian Jew since 1994 (AFP called it "unprecedented in recent years"); it does not establish that he was executed because he was Jewish.

Holocaust denial and the "anti-Zionist, not anti-Jewish" line

On 11 and 12 December 2006 the foreign ministry's Institute for Political and International Studies hosted a conference in Tehran on the "global vision of the Holocaust." Among its 67 listed attendees were David Duke, a former Ku Klux Klan leader, the convicted French Holocaust denier Robert Faurisson and six members of Neturei Karta, a small anti-Zionist Haredi group. Foreign Minister Manouchehr Mottaki said it was held "neither to deny nor prove" the Holocaust, while President Mahmoud Ahmadinejad told participants that the "Zionist regime will be wiped out soon the same way the Soviet Union was." [cite:ir3_wiki_conf2006] The UN secretary-general, Germany, Israel, the United States and the European Union condemned it. So did Motamed, who said it put pressure on Jews worldwide and upset Iran's Jews: a rare documented case of the community's representative publicly criticising a state initiative. [cite:ir3_wiki_conf2006] After Ahmadinejad left office, Foreign Minister Mohammad Javad Zarif wrote in September 2013 that "Iran never denied it"; on 20 March 2014 Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei called the Holocaust "an event whose reality is uncertain." [cite:ir3_brookings_2014] In 2025 the authorities again held an annual Holocaust cartoon competition, and the Friday prayer leader of Mashhad said "all the stories about the Holocaust are a complete lie." [cite:ir3_uscirf_2026]

The government's stated position is that its enmity is to Zionism and Israel, not to Jews. Khamenei set it out in November 2014: Israel "has no cure but to be annihilated," but that did not mean the "massacre of the Jewish people"; it meant a referendum of the "original people of Palestine including Muslims, Christians, and Jews," from which Jewish immigrants would be excluded. [cite:ir3_toi_9point] In May 2020 he called Israel "a cancerous tumor in the region." [cite:ir3_reuters_tumor2020] Critics answer that the line does not hold in practice. The State Department said in 2023 that Iranian officials "regularly" spread antisemitic views, and the US Commission on International Religious Freedom reported that after the June 2025 war the authorities "held Jews collectively responsible for Israeli actions." [cite:ir3_iranwire_rallies] [cite:ir3_uscirf_2026] Hakakian argues that the community serves the state as a "living political citation" against charges of antisemitism. [cite:ir3_tablet_hakakian]

Why this matters for the war

Israel's government framed both wars as a fight with Iran's government, not its people. On 13 June 2025 Benjamin Netanyahu told Iranians: "Our fight is against the murderous Islamic regime that oppresses and impoverishes you." [cite:ir3_toi_netanyahu2025] Iran's government, for its part, treated the community as a security question. From 23 June 2025 at least 35 Jews in Tehran and Shiraz were summoned and questioned, mainly about relatives in Israel, and warned not to contact them; the USCIRF reported "systematic" arrests in Isfahan, Yazd, Shiraz and Tehran. [cite:ir3_toi_summoned] [cite:ir3_uscirf_2026] In late July 2025 five Iranian Jews, one a US citizen, were reported still held on suspicion of collaborating with Israel. [cite:ir3_iranintl_five] The community's leaders, as the previous section showed, publicly sided with the state against Israel, under the conditions set out there.

The record examined for this book shows no reported damage to Jewish sites from the June 2025 strikes, although Hamadan, the site of Esther's tomb, was among the cities hit, and Ynet reported that prayers in synagogues had stopped during the fighting. [cite:ir3_ynet_2025] In the 2026 war, an Israeli strike during the night of 6 to 7 April destroyed the Rafi'-Nia synagogue in central Tehran, built in 1958. No deaths were reported; two people were pulled from the rubble. [cite:ir3_wiki_rafinia] Sameh said "our Torah scrolls were left under the rubble." [cite:ir3_rns_synagogue] An unnamed Israeli official first said "Israel doesn't target synagogues"; the military later said it had struck "a senior commander in Khatam al-Anbiya," Iran's emergency military command, in an adjacent building, called the synagogue's destruction "collateral damage" and expressed regret. [cite:ir3_nw_assoc2026] [cite:ir3_newser_idf] A former Jewish member of parliament, Siamak Moreh Sedgh, said there had been no military activity at the site. [cite:ir3_wiki_rafinia]

Set side by side, the two records answer the question in the part's title. The recognition is real: a named place in the constitution, a seat in parliament, open synagogues and a community continuous since antiquity. The restriction is also real: an execution for "friendship with Israel" in 1979, an espionage trial built on televised confessions, state-sponsored Holocaust denial, and questioning and arrests in wartime. Neither cancels the other, and neither answers whether the wars of 2025 and 2026 were justified. The community's existence does not show that Iran's government posed no threat, and its treatment does not show that the strikes on Iran were waged on its behalf; no Israeli or US statement examined here gave the protection of Iran's Jews as a war aim. What the record does show is that the community's public voice is exercised inside a state at war, and that its members bore costs from both directions: questioning and arrest by their own government, and the loss of a synagogue to an Israeli strike.
