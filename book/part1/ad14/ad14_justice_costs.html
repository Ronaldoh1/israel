The Criminal-Legal Bill: Bail, Fees, Phones and a Record That Follows You

The taught version of American justice says that a person accused is presumed innocent, that a person convicted pays a debt to society, and that once the sentence is served the debt is settled. The record shows a second, financial bill that runs alongside the sentence: money to get out of jail before trial, fees for the court and for supervision, prices for phone calls and soap inside, wages of cents an hour for work, child support that keeps growing while a parent is locked up, and lower earnings for the rest of a working life. This section sets out each charge, who collects it and what has changed as of October 2026. Part Four of the US System volume compares the prison systems themselves. [scene:us4_prisons|US vs Israel: prisons]

The scale

In 1980 there were 503,586 people in American prisons and jails, 220 per 100,000 residents. The number peaked at 2,307,504 in 2008, 755 per 100,000, and stood at 1,833,700 in 2023, 542 per 100,000: 664,200 in local jails, 1,013,500 in state prisons and 156,000 in federal prisons. [cite:ad14_wpb_trend] That 2023 total is 3.6 times the 1980 number (1,833,700 ÷ 503,586) and about 21 per cent below the 2008 peak; measured as a rate, which allows for population growth, incarceration is 2.5 times its 1980 level (542 ÷ 220). A widely repeated line says incarceration has risen "fourfold since the 1980s"; that was true at the peak and is no longer. About a quarter of those held, 467,600 people, had not been convicted and were awaiting trial. [cite:ad14_wpb_trend] Most convictions never reach a trial: in federal courts in 2022, 89.5 per cent of defendants pleaded guilty and fewer than 1 per cent were acquitted. [cite:us4_pew_plea2023]

Money bail

Bail was designed as a guarantee that a defendant will return to court: pay a sum, get it back on appearance. That design is intended friction. The commercial version adds a second party. A defendant who cannot pay the full amount pays a bail bond agent a non-refundable premium, usually about 10 per cent of the bond, often with collateral such as a car title; the agent posts no cash, only a promise to pay if the defendant disappears. Defendants and families pay about $2 billion a year in premiums. Insurance companies stand behind the agents and, in one review of 32 surety companies, paid out less than 1 per cent in bail losses; many forfeitures go uncollected, at least $35 million in Dallas County alone. Only two countries, the United States and the Philippines, allow commercial bail. [cite:ad14_ppi_bail] The figures come from the Prison Policy Initiative, a group that campaigns against mass incarceration, drawing on court audits and press reviews.

Illinois ended cash bail on 18 September 2023, under its Pretrial Fairness Act, leaving judges to decide release or detention on risk. In the first year, according to a review by Loyola University Chicago's Center for Criminal Justice, jail populations fell by about 14 per cent in Cook and the surrounding counties and 25 per cent in rural counties, the share of defendants missing court fell from 25.1 to 22.8 per cent across 21 counties, though it rose from 28.1 to 31 per cent for felonies that can no longer lead to detention, and reported crime fell 11 per cent in the first half of 2024, although the authors noted that crime was falling everywhere and called the court-appearance changes minimal. [cite:ad14_loyola_il] [cite:ad14_audit_dh_fta]

Fines, fees and the inability to pay

In Bearden v. Georgia, decided on 24 May 1983, the Supreme Court held that a court may not jail someone on probation for failing to pay a fine without first asking why; if the person tried and could not pay, the court must consider alternatives such as more time, a smaller fine or community service, because "it is fundamentally unfair to revoke probation automatically". [cite:ad14_bearden] The rule protects only those who can show they tried, and courts have kept adding fees for public defenders, probation and collections. Part Eleven traces how Ferguson, Missouri, budgeted court revenue and how unpaid court debt suspends driving licences. [scene:ad11_fees_everywhere|Part Eleven: fines and fees] A widely repeated claim says California banned all criminal fees in 2018; the law that went furthest, AB 1869, repealed 23 administrative fees charged to adults from 1 July 2021 and gave counties $65 million to replace the lost revenue. Fines and restitution were not part of it. [cite:ad14_ffjc_ab1869]

Phones and the commissary

Calls from prisons and jails are sold by a few private providers under contracts with corrections agencies, which for years took a share of the revenue as "site commissions". Congress passed the Martha Wright-Reed Just and Reasonable Communications Act to let the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) regulate all such calls, and in July 2024 the FCC cut the per-minute caps, capped video calls for the first time and banned site commissions. [cite:ad14_filter_fcc] On 30 June 2025 the FCC's Wireline Competition Bureau waived the compliance deadline from 1 April 2026 to 1 April 2027. [cite:ad14_filter_fcc] Then on 28 October 2025 the FCC adopted revised interim caps that raised the 2024 limits, for example from 6 to 11 cents a minute for prisons, and allowed providers to include the cost of call monitoring and other "safety and security" functions; the ban on site commissions stayed. [cite:ad14_ppi_fcc2025] Status as of October 2026: the revised caps took effect on 6 April 2026, with one provider, Securus, given until 6 July 2026 to convert its video pricing. [cite:ad14_fcc_da26319]

The commissary, the prison shop, sells food, hygiene products and stamps at prices set by contract. A study of Illinois, Massachusetts and Washington found that people spent on average $947 a year there, mostly on food, while typical prison jobs paid $180 to $660 a year; in Massachusetts, at 14 cents an hour, a $1.87 pack of dental floss cost more than 13 hours of work. [cite:ad14_ppi_commissary] The gap is filled by families outside.

Work inside

The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted", and about 20 states have similar clauses in their own constitutions. A 2022 report by the American Civil Liberties Union and the University of Chicago's Global Human Rights Clinic estimated that about 800,000 incarcerated people work, producing at least $2 billion of goods and $9 billion of prison maintenance services a year. Average pay in ordinary prison jobs ran from 15 to 52 cents an hour; Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Mississippi, South Carolina and Texas paid nothing for most jobs; and up to 80 per cent of a paycheck could be withheld for room and board, taxes and court costs. [cite:ad14_aclu_labor]

The record after release

In a field experiment in Milwaukee, the sociologist Devah Pager sent matched pairs of applicants, identical except for race and a criminal record, to apply for jobs. White applicants without a record were called back 34 per cent of the time and those with a record 17 per cent; Black applicants without a record 14 per cent and with one 5 per cent. [cite:ad14_epi_pager] "Ban the box" laws, which bar asking about records on job applications, were meant to help. A study by Amanda Agan and Sonja Starr, sending about 15,000 online applications in New York City and New Jersey, found that applicants without a record got 63 per cent more callbacks, and that after the laws took effect white applicants' callback advantage over otherwise similar Black applicants at affected employers grew from about 7 per cent to about 45 per cent, which the authors attributed to employers guessing at records by race. [cite:ad14_umich_agan] A 2020 Brennan Center study estimated that imprisonment cuts annual earnings by up to 51.7 per cent and that people imprisoned as young adults lose $267,000 (white), $358,900 (Black) or $511,500 (Latino) over their lifetimes; across everyone with a conviction or prison history it put lost earnings at $372.3 billion a year. [cite:ad14_brennan_earnings] The racial gaps in these estimates describe how the labour market treats people, as the two callback studies document; they are not traits of any group.

Debt also grows inside. About one in five people in prison or jail has a child support order, and the average parent enters prison owing about $10,000 and leaves owing about $20,000, because orders keep running while earnings stop. As of July 2019 at least nine states and territories still treated incarceration as "voluntary unemployment" that barred a reduction, despite a 2016 federal rule prohibiting that treatment. [cite:ad14_aspe_childsupport]

Interpretation, not finding: the poverty penalty as a closed loop

The Sophia platform reads these charges as one loop: poverty raises the odds of being held before trial, detention and fees deepen poverty, and the record then keeps earnings low enough to start the cycle again, with private contractors collecting at each step. Read through this lens, the Dream's promise of a fresh start after a debt is paid is withdrawn by design. The strongest counter-readings are these: each charge has a different owner and a different legal basis, and no document shows them coordinated; prices inside partly reflect real security costs, which the FCC now counts openly; and the direction since 2008 is toward fewer people in custody and lower charges, which is the opposite of a system built to maximise them. The record supports the narrower claim that each charge, unchecked, moves money from poor defendants and their families to contractors and local budgets.

Where it works as promised

Parts of the system have moved toward the taught version. The number of people in custody fell by about a fifth from its 2008 peak. [cite:ad14_wpb_trend] Illinois ended cash bail and in its first year saw fewer missed court dates overall and less reported crime, though both trends had other causes too. [cite:ad14_loyola_il] California stopped charging adults 23 administrative fees. [cite:ad14_ffjc_ab1869] Even after the 2025 revision, the FCC's caps and its ban on site commissions remain the first nationwide limits on what a call home from prison can cost. [cite:ad14_ppi_fcc2025] And the Supreme Court's 1983 rule still forbids jailing a person only because they are too poor to pay. [cite:ad14_bearden]
