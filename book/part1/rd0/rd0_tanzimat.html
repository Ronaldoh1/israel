Reform from Istanbul: Edicts, a Constitution and Jerusalem's Deputy, 1839–1878

Palmerston's instruction of 1840 reached a sultan who had, the year before, begun to reform his empire from the center. A claim met earlier in this chapter, in the sketch of the Ottoman Empire on the board of 1789–1815, holds that the Ottoman parliamentary reforms of 1876 gave Palestine representation and that "Palestinian delegates sat in the Ottoman parliament." [cite:wiki_ottoman_palestine] The claim is right in substance and loose in its detail. The record supports one named deputy for the district of Jerusalem in the parliament of 1877, a man who used his seat to oppose the sultan and lost it within a year. To see what that seat was, and what it was not, the story has to begin almost four decades earlier, with a series of promises issued from Istanbul to all the sultan's subjects.

The reform era the Ottomans called the Tanzimat opened on 3 November 1839. On that day Sultan Abdülmecid I proclaimed the Edict of Gülhane, at the urging of his Grand Vizier, Mustafa Reşid Pasha. According to a reference account, which draws on Cleveland and Bunton and on Liebesny, the edict promised three things: security of life, honor and property; a regular system of taxation in place of tax farming; and regular conscription. [cite:rs_rd0_gulhane_wiki] Its concessions were said to extend "to all our subjects, whatever religion or sect they may belong to." [cite:rs_rd0_gulhane_wiki] The same account is careful about its force. The edict changed no laws by itself and was never fully implemented. [cite:rs_rd0_gulhane_wiki] It was a declaration of the state's intentions, and its force depended on the legislation and enforcement that followed.

The mechanism matters for Palestine. On this section's reading, the two practical promises of Gülhane, regular taxation and regular conscription, required the state to know who its subjects were and what they held. That need is the thread that runs from this edict to the registration of land under the Land Code of 1858, the subject of the next section, where many cultivators avoided the register precisely because it brought taxes and conscription with it. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] Read this way, the promise of security of property and the demand for a regular revenue were the same reform seen from two sides.

The second great instrument came at the end of a war. The Reform Edict of 18 February 1856 was issued as the Crimean War ended. According to the reference account, it promised equality in education, government appointments and justice regardless of creed, freedom of worship, and mixed tribunals for cases between Muslims and non-Muslims. Among its provisions was military service for non-Muslims, which proved unpopular. [cite:rs_rd0_reform1856_wiki] Where Gülhane had extended its concessions to all subjects in general words, the 1856 edict spelled out equality in particular fields of public life. Both remained edicts: promises made from the center, whose effect in a given province depended on how far they were enforced there.

The third step went further than any edict. On 23 December 1876 an Ottoman constitution was promulgated, and on 19 March 1877 the first Ottoman parliament opened at Dolmabahçe Palace. Its deputies were not elected directly by the population. They were chosen indirectly, through the provincial councils. [cite:rs_rd0_firstconst_wiki] That detail shapes what "representation" meant. A deputy was the choice of a provincial council, not of a popular vote.

The sources disagree on the size of the chamber. The Institute for Palestine Studies' PalQuest counts 120 deputies, fourteen of them Arab. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] The reference account of the First Constitutional Era gives 115 members, and for the second elections a split of 69 Muslim and 46 non-Muslim deputies. [cite:rs_rd0_firstconst_wiki] The sources do not reconcile the two figures, and neither is adopted here as settled.

Jerusalem's deputy in that chamber was Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi. Born in Jerusalem in 1842, he was educated well beyond the city's own schools. He studied at the al-Fakhriyya school, at the British Diocesan Boys' School in Jerusalem, at a Protestant college in Malta, and in Istanbul. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] In 1875, two years before he took his seat, The Jewish Chronicle published two of his letters on the Jews of Palestine. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] He had also served as head of Jerusalem's municipality, though the sources date that service differently. PalQuest gives his mayoralty as 1870–1874, with two later terms. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] Jerusalem Story says he was appointed in September 1865, at the age of 23, and headed the municipality for nine years. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] Both agree that he governed the city before he represented it.

In 1877 al-Khalidi was elected as the sole deputy for the Jerusalem mutasarrifate, and by PalQuest's count he was one of fourteen Arab deputies among 120. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] He did not sit quietly. Both biographical sources describe him as a prominent opponent of Sultan Abdul Hamid II in the chamber. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] How that district was constituted, and how many people lived in it, is set out in [scene:s00h|the Baseline account of Ottoman Palestine].

The parliament was short-lived. Abdul Hamid II suspended both the parliament and the constitution in February 1878, on 14 February in one account and on 13 February in another. [cite:rs_rd0_firstconst_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] When the chamber was dissolved, al-Khalidi was one of ten opposition deputies ordered out of Istanbul. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] The chain of power is visible in that sequence. The constitution had created a chamber, but it had not created a body the sultan could not dismiss. The man who had promulgated it could suspend it, and the deputies who had opposed him could be sent away from the capital. Jerusalem's representation in the imperial parliament had lasted less than a year.

Measured against this record, the earlier claim can now be stated exactly. It is documented that the 1876 constitution produced a parliament in which the Jerusalem district had an elected deputy, and that this deputy, al-Khalidi, was a native of Jerusalem, a former head of the city's municipality and an outspoken member of the opposition. That is a real fact of formal political representation, and it answers any account in which the people of the country had no part in the empire's politics. What the record assembled here does not support is the plural. It names one deputy for Jerusalem. Fourteen Arab deputies sat by PalQuest's count, but the sources used here do not say which, if any, of the others sat for districts that lay in what would later be Palestine. "Palestinian delegates" should therefore read, on present evidence, "Jerusalem's deputy."

In the same year the parliament closed, religious equality in the Ottoman lands was written into an international treaty. Article 62 of the Treaty of Berlin of 1878 declared that "in no part of the Ottoman Empire shall difference of religion be alleged against any person." [cite:rs_rd0_fo1893_berlin62] Where the edicts had been the sultan's own promises to his subjects, Article 62 stood in a treaty, and foreign diplomats could invoke it. British diplomats later did exactly that, citing the article against Ottoman restrictions on Jews. [cite:rs_rd0_fo1893_berlin62] How those restrictions arose, and how Britain and the United States contested them, is told in [scene:rd0_limits|the Ottoman limits of 1882–1896].

These decades also saw the first departures in the other direction. From the 1870s Palestinian emigration to the Americas began, mostly of Christian families from Bethlehem, Beit Jala and Beit Sahour who traveled on Ottoman papers and were therefore long called "Turcos." [cite:palquest_sv] Where they settled and what became of their communities is told in [scene:dsp_s01|the account of the Palestinian diaspora].

Al-Khalidi's part in this book does not end in 1878. On 1 March 1899, through Zadok Kahn, Chief Rabbi of France, he wrote to Theodor Herzl urging that Palestine be left in peace, and on 19 March Herzl replied that Jewish immigration would benefit the country. [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_palquest] [cite:rs_rd0_khalidi_jstory] That exchange belongs to the next part of this book.

Several questions remain open on this record. The reference accounts of the two edicts say what was promised and that Gülhane was never fully implemented, but they do not measure how far either edict was carried out in the district of Jerusalem, or how the people of Palestine received them. The size of the 1877 chamber stands at 115 or 120, depending on the source. Al-Khalidi's years as head of the municipality are dated from 1865 in one biography and from 1870 in the other. The day of the suspension is given as 13 or 14 February 1878. And the sources consulted here are silent on what the people who chose al-Khalidi through the provincial councils expected of him, beyond the fact that he went to Istanbul and opposed the sultan.
