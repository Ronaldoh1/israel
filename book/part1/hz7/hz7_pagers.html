Pagers and Radios

17 September 2024. Thousands of pagers carried by Hezbollah members and others across Lebanon, and some in Syria, beeped and then exploded, in homes, shops, cars and streets. The next day Lebanon's health minister, Firas Abiad, put the toll at 12 dead, among them a girl and a boy and four health workers employed by private hospitals in Beirut's southern suburbs who carried the devices, and between 2,750 and 2,800 wounded, nearly 300 of them critically. About 1,850 of the wounded were in Beirut and its southern suburbs, about 750 in the south and about 150 in the Bekaa. [cite:hz7_na_pager_toll] On 18 September a second wave struck: handheld radios, also issued within Hezbollah, exploded. [cite:hz7_ohchr_experts] On 19 September Abiad gave per-day figures: 12 killed and 2,323 injured by the pagers, and 25 killed and 608 injured by the radios. [cite:hz7_lbci_abiad]

The toll and its limits. The counts differ by date and by what they include. Abiad's per-day figures add up to 37 dead and 2,931 injured; by November 2024 the Times of Israel was reporting 39 killed and more than 3,400 wounded across both days, and ACLED's September overview also gave at least 39 deaths. [cite:hz7_lbci_abiad] [cite:hz7_toi_netanyahu_pagers] [cite:hz7_acled_sep24] Some secondary accounts give 42 killed; this volume has not found a ministry figure to support that number and uses the range of 37 to 39. None of the official tolls separated civilians from Hezbollah members. A Hezbollah official told Reuters a week later that about 1,500 of its fighters had been put out of action by their injuries. Among the wounded was Iran's ambassador in Beirut, Mojtaba Amani. [cite:hz7_toi_netanyahu_pagers] The devices were given to people inside Hezbollah's networks, which is why most of the wounded were members; but they exploded wherever their holders happened to be, which is why children, medical staff and bystanders were among the dead and wounded.

How it was done. Reporting by the New York Times, summarised by L'Orient Today on 19 September, traced the pagers to BAC Consulting, a company registered in Budapest that held a licence to make pagers under the brand of Gold Apollo, a Taiwanese firm. The Times described BAC as a shell company set up as part of an Israeli plan to pose as an international pager producer, and reported that at least two other shell companies were created to hide who was really making the devices. The batteries, it reported, had been packed with the explosive PETN. Shipments to Lebanon began in small numbers in the summer of 2022 and grew after Nasrallah urged his followers not to use mobile phones; thousands more arrived in the summer of 2024. Gold Apollo said the design and manufacture were "the sole responsibility of BAC"; BAC's chief executive denied making the devices and said: "I am just the middleman." [cite:hz7_olj_nyt_bac] In December 2024 two former Mossad officers, interviewed under pseudonyms by CBS's 60 Minutes, gave their account. They said the operation began about ten years earlier with radios fitted with explosives, of which Hezbollah eventually bought more than 16,000; that the pager phase began in 2022; that a saleswoman Hezbollah already knew offered the first batch; and that by September 2024 Hezbollah held about 5,000 of the pagers. One said the charge had been tested repeatedly so that only the person holding the device would be badly hurt, even with a spouse or child nearby. These were the officers' own claims, and CBS did not independently verify them. [cite:hz7_cbs_60min]

Israel's position. Israel did not comment at first. On 20 September its ambassador to the UN, Danny Danon, asked about the blasts, said Israel would "do everything we can to target those terrorists," and that Israel did not seek war with Hezbollah but could not accept the existing situation on its border. [cite:hz7_toi_turk] On 10 November 2024 Netanyahu told his cabinet that "The pager operation and the elimination of [Hassan] Nasrallah were carried out despite the opposition" of senior defence officials and political figures, a remark widely read as aimed at Gallant, whom he had just dismissed; his office confirmed Israel's responsibility the next day. [cite:hz7_toi_netanyahu_pagers] Netanyahu's statement is the formal acknowledgement. Reporting that particular Israeli units or private companies supplied software or technical help to the operation rests on single sources and is not used here.

Hezbollah's response. Nasrallah spoke on 19 September. He called the detonations "a declaration of war," "an unprecedented and historic blow" that "crossed all rules and red lines," and promised "a just punishment." [cite:hz7_axios_nasrallah] Eight days later he was dead.

The legal argument. The operation set off a debate in international law that remains unsettled. On 19 September a group of UN human rights experts called the detonations "terrifying" violations of international law. "Simultaneous attacks by thousands of devices would inevitably violate humanitarian law, by failing to verify each target," they said, since no one could know who held each device or who was near it at the moment of detonation; they said the attacks could amount to war crimes, including murder and indiscriminate attack, and that a modified civilian pager could be a prohibited booby-trap disguised as a harmless object. [cite:hz7_ohchr_experts] The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, told the Security Council the next day that international humanitarian law "prohibits the use of booby-trap devices in the form of apparently harmless portable objects," called for an independent investigation and said: "This cannot be the new normal." [cite:hz7_toi_turk] Human Rights Watch pointed to Article 7(2) of Amended Protocol II to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, which bans booby-traps in the form of apparently harmless portable objects and to which both Israel and Lebanon are party; its regional director, Lama Fakih, said an explosive device whose location "could not be reliably known would be unlawfully indiscriminate." [cite:hz7_npr_law]

The opposing argument was made by military lawyers and analysts. John Spencer of the Modern War Institute at West Point said the attack met the tests of necessity, proportionality and distinction and called it "a very precise sabotage of an enemy piece of equipment used for military purposes." William Boothby, a retired legal officer of the Royal Air Force, wrote that it was probably reasonable to assume pagers issued through a military network would be with their military users, and that the real question was whether enough weight was given to expected harm to others nearby. [cite:hz7_npr_law] No court has ruled on the operation. The record supports two statements at once: the devices were aimed at an armed group's communications and most of the casualties were its members; and the people who set them off could not see where each device was when it exploded, and children and health workers were killed.
