Restoration Before Zionism: The Protestant Lobby, 1839–1891

A common assumption holds that Christian political support for a Jewish state was a product of the twentieth century: a response to the Holocaust and to the founding of Israel in 1948. The documents place its political beginnings more than a century earlier, and in a different place. Before any Jewish political movement for a state existed, a Protestant current in Britain and the United States had already put the idea of a Jewish "restoration" to Palestine before a Foreign Secretary and a President. Restorationism, the belief that a Jewish return to Palestine would fulfil biblical prophecy, was an organized political current decades before Theodor Herzl. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism]

The British story begins inside a family. Anthony Ashley-Cooper, Lord Shaftesbury, was an evangelical peer and reformer. He was also Palmerston's stepson-in-law: the Foreign Secretary, Lord Palmerston, was his wife's stepfather. In 1839 Shaftesbury pressed Palmerston to sponsor Jewish settlement in Palestine under British protection. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] [cite:wiki_shaftesbury] On this section's reading, the access mattered. Shaftesbury's request needed a government to carry it, and the Ottoman sultan, who ruled Palestine, answered to no British minister. What Shaftesbury had was the ear of the Foreign Secretary, whose instructions reached Constantinople.

Palmerston acted. In 1840 he instructed his ambassador at Constantinople to urge the Ottoman sultan to encourage Jewish settlement, and Britain's new consulate in Jerusalem was given a brief to protect Jews. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] [cite:wiki_shaftesbury] Those were the two instruments Britain used: diplomatic pressure on the sultan's government, and a consul on the ground who could take named people under his protection. Neither could settle anyone. The decision to admit or encourage settlers lay with the Porte, and the instruction asked the sultan to use a power that only he held.

The sources are frank that the motive was double. Restorationism was theological and imperial at once. Shaftesbury and Palmerston saw a protected Jewish presence as a British foothold in Ottoman Syria; American evangelicals read the same prospect as prophecy. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] A minister could pursue it as policy and an evangelical as faith, and the two readings did not have to agree for the request to be made. What neither reading drew on was Jewish opinion. Most Jewish communal bodies of the period opposed restorationism or ignored it. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The idea was advanced on behalf of Jews, by Christians, for reasons of prophecy and empire.

The restorationist current also produced the most quoted slogan in the later conflict, and its history is not the one usually told. The earliest published version of "a land without a people for a people without a land" appeared in an 1843 book by Alexander Keith, a Church of Scotland clergyman and an advocate of Jewish "restoration" who had travelled to Palestine in 1839. Shaftesbury used similar wording in 1853. [cite:meq_land_without_people] [cite:aijac_slogan_origin] His own phrase for Palestine was a country without a nation for a nation without a country. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] [cite:wiki_shaftesbury] The first use by a Zionist came only in 1901, when Israel Zangwill wrote that Palestine was "a country without a people." [cite:meq_land_without_people] [cite:aijac_slogan_origin] The phrase, in other words, was first published by a Christian restorationist more than half a century before the first recorded Zionist use.

What it meant is debated. One reading takes it literally: the land was described as empty. The other holds that it meant a land "without a nation." Shaftesbury's own wording, "without a nation," is consistent with the second reading. Against the first, the counts are plain: every Ottoman and British census shows a populated country. Keith, the man who first published the phrase, had visited Palestine and knew it was populated. [cite:meq_land_without_people] [cite:aijac_slogan_origin] Whichever reading is preferred, the phrase described a country that had people in it. The same set of checks examines two other claims that are often paired with this one, that Arab leaders "rejected every offer" and that the conflict is a religious war two thousand years old; both concern later periods and are taken up in [scene:sq_s05|three slogans checked against the record].

The American counterpart came half a century after Shaftesbury. In March 1891 the Chicago evangelist William E. Blackstone presented President Benjamin Harrison with a petition urging American support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. The Blackstone Memorial, as it became known, was signed by more than four hundred prominent Americans, among them John D. Rockefeller, J. P. Morgan and the Chief Justice. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] It preceded Herzl's Der Judenstaat by five years and the first Zionist Congress at Basel by six. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The instrument was different from Palmerston's. Shaftesbury had worked through a minister who could instruct an ambassador; Blackstone worked through public petition, using the names of men of finance and law to press a head of state. The President, like the Foreign Secretary before him, could only ask; the land was still the sultan's.

Set side by side, the two currents show the sequence that most accounts reverse. The familiar order runs from a Jewish political movement to Christian sympathy for it. The documents run the other way: Christian political support for a Jewish return came first, and Jewish political Zionism came second. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] By the time the Zionist movement had an organization, a Christian political base for a Jewish state already existed in Britain and the United States, at a time when most Jewish communal bodies still opposed or ignored the idea. That ordering helps explain why the Balfour Declaration of 1917 found a ready constituency in Britain's governing class. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism]

The constituency did not stand still after 1891. In 1909 the Scofield Reference Bible gave it a theology: its dispensationalist reading made support for a Jewish state a mass Protestant doctrine. Balfour, whose name the 1917 declaration bears, was himself a Christian restorationist. Later still, Christians United for Israel in the Hagee era stands at the twentieth-century end of a lineage that begins in 1839, not in 1948. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The Scofield Bible and its public belong to the next part of this book, in [scene:s05|the Scofield Reference Bible, 1909].

The finding should be kept at its proper size. Before the Zionist Congress, and before Rothschild money built colonies in Palestine, Protestant restorationism had already made a Jewish return a live question in British and American politics, for reasons of prophecy and empire that had little to do with Jewish opinion. That constituency, not any single family, was the pre-existing structure into which the Zionist project would later connect. It does not follow that restorationists created Zionism, which had its own Jewish sources in prayer, persecution and nationalism, nor that they could deliver Palestine: in 1840 and in 1891 the land was the sultan's, and neither a Foreign Secretary nor a President could do more than ask.

Much is left open by the record as it stands here. The text of Palmerston's 1840 instruction is not quoted in these sources, and neither the sultan's response nor any settlement it produced is recorded. The consulate's brief to protect Jews is documented; what the consuls did with it in these decades is not described here. The record does not say how Harrison answered Blackstone, or whether the memorial changed anything in American policy. And the statement that most Jewish communal bodies opposed or ignored restorationism is made in general terms, without naming the bodies or their statements. Those are the limits of what can be said about this current before 1896.
