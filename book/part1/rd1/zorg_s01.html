The Machinery of the Zionist Organization

A common impression of early Zionism is of a loose gathering of enthusiasts without formal structure. The organization founded at Basel in 1897 was something more deliberate. Membership was open to any individual or group that accepted the Basel Program and paid a small annual fee called the "shekel."[cite:palquest_wzo] It was a mass membership built on small dues.

The organization's constitution, adopted at the 1899 Congress, gave that membership a governing frame. The Congress stood at the top. Beneath it sat a seven-member Smaller Actions Committee, whose chairman was the Congress president, and a 37-member Larger Actions Committee made up of national leaders. The Congress met every year until 1901 and every two years after that.[cite:palquest_wzo] [cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] In effect, the movement had an elected assembly, an executive and a council.

Its practical instruments followed in quick succession. The Jewish Colonial Trust was established in 1899 to serve as the movement's bank. The Jewish National Fund followed in 1901 to buy land. The Palestine Office opened in 1908 to manage settlement on the ground. Each is treated in its own section of this book; together they turned resolutions passed in Switzerland into money, land and administration in Palestine.

The organization also worked through national federations, country-level bodies that recruited members and raised money. The Federation of American Zionists was founded in 1898 and later became the Zionist Organization of America. The English Zionist Federation followed in 1899.[cite:w_zf_gb]

The First World War tested the structure. Before 1914 the organization's head offices were in Germany, and once war came it declared itself neutral. In practice, though, its leadership shifted to London. There Chaim Weizmann, a Manchester chemist and president of the English Zionist Federation, and Nahum Sokolow became its de facto leaders.[cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] [cite:weizmann_papers_v9] A movement whose formal headquarters sat in one belligerent country was represented, in the capital of another, by the leaders of a national federation. That arrangement put the English Federation at the center of the movement's dealings with the British government.

It also explains a detail of the Balfour Declaration that is often misread. In May 1917 the official bodies of British Jewry, the Board of Deputies and the Anglo-Jewish Association, published an anti-Zionist statement in The Times. Days later the English Zionist Federation held a conference in London. Because the community's recognized representative bodies had publicly opposed Zionism, they could not serve as the recipients of a pro-Zionist declaration. The letter was therefore addressed to Lord Rothschild, for transmission to the Zionist Federation.[cite:jpost_why_rothschild]

The choice of addressee, then, reflected a division within British Jewry rather than the influence of a family. The Federation built in 1899 was the channel; the name on the envelope was a matter of who could receive the letter when the official institutions would not. The declaration itself, its drafting and its consequences, belong to [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration]. What this section establishes is the apparatus that was in place to receive it: a dues-paying membership, an elected Congress, an executive and council, financial and land-buying institutions, and national federations able to speak to governments.
