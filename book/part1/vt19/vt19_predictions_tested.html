The Five Tests, Scored

Part One wrote down five tests before any evidence was weighed, so that the front hypothesis could not be kept alive by reading every fact as part of the cover. [scene:vt1_front_test|What a front would look like] Each test named an observation that would favour the reading that the Holy See's refusal of Zionism was a front for private support, an observation that would favour the reading that it was real policy, and the possibility that the test would come back empty because the papers are closed or because both readings predict the same thing. Parts Two to Eighteen have now set out the record from 1904 to October 2026. This section scores each test against what those parts documented, adding no new evidence, and states for each whether it favours one reading, the other, or neither. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] [cite:vt1_va_lateran]

Test one: the private record

The front reading predicted internal papers encouraging what public statements opposed. The record runs the other way wherever it can be read. The earliest private account is Herzl's own diary of January 1904, written by the man with every reason to report success, and it records a refusal: "We cannot give approval to this movement." [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] The densest private record is the Holy See's wartime correspondence of 1943, published in its own edition of documents: Tardini wrote that the Holy See "has never approved the project of making Palestine a Jewish home", Maglione warned of "new and grave international problems", Roncalli called the restoration of a Jewish kingdom "only a utopia", and Cicognani told President Roosevelt's envoy on 22 June 1943 that a "more fitting territory than Palestine" could be found. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] [cite:vt16_jta1976] [cite:vt16_cicognani1943] These letters were private, sent to governments, and harder than anything the Holy See said in public in those years. The one later private record available, Irish diplomatic files on the Catholic-mandate idea of 1948–49, shows the Holy See declining to "disclose its hand", holding back from its public aim rather than working against it. [cite:vt17_ewtn_irishfiles]

Two items point the other way, and Part One's rule for them was that one party's report of private warmth needs a second record before it counts. Nahum Sokolow's report of a cordial audience with Benedict XV in May 1917 is one party's account, and Sergio Minerbi's archival version gives the pope's terms as protection of the holy places, to be settled "between the Church and the Great Powers." [cite:vt16_ej_minerbi] The handwritten 1944 note in which Pius XII is said to have written that "The Jews need a land of their own" was announced by the Pave the Way Foundation in 2012 without an image, a transcription or an archival reference, and no publication of it was found after the Pius XII files opened. [cite:vt16_ptwf_zenit] Neither item was confirmed by a Holy See paper. The test therefore favours real policy for 1904 to 1949, on the papers published. It is empty for 1958 to 1993, whose papers are closed, and incomplete for 1945 to 1958, whose papers are open but whose first study on Palestine, Maria Chiara Rioli's chapter of 2024, could not be checked for this volume. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] [cite:vt17_berghahn_rioli]

Test two: money and favours

The front reading predicted something of value passing from the Holy See to the Zionist movement or to Israel. No such transfer was found in any part of this volume. The Holy See's one material commitment of the years of Israel's founding went to Palestinian Arab refugees, through the Pontifical Mission for Palestine set up by Pius XII in 1949 under Monsignor Thomas McMahon. [cite:vt17_cnewa_pmp] The best-known financial link between the papacy and a Jewish banking family runs the other way and ends early: the Rothschild houses lent to the government of the Papal States from 1831 into the 1850s, with the pope as debtor, and the borrower ceased to exist in 1870. [cite:wa_ra_naples] [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] Part Eleven tested the claimed "unbroken chain" from those loans to 2026 link by link and found two dated ends with nothing between them; the appointment of François Pauly, a former head of the Edmond de Rothschild group, to chair the IOR's board in 2026 is a fact about one banker's career, with no wrongdoing documented. [cite:vt11_iv_pauly] [scene:vt11_chain_tested|The chain tested]

The favours that would matter most, diplomatic help at a decisive moment, were looked for and not found. Whether the Holy See pressed Catholic delegations at the United Nations in 1947, for or against partition, is not settled by any document checked; the one account of a meeting between the Jewish Agency's Moshe Sneh and Domenico Tardini on 3 October 1947 says Tardini "did not make any promises" and cites no archive. [cite:vt17_tenembaum_sneh] Pave the Way's further claim that the Vatican encouraged Spain to recognise Israel in 1955 names no source. [cite:vt16_ptwf_zenit] "Checked, nothing documented" is a result: the test favours real policy.

Test three: the timing of shifts

The front reading predicted softening when the movement or the state needed support. Part Seventeen dated every shift, and none fits that pattern. The theological changes of 1965, 1974 and 1985, from Nostra Aetate to the Notes, came from a council and a commission with a religious mandate and left the state aside; the Notes said the state was to be judged by "the common principles of international law." [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] The diplomatic changes followed events and the Palestinian track: the call for an internationalised Jerusalem became a call for an internationally guaranteed statute after Israel took the eastern city in 1967; the first naming of the State of Israel in a papal letter, in 1984, came with a Palestinian right to a homeland in the same text; negotiations opened in 1992 after Palestinians sat at the Madrid table; and recognition followed the Israel–PLO letters of September 1993. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] [cite:vt17_state_madrid] [cite:vt17_jcpa_schvindlerman] Public Israeli pressure, over the Arafat audience of 1982 and the Waldheim audience of 1987, did not move the Holy See. [cite:vt17_csm_arafat1982] [cite:vt17_djn_waldheim] The reasons Rabbi David Rosen recorded from inside the negotiation, the Church's communities in Arab countries, the local church and the legal security of its institutions, are the Holy See's own stated interests. [cite:vt17_rosen_1994] The test favours real policy, with one caution: the Secretariat of State's negotiating papers for 1992–93 are closed, so the reading rests on dates and published texts.

Test four: treaty behaviour

Part One warned that the neutrality clauses alone could not decide this test, because a covert partner might also sign them; the answer lay in what else the treaties contain. The Fundamental Agreement of 30 December 1993 secures what the Holy See had named since 1948: the Church's right to its institutions (Article 3), the status quo in the holy places (Article 4) and negotiation of its property and fiscal questions (Article 10). Article 11 kept recognition from becoming a position on "disputed territories and unsettled borders", and the Holy See said on the day of signing that it still sought a special status for Jerusalem. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] [cite:vt17_ap_fa1993] Nothing in the treaty concedes the city or the territories. The political clauses the Holy See did sign are in its treaties with the Palestinian side: the Basic Agreement with the PLO of 15 February 2000 called unilateral changes to Jerusalem's character "morally and legally unacceptable", and the Comprehensive Agreement of 26 June 2015 was signed with a State of Palestine whose territory the preamble defines to include East Jerusalem. Israel's foreign ministry called the 2015 treaty one-sided. [cite:vt18_va_plo2000] [cite:vt18_va_bull2015] [cite:ts11_wiki_hspal] The economic agreement promised to Israel in 1993 remains unsigned as of October 2026. [cite:vt18_press_2017] The test favours real policy.

Test five: the cost of opposition

The front reading predicted opposition that cost the movement nothing it needed. The record shows opposition that was spent and complained of, though it rarely won. The Holy See sent written objections to the League of Nations against the draft Mandate in 1922 and lost when the Mandate was approved on 24 July. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] [cite:vt16_un_mandate] It objected to the Peel partition plan in 1937, gave Moshe Shertok no support in 1945, supported an international Jerusalem that Israel opposed in the vote on resolution 303 in 1949, and withheld recognition for forty-five years. [cite:vt16_ej_vatican] [cite:vt17_wiki_res303] Israeli leaders said what that cost them: Israel's foreign ministry wrote that Paul VI's visit of 1964 made "patently, and painfully, clear" that the Holy See did not recognise Israel, and Prime Minister Shamir said in 1987 that without relations Israel "can do nothing but express its unhappiness." [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] [cite:vt17_djn_waldheim] Those are the complaints and foreign-file traces the real-policy reading predicted. The opposition did not stop the Mandate, partition or Israel's hold on Jerusalem, and most Catholic-majority delegations voted for partition in 1947. [cite:vt17_tenembaum_sneh] The test favours real policy, but it also shows a policy of limited effect.

After recognition, Part One's table predicted that a front would tilt toward Israel once the cover was no longer needed. Part Eighteen found the same interests pursued with both sides: treaties with the PLO and the State of Palestine, Francis honouring Herzl's grave in May 2014 a day after praying at the separation barrier and speaking of the "state of Palestine", and, from 2023, words on Gaza that Israel's foreign ministry called "double standards", without any rupture of relations. [cite:vt18_jta_francis2014] [cite:vt18_almon_cruelty2024] The gesture at Herzl's grave was public, made after recognition, and balanced on the same journey; it is evidence of the relationship opened in 1994, not of an alignment hidden before it. The absence of rupture matches the treaty clauses and the Holy See's practice in Berlin to 1945, Damascus and Nicaragua. [cite:vt18_wiki_orsenigo] [cite:vt18_vn_nicaragua2023] [scene:vt18_why_no_sever|Why the Holy See does not sever]

No test came back in favour of the front reading. Four came back clearly for real policy, one of them with a closed archive behind it, and the fifth showed a real policy that cost Israel something without changing the decisive outcomes. What that adds up to, and how strong the answer is, is the subject of [scene:vt19_verdict|the next section].
