The Shepherd, the Dealer and the Advertisement

The Israel Antiquities Authority tells the story as it has come down: in 1947 a young Bedouin shepherd, searching for a stray animal, threw a stone into a cave near Qumran, on the northwest shore of the Dead Sea, and heard pottery break. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] The shepherds returned with seven scrolls. Two dealers in Bethlehem bought them: Kando, a dealer in antiquities, bought four, and another dealer, Salahi, bought three. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] Kando sold his four to Archbishop Samuel of St Mark's Monastery, the Syrian Orthodox metropolitan in Jerusalem, who took them to the American Schools of Oriental Research in East Jerusalem to learn whether they were genuine. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] [cite:ds_cojs_acq]

The other three went to a Jewish scholar. Eleazar Sukenik, a professor at the Hebrew University, recognised them as texts from the Second Temple period and acquired them. [cite:ds_cojs_acq] The date of his trip later became part of the legend. According to one account, on 29 November 1947 he travelled by bus to Bethlehem, where the dealers lent him three parchments to examine; he was studying them at home when the radio reported that the United Nations had voted to partition Palestine, and by the next morning fighting had broken out across the country. [cite:ds_icej_sukenik] His son, the soldier and archaeologist Yigael Yadin, later wrote that he could not avoid the feeling that there was something symbolic in the timing. [cite:ds_icej_sukenik]

The archbishop's four scrolls took a longer road. One of them was the Great Isaiah Scroll; the archbishop brought it to the United States in 1949, and the American Schools published it in 1950. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] On 1 June 1954 an advertisement appeared in The Wall Street Journal: "The Four Dead Sea Scroll - Biblical Manuscripts dating back to at least 200 BC, are for sale." [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] The advertisement suggested they "would be an ideal gift to an educational or religious institution." [cite:ds_cojs_acq] Yadin, acting for the State of Israel through an American middleman, bought them for $250,000 with the help of the American philanthropist D. S. Gottesman. [cite:ds_cojs_acq] [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] The purchase was announced in February 1955, and the scrolls were brought to Israel. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] Yadin was Sukenik's son; his father had died before he could acquire them. [cite:ds_cojs_acq]

Meanwhile the caves kept giving. Over the nine years after the first find, ten more caves near Qumran yielded scrolls, eleven in all, and the collection grew to the remains of more than 900 manuscripts in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek, broken into tens of thousands of fragments. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] Encyclopaedia Britannica counts about 15,000 fragments, most of them tiny, from roughly 800 to 900 original manuscripts, dating from the third century before Christ to the second century after. [cite:ds_brit_dss] Qumran itself had passed under Jordanian control in the war of 1948, and the later finds went to the Palestine Archaeological Museum in East Jerusalem, now the Rockefeller Museum. [cite:ds_cojs_acq]

The money shows who could afford the past. The shepherds sold to dealers in Bethlehem; the dealers sold to a churchman and a professor; the churchman sold to the highest bidder through a financial newspaper in New York. Seven scrolls found by a shepherd ended up, within eight years, as the property of a state that had not existed when they were found, paid for by an American philanthropist. [cite:ds_cojs_acq] The same dealer, Kando, and his family remained at the centre of the scroll trade for the next seventy years, as Part Six shows. [cite:ds_ng_forgeries]
