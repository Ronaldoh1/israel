Palestine in Congress and the Party Platforms, 1944–1945

It is often assumed that Congress and the two American parties took up Palestine only after the war, and then on its merits. The record of 1944 tells a different story. In that election year a resolution for a Jewish commonwealth went before both houses, the Army and the War Department asked Congress to shelve it, both parties wrote Palestine into their platforms, for the first time by the council's own appraisal, and the War Department dropped its objection days before the November vote. The State Department then held the resolution back again. A changed version passed only in December 1945. The story turns as much on the timing of these moves as on their content, and much of it can be read in the American Zionist Emergency Council's own records.

The longer frame. Congress had spoken on Palestine before. After President Wilson endorsed the Balfour Declaration in 1918, Congress passed the Lodge–Fish resolution unanimously in 1922, an episode told in an earlier chapter of this library. Over the following three decades its pattern held. Congress passed or pushed resolutions of support. It never voted to create a Jewish state or to recognize one: partition and recognition, when they came, were acts of the executive [cite:w_celler] [cite:w_sol_bloom]. Congress shaped the climate; the executive made the decisions. The resolutions of 1944–45 belong to that pattern.

The resolutions of January 1944. Early in 1944 two matching resolutions were introduced, each with one Democratic and one Republican sponsor. In the House they were Representatives James Wright, a Democrat of Pennsylvania, and Ranulf Compton, a Republican of Connecticut. In the Senate they were Robert Wagner, a Democrat of New York, and Robert Taft, a Republican of Ohio. Both called for unrestricted Jewish immigration to Palestine and for a "free and democratic Jewish commonwealth" [cite:djn_1944_hearings] [cite:aja_azec_dec1944]. Both demands ran against the British policy of the day, set out in [scene:rd4_white_paper|the White Paper of 1939]. The council's local committees began their first nationwide letter campaign the same month, as the previous section describes [cite:aja_azec_local_1944].

The House Foreign Affairs Committee held hearings. Not every Jewish witness supported the resolution. Among the opponents was Rabbi Morris Lazaron, who told the committee that Zionism was an internal Jewish matter and not a question for Congress [cite:djn_1944_hearings]. His testimony is a reminder that American Jewish opinion in 1944 was not unanimous, and that some of the resistance came from inside the community the council claimed to speak for.

The generals ask for delay. In March 1944 the Army Chief of Staff, General George Marshall, and the Secretary of War, Henry Stimson, asked Congress to postpone action for military reasons. The committee deferred [cite:djn_1944_marshall]. The council's records describe the same step: Stimson opposed the Wagner–Taft resolution on military grounds, and it was put aside [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:aja_azec_1944_record]. The War Department's case was about the conduct of a war still being fought. The sources here do not set out its full reasoning. The State Department, for its part, had its own plan, approved by Roosevelt, to suppress public discussion of the Palestine question for the duration of the war; that plan and Roosevelt's two registers on Palestine are examined in [scene:s07c2|Roosevelt's wartime position].

"The first time in American history." Blocked in Congress, the issue moved to the parties. That summer both party conventions adopted Palestine planks. The council's own appraisal of the year called it "the first time in American history that Palestine was included in the party platforms" [cite:aja_azec_appraisal_1944]. For an organization founded only the previous August, this was a striking result. It meant that in a presidential election year Palestine had become a matter on which both parties had taken a public position, and on which their candidates could be held to account.

The objection withdrawn, days before the election. In October Stimson wrote to Senator Taft. According to the record of that letter, he told Taft that political considerations now outweighed military ones [cite:aja_silver_1944]. Days before the November election, the War Department had in effect withdrawn its objection [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:aja_azec_1944_record]. On 30 November, after the election, the House Foreign Affairs Committee approved the resolution [cite:aja_azec_dec1944]. Then, in December 1944, action was deferred again. The council's records attribute the deferral to the insistence of the State Department; another account describes it as a deferral at the administration's request [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:aja_silver_1944]. The setback prompted an internal crisis at the council [cite:aja_azec_1944_record]. Silver resigned as co-chairman during a 1944–45 controversy over how hard to press the government, as the previous section notes; the record here does not say whether that controversy and this crisis were one and the same.

December 1945: a different text. The resolution came back after the war, with Truman in the White House. On 17 and 19 December 1945 both houses adopted a concurrent resolution [cite:frus1945_concres]. It had the support of the American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council on Palestine [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee]. Its wording had changed, and the State Department noticed. A department memorandum observed that the resolution called not for a "Jewish commonwealth" but for a "democratic commonwealth in which all men, regardless of race or creed, shall have equal rights" [cite:frus1945_concres]. The phrase that had defined the 1944 text was gone.

Who carried it. The champions in Congress were named members of both parties. Wagner and Taft led in the Senate. In the House, Representative Emanuel Celler of Brooklyn and Sol Bloom, chairman of the Foreign Affairs Committee, were among the leading supporters [cite:w_celler] [cite:w_sol_bloom]. Bloom was also, as the council's own files show, a target of its telegram campaigns: the council pressed its allies as well as its opponents [cite:aja_azec_local_1944].

How the structure worked. The episode shows who held which lever. Members of Congress could introduce and approve resolutions, and the party conventions could adopt planks; both were open to organized constituent pressure, which is where the council concentrated its work. The War Department and the State Department could not vote, but they could ask committees to wait, and in 1944 they did so twice. The force of a resolution or a plank, as the council's own use of them shows, lay in putting members and parties on record. By 1944 Palestine had become a party-platform and election-timing issue, with the military and diplomatic bureaucracies on the other side. It is the same alignment the record shows again in Truman's decisions of 1947–48.

Reading the timing. The sequence invites two readings, and the record supports parts of each. One reads the year as the work of electoral politics: a resolution shelved on military grounds in March was cleared by the Secretary of War in a letter to a Republican senator days before a presidential election, after both parties had written the issue into their platforms. Stimson's own reported words, that political considerations now outweighed military ones, give this reading its strongest support [cite:aja_silver_1944]. The other reading takes the military objection at face value. The resolution came while the war was still being fought; Marshall and Stimson asked only for postponement, not rejection; and after the election the executive branch, through the State Department, again asked Congress to wait, which an administration acting only on electoral motives had no reason to do. The second deferral fits that reading better than the first.

What the record does not settle. The sources here do not show who asked Stimson to write to Taft, or what he weighed beyond the one sentence the record preserves. They do not give the vote counts of December 1945, or explain who changed the resolution's wording from "Jewish commonwealth" to "equal rights," and why. They do not say whether the 1945 text kept the 1944 call for unrestricted immigration. And they do not measure how far the council's letters, telegrams and visits moved any member's position, as against the views members already held. What they do show is that by the end of 1944, four years before statehood, Palestine had been placed inside American electoral politics by an organized campaign, and that the War and State Departments were already its main opponents inside the government.
