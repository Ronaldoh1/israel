The declaration of May 1948 ended one story and opened another. The years that followed were spent consolidating what the war had produced and, more slowly, accounting for it. This stage covers both tasks. It follows the new state's relationship with Washington through an election year, a loan, a formal recognition and a threatened revision of policy. It follows the laws that turned wartime flight into permanent title, the conference at Lausanne where return and borders were negotiated and not resolved, and the single paragraph of a United Nations resolution on which every later negotiation would turn.

It also steps back. The same week in December 1948 that produced the modern human-rights order also produced the refugee resolution, and this stage asks what that order could and could not do for the people already displaced. It traces how the flight of 1948 was explained to Western readers, and how long it took the archives to correct that account. And it closes the books on the pre-state period itself: who paid, through which channels, who connected to whom by what documented tie, and what the record can and cannot show about money and politicians.

The question running through every section is the same. Once the state existed, what did its founding actually rest on, and what did it leave unsettled?
