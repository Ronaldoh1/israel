No State, No Currency: How the Money Moved

It is usually assumed that a new state needs years to build its own financial system. Israel did not have to. When independence came in 1948, the institutions that would carry its money had been operating for decades, and the first banknotes it would use had already been arranged, quietly, in New York.

Under the Mandate, the currency of the country was British in origin and in management. In 1926 the British Colonial Secretary appointed a Palestine Currency Board. From 1 November 1927 it issued the Palestine pound, at par with sterling and divided into 1,000 mils [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. It was a currency managed from London. Its notes remained in circulation until 18 August 1948 [cite:israelmint_pcb_notes].

The Zionist movement's own finance ran through a separate channel, also anchored in London. The Jewish Colonial Trust had been chartered there in 1899, and its operating subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Bank, was founded in 1902. The bank was London-based and owned through the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_israeli_pound] [cite:wiki_palestine_pound]. For half a century these two institutions carried the movement's money. They were, in effect, a banking system without a state.

The decisive step came just before independence. The Anglo-Palestine Bank's chairman, Eliezer Hoofien, persuaded the American Bank Note Company in New York to print new notes [cite:israelmint_aplb_series]. Because the name of the state and its currency had not yet been decided, the notes were denominated in "Palestine pounds." According to Israel's state mint, the notes were deliberately left unmarked in two respects: they did not carry the words "legal tender," and the printer's name was omitted. The purpose, the mint states, was to circumvent the need for approval by the United States Secretary of State to print foreign currency [cite:israelmint_aplb_series].

The notes reached the country secretly in July 1948. They became legal tender by law on 17 August 1948 [cite:jhom_anglo_palestine_notes]. The British-issued Palestine pound left circulation the following day. A currency printed abroad, without its printer's name or its legal status on its face, had become the money of the new state.

The transition from movement bank to national bank was completed three years later. On 1 May 1951 the Anglo-Palestine Bank's assets passed to Bank Leumi le-Israel, and the currency became the Israeli pound [cite:wiki_israeli_pound].

The sequence answers a practical question: what currency the new state used, which banks held its money, and how it obtained money it could spend. The answer is that the financial system came before the state. A bank whose parent trust was chartered in London in 1899 had operated through the entire Mandate, and when the moment came it was that bank's chairman who arranged for the state's first currency. The Anglo-Palestine Bank would also play a part in one of the most contested arrangements of the 1930s, the transfer agreement with Nazi Germany, described in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement].
