Roosevelt and Ibn Saud: The Promise and Its Fate

On 14 February 1945, aboard an American warship in the Suez Canal, Franklin Roosevelt sat down with the king of Saudi Arabia. It is commonly assumed that American support for the creation of Israel ran in an unbroken line from Roosevelt to Truman. That meeting complicates the assumption. There Roosevelt gave the king a direct assurance about Palestine, and he later put it in writing. Within three years his successor had acted without honoring it.

Roosevelt was returning from the Yalta Conference. He met King Abdulaziz Ibn Saud aboard the USS Quincy in the Great Bitter Lake. The meeting was arranged deliberately, in person, not fitted in as an incidental stop. It required Ibn Saud, who rarely left Arabia and had serious reservations about the journey, to travel by ship for the encounter. Roosevelt, two months from his death and visibly declining, sat with him for roughly five hours, longer than most of his wartime summit sessions with other heads of state [cite:brookings_quincy_75years] [cite:wiki_uss_quincy_ca71]. Both sides, in other words, invested real effort. Roosevelt later called the meeting "so outstanding a success," and told his daughter Anna that he had "learned more about the whole problem of Palestine and the Middle East by talking with Ibn Saud for five minutes than I could have learned in an exchange of two or three dozen letters" [cite:brookings_quincy_75years].

The context was not only diplomatic. By 1945 the Arabian American Oil Company, ARAMCO, had already established the core of the American oil relationship with Saudi Arabia [cite:loc_aramco_oil_pamphlet]. The meeting reflected a high-stakes strategic partnership that the American government clearly understood. It was not a courtesy call.

Ibn Saud came to press the question of Palestine, and he did. He was the founding monarch of Saudi Arabia, reigning from 1932 to 1953. Multiple accounts describe him pressing Roosevelt directly and forcefully, treating continued large-scale Jewish immigration as a fundamental threat to Arab interests in the region. When Roosevelt argued for a Jewish state in Palestine, Ibn Saud reportedly countered that the Jews should instead be given a state in Bavaria, in Germany [cite:wiki_abdulaziz_saudi] [cite:brookings_quincy_75years]. The exchange shows how sharply the substance was contested. It should not be taken as the whole of the king's position or of the conversation. No complete stenographic record of the meeting survives, so quoted exchanges beyond the written correspondence that followed should be treated with caution.

What is firmly documented is the assurance itself. Roosevelt told Ibn Saud that the United States would make no decision altering the basic situation in Palestine without full prior consultation with both Arabs and Jews, and that it would take no action hostile to the Arab people. He did not leave it as a spoken impression. On 5 April 1945, one week before his death, he reaffirmed the same commitment in a letter to the king [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition]. Did the highest level of American leadership understand the stakes? Roosevelt's own words answer that. He heard the concerns directly from the head of state most affected, and he made a specific, written promise in response.

Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945. On 14 May 1948 his successor, Harry Truman, recognized Israel eleven minutes after its declaration, without the prior consultation with Arab leaders that Roosevelt had promised [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud]. The process that led to recognition ran through Clark Clifford's domestic electoral calculations, not through any such consultation. That was a documented change of approach between two presidents, not a continuation of Roosevelt's stated policy.

Two limits keep this finding honest. First, the record cannot say what Roosevelt himself would have done in 1947 and 1948. He died before partition or recognition came before him, and there is no evidence of how he would have weighed his promise against the domestic pressures his successor faced. Second, the record does not show that Truman knew of Roosevelt's specific promise, felt bound by it, or set out to break it. Institutional continuity makes his awareness a reasonable inference, but it remains an inference. What the documents establish is narrower and firmer: a written presidential promise in April 1945, and a different course taken in May 1948.
