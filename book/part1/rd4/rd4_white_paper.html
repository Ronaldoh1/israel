The White Paper of May 1939

Partition had been proposed in 1937 and abandoned by the end of 1938, and the London conference of early 1939 had ended without agreement (see [scene:rd4_woodhead|how partition was dropped]). Britain then set its own course. The result was a command paper, Palestine: Statement of Policy, Cmd 6019, dated 17 May 1939. Parliament approved the policy on 23 May. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_kramer]

The familiar account treats Israel's founding as the gradual fulfilment of the Balfour Declaration's promise, unfolding step by step from 1917 to 1948. The White Paper is the clearest evidence against that picture of a smooth path. In 1939 the power that had issued the Balfour Declaration and held the Mandate wrote down, in its own words, a policy that pointed in the other direction, and the men who led the Zionist movement read it as Britain reneging on Balfour entirely. [cite:whitepaper1939]

The text was direct on the central question. It declared that "it is not part of their policy that Palestine should become a Jewish State." Its stated objective was "the establishment within ten years of an independent Palestine State," bound to the United Kingdom by treaty, after a transitional period. A review body was to be set up five years after the restoration of peace and order. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d826]

On immigration the paper set a number and a stopping point. It provided for "some 75,000 immigrants over the next five years": a quota of 10,000 Jewish immigrants a year for five years, plus 25,000 refugees to be admitted when the High Commissioner was satisfied that provision could be made for them. Illegal immigrants were to be deducted from the yearly quotas. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_bjpa_jacase] After the five years, "no further Jewish immigration will be permitted unless the Arabs of Palestine are prepared to acquiesce in it." [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text] The effect, as Churchill quoted the paper back to the Commons, was to bring the Jewish population to approximately one-third of the total. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_kramer] On land the paper was brief: "the High Commissioner will be given general powers to prohibit and regulate transfers of land." [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text]

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. Every lever in the paper sat with the British administration. The High Commissioner decided when the 25,000 refugees could enter and gained general powers over land transfers. The deduction rule meant that each person who arrived without a certificate reduced the legal quota by one. After five years the decision passed, on immigration alone, to the Arabs of Palestine, whose acquiescence was now the condition for any further Jewish entry.

Timing shaped how the cap was received as much as the number did. The same limit would have read very differently in a calm decade. Issued in May 1939, months before the war that would make Palestine one of the few remaining escape routes for European Jews, it arrived, as Zionist leaders saw it, at the exact moment the need to leave was most urgent. [cite:whitepaper1939] Six months earlier, Kristallnacht had shown what that need meant inside Germany. The sources for this account document the restriction and its reception; they do not set out Britain's full stated rationale for it, which this book treats in its account of the Mandate's strategic and administrative considerations elsewhere.

The paper was debated in the House of Commons on 22 and 23 May 1939, and some of the sharpest criticism came from the government's own side and from the Labour benches. Leo Amery called the policy a repudiation of Britain's pledges. Winston Churchill called it "an act of repudiation" and said he would vote against it. For Labour, Herbert Morrison called it "a cynical breach of pledges given to the Jews and the world, including America," and urged that it be referred to the Permanent Mandates Commission of the League of Nations. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jvl_debate] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jewishlink] The government nonetheless carried the House, and the policy was approved on 23 May. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841] (Labour's later record against the paper, and what it did once in power, is followed in [scene:rd4_labour|the British side as the war ended].)

The Jewish Agency answered in its own terms, as a legal case. On 31 May 1939 Chaim Weizmann wrote to the High Commissioner that the White Paper meant "denying to the Jewish people the right to reconstitute their National Home in Palestine." The Agency's memorandum to the Permanent Mandates Commission, published in London in June as The Jewish Case against the Palestine White Paper, argued that the paper breached Articles 2, 6 and 15 of the Mandate, and that an Arab veto on immigration could not be squared with the Mandate's duty to "facilitate Jewish immigration." [cite:rs_rd4_wp_bjpa_jacase] In its strongest form, the case was that the Mandate bound Britain, and that a duty to facilitate immigration could not be turned into a power to end it.

The Arab reception is harder to state in one line, and the sources here pull in two directions. The United States consul general in Jerusalem, George Wadsworth, reported to Washington in early June 1939 that the promise of an independent state within ten years was broadly acceptable to Arabs and strongly opposed by Zionists. Jews, he wrote, responded with bitterness and "a sullen determination to continue" building the national home. Arabs showed "very obvious relief," but distrusted British intentions and resented the indefinite ban on the return of the Mufti of Jerusalem, Hajj Amin al-Husseini. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d826]

The Arab Higher Committee nevertheless rejected the White Paper. It demanded a complete prohibition of Jewish immigration and, by one account, dismissed the promised independence as illusory. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_wiki] Why it did so is disputed. According to a secondary summary of Benny Morris's work, Morris attributes al-Husseini's rejection in part to the fact that the plan did not place him at the head of the future state; Morris's own text has not been read for this book, and the attribution should be treated as his interpretation, reported at second hand. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_wiki] The other reading takes the committee at its own word: it wanted a complete halt to Jewish immigration, which the paper did not give, and it did not trust the independence the paper promised. Either reading can stand alongside Wadsworth's report, which describes relief at the paper's provisions together with distrust of British intentions; the formal rejection came from the committee, the relief from the wider Arab public he observed.

The Permanent Mandates Commission took up the paper at its 36th session, from 8 to 29 June 1939. Its members held unanimously that the policy was not in accordance with the interpretation the Commission had always placed on the Mandate. On the harder question, whether the policy nevertheless conformed to the Mandate, they split. By a majority of one, four members to three, the Commission found that it could not say the policy conformed. The minority of three held that existing circumstances could justify the policy, provided the League Council did not object. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jvl_league] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_wiki_pmc] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jewishlink]

So the legal question divided the bodies that weighed it. Against the paper's conformity with the Mandate stood the Jewish Agency, Morrison and Churchill in the Commons, and four of the Commission's seven members. For it, or for its justification in the circumstances, stood the British government, which carried Parliament, and the Commission's minority of three. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_bjpa_jacase] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jvl_debate] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841] The body that could have decided the matter for the League never did. War broke out before the League Council could act, and the White Paper never came before it. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_jvl_league] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_wiki_pmc]

Britain had meanwhile begun to apply the paper's terms. In July 1939, citing illegal immigration, it suspended the next six-month quota of controlled Jewish immigration. In the Commons debate of 20 July Malcolm MacDonald said it "would have been a breach of that policy" not to suspend it. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841] The deduction rule written into the paper in May had become, within two months, the ground for closing the legal door for half a year. What followed at sea, as ships carrying refugees were turned away, is told later in this chapter.

Once the war began, the Jewish leadership in Palestine faced a Britain that was enforcing the White Paper and fighting Germany at the same time. David Ben-Gurion's answer, as it is usually given, was to help the British army "as if there were no White Paper" and to fight the White Paper "as if there were no war." That wording comes from a secondary summary; the original source has not been read for this book. [cite:rs_rd4_wp_wiki] Three years later, in a New York hotel, the movement's answer to the White Paper took the form of an explicit demand for a Jewish commonwealth (see [scene:s07b|the Biltmore Conference]).

The one-third figure can be set against the population record. On the figures compiled for this library's account of the censuses, Jews made up about 11 percent of Palestine's population in 1922 and 17 percent in 1931; by the United Nations committee's estimate of late 1946 the share was 33 percent. The White Paper's ceiling of approximately one-third [cite:rs_rd4_wp_kramer] sat close to the share recorded in 1946. The sources here do not break that growth down by legal quota, unauthorized entry or natural increase, so the comparison shows only where the line ended, not how it got there.

Several things remain unsettled by the sources gathered here. They do not give the Commons division on 23 May, the number of the 75,000 places actually used by the end of the five years, or the text and zones of the land-transfer regulations Britain issued under the paper's land clause; each of these is reported elsewhere but has not been verified for this book, and the account above does not rely on them. Nor do they settle what moved the Arab Higher Committee to reject a paper that its own public appeared to welcome. And the legal question was left open by history itself: the only body empowered to rule on the paper's conformity with the Mandate never met to do so.
