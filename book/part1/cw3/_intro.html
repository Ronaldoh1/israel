On 26 July 1956 Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, a joint British and French enterprise that had run the Canal since 1869, and offered "full economic compensation" for it. He acted after the United States and Britain had refused Egypt economic aid, and Eden judged in private that "the Egyptian has his thumb on our windpipe." [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] Israel's grievances with Egypt were older. The Security Council had called on Egypt in 1951, by eight votes to none, to end its restrictions on shipping through the Canal, and the restrictions still stood; in September 1955 Egypt had closed the Straits of Tiran to all vessels; in October 1955 it had bound Syria, and later Jordan, into a military treaty; and it was receiving what Ben-Gurion called "enormous quantities of heavy arms" from Czechoslovakia. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov] Through August, September and October Britain hinted at force, Dulles's users' association of 18 maritime nations failed to win full support, and Washington's files filled with violence on the Israel–Jordan border, an Iraqi proposal to move troops into Jordan and Israeli mobilization. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3]

This stage asks how a war planned in secret by three governments was fought, stopped and undone within about seven months, and what that reversal left in place. It asks with equal weight what the war did to the people who had no say in it: Arab citizens of Israel under curfew, refugees in Gaza under a new occupation, and the Jews of Egypt.

The cast spans four capitals and the United Nations. In Jerusalem, Ben-Gurion was prime minister, with Moshe Dayan commanding the campaign, Shimon Peres dealing with France, Abba Eban as ambassador in Washington and, by the spring, Golda Meir speaking for Israel at the United Nations as foreign minister. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] In Paris stood Mollet, Pineau, Bourgès-Maunoury and General Challe; in London, Eden and Selwyn Lloyd. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] In Cairo, Nasser. In Washington, Eisenhower and Dulles, with Treasury Secretary Humphrey holding the purse and a Congress whose leaders would not follow the President all the way. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] Moscow spoke through Bulganin's notes. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin1] At the United Nations, Hammarskjöld, Canada's Lester Pearson and the Director of UNRWA each left a record that shaped the outcome or the account of it. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d467] [cite:rs_cw3_unrwa_a3212] And in a village called Kafr Qasim, a brigade commander, Issachar Shadmi, a Border Police major, Shmuel Malinki, and a lieutenant, Gabriel Dahan, stood in a chain of command whose last link was a platoon at the village entrance. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq]

The sections follow events in order. They begin with [scene:cw3_road_to_war|the road to war] and Israel's case for it in its own words, then the conference at Sèvres, where Britain, France and Israel wrote down who would attack whom, and when. The first evening of the war brings the killings at Kafr Qasim, inside Israel. The fighting itself follows, in Sinai and at Port Said, with every casualty figure and its source. Washington then turns on its allies at the United Nations and with money and oil; Khan Yunis and Rafah record what happened in Gaza in the first days of occupation; Moscow's notes arrive in the week of Hungary; and Israel moves in about thirty-six hours from talk of a "Third Kingdom" to agreeing to withdraw. Egypt's measures against its Jews, Israel's four months of rule in Gaza and the withdrawal fight of early 1957 come next. Two sections then follow Kafr Qasim through its trial and its long memory, beyond the period of this stage, and the last, [scene:cw3_aftermath|the architecture in spring 1957], takes stock of the order that replaced the one the war broke.
