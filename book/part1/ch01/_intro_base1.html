Every argument about Israel and the Palestinians uses a handful of terms before it uses any history: a map, a population, a passport and three legal words. This chapter fixes them first. The chapters that follow lean on them on nearly every page, and a great deal of public confusion begins with them rather than with the past. The chapter also states how this book weighs evidence, so that a reader can hold each later claim against a rule set out in advance.

It opens with [scene:sk_s00|the method]: four levels of evidence (documented, reported, contested and unresolved), both sides' official records, advocacy labeled as advocacy, and three questions for testing a viral claim. The map follows: Israel within the 1949 armistice lines, the West Bank as divided by the Oslo II agreement of 1995 into Areas A, B and C, Gaza, and East Jerusalem, annexed under a Basic Law of 1980 that the Security Council declared null and void.[cite:wiki_oslo_areas] [cite:unsc478_text] Next comes the population: about 14.8 million Palestinians worldwide in 2024, living in four legal situations that run from Israeli citizenship to refugee registration, with the military government of 1949–1966 and the Nation-State Law of 2018 as two tests of what citizenship has meant.[cite:alquds_pcbs_refugees] [cite:jta_nsl_upheld] A section on citizenship reads the Law of Return of 5 July 1950, the Nationality Law of 1952 and the family-unification ban of 2003 from their own texts.[cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] [cite:rs_ch01_idi_citizenship] The chapter ends with occupation, apartheid and genocide: what each means in law, which bodies have applied each to Israel, among them the International Court of Justice in its advisory opinion of 19 July 2024, and Israel's rejection of every finding.[cite:icj_press_19jul24]

Nothing here settles a political question. The chapter's task is narrower: to make sure that when later chapters say "Area C," "a citizen" or "the court found," the reader knows exactly what is meant, and what is not.
