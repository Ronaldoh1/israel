Eleven Minutes: How Washington Recognized Israel, and What It Withheld in 1948

The government housed in the federal district acted on Palestine through three offices that the Constitution keeps separate: the President, who recognizes foreign states; the State Department and the military, who advise him; and Congress, which passes resolutions and appropriates money. In the founding year these three pulled in different directions, and the record of 1947 and 1948 shows Washington giving the new state one indispensable thing, recognition, while withholding another, arms. Volume II tells the story in full; this section sets out the decisions in order and what each one shows about how the federal city works. [scene:rec_s01|Recognition in eleven minutes]

The warnings, autumn 1947. In the weeks before the United Nations voted on partition, the President's own advisers warned him against it in writing. Loy Henderson, who directed the State Department's Office of Near Eastern and African Affairs, told Secretary of State George Marshall that partition was "not workable" and would lead to "untold troubles," and that his view was shared by nearly every Foreign Service officer who had worked on the region. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] The Joint Chiefs of Staff warned that partition would let the Soviet Union displace American and British influence and would endanger American access to Middle Eastern oil. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] Defense Secretary James Forrestal called oil "the life blood of a war machine." [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] The United States supported partition anyway, and on 29 November 1947 it was among the thirty-three votes for Resolution 181. [cite:dv3_unproject_181]

The embargo. In the same month Washington imposed an arms embargo covering both the Jews of Palestine and the Arab states. [cite:dv4_herf_embargo] On paper it was even-handed. Critics at the time argued that, imposed just as Arab attacks began, it fell most heavily on the Jews, who had no state and no army supplied by a foreign government. [cite:dv4_herf_embargo] The weapons that carried the Jewish side through 1948 did not come from the United States. They came from Czechoslovakia, whose first contract with the Jewish Agency was signed on 14 January 1948 and whose secret airlift, Operation Balak, ran from May to August 1948, with Stalin's consent. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] [cite:wiki_operation_balak] The Soviet Union, not the United States, was the first state to grant Israel full legal (de jure) recognition, on 17 May 1948. [cite:israeled_gromyko_timeline]

The meeting of 12 May 1948. Two days before the British Mandate ended, the President met his Secretary of State, the Under Secretary, Robert Lovett, and three White House aides, Clark Clifford, David Niles and Matthew Connelly, to decide whether to recognize the Jewish state when it was proclaimed. Marshall's own memorandum of the meeting survives. Clifford argued for prompt recognition. Marshall replied that he "could not help but think that the suggestions made by Mr. Clifford were wrong," that they were "based on domestic political considerations" while the problem "was international," and that recognition on those grounds would be a "transparent dodge to win a few votes" that would diminish "the great dignity of the office of the President." Then he went further: "if the President were to follow Mr. Clifford's advice and if in the elections I were to vote, I would vote against the President." [cite:dv4_frus_may12_1948]

Eleven minutes. On 14 May 1948 the State of Israel was proclaimed in Tel Aviv. Eleven minutes later the White House issued a short press release announcing that the United States recognized the provisional government as the de facto authority of the new state. [cite:truman_recognition] The speed was possible because recognition is the President's alone. Article II, section 3 of the Constitution gives him the duty to "receive Ambassadors and other public Ministers," and in Zivotofsky v. Kerry (2015) the Supreme Court held that this makes the power to recognize foreign sovereigns his exclusively. [cite:const_art2s3] [cite:scotus_zivotofsky] Congress had no vote on it. Full de jure recognition followed on 31 January 1949. [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:docsteach_dejure_1949]

What the decision shows about Washington. Three features of the federal city's architecture are visible in this one week. First, the decisive power was concentrated in a single office, and a President could use it against the advice of his Secretary of State and his military chiefs. [cite:dv4_frus_may12_1948] [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] Second, the reasons given inside the room were openly domestic: the Secretary of State accused the White House staff of trading foreign policy for votes in the coming election. [cite:dv4_frus_may12_1948] Third, recognition and support were not the same thing. In 1948 Washington recognized Israel within minutes and embargoed its weapons for months; Israel's first year was carried by Czech arms, Soviet votes and its own forces, not by American supplies. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] [cite:dv4_herf_embargo]

What came after. The early relationship stayed conditional. In September 1948 Marshall endorsed the plan of the murdered UN mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, which would have given the Negev to the Arab side, and Clifford protested that he had done so without the President's approval. [cite:jvl_clifford_bernadotte] In May 1949 Truman sent Ben-Gurion a note expressing displeasure over Israel's position on territory and refugees. [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] For more than a decade after the founding, American assistance was economic aid and loans, not weapons: Washington avoided selling arms directly, for fear of alienating the oil-producing Arab states. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] The material partnership that makes Washington a candidate for this volume's title was built later, step by step, and the next section traces how.
