Part Twenty-Six established three things. The Gospel of Mary, known from the Berlin Codex bought in Cairo in 1896 and two third-century Greek fragments, teaches that the Son of Humanity is within, warns against laying down rules beyond the Saviour's, and ends with Levi rebuking Peter for rejecting a woman's teaching; no ancient authority named it, but Gregory I's homily of about 591 fused Mary Magdalene with a sinful woman, a reading the Roman calendar dropped in 1969 before her memorial was raised to a feast on 3 June 2016, while ordination remains reserved to men. [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex] [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt26_pearse_greg33] [cite:vt26_va_feast2016] [cite:vt26_va_ordsac1994] The Gospel of Judas was condemned by Irenaeus around 180 and survived in a single codex copied between about 220 and 340, published on 6 April 2006; its scorn for the twelve and their sacrifice is clear, while whether its Judas is hero or demon remains disputed. [cite:vt26_na_iren131] [cite:vt26_natgeo_journey] [cite:vt26_getrel_deconick] Pistis Sophia, in London since 1785, tells Wisdom's fall and repentance, gives Mary the leading voice against Peter's objections, and lays down a long moral code of its own; nothing in the record shows it was ever condemned. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] [cite:vt26_mead_ch72] [cite:vt26_mead_ch102] Part Twenty-Seven turns to a Bible that Rome never governed: the 81-book canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the Book of Enoch it kept.
