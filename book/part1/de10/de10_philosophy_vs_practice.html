Philosophy Against Practice

Thiel's critics often say that what he argues and what he does point in opposite directions: the champion of monopoly who sells to the state, the man who wrote that freedom and democracy are incompatible and then spent more than $30 million on elections, the defender of privacy whose best-known company is a data-analysis contractor to intelligence and immigration agencies. [cite:de10_cato] [cite:de10_cnbc_2022] [cite:de10_cnn_privacy] Some of these tensions are real and documented. Some dissolve when the texts are read whole. This section sets each argument beside the record of his companies and his money, gives his own explanation where he has offered one, and says which kind of tension each is. These are not claims contradicted by documents, which [scene:de9_claims_vs_record|Part Nine] grades on the book's red-to-grey scale, but gaps between a philosophy and a practice, and they are judged here on their own terms.

Monopoly, and a company that sells to the government

Zero to One argued that the best companies escape competition and become "creative monopolies," and drew a line between these and monopolies that are "illegal bullies or government favourites." [cite:de10_netzpolitik] Palantir, which Thiel co-founded in 2003 and whose early history is in [scene:de9_paypal_palantir_facebook|Part Nine], built much of its business on government contracts. In 2025 its revenue was $4.475 billion, of which $1.855 billion came from the United States government, a figure that grew 55 percent in a year. [cite:de10_pltr_fy25] Its contracts with immigration enforcement are set out in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty].

The record here is more interesting than the slogan suggests. Palantir did not get its foothold in the Army by being a favourite. In 2016 it sued the Army in the Court of Federal Claims, arguing that the service had broken the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act of 1994 by designing a new intelligence system in-house without checking whether a commercial product could do the job. In October 2016 the court agreed and ordered the Army to consider commercial options; in March 2019 Palantir won a contract for the system worth up to $876 million over ten years. [cite:de10_defnews] The company that a monopoly theorist co-founded forced its way into a market by suing for the right to compete. Once inside, a supplier of software that becomes woven into an agency's work can become hard to replace, a question of lock-in that [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Nine] examines. Whether that is a "creative monopoly" or a "government favourite" in the book's own terms is a judgment the record does not settle; what it shows is that the government is the largest single customer of a company whose co-founder distrusts the government.

Against democracy, and $32 million on elections

The second tension is the one most often cited. In 2009 Thiel wrote that he no longer believed freedom and democracy were compatible and recommended "focusing energy elsewhere, onto peaceful projects that some consider utopian." [cite:de10_cato] In 2016 he spoke at the Republican convention and pledged $1.25 million to help elect Donald Trump. [cite:de9_fortune_rnc] [cite:de9_cnn_transition] In 2022, according to FEC records reported by CNBC, he put $15 million behind J.D. Vance's Senate primary in Ohio and $17.5 million behind Blake Masters's in Arizona, about $32 million across the midterms. [cite:de10_cnbc_2022] [scene:de9_money_and_influence|Part Nine] gives the full ledger.

The tension is real, but it is not a contradiction of the 2009 text. That essay never said elections did not matter; it said they were not where freedom would be won, and the follow-up already conceded that "voting is not under siege in America." [cite:de10_cato] What changed is his judgment about where to put his energy. He has given his own reasons in pieces. In June 2025 he described the 2016 turn as a choice for a candidate who was not offering what he called the old Republican formula, and said populism was "still by far the best option we have." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] In late 2024 he told Fortune that he had stayed out of that year's presidential race because the campaign was not about "ending our decades-long technological and economic stagnation." [cite:de16_fortune_thielnet] On his own account, then, electoral spending is a tool for a goal, technological progress, that he does not expect democracy to deliver by itself. That is consistent with the 2009 essay's distrust of mass politics, and it is a reversal of its advice to stay out.

Privacy, and surveillance

On 15 August 2016, after the jury verdict against Gawker that his money had funded, Thiel wrote in the New York Times that "it is ridiculous to claim that journalism requires indiscriminate access to private people's sex lives," and that "protecting individual dignity online is a long-term project." [cite:de10_cnn_privacy] The Gawker case itself is in [scene:de9_gawker_citizenship_and_tax|Part Nine]. In the Antichrist lectures of 2025 he listed financial surveillance, tax treaties and the sanctions system among the marks of the world order he feared. [cite:de10_reason] Meanwhile Palantir sells data-integration software to the Army and other federal agencies, including immigration enforcement ([scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty]), [cite:de10_defnews] [cite:de10_pltr_fy25] and Thiel and the investor Naval Ravikant together put more than $200,000 into the facial-recognition company Clearview AI, money later converted into equity. [cite:de10_wiki_clearview]

Thiel has addressed the tension twice in his own words. In 2016 he told Fortune that the government had been collecting more data than it could analyse, and that "if we could help them make sense of data, they could end indiscriminate surveillance." [cite:de10_fortune_palantir] In 2025, when Ross Douthat put it to him that an Antichrist might use the surveillance tools he had invested in, he answered: "I obviously don't think that that's what I'm doing." [cite:de10_douthat_tx] His argument, then, is that targeted analysis replaces mass collection rather than adding to it. That is a claim about effects, and it can be tested: [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Nine] and [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty] set out what the software has been used for. The 2016 op-ed concerned the press publishing intimate details of private lives, which is a different thing from state use of data, so the two positions are not formally inconsistent. They do rest on different views of who should be trusted with information about a person.

Exit, and the turn to the nation

The last tension is the largest and the least discussed. Thiel's 2009 programme was exit: seasteads, cyberspace and space, and he backed the Seasteading Institute with money reported at $1.25 million or $1.7 million before calling the idea "not quite feasible." [cite:de3_cato_thiel] [cite:de3_bi_tsi] By 2019 he was opening a conference of national conservatives, and in 2022 he signed their statement of principles, which calls for "a world of independent nations" and a state "subject to constitutional restraints." [cite:de16_bloomberg_natcon] [cite:de16_tac_natcon] [cite:de16_natcon_signers] In the 2025 lectures, as reported, he called the American empire "the natural candidate" for the restraining force he hopes will hold off a world state. [cite:de10_reason] The path from leaving the state to defending a strong nation against a global one is documented step by step in his own words. He has not, in any text found, explained the shift as a change of mind; read together, the texts suggest the enemy stayed the same, concentrated authority that stops innovation, while the preferred defence moved from the ocean to the nation.

The worst-case reading, tested

The book's working hypothesis, stated in the preface, is that immigration enforcement and government cuts in 2025 and 2026 were the occasion, and a sovereign executive without a veto the destination. This part's material bears on it in four ways.

Documented: Thiel has distrusted mass democracy in writing since at least 2004, preferred intelligence services to parliamentary deliberation in foreign affairs, and in 2009 wrote that freedom and democracy were incompatible. [cite:de10_straussian_es] [cite:de10_cato] Documented, too, is that his money backed the 2022 Senate campaign of the man who became Vice President and that a company he co-founded earns more than $1.8 billion a year from the federal government, including immigration enforcement. [cite:de10_cnbc_2022] [cite:de10_pltr_fy25] The materials of the worst case, a distrust of voters, money in elections and a data contractor to the agencies that carry out the occasion, are all on the record.

Not supported: that Thiel's own texts aim at a sovereign American executive. His 2022 signature endorses the Constitution of 1787 and a limited state; his 2019 speech asked existing agencies to act; and the danger his 2025 lectures name is concentrated global authority, not too little power in the White House. [cite:de16_tac_natcon] [cite:de16_bloomberg_natcon] [cite:de10_reason] On the record of his writing, he sits on the rung of an on-record critic of democracy, not an author of the design Yarvin proposes, and he has said Yarvin's ideas would not work. [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin]

Probable, at most, as a tension rather than a plan: that his theology of the katechon gives a religious warrant to treating the United States as the force that must not be restrained, which in practice argues against international limits on American technology and power. That is a reading of the lectures, which were private and are known from recordings, and no document shows it guiding a policy. [cite:de10_wapo] [cite:de10_reason] Where the record runs against the hypothesis is in his explicit target. A man whose stated fear is a world government that halts science in the name of "peace and safety" has said, in public and in private, that the threat comes from the opposite direction to the one the worst-case reading follows. [cite:de10_douthat_tx] The next part turns to the second man in the PayPal story, Elon Musk.
