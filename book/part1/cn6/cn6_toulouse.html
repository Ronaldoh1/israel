Toulouse, 1229: The Laity Lose the Book

For the first thousand years of Christianity no council forbade lay people to own the scriptures. Few owned them, because books were costly and most people could not read Latin, but the ban, when it came, was new. It came in 1229, at Toulouse, at the end of a war, and it was aimed at a movement whose members read the gospels in their own language. The document says what it forbids in plain words, and the circumstances show what was at stake.

The movement was the one its enemies called Albigensian, after the town of Albi, and modern historians call Cathar. By 1200 it had its own bishops in the south of France and northern Italy, and its own rites. [cite:vt28_brit_cathari] [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Innocent III, pope from 1198, sent preaching missions first, with little success. When his legate Pierre de Castelnau was murdered in January 1208, the morning after a meeting with Count Raymond VI of Toulouse, the pope declared a crusade, and he offered the crusaders the lands of the heretics and their protectors. [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 wrote the arrangement into church law. Its third canon ordered that condemned heretics be handed to the secular rulers, that the property of lay heretics be confiscated, and that Catholics who took up arms against heretics receive "the indulgences and privileges granted to those who go in defense of the Holy Land". [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3]

The war lasted twenty years. Carcassonne surrendered on 15 August 1209 and its people were driven out; at Minerve in July 1210, 140 people who refused to recant were burned; at Lavaur in 1211 several hundred more. [cite:vt28_audit_wiki_crusade] [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] It ended in 1229. On 12 April of that year Raymond VII of Toulouse did public penance in Notre-Dame in Paris, pledged to pull down the walls of his city and to give his daughter Jeanne in marriage to Alphonse, brother of King Louis IX. [cite:vt28_audit_cathen_raymond7] Later that year a council met at Toulouse itself, called by its bishop, Folquet de Marselha, to finish the work the soldiers had begun. [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse]

The council ordered searches of every parish for heretics and the destruction of any house in which one was found. It then turned to books. "We prohibit also that the laity should be permitted to have the books of the Old and the New Testament", it decreed, "unless anyone from the motives of devotion should wish to have the Psalter or the Breviary for divine offices or the hours of the blessed Virgin; but we most strictly forbid their having any translation of these books." [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] Translations into the vernacular were to be destroyed. [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] A council at Tarragona in 1234 ratified the Toulouse decisions and repeated the ban on lay possession of the Old and New Testaments in its second canon. [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] Four years after Toulouse, on 20 April 1233, Gregory IX established a permanent inquisition for France, whose judges could open proceedings on their own initiative. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] [cite:vt28_herodote_1233]

The ban was regional and did not become the law of the whole Western church. Its logic is what matters here. The Cathars rejected the Catholic Church's land and wealth; their perfect lived in poverty, supported by believers who competed to house them, and they built their faith on the gospels. [cite:vt31_wh_cathars] [cite:vt31_wiki_credentes] [scene:vt31_two_economies|Two economies of the sacred] The council's answer was not to dispute their reading but to take the book away from everyone outside the clergy. A lay person might keep a psalter for prayer. The narrative of the gospels and the letters of Paul, the texts on which a rival interpretation could be built, were reserved to those trained and licensed to read them in Latin.

The numbers from the inquisition that followed are more modest than legend suggests, and they belong in the record. In the register of Bernard Gui, inquisitor at Toulouse in the early fourteenth century, historians count 916 decisions on 636 individuals or communities; about 45 people, roughly six per cent, were burned, most of them relapsed, while nearly thirty per cent were released and more than half were sentenced to prison or penance, including the wearing of yellow crosses. [cite:vt28_mednet_gui] The machinery did not need to burn many. It needed the lands, the confessions and the books, and after 1229 it had all three.
