Long before anyone convened a Zionist congress, the pieces were already on the board. Crowns and empires held the territory. Houses of finance lent to those crowns and, in one case, to the papacy itself. Churches supplied both a political constituency and a body of prophecy. An Ottoman reform turned farmland into registered deeds that could one day be sold. And inside Judaism, rabbis argued over whether scripture permitted a return to the land at all. These were the structures that would fund, permit, bless or block a Jewish state.

This opening part of the book surveys that board as it stood before the game began. It visits the City of London, with its unusual privileges, and the London house of N M Rothschild & Sons. It examines the 1831 banking arrangement between the Rothschilds and the Holy See, the government of the papacy. It follows the Protestant restorationists, the Christian lobby that pressed for a Jewish return to Palestine in 1839 and again in 1891. And it surveys the great powers of the Napoleonic age, the Ottoman Land Code of 1858, and the religious debate over the Three Oaths.

One question runs through every section: what actually existed, on the documentary record, before political Zionism did? Answering it means separating what can be shown from the legends that have grown around these institutions, some of them deliberately manufactured. Where the record is firm, this book says so. Where it is thin or disputed, it says that too.
