The first half of the line rests on real history; the second half does not. Jewish kingdoms existed in the land in the first millennium BCE, and after Rome crushed the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE), barred Jews from Jerusalem and renamed the province Syria Palaestina, a smaller Jewish presence continued, centered in Galilee. [cite:wiki_syria_palaestina] [cite:encyclo_israel_history] That ancient connection is not in dispute among mainstream historians.

But the Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians who became the majority over the following centuries were a resident population, not a colonial one. The Ottoman census of 1871–72 counted 381,954 residents, roughly 85% Muslim, 11% Christian and 4% Jewish, and genetic and archaeological studies find strong continuity with long-term local ancestry. [cite:cjpme_historical_demography] [cite:factually_archaeological_evidence] Formal national identity is modern on both sides: Rashid Khalidi dates Palestinian national consciousness to the late Ottoman period and 1917–1923, just as political Zionism was founded at Basel in 1897. [cite:cup_khalidi_palestinian_identity] Both histories are documented; neither erases the other.
