The Pipeline

The most durable product of the alliance is a pipe in the Negev desert. It began with the Suez Canal. When Egypt closed the canal during the 1967 war, Iranian tankers bound for Europe had to sail around Africa, and Israel, which now controlled the Straits of Tiran, offered a shortcut. Golda Meir had proposed a joint venture during a secret visit to Tehran in July 1965: Iranian crude would be shipped to Israel's Red Sea port of Eilat and carried by pipeline to the Mediterranean. After the canal closed, Iran commissioned a feasibility study, and the two governments went ahead. [cite:ir1_granta_khalili] [cite:ir1_thompson_energy] The agreement was signed in February 1968, and oil began flowing within about eighteen months. [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024]

A company with hidden owners

The Eilat Ashkelon Pipeline Company, known as EAPC, was founded by the Israeli and Iranian governments as equal partners. [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] Iran did not want to be seen trading with Israel, so its half was held by the National Iranian Oil Company through companies registered abroad: among them Trans-Asiatic Oil, registered in Panama, and APC Holdings, registered in Canada. The 254-kilometre, 42-inch line ran from Eilat to Ashkelon, where most of the oil was loaded onto tankers for Europe and a smaller share went to Israeli refineries. The franchise ran for 49 years. [cite:ir1_wiki_eapc] [cite:ir1_granta_khalili] [cite:ir1_ih_swiss2016] Throughput peaked at about 10 million tons in 1970, well below the planned capacity; Iranica notes that even that initial flow exceeded Israel's own annual consumption. [cite:ir1_wiki_eapc] [cite:ir1_iranica_israel]

Secrecy was written into Israeli law from the start. A decree of 17 July 1968 made information about the pipeline a state secret, and the company was exempted from ordinary disclosure rules. [cite:ir1_wiki_eapc] [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] In December 2017, when the original franchise expired and the company was reorganised, the Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Defence Committee kept it under secrecy rules that made publishing classified information about it punishable by fifteen years to life in prison; after a petition by an environmental group, environmental and safety matters were exempted, but the company's oil sources and core business remained classified. Israel's stated reason has been national security. [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] A 2024 amendment narrowed the order slightly, and in January 2025 Israel's State Comptroller reported significant failures in the company's financial and legal conduct and its maintenance record; environmental groups continue to press the Knesset to lift the secrecy. [cite:ir1_adamteva_2025]

After 1979

The revolution ended the partnership. Iran ended official oil sales to Israel and cut ties, and Israel ended the partnership and refused to compensate the new government. [cite:ir1_thompson_energy] [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] The pipeline did not fall idle: in the years after 1979, Helen Thompson writes, Israel relied on spot markets and clandestine trade through it, organised by the commodities trader Marc Rich. [cite:ir1_thompson_energy] Laleh Khalili writes that the National Iranian Oil Company "continued to honour its deals with Marc Rich," much of that oil being routed to Eilat, and that over two decades Rich supplied up to 20 per cent of Israel's annual oil needs; that is her account, drawn from the trade's historians, not a disclosed Iranian or Israeli record. [cite:ir1_granta_khalili] In 2003 its flow was reversed so that oil from the Black Sea and the Caspian could be shipped east from Eilat. [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] It remains a strategic asset, and since 2020 it has been part of plans to move Gulf oil to the Mediterranean. [cite:ir1_wiki_eapc]

The arbitration

The Islamic Republic did not give up its half. Iran's claim, filed in 1994, sought two things: payment for the last oil shipments delivered to Eilat before the revolution, which were never paid for, and Iran's share of the company's assets. Israel counterclaimed that Iran had broken its contract to supply oil. [cite:ir1_toi_2016] The cases went to arbitration, first in France and then in Switzerland. [cite:ir1_ih_swiss2016] In May 2015 an arbitral tribunal ordered Israel's side to pay Iran about $1.1 billion, plus interest, for roughly fifty shipments of crude delivered in 1978. [cite:ir1_asil_2015] [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024] Some accounts give the principal as $1.2 billion with $362 million in interest. [cite:ir1_wiki_eapc] Israel appealed to Switzerland's Federal Tribunal, which rejected the appeal in a ruling dated 27 June 2016 and ordered Israel to pay 450,000 Swiss francs in costs. [cite:ir1_ih_swiss2016] [cite:ir1_ogj_2016] A separate claim to Iran's share of the company's assets could, according to Global Arbitration Review, be worth up to $7 billion together with the first. [cite:ir1_toi_2016] [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024]

The two governments' positions are set out in the proceedings and in Israeli statements. Israel's Finance Ministry said in 2016: "Under the laws of trade we cannot transfer funds to an enemy country." Israel designated Iran an enemy state in 2011 and relies on its Trading with the Enemy Ordinance; it also argues that the company had no value in 1979 because it was making heavy losses, and that Iran broke the agreement first. [cite:ir1_toi_2016] [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024] [cite:ir1_asil_2015] Iran, which does not recognise Israel, has pursued the claim through the arbitration; its filings, as reported by Shomrim, argue that the National Iranian Oil Company is separate from the Iranian state, that the revolution was force majeure, and that it is owed a share of the benefits Israel drew from the pipeline. [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024]

Status as of October 2026

Israel has not paid. In December 2024 the Israeli investigative outlet Shomrim reported that in late 2017 and January 2018 the two sides' representatives had written to the tribunal that they were willing to negotiate a settlement; a hearing set for Geneva in 2019 was apparently postponed and never held. Israel's Finance Ministry and the Prime Minister's Office declined to answer Shomrim's questions. [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024] In April 2024, after Iran's missile and drone attack on Israel of 13 April, Yedioth Ahronoth reported that senior Israeli officials had discussed taking money from a fund, believed to hold more than $1 billion, into which the company sets aside half its annual profits, managed by the Finance Ministry and reportedly kept against the possibility of future relations with a different Iranian government, to offset the cost of the attack. [cite:ir1_newarab_2024] No decision on that was reported. The public record examined for this book, as of October 2026, shows no payment to Iran, no published settlement and no final ruling on the larger asset claim; reporting found for this book does not show whether the wars of 2025 and 2026 changed the arbitration. [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024] [cite:ir1_adamteva_2025]
