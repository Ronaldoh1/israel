The United States: Warrants, Loopholes and Immigration Data

The American framework was built after a scandal. In January 1975 the Senate voted 82–4 to create a select committee under Senator Frank Church to study the intelligence agencies. It documented the FBI's COINTELPRO programme against civil-rights and anti-war groups, including Martin Luther King Jr., and two NSA programmes, SHAMROCK and MINARET, that monitored communications into and out of the country. Its final report of 29 April 1976 found that "Intelligence agencies have undermined the constitutional rights of citizens." Congress responded with a permanent Senate Intelligence Committee in 1976 and the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) of 1978, signed by President Jimmy Carter, which created a special court to approve national-security wiretaps. [cite:us10_senate_church] The ordinary criminal wiretap still needs a judge's order under the Fourth Amendment, and the courts publish a count every year. In 2025 federal and state judges authorised 1,735 wiretaps, 873 federal and 862 state, 24 per cent fewer than in 2024 and 58 per cent fewer than the 4,148 of 2015; narcotics cases accounted for 51 per cent of applications. [cite:us10_usc_wiretap2025]

From bulk collection to Section 702

After 2001 the national-security side grew past the criminal side. In June 2013 documents leaked by Edward Snowden showed that a FISA Court order required Verizon to hand over the call records of its customers in bulk. On 7 May 2015 the Second Circuit Court of Appeals held in ACLU v. Clapper that the programme "exceeds the scope of what Congress has authorized" under section 215 of the Patriot Act, and on 2 June 2015 Congress passed the USA FREEDOM Act, which restricted that kind of collection. [cite:us10_wiki_clapper] The same archive showed that since a 2009 agreement the NSA had sent Israel's Unit 8200 raw intercepts that could include Americans' communications; the NSA said such information was handled under procedures designed to protect privacy rights and that it could not use these relationships to circumvent US law. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [scene:ow4_memorandum|The NSA–8200 memorandum]

The larger authority is Section 702 of FISA. It lets agencies collect, without an individual warrant, the communications of foreigners reasonably believed to be abroad, from American providers; Americans' messages are collected when they are in contact with those targets, and the FBI may then search the stored data by an American's name or number, the so-called backdoor search. The number of targets has risen every year the government has reported it: 89,138 in 2013, more than 200,000 in 2021, 268,590 in 2023 and 349,823 in 2025, by the reports of the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI). A target can be a group or a facility as well as a person. [cite:us10_db_2013targets] [cite:us10_jurist_fbi2021] [cite:us10_odni_astr2026] The FBI's searches for Americans moved the other way: nearly 3.4 million in 2021 (counting each query, some with thousands of terms), 57,094 in the year to November 2023, 5,518 in the year to November 2024 and 7,413 in the year to November 2025. [cite:us10_jurist_fbi2021] [cite:us10_odni_astr2026]

The 2026 lapse

Section 702 was due to expire on 20 April 2026. [cite:us10a_wamc] The dispute was the one that had run since 2013: lawmakers of both parties wanted a court's approval before agents could read Americans' data in the 702 databases, and the intelligence agencies said that would weaken the tool. The House failed to pass either a five-year renewal or the 18-month renewal President Donald Trump had asked for, and Congress passed a ten-day extension. [cite:us10_npr_702april] On 30 April the House voted 261–111 for a six-week extension to 12 June. [cite:us10_bgov_702] At midnight on 12 June the statute lapsed. The Electronic Frontier Foundation, which opposed renewal, tied the lapse to President Trump's nomination of Bill Pulte as Director of National Intelligence after Tulsi Gabbard announced her resignation: Senate Democrats, it reported, then refused to move their reauthorisation bill and the House refused a further short-term extension. [cite:us10_eff_702lapse] [cite:us10a_eff] Collection did not stop. The FISA Court had approved the year's certifications on 17 March 2026, and by the Brennan Center's reading of the statute they remain valid until 17 March 2027; as of 22 September 2026 the providers were still cooperating, and the government was reported to be pressing for renewal by November. [cite:us10_bcj_702] As of October 2026 no renewal had been enacted that this book could find; the question will fall to the Congress chosen on 3 November.

Buying what a warrant would require

The Supreme Court has narrowed the old rule that information handed to a company carries no privacy protection: Carpenter v. United States (2018) required a warrant for historical cell-site records, and Chatrie v. United States, decided 6–3 on 29 June 2026, held that obtaining two hours of Google location records is a search. Neither ruling addresses data the government buys. [cite:ow9_cornell_tpd] [cite:ow9_pw_chatrie] Agencies do buy it. The NSA's director confirmed in a letter published in January 2024 that it purchases commercially available internet netflow data that includes domestic communications, and Customs and Border Protection and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) have bought phone-location data from brokers. [cite:ow9_tc_nsanetflow] [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] The House passed the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act, 219–199, on 17 April 2024, over the White House's opposition; it did not become law. [cite:ow9_cs_4thact]

Immigration enforcement and government data

Since 2025 the sharpest change has been the use of other agencies' records for immigration enforcement. In April 2025 Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem signed an agreement for the Internal Revenue Service to check names for ICE. ICE asked about some 1.28 million people, and the IRS verified about 47,000. The administration said the agreement helped carry out the president's agenda to secure the borders. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10a_abc] Two lawsuits produced different results. In one, a District of Columbia Circuit panel declined on 24 February 2026 to block the sharing, finding the plaintiffs unlikely to win; Attorney General Pam Bondi called it a "crucial victory". In the other, Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly had stayed the agreement in November 2025 and in February 2026 found that the IRS broke the law "approximately 42,695 times" by disclosing confidential addresses; a unanimous panel of the same appeals court upheld her block on 8 September 2026. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] Medicaid data followed: 404 Media reported in January 2026 that an ICE tool called ELITE draws on Medicaid records under an agreement covering nearly 80 million patients. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite]

Much of this runs on private software, some of it Israeli. ICE paid Palantir $29.9 million in April 2025 for ImmigrationOS, a system to prioritise targets and track departures, on the ground that Palantir was "the only source", and in February 2026 the Department of Homeland Security signed a purchase agreement with Palantir worth up to $1 billion over five years. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:ow9_sa_dhsbpa] [scene:ow9_ice|Palantir and ICE] ICE's $2 million contract for Graphite, the spyware of the Israeli-founded firm Paragon, was paused under President Biden's 2023 order on commercial spyware and reactivated in 2025; in April 2026 the acting ICE director said it was used against drug-trafficking groups and certified no significant counterintelligence risk. [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] [cite:ow10_reg_paragon] [cite:ow10_tc_lyons] [scene:ow10_paragon|Paragon and ICE] The FBI had bought and tested Pegasus before deciding in July 2021 not to deploy it. [cite:ow10_globe_fbi]

Oversight

The independent watchdog for counterterrorism surveillance, the Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board, was left without a quorum in 2025 after President Trump removed its Democratic members Travis LeBlanc and Edward Felten. Judge Reggie Walton ruled on 21 May 2025 that the removals were unlawful; the White House said it expected "ultimate victory". [cite:us10_rec_pclob] This book could not confirm the board's status as of October 2026.

The two systems fail in different places. Israel's courts have set limits case by case, but its security service holds standing access to every subscriber's metadata on the executive's authority alone, and the state publishes no counts. The United States publishes counts and requires a judge for criminal wiretaps, but its largest programme searches Americans' messages without a warrant, its agencies buy data a warrant would otherwise be needed for, and since 2025 records given to the tax and health systems have been opened to immigration enforcement, with the courts divided on whether that is lawful.
