Non-Identification: Israel Between the Two Powers, 1948–1950

Volume II followed American policy toward the new state decision by decision, from Truman's retreat on the Bernadotte plan in 1948 to the de jure recognition of 31 January 1949, the secret note of May 1949 warning that "a revision of its attitude toward Israel has become unavoidable," and the joint declaration with Britain and France in May 1950. [cite:jvl_clifford_bernadotte] [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] That story, told in [scene:tr_s02|Truman after the money], was written from Washington. The question for this volume begins at the other end. In its first two years the state of Israel had to decide where it stood between the United States and the Soviet Union, the two powers that had each, in different ways, helped bring it into being. How the CIA and the State Department had argued against recognition in 1947–48, and were overruled by the President, is told in [scene:cia_s02|the CIA in 1948]. What follows is the Israeli government's own answer to the Cold War, as its leaders stated it, and the first act that put that answer to a test.

It is common to describe Israel as an American ally from its birth. The record of its first years does not support that. The government's first stated position was that it would belong to neither side. The reasons both powers mattered are taken up in the section on the Soviet break: in 1947 Washington and Moscow had both voted for partition, the Soviet Union had extended recognition, and the Eastern bloc had been Israel's main arms supplier in 1948. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] A state with a patron on each side had an obvious interest in keeping both.

David Ben-Gurion put the position in a sentence on 27 September 1948, speaking to the Provisional State Council: "We do not want to subordinate ourselves to anyone, and we do not believe that angels rule anywhere." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The second half of the sentence matters as much as the first. Ben-Gurion was not claiming that Israel stood above the quarrel between the two powers on principle. He was saying that neither of them deserved unconditional trust.

Six months later the position became government policy. The Basic Principles of the Government Programme, dated 9 March 1949, placed friendship with both superpowers first among the principles of foreign policy. The text spoke of "friendship with all freedom-loving states, and in particular with the United States and the Soviet Union." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The document set the two powers side by side in a single clause.

The policy had a name in Hebrew, i-hizdahut, non-identification. The diplomat Michael Comay later summed it up in one line: "To try to retain the support of both, we adopted a posture of non-identification, of keeping out of the Cold War." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Its chief advocate inside the government was Moshe Sharett, who urged Israel to go "knocking on every door". [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west]

The word "neutrality" is often used for this period, and it can mislead. Comay's own sentence gives the policy's purpose: not to stand apart from the powers but to keep the support of both. Avi Shlaim reads non-identification in exactly those terms, as a pragmatic policy of national interest rather than an ideological neutrality. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] That is an interpretation, but it rests on the words of the men who made the policy. Nothing in these sources presents non-identification as a doctrine of principle. It was a working rule for a new state that hoped to draw support from both sides of the line between East and West.

The mechanism was simple and lay in few hands. The prime minister stated the position to the legislature. The government wrote it into its programme. Sharett, its chief advocate, carried it into practice, through votes at the United Nations and through recognitions. Those who could not shape it from inside were the two powers themselves: each could reward or punish Israel's choices, but neither could make them. The policy's value lay in that freedom, and it lasted only as long as both powers were willing to tolerate it.

The first strain came from Moscow. On 9 December 1949 the Soviet Union voted at the United Nations for the internationalization of Jerusalem, marking a more critical line toward Israel; that turn, and what followed it, is told in the section on the Soviet break. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west]

The clearest single act of non-identification came a month later. On 9 January 1950 Israel recognized the People's Republic of China. It was the first country in the Middle East to do so, and the seventh in the non-communist world. [cite:rs_cw1_inss_shichor] The decision fitted the policy's terms precisely. A government that had promised friendship "in particular with the United States and the Soviet Union" recognized a communist government a year after Washington's de jure recognition of Israel. It was a door knocked on.

The door did not open. Formal diplomatic relations between Israel and China followed only on 24 January 1992, forty-two years after the recognition. [cite:rs_cw1_inss_shichor] The scholar Yitzhak Shichor has traced why, and his account names two causes, one in Beijing and one in Washington. According to Soviet documents he cites, Zhou Enlai told the Soviet ambassador in July 1951 that China would not establish diplomatic relations with Israel. Shichor also says that Washington, with the Korean War in the background, urged Israel not to forge relations with Beijing, "even using threats", and he identifies the United States as the main factor keeping the two states apart. [cite:rs_cw1_inss_shichor] The two causes are not in conflict. By the summer of 1951 China had decided not to proceed, and Israel was under American pressure not to press it.

The Chinese case shows the limits of the policy within a year of its adoption. Non-identification allowed Israel to recognize whom it chose. It could not make the recognized government reciprocate, and it could not shield Israel from the cost Washington might attach to the choice once the Korean War had begun. That war, and the vote in which Israel took the American side, is where non-identification ended at the level of declarations. It is told in [scene:cw1_korea|the Korean decision].

Several things remain unsettled by the record gathered here. The Israeli statements come to this book through one scholar's article, which quotes them; the full text of the Basic Principles is not before us, so what else they said about foreign policy, and how they ranked other commitments against the friendship with both powers, cannot be stated. Shichor's account of American pressure "even using threats" does not specify, in the material available, what was threatened, by whom, or when. And whether Israel would have opened relations with Beijing had Washington stayed silent cannot be known, because China, on Shichor's own evidence, had by July 1951 decided against it for reasons of its own.
