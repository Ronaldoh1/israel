The Break: February 1979

The alliance described in Part One ended within three weeks of Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini's return from exile at the start of February 1979. [scene:ir1_periphery|The periphery doctrine] Khomeini's hostility to the Shah's ties with Israel was not new. He had been arrested in 1963 after denouncing the Shah and Israel, and a manifesto he signed that year with eight other senior clerics accused the Shah of submission to the United States and Israel. [cite:ir2_wiki_1963] Within weeks of taking power, the new authorities cut the relationship he had attacked for sixteen years.

The mission and the keys

Israel never had a formally recognised embassy in Tehran. Iran's de facto recognition of Israel in March 1950 allowed a political representation that opened on 6 March 1950 and worked without the public standing of an embassy; its heads included Uri Lubrani in the 1970s and, from 1978, Yossef Harmelin, the last Israeli representative in Iran. [cite:ir2_wiki_embassy] Harmelin and his staff left the country on 18 February 1979. (One summary of the record gives the closing date as 17 February.) [cite:ir2_wiki_embassy]

Yasser Arafat, the chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization, arrived in Tehran on 17 February 1979, two weeks after Khomeini's return, as the first official visitor to the new government. Iran severed its ties with Israel during the visit. [cite:ir2_olj_arafat] [cite:hz1_aawsat_arafat] On 19 February his delegation was handed the keys of the former Israeli mission. The New York Times reported that day that "the sacked Israeli mission here has been donated to the Palestinian movement." The street outside, Kakh Street, was renamed Palestine Street, and the building became the Palestinian embassy, headed by the Fatah official Hani al-Hassan. [cite:ir2_wiki_embassy] [cite:ir2_olj_arafat]

The embrace did not last. One account, from a Palestinian militant close to Arafat's deputy Abu Jihad, holds that Khomeini told Arafat at their meeting that "there is no Palestinian cause; it is an Islamic cause"; the remark rests on that one recollection, given to L'Orient-Le Jour forty years later, and is reported here as such. [cite:ir2_olj_arafat] What is better documented is the rupture that followed: when Iraq invaded Iran in September 1980, Arafat sided with Iraq while trying to stay on terms with Tehran, and after the PLO left Lebanon in 1982 Iran built its own Lebanese ally in Hezbollah. [cite:ir2_olj_arafat] The Hezbollah volume tells that story. [scene:hz2_founding|The Hezbollah Architecture: the founding]

What Khomeini said

The new state wrote its foreign ambitions into its founding documents. The constitution adopted in late 1979 described itself as a basis for "the continuation of the Revolution at home and abroad," and gave the armed forces "the ideological mission of jihad in God's way." It recognises Iran's Jewish community, which Part Three examines, and does not mention Israel or Zionism. [cite:hz1_iran_const] [scene:ir3_community|The Jews of Iran] In his New Year message broadcast by Radio Tehran on 21 March 1980, Khomeini said "we should try hard to export our revolution to the world," named "America, Israel and Zionism" as enemies, and pledged support for "beloved Palestine and beloved Lebanon." [cite:hz1_merip_khomeini]

On 7 August 1979 Khomeini called on Muslims to "consecrate the last Friday of the holy month of Ramadan as Al-Quds Day," a day to show solidarity with the Palestinians against Israel. In Iran the government organises the day's marches; officials give speeches against Israel and the United States, and crowds chant "Death to Israel" and "Death to America." The day is also marked by Hezbollah in Lebanon, by Hamas and Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and by smaller gatherings in Europe and North America, but in the BBC's assessment it never took root among Muslims at large and remained mainly an Iranian event. [cite:ir2_wiki_quds] Part Nine sets out what Iran's leaders said about Israel in the decades that followed. [scene:ir9_record|Iran's own record]

What happened to the alliance's assets

The break was costly for Israel in plain commercial terms. Trade between the two countries had run at about $200 million a year in the Shah's last years by a conservative estimate, and after Khomeini cut ties hundreds of workers were laid off in Israel's defence industries, which had sold heavily to the Shah. [cite:ir2_csm_plays86] The joint oil pipeline company, whose disputes would reach a Swiss arbitration decades later, is covered in Part One. [scene:ir1_pipeline|The pipeline and the oil] The Israeli journalist Ronen Bergman, who wrote the fullest account of Israel's dealings with Iran in these years, argues that the Shah's fall also stopped advanced Israeli weapons cooperation; he writes that had Khomeini come to power later, Iran might have had "long-range missiles capable of carrying nuclear warheads." That is his assessment of a programme that was never completed, not a documented capability. [cite:ir2_hnn_bergman]

What did not end was the interest both governments had in each other. Israel's defence industries had lost a major customer, and Iran's armed forces, built up under the Shah with American weapons, were cut off from American spare parts by a US arms embargo. Within a year, as the next section shows, Israeli spare parts were again reaching Iran, in the months when Iran was holding American diplomats hostage.
