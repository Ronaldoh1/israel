Black Sabbath: Britain Cracks Down

On Saturday, June 29, 1946, British troops and police fanned out across Mandate Palestine, the territory Britain then governed. It is often said that relations between Britain and the Jewish community there soured gradually, without a single sharp turning point. The record identifies one. That day Britain launched Operation Agatha, a coordinated raid across the country. Because it fell on the Jewish Sabbath, the community it struck named it "Black Sabbath."

The scale was enormous. Roughly 17,000 British troops and police searched Jewish settlements, kibbutzim and offices at the same time. More than 2,700 people were arrested, among them senior officials of the Jewish Agency, the body that represented the Jewish community. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] The operation was a direct response to mounting attacks on British installations by the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi. Its targets, however, went beyond the armed groups. By arresting the Agency's own senior officials, Britain struck at the institutions of the mainstream Zionist leadership itself. The sources give the operation's overall scale and its targeting of the leadership. They do not list every site searched or every person detained.

Part of what was at stake was paper. British forces seized a large cache of documents from the Jewish Agency's headquarters in Jerusalem. The material was believed to include evidence linking the Agency's mainstream leadership to Haganah operations. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] If that link could be shown, it would undo the careful separation the movement had kept between its political leadership and armed resistance. The same kind of separation had been built, on the other side of the Atlantic, into the procurement network described in [scene:s07d|the Sonneborn meeting]. The sources do not specify what the seized documents contained. They document what the documents were believed to contain, and why that mattered.

The operation's two names tell their own story. "Agatha" was the official British codename, and there is no reason to think it carried any special meaning; codenames of the period were typically assigned without symbolic intent. "Black Sabbath" was the name given by the community whose settlements and institutions were searched. It records how the event was lived and remembered. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha]

The raid's most important consequence followed quickly, along a line that can be traced. The Irgun believed the seized documents were being processed at the King David Hotel in Jerusalem. The hotel's southern wing housed the Mandate Secretariat, the central administrative offices of the British government in Palestine, and served as regional British military headquarters, while hotel business went on in the rest of the building. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] To Irgun planners, the building was both a symbol of British authority and the place where the evidence against the Jewish Agency's leadership was kept. Irgun intelligence assessed that the Agatha documents were held in the Secretariat wing specifically. The documented objective of what followed was to destroy that evidence and to strike the administration that had just carried out the mass raid and arrests. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

Irgun planning for the attack began in direct response to Black Sabbath. The bombing took place on July 22, 1946, twenty-three days after the raid. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The chain ran from raid to seizure, from seizure to an intelligence assessment, from that assessment to the choice of target, and from there to the attack.

Tracing that chain is not the same as excusing what came at its end. Ninety-one people died in the bombing, many of them with no connection to the administrative work being targeted. Nor was retaliation for Black Sabbath necessarily the only motive; the attack's planning and stated objectives are examined more fully in the next chapter. Explaining why an act happened and judging it are separate tasks. This chapter attempts only the first.
