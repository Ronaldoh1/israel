The Arab States' War, May 1948 – 1949

The British Mandate ended at midnight on 14 May 1948. Within hours the fighting already under way inside Palestine became a war between the new State of Israel and the armies of its neighbours. The fighting that followed lasted, with two long truces, into the winter. It ended not in peace treaties but in a set of separate armistice agreements signed in 1949, whose lines became the borders of the next two decades.

The familiar account of this war has two versions, and each tells only part of the story. In one, five Arab armies set out to destroy a newborn state on the day of its birth. In the other, the Arab states sent their armies to protect the Palestinian Arabs from a Jewish force already on the offensive. The documents show that both sides had grounds for their account. They also show that the armies did not come as one, did not come in the strength their own planners had called for, and did not all come for the same purpose.

The League's own words. On 15 May 1948 the Secretary-General of the Arab League sent a cablegram to the Secretary-General of the United Nations, registered as document S/745. It is the Arab states' own statement of why they were going to war, and it deserves to be read as such. The Mandate, it said, had ended "leaving no legally constituted authority behind," and the Arab states were therefore intervening "to restore law and order and to prevent disturbances prevailing in Palestine from spreading" into their own territories. [cite:rs_rd5_un_s745] The cablegram made two further claims. It stated that "approximately over a quarter of a million of the Arab population have been compelled to leave their homes," and it declared support for "the creation of United State of Palestine based upon the democratic principles." [cite:rs_rd5_un_s745] The first was a statement about the displacement already under way before a single Arab army had crossed the frontier; the second was a political aim, a single state in place of the partition the General Assembly had voted for six months earlier. The document thus carries both readings of the war within it. It framed the intervention as an act of order and pointed to a displacement already under way; it also set out, in its own words, to replace the partition plan with a single state.

Who came, and how. The US State Department's Office of the Historian records that the war began with an Arab air attack on Tel Aviv on the eve of 14 May. Armies from Lebanon, Syria, Iraq and Egypt invaded; a Saudi formation fought under Egyptian command; and Transjordanian forces intervened only in the areas designated for the Arab state or for the Jerusalem corpus separatum, the international zone the partition plan had drawn around the city. Through all of it the United States kept an arms embargo on all belligerents. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] Other accounts in this library describe the entry differently. The record on Lebanon says that on 15 May Lebanon "joined Egypt, Syria, Jordan, and Iraq in declaring war and invading the new state," and the record on Egypt likewise counts Jordan among the invaders. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] [cite:wikipedia_1948_war] The difference matters. Whether Transjordan's Arab Legion invaded Israel or entered the parts of Palestine the United Nations had assigned to the Arabs is the heart of a long argument about King Abdullah's intentions, told in the section on his dealings with the Jewish Agency.

Each state made its own decision. Lebanon's is often passed over. It fielded the smallest of the five Arab armies, and its choice to fight was its own. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] According to Makram Rabah, a history lecturer at the American University of Beirut, the country's founding leaders, Riad al-Solh and Bechara el-Khoury, who had led Lebanon to independence from France in 1943, had debated and rejected an alliance with the Zionist movement before 1948, concluding that they could not keep relations with the neighbouring Arab states while holding one. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] Lebanon's posture was thus a deliberated political choice, not simply a reaction imposed from outside. Egypt joined the invasion in May 1948 after the end of the Mandate. [cite:wikipedia_1948_war] Saudi Arabia did not launch an invasion of its own. Its part was a small contingent of troops placed under Egyptian command. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] Accounts of the kingdom's role explain the modest scale by its greater distance from Palestine. Its larger weapon was economic. The Arab League's boycott of Israel, formalized in 1948, barred member states from any direct economic relationship with the new state; a secondary boycott added in 1950 extended the bar to foreign companies doing business with Israel, and a tertiary boycott later reached companies dealing with those on the secondary list. Saudi Arabia was among its most consistent enforcers and kept it in some form for over seven decades. It never established diplomatic relations with Israel.

How many men. The size of the armies is itself disputed. Avi Shlaim gives the forces on 15 May 1948 as 35,000 Israeli troops against 20,000–25,000 Arab troops, and argues that "illicit arms-imports decisively tipped the military balance in favour of Israel." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_war] His seminar notes add that the forces the Arab states actually sent were "well below the level demanded by the Military Committee of the Arab League," and that the Israeli army had more men but weaker firepower. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] A US assessment of July 1948, discussed in [scene:s10b|the next section], counted about 90,000 Israeli forces by then. The two figures describe different dates and should not be merged. The fuller argument over the balance, and over whose numbers the "outnumbered" story rests on, belongs to that section.

The first round, 15 May to 11 June. Shlaim's notes give the outline of the first four weeks. The Egyptian advance stalled at Ramat Rahel on 24 May and at Ashdod on 29 May. The Syrians were stopped at Degania but held Mishmar Hayarden. The Iraqis held the triangle of Jenin, Nablus and Tulkarem. In Jerusalem the Jewish Quarter surrendered on 28 May, and three frontal Israeli attacks on the Arab Legion's position at Latrun, on 25 and 30 May and on 9 June, were repelled. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war] Syria's army fought on the northern front and ended the war holding small areas west of the international border. [cite:tf_zaim] Lebanon's invasion was repelled, and Israeli forces for a time occupied part of southern Lebanon. [cite:lebanon_1948_war] The first round thus ended with the Egyptian and Syrian advances stopped, the Iraqis holding their triangle, the Jewish Quarter lost and Latrun still in the Legion's hands. Shlaim calls the truce that followed "a turning-point." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_war]

The first truce, 11 June to 9 July. The United Nations mediator, Count Folke Bernadotte, had been appointed on 21 May. The truce he supervised ran 28 days, from 11 June to 9 July 1948, with observers from Belgium, France, Sweden and the United States. According to the Israel Defense Forces' own account, published by the Jewish Virtual Library, materiel reached the Israeli army during the truce from Europe, "particularly Czechoslovakia." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce1] Bernadotte used the pause to propose a settlement: the Galilee to Israel, the Negev to the Arabs, and Jerusalem under the United Nations. Both sides rejected it. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce1] The arms that crossed during those four weeks are the subject of the section on the Czechoslovak pipeline; Bernadotte's own fate, at the hands of Lehi in September, is told in the section on the founding of the army.

Lydda and Ramla, July 1948. Fighting resumed on 9 July. Operation Dani was launched from Ben Shemen on the night of 9–10 July. The assault on Lydda began on 11 July, and by 12 July the town was under full Israeli control, without a formal surrender. According to Sana Hammoudi's account in the Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question, between 80 and 176 people were killed at the Dahmash mosque and more than 400 in the town. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] Both that account and Zachary Lockman, each citing the Israeli historian Benny Morris, report that on 12 July David Ben-Gurion answered a question about the inhabitants with the words "Expel them." Yitzhak Rabin then signed an order to expel Lydda's inhabitants quickly. Israeli censors later made Rabin remove the episode from his memoir. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war]

The numbers are disputed, and the two sources should be read side by side. Lockman, summarizing Morris, puts the combined population of Lydda and Ramla at 50,000–70,000, about 15,000 of them refugees, with 250–400 Palestinian civilians and four Israeli soldiers killed. The Palestinian encyclopedia estimates that about 70,000 people were forced out on 13 July and that close to 1,000 died in the towns and on the march that followed. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war] [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] Neither figure is a count; both are estimates, and the higher one includes deaths on the march.

The second truce and Jerusalem. The second truce began at 19:00 on 18 July 1948. It did not bring quiet to Jerusalem. According to the IDF account, the Arab Legion continued to shell West Jerusalem through October and on 12 August demolished the pumping station at Latrun, then held by the United Nations. A "sincere cease-fire" in the city was agreed only on 30 November. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce2]

The armistices of 1949. The war ended in separate armistice agreements between Israel and Egypt, Lebanon, Transjordan and Syria; Egypt kept the Gaza Strip and Jordan the West Bank. The Office of the Historian dates them, collectively, to "February 1949." [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] The individual agreements in the record carry later dates for two of them. The agreements with Egypt were negotiated on the Greek island of Rhodes; they set a ceasefire line, not a peace, which served as the de facto border along the edge of the Gaza Strip for the next two decades. [cite:wikipedia_1948_war] From 1948 to 1967 Egypt governed Gaza directly as a military protectorate, and its officials maintained throughout that it had not annexed the territory. Why Cairo avoided annexation is not settled by the record: some accounts stress an Arab League consensus against it, others Egyptian strategic caution. Lebanon's armistice, signed on 23 March 1949, restored the internationally recognized border. [cite:lebanon_1948_war]

Syria's took longer, and the delay holds one of the war's least-known episodes. After Husni al-Za'im seized power in Damascus in March 1949, armistice talks opened on 5 April; Za'im offered a peace treaty and military cooperation, proposed meeting Ben-Gurion or Moshe Sharett, and offered to resettle 250,000–300,000 Palestinian refugees in Syria, with the border drawn through the middle of Lake Hula and the Sea of Galilee. [cite:tf_zaim] [cite:ecf_zaim] [cite:majalla_zaim] Ben-Gurion would not stake policy on him, partly from distrust of his ties to Washington. [cite:majalla_zaim] The armistice signed on 20 July 1949 turned the areas Syria evacuated into demilitarized zones, which became the fault line of the frontier; in August Za'im was deposed and executed in Syria's second coup of the year, and the offer died with him ([scene:sy_s01|Syria and Za'im's offer]). [cite:ecf_zaim]

The cost. More than 6,000 Israelis were killed in the war, about 1% of the roughly 650,000 Jews then in the country, according to a Reuters figure published by Ynet in 2005. [cite:rs_rd5_ynet_dead] The war's other costs fell on the Palestinian Arabs: the quarter of a million already displaced by 15 May on the League's own count, and many more after it, as told in [scene:rd5_refugees|the section on the refugees]. In Lebanon alone an estimated 100,000–110,000 arrived from northern Palestine. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy]

What the record does not settle. Several things remain open on the sources gathered for this book. The number of Arab soldiers and civilians killed in the war has not been established here, and no figure is offered. The fighting of the autumn and winter of 1948–49, between the second truce and the armistices, is not covered by the research behind this section beyond the Jerusalem front. The sources disagree on whether Transjordan invaded Israel or entered only the areas assigned to the Arab state, and on the date of the armistices. The tolls at Lydda and Ramla rest on estimates that differ by a factor of two or more. And the deepest question, whether the armies came to save the Palestinian Arabs, to prevent a Jewish state, or to take territory for themselves, has no single answer: the League's cablegram, Lebanon's deliberated choice, Saudi Arabia's distant contingent, the Legion's entry, on the Office of the Historian's account, only into the areas assigned to the Arab state or the international zone, and Egypt's nineteen-year administration of Gaza without annexation point in different directions, because the states that went to war in May 1948 were pursuing different ends.
