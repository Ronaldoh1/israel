Outside the Command: Western Defence Plans and Israel, 1951–1953

By 1951 Israel's government had moved toward the West; how that happened, through the Korean vote and Ben-Gurion's offers of 1950, is told in [scene:cw1_korea|the Korean decision]. Moving toward the West did not make Israel part of the West's defence plans for the region. The plans Britain and the United States drew up in 1951 were built around a different country, Egypt, and they treated Israel in two ways that Ben-Gurion found equally unwelcome: first as a site for British bases, then as a state to be told about a command it had not been asked to found. This section follows those plans from the first British approach in February 1951 to the fading of the idea in 1953.

The British approach came first. Before General Sir Brian Robertson visited Israel on 19 February 1951, Sir William Strang had already floated two possibilities: a British base in Gaza with a corridor running through Israel, or British bases in Israel itself under a treaty. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Ben-Gurion's answer, as Avi Shlaim records it, was that this was "an insulting proposal." What he asked for instead was a relationship of a different kind: that Israel be treated "as if they were Australia or New Zealand." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The comparison asked for the standing of a partner rather than the role of a host. Ben-Gurion was not refusing alignment with the West. He was refusing a form of it in which Israel supplied ground and Britain supplied the terms.

The British reply did not meet him. Herbert Morrison's answer of 23 April 1951 was, in Shlaim's description, lukewarm, and offered Israel only a back-door association with a regional defence body. Ben-Gurion insisted on full membership. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The gap between the two positions was the whole of the question. Britain wanted Israel's territory, or at least its association, in a scheme whose centre lay elsewhere; Israel wanted a seat at the table on the same footing as the scheme's founders.

Where the centre lay became clear in October. On 13 October 1951 the United States, Britain and Turkey, joined by France, formally tendered to Egypt an invitation to join a Middle East Command "as a founding member." [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_note] The timing could hardly have been worse for the sponsors. By the day the invitation was delivered, Egypt had already abrogated its 1936 treaty with Britain and the 1899 agreements on the Sudan. On 15 October it rejected the invitation. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_note] The United States did not accept the abrogation. Acheson's note to Egypt, sent through the American embassy, called it "without validity" and a "serious retrograde step" for collective defence. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_egypt_note] Six weeks earlier, the United States had voted for the Security Council resolution calling on Egypt to end its restrictions on Israel-bound shipping in the Suez Canal, as told in [scene:cw1_suez_blockade|the Canal closed to Israel]; now it was asking the same government to be the founding regional member of its defence command and protesting when that government abrogated its treaty with Britain.

The rest of the region was to be handled differently. The State Department proposed that the four sponsoring powers inform the Arab states, Egypt excepted, and Israel that they "intend establish MEC," without exhorting them to join. Israel was to receive a separate approach in Tel Aviv, asking it to "give most serious consideration" to a question that "involves vitally" its security. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_circular] The wording sets out the hierarchy exactly. Egypt was invited to found the command. The other Arab states and Israel were informed that it would be established. Israel alone was told, separately, that the matter concerned it vitally, and was asked to consider it seriously. It was not asked to found anything.

One reading, offered here as interpretation, is that the structure reflects a convergence of interests rather than any single design. Three interests met in these documents, and none of them was secret. Britain's was a base: Strang's proposals of early 1951 were proposals for British bases and a corridor, and the Middle East Command was offered first to Egypt. Egypt's was sovereignty: the abrogation of the 1936 treaty and the Sudan agreements came before the invitation was delivered, and Egypt rejected the command two days after receiving it. Israel's was recognition as a partner: Ben-Gurion rejected the base proposal as insulting and asked for full membership on the model of Australia and New Zealand. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_note] The American documents show Washington trying to hold these together, protesting Egypt's abrogation while keeping the invitation open, and approaching Israel with care but without an offer of founding status. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_egypt_note] [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_mec_circular] No one in the record cited here proposed a command in which all three interests could be satisfied at once.

The mechanism, on the same reading, also suggests who could act. The sponsoring powers decided who would be invited and on what terms. Egypt, as the invited founding member, held a veto in practice: without it, a command built around it had no centre, and Egypt used that position to refuse. The other Arab states and Israel could only respond to a decision already taken. Israel had the clearest wish to join and the least say in the design. Its willingness to join gave it little leverage while the sponsors were still seeking Egypt.

The scheme did not recover from Egypt's refusal. According to Shlaim, a British military delegation failed to break the deadlock in October 1952, and in 1953 the idea of a Middle East Command faded. Israel's hope of military integration with the West, he writes, faded with it. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Egypt itself had changed in the meantime: its Free Officers overthrew King Farouk in July 1952, and the new regime's course belongs to Stage Two. [cite:w_1952_egypt] The new American administration's own reassessment of the region in 1953, and of Israel's place in it, is told in [scene:cw1_dulles_1953|Dulles's line].

The record cited here leaves several things open. It does not show how Israel answered the separate approach in Tel Aviv, or whether the four powers' intended notice to the Arab states was ever delivered in the form the State Department proposed. It does not record the Arab states' responses to the command. It gives Ben-Gurion's reaction to the British proposals through Shlaim's account, not through the British or Israeli documents themselves, and it does not give the text of Morrison's reply. What it does establish is the order of priority in 1951: in the West's plans for the region's defence, Egypt was the state to be won, and Israel, however willing, was a state to be informed.
