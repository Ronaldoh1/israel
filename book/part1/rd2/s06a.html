The Churchill White Paper of 1922: The First Narrowing

The policy paper that marked the second stage of that separation said a good deal more. On 3 June 1922 the Colonial Secretary, Winston Churchill, published a command paper titled "Palestine: Correspondence with the Palestine Arab Delegation and the Zionist Organisation." Historians call it the Churchill White Paper. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] It was made up of nine documents, with Churchill's own memorandum as an enclosure, and it was an official British clarification of what the Balfour Declaration meant, put on the record five years after the declaration itself. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] It appeared seven weeks before the League Council's conditional approval of the Mandate text in July 1922.

Why 1922. The paper was drafted partly in response to the Jaffa riots of May 1921, in which dozens were killed on both sides. [cite:wiki_jaffa_riots_1921] Those riots pushed Britain toward its first restatement of policy; their course and Britain's inquiry into them are told in [scene:ri_s01|the first riots and the first inquiries]. [cite:wiki_jaffa_riots_1921] A major narrowing of policy thus came within roughly a year of serious violence. The record does not show that the riots were the only cause, and Britain's wider strategic and administrative concerns were almost certainly in play as well.

What the paper said. The White Paper reaffirmed the Balfour Declaration. In the same breath it set limits on what the declaration meant. Palestine, it insisted, was not intended to become "as Jewish as England is English." [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] That sentence did not withdraw the promise of a national home. It narrowed what the promise could be taken to mean, and it did so in Britain's own official words.

The second limit concerned immigration. The White Paper laid down the principle that Jewish immigration should be limited by the country's economic capacity to absorb new arrivals. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] This was the first formal mechanism under the Mandate for limiting immigration, seventeen years before the 1939 White Paper imposed a harder, fixed numerical ceiling. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] The 1922 rule was a standard rather than a number. It tied the flow of immigrants to an economic judgment about what the country could take. How the rule had been foreshadowed in Herbert Samuel's statements of 1921, and how immigration actually moved under it in the early 1920s, belongs to the story of the Third Aliyah.

The third limit was territorial. The White Paper is associated with the separation of Transjordan, the land east of the Jordan River and roughly three-quarters of the Mandate's total area, from the part of the Mandate open to Jewish settlement. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] It was one stage of that separation, not the whole of it. Abdullah had been installed in Transjordan in March 1921; the League approved the memorandum that formally excluded the national-home provisions on 16 September 1922; and the separation was complete by 1923. [cite:w_cairo1921] [cite:rs_md_transjordan] [cite:wiki_borders_of_israel] Those stages, and the arguments later built on them, are traced in [scene:s06a2|the separation of Transjordan]. The separation was the largest territorial reduction in the whole pre-1948 history, and it came by administrative decision, not by war and not through negotiation with the Zionist leadership. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922]

How it was received. The paper's own title shows that it was framed as an answer to two parties at once, the Palestine Arab Delegation and the Zionist Organisation. The Arab Delegation, then in London, had demanded a "national independent Government" and refused the Balfour Declaration as a basis for discussion; the White Paper gave it neither, and its exchanges with the Colonial Office are set out in the account of the Palestinian national movement. [cite:rs_mca_cmd1700] The Zionist leadership accepted the paper without treating it as a triumph, and Chaim Weizmann and others believed the absorptive-capacity principle could still allow a Jewish majority to be built; that acceptance, and the Arab refusal of the 1922 constitution, are treated in [scene:rd2_council|the Legislative Council]. [cite:rs_lc_cojs_whitepaper] Acceptance did not mean agreement. The record of the Mandate years shows continuing tension between the Zionist Organization and Britain over exactly this kind of step-by-step retreat, and real Zionist objection to the territorial decision, even though it was never reversed. The sharpest objection came from Jabotinsky, who built the Revisionist movement in part on rejecting the separation of Transjordan. [cite:sunnafiles_jordan]

What happened to immigration anyway. The policy limited growth; it did not stop it. By the figures given in one account of the White Paper, the Jewish population of Palestine grew under the absorptive-capacity rule from about 80,000 in 1922 to roughly 380,000 by 1936, nearly a fivefold increase in fourteen years. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] The 80,000 is a round estimate; the British census of October 1922 counted 83,794 Jews. [cite:rs_ta_report1922] The same pattern of a formal restriction followed by continued growth recurs with the harder cap of 1939.

Reversal or narrowing. The familiar account holds that the Balfour promise stood unchanged from 1917 until Britain suddenly reversed it in 1939. The documents tell a different story. Within five years of making the promise, Britain narrowed its meaning, attached an economic test to immigration, and set in motion the removal of roughly three-quarters of the territory from the area open to Jewish settlement, seventeen years before the better-known reversal. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922] Whether 1922 should be called the first reversal or only a narrowing is a matter of judgment, and the record supports both readings in part. The case for "reversal" rests on scale: the territorial reduction was larger than any later one before 1948, and the immigration rule was the first formal mechanism for limiting immigration. The case for "narrowing" rests on the paper's own words: it reaffirmed the declaration, kept immigration open, and left the national home in force west of the Jordan, where the Jewish population then grew almost fivefold. On either reading, the 1939 White Paper was not the first time Britain redefined its promise. It was the second.

What the record leaves open. The growth figures show that the absorptive-capacity rule did not prevent large-scale immigration. They cannot show what immigration would have been without it: growth might have been faster with no cap at all, and the record here makes no counterfactual claim. Nor does it show that "economic absorptive capacity" was applied consistently or predictably in the years that followed. The weight of the Jaffa riots against Britain's other motives in drafting the paper is likewise unsettled; the riots are documented as a direct contributing factor, not as the sole cause.
