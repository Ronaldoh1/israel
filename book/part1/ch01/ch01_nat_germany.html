Germany: Teaching Through Responsibility

No country's classrooms carry this history with more weight than Germany's. The previous section shows how the Holocaust is anchored in the history curricula of all sixteen German states. This section looks at what follows from it in the classroom: how German schools are told to present Jews and Israel, and what happened when the conflict itself walked into German schools in October 2023.

Responsibility written into the books

The starting point is the same as for German foreign policy: responsibility for the Holocaust, which successive governments have described as part of the country's reason of state (see the survey that opens this part). In the schools, that responsibility is carried by agreement among the sixteen states, not by a national ministry, as the section on three ways a country holds the pen describes. [cite:pen_kmk_history]

Two joint efforts show how it is applied to textbooks. In 2011 the German and Israeli governments set up a joint commission to examine how each country appears in the other's schoolbooks; it presented its findings and recommendations in Berlin in June 2015 under the title "Images of others." [cite:nat_euroclio_gitc2015] In December 2024 the conference of German education ministers, the Central Council of Jews in Germany and the association of educational publishers adopted a twelve-point declaration on how Judaism should appear in German teaching materials. It commits them to showing Jewish life in its present-day variety, presenting Jews as part of German society rather than as outsiders, avoiding stereotypes, and teaching both historical and current hatred of Jews in a way suited to each age. It includes a dedicated section on the State of Israel. [cite:nat_kmk_decl2024]

Both efforts were negotiated with Jewish and Israeli partners. The record located for this book shows no comparable arrangement with Palestinian institutions over how Palestinians appear in German schoolbooks. That is a structural fact about who has a seat at the table, not proof of any particular bias in the books themselves.

October 2023 in the schoolyard

After the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, state education ministries sent guidance to schools. Hessen assembled classroom materials and help with conflicts; its minister said hostility toward Jews and "aggression toward Israel" were incompatible with the state's values. North Rhine-Westphalia, Thuringia and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania issued their own guidance. [cite:nat_n4t_states2023]

Berlin went furthest. On 14 October 2023 its education senator, Katharina Günther-Wünsch, wrote to school principals that they could ban symbols, statements and actions that could be read as endorsing the attacks or supporting Hamas, including conduct short of a crime. Her examples included wearing a keffiyeh, the Palestinian scarf; "Free Palestine" stickers; and a map of Israel in Palestinian colours. Violations could bring anything from a reprimand to exclusion from class. Members of her own coalition partner, the Social Democrats, objected: they agreed Hamas symbols must be banned, but called a blanket ban on legal Palestinian symbols constitutionally doubtful and said it treated Hamas and moderate Palestinian positions as one. The senator answered that tolerance ends where terrorist violence is supported as propaganda. [cite:nat_n4t_berlin2023]

What the German case shows

Germany teaches the Holocaust more thoroughly than almost any country, and that teaching has made its schools exceptionally alert to hatred of Jews. The same commitment shapes how the conflict can be discussed. A Palestinian pupil in Berlin in late 2023 could find a family symbol treated as a possible sign of support for an attack. A Jewish pupil could find the school more ready to protect them than schools in many other countries. Both follow from the same structure: a national memory written into the curriculum by agreement, and applied to a conflict whose other side was never party to that agreement.
