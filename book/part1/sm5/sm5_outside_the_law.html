Outside the Law

In July 1945 a network founded at Rudolf Sonneborn's apartment in New York began buying weapons, aircraft and machinery in the United States and moving them to the Haganah through neutral countries, at a time when American law forbade the export. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The prosecutions under the Neutrality Act were few. Charles Winters was convicted and served eighteen months; Hank Greenspun and Al Schwimmer were convicted and served no prison time. All three were later pardoned, Greenspun by Kennedy in 1961, Schwimmer by Clinton in 2000 and Winters, after his death, by George W. Bush. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] [cite:nbc_bush_pardons_winters]

Once the state existed, the cases changed. In July 1954 an Israeli sabotage cell was caught in Egypt in what became known as Operation Susannah or the Lavon affair; the head of military intelligence, Binyamin Gibli, said he had a verbal instruction from the Defense Minister, Pinhas Lavon, who denied any knowledge, and the prime minister had been kept out of the loop. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] At Dimona, Kennedy demanded American visits to a reactor built with French help, and the terms were still disputed when he died. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The FBI's inquiry into NUMEC, a Pennsylvania nuclear company that could not account for enriched uranium, ran from 1966 and ended in 1980 without a charge or a finding. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec]

The modern cases are documented in court and in government records. Jonathan Pollard, a Navy analyst, was arrested in 1985 for spying for Israel and sentenced to life in 1987. [cite:ow13_upi_1987] [cite:ow13_wiki_pollard] NSO Group's Pegasus spyware, exported under Israeli Defense Ministry licence, was used against State Department employees, and the Commerce Department placed the company on its blacklist. [cite:ow10_reu_state] [cite:ow10_doc_entity] The private intelligence firm Black Cube was hired against former American officials. [cite:ow10_cnbc_bc]

These are acts of particular agencies, firms and people, and the record holds both outcomes: a pardon for the smugglers of 1948 and a life sentence for Pollard. What changed is the tool, from smuggled aircraft to spyware. What recurs is that the consequences, where they came at all, rarely matched the scale of the act.
