Part Twenty-Two established what the Council of Nicaea did and did not do. Summoned by Constantine, carried by the imperial post and attended by 250 to 300 mostly eastern bishops, with Rome represented by two presbyters and no Ethiopian bishop yet in existence, the council adopted a creed defining the Son as "of one substance" with the Father, a term that Eusebius of Caesarea says the emperor himself urged. [cite:vt22_vc3] [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] [cite:vt22_socrates1] Arius and the two bishops who refused to sign were exiled, and Constantine ordered Arius's writings "consigned to the flames" on pain of death; the council also fixed a common Easter, contained the Melitian schism, and in canon 6 recognised the regional authority of Alexandria, Rome and Antioch. [cite:vt22_socrates1] [cite:vt22_canons] It did not choose the books of the Bible, a story that goes back to the Synodicon Vetus of about 887 to 920 and was popularised by Voltaire; it did not invent belief in Christ's divinity, attested by Pliny around 111 to 113; it ordered no gospel burned; and it said nothing about reincarnation, a matter confused with the anathemas against Origen of 553. [cite:vt22_wiki_synodicon] [cite:vt22_pliny] [cite:vt22_origen_anath] Its lasting finding is a method: belief defined in a written formula, adopted under an emperor and enforced by the state. The books Christians would read were settled more slowly, through letters and regional councils, and Part Twenty-Three follows that process.
