The Architecture, in One Page

Jeffrey Epstein was a man of no inherited wealth who became the point where very large sums of money, very powerful people and a supply of girls and young women met. The documents show how each part worked. A small group of rich men, almost all American, paid him or gave him control of their fortunes. Banks kept him as a client after he was a registered sex offender, overruling their own compliance staff. Staff scheduled, recruited and flew. A reputation machine of publicists, editors, scientists and friends made a convicted man respectable again. Governments on six continents dealt with him as a broker of access.

And at every point where the system might have stopped him, it stopped short instead. The FBI logged a complaint in 1996 and opened no case for ten years. A state prosecutor took a weak charge to a grand jury. A federal prosecutor overruled his own line attorney and signed an agreement that immunised his helpers and was kept from his victims. Banks settled without admitting anything. A jail failed in every routine duty around its most important prisoner. The department responsible for releasing the files exposed victims and withheld half the record. No document shows these decisions were coordinated. None needed to be. Each institution, facing the choice, found it cheaper to stop than to go on, and the people who could make it expensive were the people around Epstein.

What finally moved the case came from the edges: a parent, a local police chief, survivors and their lawyers, a reporter, a judge, a territory, a state, a Congress forced to vote. One person has been convicted. The structure that protected the rest is still in place.
