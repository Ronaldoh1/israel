From Tiran to the Cabinet Decision, 22 May–4 June 1967

By 19 May 1967 the United Nations force had been asked to leave Sinai and Egyptian troops were moving in, as told in [scene:cw6_may_crisis|the crisis's first week]. What followed over the next thirteen days was a contest of speeches, letters and meetings among five governments, which ended on 4 June with a decision by Israel's government. This section tells those thirteen days from the documents, and stops there. Three questions about them are still argued: whether Israel faced an imminent attack, whether the United States gave Israel a "green light," and whether the Strait of Tiran was in fact closed. A fourth, smaller question, how Israel's ministers voted on 4 June, turns out to be unsettled too.

Nasser's speech, 22 May. On 22 May Nasser spoke to the Egyptian armed forces. He claimed the Gulf of Aqaba for Egypt:

The Aqaba Gulf constitutes our Egyptian territorial waters. Under no circumstances will we allow the Israeli flag to pass through the Aqaba Gulf.

[cite:rs_cw6_nasser_22may] In the same speech he said that Israel had decided "to carry out an aggression against Syria as of May 17," the claim of the Soviet warning that the UN observers had found no troop concentrations to support, and he said: "we are ready for war." [cite:rs_cw6_nasser_22may]

U Thant in Cairo, and Israel's casus belli. U Thant learned of the restrictions at Tiran while on his way to Cairo, where he stayed from the afternoon of 23 May until 25 May. [cite:rs_cw6_s7906_t] His report of 26 May records the two positions in the language of law. Israel held that closing the Strait to Israeli ships, or restricting cargoes bound for Israel, would be a casus belli, a cause for war, and it claimed a right of innocent passage. The UAR claimed the waters as its own territorial waters. [cite:rs_cw6_s7906_t] Each government thus presented the same strait under a different legal rule, and each rule, if accepted, settled the question in its favour.

Was Tiran closed? The point seems obvious, but the sources do not agree on it. Nasser announced the closure on 22 May, and Israel treated it as a casus belli, as the UN record shows. [cite:rs_cw6_nasser_22may] [cite:rs_cw6_s7906_t] The Library of Congress study of Egypt, however, says that he never carried out the threat. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt_t] The sources fetched here do not record a ship stopped at the Strait between 22 May and 4 June, nor do they record one passing through. What is documented is the announcement and the response to it; whether the blockade was enforced in practice is not settled by these documents. For Israel's cabinet, the announcement was the act that mattered.

The cabinet, 23–27 May. Israel's government met repeatedly and could not agree. According to the Israel State Archives' summary of its cabinet meetings, on 23 May the cabinet defined the blockade as aggression but reached no consensus on a military response, and gave diplomacy time. On 26 May it could not agree whether to act. An all-night session on 27 May weighed the appeals for restraint that had come from foreign leaders without agreeing on a plan. [cite:rs_cw6_isa_cabinet_t] Five days after the announcement, the government that had declared closure of the Strait a cause for war had not decided to go to war over it.

The military advice reaching the government was not uniform either. Fanack records that the chief of staff, Rabin, called the blockade a "to be or not to be" issue, and that the head of military intelligence, Aharon Yariv, warned Eshkol that Egyptian forces in Sinai had moved to an offensive posture. [cite:rs_cw6_fanack_t] Tal records that on 22 May, the day of the speech, Rabin saw no sign that Nasser intended war. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_t] The two statements are not necessarily in conflict; they may describe a judgment that changed as the Egyptian deployment grew. But the sources do not date Rabin's "to be or not to be," so the change cannot be traced day by day from them.

Eban and Johnson, 26 May. On 26 May Eban met Johnson. According to the American record of the conversation, Johnson told him: "our best judgment is that no military attack on Israel is imminent." McNamara cited three intelligence groups that had judged the Egyptian deployments defensive. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d77] Johnson called the closure of the Strait illegal. And he put the American position in a sentence that would be repeated:

Israel will not be alone unless it decides to go it alone.

[cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d77] He stressed "Constitutional processes," and the need for the support of Congress before the United States could act. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d77] The President was telling Israel two things at once: that the United States regarded the closure as unlawful and would stand with Israel, and that it would not act without the support of Congress.

Nasser to the trade unionists, 26 May. On the same day, Nasser spoke to Arab trade unionists in terms that went beyond the Strait. According to the Israel Ministry of Foreign Affairs' translation, he said: "The battle will be a general one and our basic objective will be to destroy Israel." He said too that Egypt could have told UNEF to leave "within half an hour." [cite:rs_cw6_nasser_26may] The Library of Congress study adds a qualification from the other direction: Nasser publicly denied that Egypt would strike first, while Amer and his defence minister, Shams Badran, reportedly urged him to. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt_t] The statement of 26 May is a head of state's declared aim of destroying a neighbouring state, and it is quoted here exactly. It is also a public speech, made on a day when the same man was publicly denying an intention to strike first, and the record does not establish which of the two governed Egypt's planning.

Kosygin and Badran in Moscow, 26 May. Badran was in Moscow that day. According to the Egyptian minutes, Kosygin told him: "Politically, you prevailed. Militarily you prevailed." [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy_t] Elbahtimy reads the Egyptian record of these talks as Moscow urging restraint: Egypt had won its point, and should stop there. [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy_t] On the same day Kosygin wrote to Eshkol urging him to prevent a conflict, as told in the previous section.

Jordan joins, 30 May. On 30 May Hussein signed a military alliance with Egypt that placed the Jordanian army under the field command of an Egyptian general officer. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_t] [cite:rs_cw6_tal_t] For the Israeli argument that the country was being encircled, the pact of 30 May was the decisive new fact: Egypt in Sinai, Syria bound to Egypt by the pact of November 1966, and now Jordan.

Dayan and Begin, 1 June. On 1 June Moshe Dayan became defence minister. That day Dayan and Menachem Begin attended a cabinet meeting for the first time, before they had been sworn in. [cite:rs_cw6_isa_cabinet_t] [cite:rs_cw6_fanack_t] Laurens describes the result as a unity government. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_t] Dayan was a member of Rafi, Ben-Gurion's list. Rafi and Gahal had both been left outside the coalition Eshkol formed after the 1965 election, as told in the section on Israel at home, and on the eve of the war he brought both into a national unity government. [cite:rs_cw6_idi_1965] [cite:rs_cw6_idi_rafi] The government that would decide on war was therefore wider than the one that had deadlocked in May.

Amit and McNamara, 1 June. On the same day Meir Amit saw McNamara. The American memorandum for the record says that Amit told him Israel's mobilization of about 100,000 people was straining the economy and that "time is very much against Israel." He called the Soviet claim of a planned Israeli attack on Syria a "sheer fabrication." He estimated about 4,000 casualties in a campaign in Sinai. McNamara promised to convey his views to the President. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d124]

Twenty-five years later Amit gave a fuller account. In 1992 he said that he had told McNamara he would personally recommend action, and that McNamara had replied, "the president knows that you are here." The contemporary American record notes no such exchange. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d124] The two are different kinds of evidence: a memorandum written at the time by the American side, and a recollection given a quarter of a century later by the Israeli participant. Neither can be checked against a third record from the sources here.

Johnson's letter, 3 June. On 3 June Johnson wrote to Eshkol. The United States, he said, was pursuing a declaration by the maritime powers on passage through Tiran. He repeated, in slightly different words from 26 May, that "Israel will not be alone unless it decides to go alone," and he urged Israel "not to make itself responsible for the initiation of hostilities." [cite:rs_cw6_lbj_3june] The same day, Laurens notes, Rusk sent a telegram describing an "apocalypse psychology" on both sides. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_t]

The decision of 4 June. On 4 June the ministerial defence committee met with the rest of the government. The IDF and Defense Establishment Archives give the decision as one "to take military action to free Israel from the suffocating aggression grip tightening around it." [cite:rs_cw6_mod_going_to_war] The decision as the Ministry of Foreign Affairs published it is worded differently: it authorized "the Prime Minister and the Minister of Defence to take steps necessary for the protection of Israel." [cite:rs_cw6_mfa_4june] The historian Yaakov Lozowick, who read the Security Cabinet transcripts after their release (935 pages, covering 36 meetings from January to July 1967), writes that on 4 June the cabinet "directed the IDF to break the Arab choke ring" and left the details to Eshkol and Dayan. [cite:rs_cw6_lozowick]

On the vote, the records diverge. The IDF archives state that the decision was taken "by a majority of 18." [cite:rs_cw6_mod_going_to_war] Lozowick, reading the transcripts, gives no vote count and records no formal dissent; the two Mapam ministers, Barzilai and Bentov, still wanted a final message sent to the great powers first. [cite:rs_cw6_lozowick] The archive's phrase is not explained in the sources here, and the transcripts as Lozowick reads them record no division. The count remains open.

Did Israel face an imminent attack? This is the first and largest of the disputes about these thirteen days. The case that it did rests on documents of its own. Israeli ministers feared that they would be struck first; Tal quotes the fear in the words "If we will not be the first to strike, Nasser will eliminate us." [cite:rs_cw6_tal_t] The head of military intelligence warned that the Egyptian forces in Sinai had shifted to an offensive posture. [cite:rs_cw6_fanack_t] And Nasser had said in public, on 26 May, that Egypt's basic objective in the coming battle was to destroy Israel. [cite:rs_cw6_nasser_26may] Add the Egypt–Jordan pact, the departure of UNEF, and a mobilization that, by Amit's account, was straining the economy, and the strongest form of the Israeli case is that waiting was itself a risk the state could not take.

The case that it did not rests on other documents. American intelligence told Eban on 26 May that no attack was imminent, and that three intelligence groups judged the Egyptian deployments defensive. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d77] Rabin, on 22 May, saw no sign that Nasser intended war. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_t] Laurens and Tal read Nasser's conduct as deterrence and brinkmanship, not a plan to attack. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_t] [cite:rs_cw6_tal_t] On this view the danger was real but not imminent. Laurens's own conclusion, drawing on Rusk's "apocalypse psychology," is that the war came from miscalculation on both sides, not from a deliberate trap laid by either. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_t] The documents support both readings in part. They show that Israel's government believed itself in danger, and that the American government, on its own intelligence, did not believe an Egyptian attack was imminent. Which judgment was correct is not something the record of 22 May to 4 June can settle.

Did Washington give a "green light"? The written American record is cautionary throughout. Johnson's words of 26 May and his letter of 3 June both told Israel not to begin hostilities and tied American action to Congress and, by 3 June, to a declaration by the maritime powers. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d77] [cite:rs_cw6_lbj_3june] Against that stand Amit's 1992 account of his meeting with McNamara, and the historians cited by Elbahtimy, who speak of an "amber" light given during Amit's visit of 1 June. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d124] [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy_t] The contemporary memorandum of that meeting records McNamara promising only to convey Amit's views. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d124] The documents show what Washington wrote; what Israel's leaders understood from what was said but not written down is the question the "amber light" reading addresses, and the record here does not answer it.

What the record does not settle. Several points remain open. Whether the Strait of Tiran was closed in practice, as opposed to by announcement, is disputed between Nasser's own words and the Library of Congress study. How many ministers voted for the decision of 4 June is given as "a majority of 18" in one archive and not at all in the transcripts as Lozowick reads them. What McNamara said to Amit beyond what the American memorandum records rests on a single later recollection. Whether Egypt's leaders meant to attack is contested between their public words, which spoke of destroying Israel, and the assessments of American intelligence, which found their deployment defensive. What is documented is the sequence: an announcement at Tiran, a deadlocked Israeli cabinet, an American President urging Israel not to move first while calling the closure illegal, a Jordanian army placed under Egyptian command, a government widened to include Dayan and Begin, and, on 4 June, a decision authorizing military action.

That is where this volume ends. What followed is told in [scene:s12|the Six-Day War]; the question of who fired first, and how it was reported, in [scene:iaa_s04|who fired first].
