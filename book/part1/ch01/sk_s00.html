How This Book Weighs Evidence

A reader coming to this history for the first time is entitled to suspect that any account of it is someone's opinion dressed up as history. The suspicion is reasonable, and the only honest answer is to state the method in advance, so that each claim can be checked against it. What follows is that method: what counts as evidence in this book, how sources were chosen, who produced the work, and how errors are handled.

Claims in this book rest on four levels of evidence, and the prose signals which level is in play. A documented claim rests on a primary record: a law, a court ruling, an official filing, a census, a signed document. A reported claim rests on a named news outlet or a named investigator. A contested claim is one on which credible sources disagree; in those cases both positions are set out and the dispute is left open rather than settled by the author. Some questions are stated as unresolved because the record itself does not settle them. One example, discussed in later chapters, is how many of Gaza's dead were combatants. Where a passage offers a symbolic or interpretive reading rather than a documentary finding, it is identified as interpretation.

The choice of sources follows a simple principle: each side's own official records are used. Israeli deaths are drawn from Israel's National Insurance Institute and Defense Ministry figures. Palestinian deaths are drawn from the Gaza Health Ministry, United Nations agencies and peer-reviewed studies. Court records and archives come from Israeli, Palestinian, American and international sources alike. Advocacy organizations on every side, among them UN Watch, IMPACT-se, Track AIPAC and the Washington Report, are identified as advocacy groups whenever they are cited, so that the reader can weigh them accordingly.

This book is the work of Observe The System, an independent research project. No government, party, lobby or donor funds it or reviews it. When a source turns out to be wrong, or has been misread, the claim is corrected and the change is noted.

Every claim carries a citation, and the reader is invited to follow it. A reader who disagrees with a reading of a document can consult the document directly. That is the purpose of the apparatus: not to ask for trust, but to make trust unnecessary.

Why might the record differ from what readers learned? Most people encounter this history through textbooks, speeches and news headlines. Those are summaries, and summaries leave things out. This book places the underlying documents beside the summaries so that the two can be compared.

The same rules apply in every direction. A claim favorable to one side receives the same scrutiny as a claim unfavorable to it, and the standard applies to this book's own conclusions as much as to anyone else's. Where the evidence is strong, the prose says so. Where it is thin, disputed or absent, the prose says that too.
