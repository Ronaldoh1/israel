The Keepers

In the Andes the sacred was kept in places, in ancestors and in knotted cords. The Third Council of Lima, in 1582 and 1583, ordered bishops to destroy completely the quipus that recorded non-Christian rites, calling them "pernicious instruments", while encouraging Christians to use quipus for confession. [cite:vt34_wiki_lima3] In the 1560s a movement called Taki Onqoy, "the dancing sickness", taught that the huacas, the sacred places and beings of the Andes, had revived to defeat "the God of the Christians"; clerical visitors suppressed it with public shaming, whipping and exile, and the historian Pedro Guibovich argues that its scale was exaggerated by the man who built a career on suppressing it. [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki]

From 1609 the archdiocese of Lima ran formal campaigns, the "extirpation of idolatries", in three waves until 1670. Visitors questioned villages, punished native ritual specialists with whipping, exile and in some cases death, and burned the huacas and the mummified ancestors that families kept and honoured. The historian Kenneth Mills called it "perhaps the most sustained religious persecution" of Indigenous people in colonial Spanish America; Nicholas Griffiths reads the campaigns, given how irregularly they came, as "aberrations, not a commonplace phenomenon". [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The Jesuit Pablo José de Arriaga's manual for the campaigns, printed at Lima in 1621, is itself an eyewitness record of the rites it set out to end. [cite:vt34_wiki_arriaga]

The bans in North America were the work of governments. The Sun Dance, the great annual ceremony of many Plains peoples, was targeted in 1883, when the US Secretary of the Interior called for an end to "the old heathenish dances, such as the sun-dance", and rules for Courts of Indian Offenses enforced it with penalties from withheld rations to prison. No act of Congress authorised the code. [cite:vt35_tah_teller] [cite:vt35_narf_civ] People kept the ceremony alive quietly. A circular of 1934 opened the way to its public revival, and the American Indian Religious Freedom Act of 1978 declared a policy to protect Indians' "inherent right of freedom to believe, express, and exercise" their traditional religions. [cite:vt35_audit_nlm_collier] [cite:og_mtsu_airfa]

The Native American Church keeps an all-night ceremony in a tipi, led by a Roadman, with a Fireman tending the sacred fire from sundown until sunrise, in which peyote is taken as a sacrament. [cite:og_unl_nac] In Employment Division v. Smith in 1990 the Supreme Court held that a state need not exempt sacramental peyote use from its general drug laws. [cite:og_mtsu_airfa] Congress answered in 1994: "the use, possession, or transportation of peyote by an Indian for bona fide traditional ceremonial purposes" is lawful. [cite:og_mtsu_airfa]

The record shows the keepers surviving each wave. The Andean ancestors were burned, and Andean religion adapted rather than disappeared, as Mills's work shows; the Sun Dance was banned and is held every year in many communities; the peyote ceremony lost in the Supreme Court and won in Congress.
