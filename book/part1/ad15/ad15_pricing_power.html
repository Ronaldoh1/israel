Pricing Power: Concentration, Markups, Algorithms and the Right to Repair

The taught version of a market economy rests on competition. Many sellers compete for customers, so no one firm can set prices far above its costs for long, and what people pay, like what they earn, reflects market value. Economics standards written for American high schools put it in those terms, and the naturalisation test describes the country's system as a "free market economy". [cite:ad1_cee2010] [cite:ad1_uscis2025] This section tests the price side of that promise. It sets out what is known about whether markets have become less competitive and whether firms now charge more above cost, then follows three newer ways of setting prices: shared algorithms, prices tailored to the individual buyer, and fees added at the end of a purchase. It closes with the record on products built to be replaced, and the laws that have pushed back.

Concentration and markups: the evidence and its critics

Concentration measures how much of a market the largest firms hold. A 2019 study by Gustavo Grullon, Yelena Larkin and Roni Michaely found that "since the late 1990s, over 75% of US industries have experienced an increase in concentration levels", and that firms in the industries where concentration rose most earned higher profit margins. [cite:us11_gl_concentration] The Information Technology and Innovation Foundation, a Washington technology-policy think tank, disputed the scale: in Census data for 2002 to 2012 the four largest firms' share fell or held steady in 319 of 792 detailed industries (40 per cent), and measured from 1972 instead of 1997 the rise in the typical industry is much smaller. [cite:us11_itif_kennedy] Local markets, where shoppers actually buy, have in several studies become less concentrated even as national ones became more so; one Census-based study found local employer concentration fell to about three-quarters of its 1976 level by 2015. [cite:ad5_rinz2022] [scene:ad5_monopsony_lockin|Part Five]

A markup is the ratio of a product's price to the cost of producing one more unit of it; a markup of 50 per cent means the price is one and a half times that cost. In the most cited estimate, published in 2020 by Jan De Loecker, Jan Eeckhout and Gabriel Unger, the average markup of US public companies rose from 21 per cent above marginal cost in 1980 to 61 per cent in their latest data, and the average profit rate from 1 per cent to 8 per cent. The rise came mainly from the firms with the highest markups; the median firm's markup did not change. [cite:ad15_qje_markups] The economist Susanto Basu, reviewing this and competing estimates in 2019, concluded that "existing methods cannot determine whether markups have been stable or whether they have risen modestly", because each method depends on assumptions about firms' costs that are hard to check. He added that even a modest rise could matter for workers' share of income. [cite:ad15_basu] The record therefore supports a narrower claim than the popular one: the most profitable firms have pulled away from the rest, and whether prices across the economy have risen relative to costs is disputed.

Algorithmic pricing: the rental software case

Part Six followed one documented case of shared pricing software in housing. [scene:ad6_eviction_and_traps|Part Six] On 23 August 2024 the Justice Department and eight states sued RealPage, whose revenue-management software recommends rents to landlords, alleging that competing landlords fed it their private rent data, that its features pushed them to accept its recommendations, and that it held about 80 per cent of the market for such software in commercial multifamily housing. [cite:ad6_doj_realpage24] RealPage did not admit wrongdoing. Under a settlement announced on 24 November 2025 and approved by the court in May 2026, it may no longer use competitors' non-public data in real-time pricing, must train its models only on non-public data at least a year old, may not build models narrower than a state, must remove features that discourage rent cuts, and will work under a court-appointed monitor. [cite:ad6_doj_realpage25] [cite:ad6_wsoc_realpage] As of October 2026 North Carolina's separate case continued, and a federal court had granted RealPage a preliminary injunction against New York's 2025 ban on algorithmic rent-setting, on free-speech grounds. [cite:ad6_wsoc_realpage] [cite:ad6_dive_ny]

Surveillance pricing

A newer practice sets prices for one buyer at a time. In July 2024 the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), by unanimous vote, ordered eight firms that sell pricing and data services to explain how they work. On 17 January 2025 its staff released initial findings: these intermediaries can draw on a shopper's demographics, location, browsing history and inferred behaviour, such as leaving an item in a basket, and the same tools used to target advertisements can be used to set an individual's price. [cite:ad15_bclp_surv] [cite:ad15_ftc_surv] The staff called the practice surveillance pricing. Commissioners Andrew Ferguson, who became chair three days later, and Melissa Holyoak dissented from releasing preliminary findings, while agreeing that more work was needed and that a final, fact-based report should follow. [cite:ad15_bclp_surv]

One case reached the public in December 2025. An investigation by Consumer Reports with two advocacy groups found that Instacart's software let grocery retailers show different shoppers different prices for the same item at the same store at the same time. On 22 December Instacart said it would end those price tests immediately, while maintaining that its software did not meaningfully raise grocery prices; the FTC said it was "disturbed" by the reports, and the attorneys general of New York and California opened inquiries. [cite:ad15_cr_instacart] New York became the first state to require disclosure: since 8 July 2025 a business that sets a price by algorithm using a shopper's personal data must say so, in capitals, next to the price, with fines of up to $1,000 a violation. A federal judge dismissed the retail industry's challenge to the law in October 2025. [cite:ad15_rin_nylaw]

Junk fees

Some pricing power works through how a price is shown rather than its level: a low headline price followed by mandatory fees at checkout. The FTC's rule on unfair or deceptive fees, finalised in December 2024 and in force since 12 May 2025, requires sellers of live-event tickets and short-term lodging, including resale and booking platforms, to show the total price up front. [cite:ad15_tc_junkfee] Fees on bank accounts, cards and other services are measured in Part Eleven. [scene:ad11_fees_everywhere|Part Eleven]

Built to be replaced, and the right to repair

Planned obsolescence, the practice of designing products to wear out or become unusable sooner than they need to, is often alleged and rarely documented. The best-known documented case involved Apple. A January 2017 software update slowed iPhones with ageing batteries, which Apple did not disclose until December 2017. In 2020 Apple agreed to pay up to $500 million, at least $25 for each affected iPhone 6, 6s, 7 and SE, to settle the resulting class action, without admitting wrongdoing and, by its account, to avoid the cost of litigation. [cite:ad15_9to5_apple] Repair restrictions are more common than proven obsolescence: manufacturers that limit access to parts, tools and manuals can steer repairs to their own service or toward replacement. In May 2021 the FTC, in a unanimous report requested by Congress, found "scant evidence to support manufacturers' justifications for repair restrictions", including claims about safety and data security. [cite:ad15_ftc_nixing]

Where it works as promised

On repair, the counter-evidence is legislation. New York's law took effect on 28 December 2023, California's and Minnesota's on 1 July 2024 and Colorado's on 1 January 2026, and Oregon has enacted its own. Each requires makers of consumer electronics to offer parts, tools and documentation to owners and independent shops on fair and reasonable terms; California requires parts at the same price and terms offered to authorised repairers, and Oregon and Colorado restrict "parts pairing", the use of software to reject replacement parts not approved by the maker. [cite:ad15_wiley_r2r] The broader record is mixed in the same way. Antitrust enforcement produced the RealPage settlement; New York's disclosure law survived its first court test; Instacart ended its price tests within days of publication; and on markups, the leading estimate finds the rise concentrated in a minority of firms while the median firm's markup did not change. [cite:ad6_wsoc_realpage] [cite:ad15_rin_nylaw] [cite:ad15_cr_instacart] [cite:ad15_qje_markups] [cite:us11_itif_kennedy] What the record does not support is the assumption that competition alone keeps every price close to cost: in each of these recent cases, the check came from a regulator, a court, a state legislature or a published investigation.
