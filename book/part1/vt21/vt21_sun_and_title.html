Sun, Sunday and the Chief Priest's Title

A large body of popular writing, including earlier Observe The System material, holds that Constantine fused the worship of the sun with Christianity and handed the papacy the pagan high priest's title, and that Christmas, Sunday and much of Christian ritual were borrowed from the cults of Sol Invictus and Mithras. Some of this rests on solid evidence and some on nineteenth-century speculation repeated until it sounded like fact. This section takes the four strands one at a time (the sun on the coins, the Sunday law, 25 December, and the title Pontifex Maximus) and separates what the documents show from what has been added since.

Sol on the coins

The cult of Sol Invictus, the Unconquered Sun, was given new imperial standing by the emperor Aurelian in 274, who built a temple to Sol in Rome and created a new college of priests for him. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] Constantine's family had a particular attachment to the sun god. From 310 Sol replaced Mars on his coinage, and Sol continued to appear on his official coins until 325 or 326, more than a decade after the Milvian Bridge; one older study puts the last issues in 323. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] After that the labarum and other Christian signs took its place. [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol]

This is a real and well-documented overlap. It shows an emperor who presented himself under the sun god's protection while he was already funding Christian churches. What it does not show is a planned merger of the two religions: the coins are evidence of what Constantine's mints put out, not of a policy to make Christians worship the sun. A mosaic in the Tomb of the Julii beneath St Peter's, dated to around 300 and so before Constantine, shows a figure in a sun chariot that most scholars read as Christ pictured as the sun; Steven Hijmans has argued it may simply show Sol. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol]

The Sunday law of 7 March 321

On 7 March 321 Constantine ordered: "On the venerable day of the Sun let the magistrates and people residing in cities rest". People working the land were left free to carry on. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] The law names the day by its planetary name, which pagan and Christian subjects alike used. Eusebius, describing the same policy, says he "enjoined on all the subjects of the Roman empire to observe the Lord's day, as a day of rest", and adds that soldiers were assembled on an open plain each Lord's day to pray. [cite:vt21_vc4]

Did this move Christian worship from Saturday to Sunday? No. Christians had gathered on Sunday for nearly two centuries. Justin Martyr, writing in Rome around 155, explained to the emperor that "on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place", because it was the first day of creation and the day of the resurrection. [cite:vt21_justin67] What the 321 law did was new in a different way: for the first time, the Roman state set a weekly day of rest that matched the Christian day of worship, and made rest on it a legal requirement for city dwellers. A day the church kept by its own choice became a day the state enforced. How later church law turned Sunday into a duty under pain of sin is traced in Part Thirty-One. [scene:vt31_sunday_and_law|Sunday and law]

December 25

The first Christian record that Christ was born on 25 December is a Roman almanac of the mid-fourth century, the Philocalian calendar, part of the Chronography of 354, which notes "Christ was born in Bethlehem of Judea" under that date. The same almanac marks 25 December as a festival of the Unconquered (Natalis Invicti). [cite:vt21_bas_mcgowan] [cite:vt21_wiki_sol]

Scholars offer two explanations, and the evidence does not settle between them. The "history of religions" theory holds that Christians in Rome placed Christ's birthday on a sun festival to replace it. Its difficulty, as Andrew McGowan sets out, is that no early Christian writer describes choosing the date for that reason, and the first explicit claim that the feast was moved to match the sun festival is a marginal note in a twelfth-century Syriac manuscript. The "calculation" theory holds that Christians dated Christ's conception to 25 March, the date they gave his death (Tertullian around 200 already equated the crucifixion with 25 March), and counted nine months forward. McGowan concludes that "we cannot be entirely sure". [cite:vt21_bas_mcgowan] Even the older side of the comparison is less firm than it is often stated: Hijmans argues that the earliest certain evidence for a sun festival on 25 December comes from the reign of Julian (361–363), not from Aurelian. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol] Neither theory involves Constantine: no law or letter of his concerns the date of Christmas.

Mithras: what the sources show

The claim that Mithras was born of a virgin on 25 December, had twelve disciples and rose from the dead circulates widely. The surviving evidence for the Roman Mithras cult, which flourished among soldiers from the first to the fourth century and had disappeared by about 400, does not support it. Mithras is shown being born from a rock, already a youth, with a dagger and a torch. The idea that his birthday was 25 December is a modern scholarly proposal, made by Maarten Vermaseren and rejected by Roger Beck; Manfred Clauss treats the 25 December festival as a general feast of the sun rather than a Mithraic one. Twelve figures do appear around some Mithraic reliefs, but they are the signs of the zodiac. [cite:vt21_wiki_mithraism]

There is one genuine ancient comparison. Justin Martyr, writing around 150, said that "wicked devils have imitated" the Christian Eucharist in the Mithraic rites, in which bread and a cup of water were offered to initiates; Tertullian later called a Mithraic washing and a mark on the forehead a counterfeit of baptism. [cite:vt21_wiki_mithraism] Christian writers themselves noticed the likeness and explained it as demonic imitation. Whether either rite influenced the other, and in which direction, cannot be shown from the surviving sources.

Pontifex Maximus: whose title, and when

The pontifex maximus was the head of the college of priests who oversaw the public cults of Rome. Augustus took the office, and from then on every emperor held it as one of his titles. Constantine kept it, as did his Christian sons; the Christian emperors appear to have treated it as a formality of the imperial style. [cite:vt21_cameron_jrs] [cite:vt21_wiki_pontmax]

The emperor who gave it up was Gratian. The only ancient source for the event, the pagan historian Zosimus, says Gratian refused the priestly robe because he thought it improper for a Christian, and places this at the start of his reign. Alan Cameron showed that this early date cannot be right, since Gratian was still addressed as pontifex in January 379; the repudiation came later, probably in the early 380s, when Gratian also cut the public funding of the pagan priesthoods and removed the Altar of Victory from the Senate house. [cite:vt21_cameron_jrs] [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] His eastern colleague Theodosius used a different style, pontifex inclytus. [cite:vt21_wiki_pontmax]

The claim that the title passed straight from the emperors to the bishop of Rome does not hold. Christian writers did call bishops pontifex and summus pontifex, but loosely and of bishops in general; Tertullian, around 220, used "pontifex maximus" sarcastically of a bishop he was attacking. [cite:vt21_wiki_pontmax] Popes began to use "Pontifex Maximus" as a regular honorific in the fifteenth century, during the Renaissance revival of classical Latin; Paul II (1464–1471) is an early example. [cite:vt21_sn_pontmax] It is still not one of the official titles listed in the Holy See's yearbook, the Annuario Pontificio, where the nearest is "Supreme Pontiff of the Universal Church". [cite:vt21_wiki_pontmax] The papacy did borrow a pagan Roman title, but more than a thousand years after Constantine and as a scholar's flourish, not as a legal inheritance.

The honest summary is narrower than the popular one, and still significant. Constantine ruled as a Christian patron while keeping the sun god on his money and the pagan high priesthood among his titles, and he gave the church a state-enforced weekly holiday. Those are documented facts about how imperial power and Christian practice met. The stronger claims (a deliberate fusion of sun cult and church, a Mithraic Christmas, a papal title inherited from Constantine) are not supported by the sources cited for them.
