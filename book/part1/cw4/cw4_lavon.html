The Lavon Affair Reopened, 1960–1961: A Question That Brought Down a Government

On 25 December 1960, while Washington was still pressing Ben-Gurion over Dimona, a committee of his own ministers reported on a question from 1954, and his government began to come apart. Stage Two told the operation itself: how Israeli military intelligence activated a cell of Egyptian Jews in July 1954 to plant incendiary devices at American libraries, British-owned cinemas and a post office in Egypt, how the cell was caught, who was executed and imprisoned, and how Defence Minister Pinhas Lavon resigned in 1955 without anyone establishing who had given the order. That account is in [scene:lv_s01|Operation Susannah and the Lavon affair, 1954–1955]. This section takes up the second round. Six years after the operation, the dispute it left behind returned to Israeli politics with a new charge, that the case against Lavon had been built on forged documents and false testimony. Within four months the quarrel had produced a ministerial inquiry that cleared him, a Prime Minister who refused to accept the result, and a resignation that ended the government; an early general election followed in August 1961.

The familiar summary of the affair says that the reopened scandal "contributed to Ben-Gurion's resignation in 1963." [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] That compresses two separate events. The record shows that the affair produced its own resignation first: David Ben-Gurion resigned as prime minister on 31 January 1961 in protest at the cabinet's exoneration of Lavon, and that resignation forced an election in August 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_bg_resigns] [cite:rs_cw4_brit_lavon] His later resignation of 1963 belongs to Stage Five, where its date and circumstances are examined in [scene:cw5_bengurion_resigns|Ben-Gurion's resignation of June 1963]. Both resignations belong to the same long quarrel. They should not be merged into one.

The question underneath both rounds was never whether Israel had carried out the operation. Israel later honoured the surviving operatives publicly, in 2005, as Stage Two records. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The argument of 1960 and 1961 was about something narrower and, for the governing party, more dangerous: which Israeli official had given the order, and whether the evidence that had forced a minister out in 1955 was genuine.

The charge, October 1960. By 1960 Lavon had a new office. He was secretary-general of the Histadrut. In October 1960, according to reports of the Jewish Telegraphic Agency carried in The Detroit Jewish News, he charged that forged documents and perjured testimony had been used to force his resignation as defence minister in 1955. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] A committee under Supreme Court Justice Haim Cohn examined it. The JTA reported that the Cohn committee recommended further investigation. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] Michael Oren, writing later, goes further: he states that Cohn found evidence of perjury. [cite:rs_cw4_oren_lavon] The two descriptions are compatible, a finding of possible perjury being a reason to investigate further, but they are not the same statement, and the sources used here do not include the committee's own text.

What to do with the Cohn report became a cabinet matter. The Attorney-General, Gideon Hausner, wrote an opinion on it, and the cabinet voted six to five to publish that opinion. Ben-Gurion abstained. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] The vote's margin shows how far the government was already divided, and the abstention marks the Prime Minister's position from the start: he did not want the matter settled by ministers.

Who could decide. The mechanism of the affair in 1960 was a contest over forum, not only over facts. Three bodies claimed or were offered the right to establish what had happened in 1954. The Knesset's Foreign Affairs and Security Committee took testimony; Shimon Peres, then Deputy Defence Minister, appeared before it for two and a half hours. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] The cabinet could appoint ministers to examine the evidence. And Ben-Gurion insisted on a third course: a new judicial commission, not the Knesset committee, should establish the facts. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10]

The JTA's report of 28 October 1960 described what the affair was doing to the governing party. It split Mapai between its veterans and its "young guard." [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10]

A compromise was available. Oren writes that Moshe Sharett and Levi Eshkol proposed a way to clear Lavon without implicating other members of Mapai, and that Ben-Gurion rejected it. [cite:rs_cw4_oren_lavon] The compromise would have closed the case politically. Ben-Gurion's refusal kept it open, consistent with his demand that the facts be found by a judicial body.

The Committee of Seven, December 1960. The cabinet chose the ministerial course. A committee of seven ministers, chaired by the Justice Minister, Pinhas Rosen, examined the case and reported on 25 December 1960. It cleared Lavon of responsibility. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] [cite:rs_cw4_nli_lavon] The National Library of Israel, in its collection on Israeli elections, summarizes the committee's conclusion: Lavon had not given the order and had not been aware of the operation. It adds that Colonel Binyamin Gibli was forced to leave the army. [cite:rs_cw4_nli_lavon] Gibli had been the military intelligence chief in 1954, and his account had been that Lavon gave the order. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

The committee's finding thus reversed the burden that Lavon had carried since 1955. In 1955 he had resigned, with the order unresolved. Now a body of the government had found for Lavon and against Gibli's account, and Gibli's military career ended.

"Half-truths." Ben-Gurion rejected the report. According to the JTA, reporting in late January 1961, he called the committee's report "half-truths, bias and miscarriage of justice." [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] He then threatened to resign. Eshkol dissuaded him, and Ben-Gurion wrote accepting the report as "the last word." [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] The retreat was brief.

The fight moved to the Histadrut, where Lavon held office. Three Mapai members of the Histadrut's central committee resigned, citing loss of confidence in Lavon. Lavon refused to step down. "If the party decides it has no confidence in me," he said, "that is a different matter." [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] The sentence placed the decision exactly where the party's leadership did not want it: Lavon would leave only if Mapai, as a party, voted against a man its own government had just exonerated.

The resignation of 31 January 1961. Ben-Gurion resigned as prime minister on 31 January 1961, in protest at the cabinet's exoneration of Lavon. [cite:rs_cw4_cie_bg_resigns] [cite:rs_cw4_oren_lavon] Britannica records that he said he would not return to office while Lavon remained secretary-general of the Histadrut, and that Mapai's central committee then voted to remove Lavon from that post. [cite:rs_cw4_brit_lavon] The order of events is the substance of the episode. A ministerial committee cleared a minister; the Prime Minister resigned over the clearance; the party then removed the cleared man from his office in the labour movement, and the Prime Minister's condition for returning was met.

The two cases can be stated at full strength. Lavon's case was that a committee of the government had examined the evidence and found that he had not given the order and had not known of the operation, and that the account against him had come from an officer who was then forced out of the army. [cite:rs_cw4_nli_lavon] [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] Ben-Gurion's case was that the facts had to be established by a judicial commission, not by the Knesset committee or by ministers, and that the committee's verdict was, in his words, "half-truths, bias and miscarriage of justice." [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] Each case was about the forum as much as the facts. Neither was tested before a court.

The election of 15 August 1961. The resignation brought the country to the polls early. The election to the Fifth Knesset was held on 15 August 1961, about 21 months after the previous one; the Israel Democracy Institute attributes the early date to the Lavon affair. [cite:rs_cw4_idi_1961] Turnout was 81.6% of 1,271,285 eligible voters. Mapai won 42 seats, five fewer than before. Herut and the Liberals won 17 each, the National Religious Party 12, Mapam 9 and Ahdut HaAvoda 8. [cite:rs_cw4_idi_1961]

Mapai remained by far the largest party, but five seats had gone, and forming a government took four months of coalition talks. [cite:rs_cw4_idi_1961] The Israel Democracy Institute's judgment of the affair is broad: it touched nearly every public institution, and it may have marked the start of Mapai's decline. [cite:rs_cw4_idi_1961] The second half of that judgment is offered as a possibility, and it is reported here in the same terms. What the figures show directly is a governing party that kept power but paid for the quarrel in seats and in months.

When the charge came: a conflict in the sources. One reference work dates the affair's second round differently. Britannica places Lavon's accusation against the officers in September 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_brit_lavon] That date cannot be reconciled with the contemporary record. The JTA reported the charge, the Cohn committee and the cabinet's six-to-five vote in October 1960, and the Center for Israel Education likewise has the affair resurfacing in 1960. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] [cite:rs_cw4_cie_bg_resigns] A September 1961 accusation would also fall after the committee that answered it, after the resignation that protested that answer, and after the election that the resignation caused. This section follows the contemporary reports and notes the conflict rather than silently correcting it.

Was Lavon cleared? The answer depends on what counts as clearing. In one sense he plainly was: a committee of seven ministers found on 25 December 1960 that he had not given the order and had not known of the operation. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] [cite:rs_cw4_nli_lavon] In another sense he was not. The Prime Minister called the finding "half-truths, bias and miscarriage of justice" and demanded a judicial inquiry; none was held in the record used here, and the origin of the order was never settled by a court. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1961_01] [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] The common summary, that the question of the order's origin "remained formally unresolved," still stands at the end of this round. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The only formal finding was a ministerial one, and the man who rejected it was the Prime Minister.

The cost of the second round, like the first, fell unevenly. In 1954 and 1955 the operatives in Egypt had paid with their lives and liberty, two executed, one dead by his own hand in prison and others sentenced to long terms, while the dispute among those who planned the operation played out in Israeli politics. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] In 1960 and 1961 the cost was political and fell on the men in Israel: Gibli lost his army career after the Committee of Seven reported, Lavon lost the Histadrut after a party vote, and Ben-Gurion gave up the premiership and went to an election that cost his party five seats. [cite:rs_cw4_nli_lavon] [cite:rs_cw4_brit_lavon] [cite:rs_cw4_idi_1961]

What the record does not settle. Several points remain open on the sources used here. The exact date and vote by which Mapai's central committee removed Lavon from the Histadrut are not established; Britannica records the decision but the sources used for this section do not give its date or tally, and a date of February 1961 has not been verified. [cite:rs_cw4_brit_lavon] The text of the Cohn committee's report and of Hausner's opinion is not among them, so whether Cohn "found evidence of perjury," as Oren writes, or recommended further investigation, as the JTA reported, cannot be checked against the documents themselves. [cite:rs_cw4_oren_lavon] [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1960_10] And the question that began the affair, which official gave the order in July 1954, was answered by a committee of ministers in December 1960 and by no court, then or afterwards, in the record used here.
