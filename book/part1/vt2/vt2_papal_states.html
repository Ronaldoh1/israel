A State Before the State: The Papal States, 754–1870

The Vatican City of today is 44 hectares created by a treaty in 1929. [scene:dv1_three_cities|Part One of The Divine 3+1: the three cities measured] The popes, however, had governed territory for more than eleven centuries before that treaty, and the 1929 settlement makes sense only as the end of that older story. Its own Financial Convention says so: the money Italy paid in 1929 was compensation for "the events of 1870," the year the last of the papal territory was taken. [cite:vt2_lateran_text] This section traces how a bishop of Rome came to rule a state in central Italy, how large that state was, how it was run, and why it was already failing well before Italian troops entered Rome.

The bargain with the Franks, 754–756

The usual starting point is a meeting between a pope and a king. In January 754 Pope Stephen II crossed the Alps and met the Frankish king Pippin (Pepin the Short) at the palace of Ponthion. The Lombard king Aistulf had seized Ravenna and was pressing on Rome, and the Byzantine emperor, nominally the lord of both, was not coming to help. At Quierzy in April 754 Pippin promised to restore to the pope the lands the Lombards had taken; no record of that promise survives. The same year Stephen anointed Pippin and his sons, Charlemagne and Carloman, as kings, giving the new Carolingian dynasty a sanction it needed after Pippin had deposed the last Merovingian king in 750. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin]

Pippin kept his side. He invaded Italy in 755 and 756, defeated Aistulf and imposed a peace. In 756 the keys of the cities that had submitted, with a list of them, were laid on the altar of Old St Peter's. Papal accounts say the grant covered the former Byzantine exarchate, including Ravenna, and the duchy of Rome. Britannica notes that much of it was imperial territory "to which Pippin had no clear legal claim." Charlemagne and later Louis the Pious, in 817, confirmed the donation. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin] Britannica dates the Papal States from 756 to 1870. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin] (Some popular timelines give 752 for the donation; the documented dates are 754 for the promise and 756 for the grant.)

The answer to "why did a church get a state" is therefore a specific exchange, not a mystery. A Frankish dynasty wanted religious legitimacy; a papacy without an army wanted a protector against the Lombards and independence from Constantinople. Each gave the other what it lacked. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin]

The forged title deed

A second, older-sounding title soon appeared beside the real one. The Donation of Constantine claims to record the emperor Constantine granting Pope Sylvester I primacy over the great sees of Antioch, Alexandria, Constantinople and Jerusalem, together with the imperial palace in Rome and "the regions of the Western Empire." It was written by an unknown author in the eighth century, most likely in the 750s or 760s, the same decades as Pippin's grant; its earliest surviving manuscript is from the ninth century. Leo IX (1049–54) was the first pope to cite it in an official act, and later popes used it against emperors and kings. [cite:vt2_britannica_constantine]

Doubts were raised around the year 1000 in the circle of the emperor Otto III. In 1440 the humanist Lorenzo Valla proved the document a forgery by showing that its Latin was not the Latin of Constantine's century. [cite:vt2_britannica_constantine] The forgery matters to this volume for a narrow reason. The papacy's claim to temporal power rested in part, for several centuries, on a fabricated document, and the case against that document was made on evidence of language and date. The real foundation, Pippin's grant, stands on its own record; the forged one fell to a philologist.

Extent and government

The territory changed size often. At its fullest it covered most of modern Lazio, Umbria and the Marche and part of Emilia-Romagna, a band across the middle of Italy from the Tyrrhenian to the Adriatic. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin] During most of the fourteenth century the popes lived at Avignon, and Avignon and the surrounding Comtat Venaissin were added to their holdings. In Italy, local lords and cities slipped out of papal control, and on 29 April 1357 Cardinal Gil Álvarez Carrillo de Albornoz, sent as legate with a mercenary force, issued the Constitutiones Aegidianae, a single civil code for the state that stayed in force until 1816. Alexander VI and Julius II, the "warrior pope," reasserted papal control over autonomous fiefs by war, and Parma and Piacenza were annexed in 1512. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates]

The state was governed by clergy. The pope was an elective absolute monarch; the major provinces, the legations, were run by cardinal legates, and the central administration worked through congregations led by cardinals. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates] Before 1859 the state covered about 44,000 square kilometres, a thousand times the size of today's Vatican City, and a census of 1853 counted 3,124,668 inhabitants. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates]

Revolution and restoration, 1796–1850

The French Revolution broke the arrangement. Napoleon annexed the Marche to his Kingdom of Italy on 2 April 1808 and, on 17 May 1809, made Rome and the rest of the state into French departments. The Congress of Vienna restored the Italian territories in 1815 but not Avignon. The restored clerical government faced revolts in 1830 and 1848, aimed at what opponents saw as a corrupt administration run by priests, and Austrian troops put them down. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates]

The crisis of 1848–49 showed how far papal rule now depended on foreign armies. On 15 November 1848 Pius IX's chief minister, Pellegrino Rossi, was assassinated; on the night of 24 November the pope fled to Gaeta in the Kingdom of Naples. A Constituent Assembly elected by universal male suffrage on 21 January 1849 proclaimed the Roman Republic on 9 February 1849, and from late March it was led by a triumvirate of Carlo Armellini, Giuseppe Mazzini and Aurelio Saffi. Its constitution abolished the death penalty and guaranteed freedom of religion while leaving the pope his spiritual government. Pius IX appealed to the Catholic powers. French troops under General Oudinot landed at Civitavecchia on 25 April 1849, and after a siege entered Rome on 3 July 1849. Pius IX returned in April 1850. [cite:vt2_wiki_romanrep1849] [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates]

From then on a French garrison held Rome for the pope. In 1860 the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia conquered the eastern two-thirds of the state, much of it already in revolt; by November 1860 Bologna, Ferrara, Umbria, the Marche, Benevento and Pontecorvo had been annexed. Only Rome and Lazio remained. In March 1861 the first Italian parliament declared Rome the capital of a kingdom that could not yet enter it, because French troops stood in the way. [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates] How that ended is the subject of the next section. [scene:vt2_roman_question|The Roman Question, 1870–1929]

The record so far answers the first of this volume's questions in plain terms. The papacy held a state because a Frankish king granted one in 756 in exchange for religious legitimacy, because a forged document later lent that state an imperial pedigree, and because, from 1815 onward, Catholic powers were willing to defend it with their armies. When Austria and then France stopped doing so, the state fell. [cite:vt2_britannica_pippin] [cite:vt2_britannica_constantine] [cite:vt2_wiki_papalstates]
