The Arab Revolt, 1936–1939

The revolt that opened this period had a prelude. In November 1935 British forces killed Izz al-Din al-Qassam, and historians name his death as part of the background to what followed. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] The broader timing is also documented. The uprising of 1936 followed the sharpest rise in Jewish immigration under the Mandate, and it came in the same decade that produced the Irgun (1931) and, later, Lehi (1940). Each of these movements, Arab and Jewish, formed in reaction to the other side and to British policy. Naming that pattern explains when the armed movements arose. It excuses none of them, and their attacks on civilians are part of the record on every side.

The immediate trigger came on 15 April 1936. Palestinian Arabs killed two Jews near Tulkarm, Jewish retaliation followed, and riots broke out in Jaffa and Tel Aviv. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert] The sources do not agree on the first incident. The Jewish Virtual Library describes it as an attack on a Jewish bus and gives three Jewish dead, not two. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots]

The strike that followed did not begin at the top. On 19 April a newly formed Arab National Committee in Nablus called for a general strike, the withholding of taxes and a boycott of Jewish products. Local national committees across the country then united on 25 April as the Arab Higher Committee. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_mee_strike] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] The new committee was led by Hajj Amin al-Husseini, Mufti of Jerusalem. It declared a general strike with three demands: an end to Jewish immigration, an end to land sales to Jews, and an independent national government. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots] Another account dates al-Husseini's own strike call, as the committee's president, to 16 May 1936. [cite:ushmm_arab_revolt] Read together, the dates suggest that local committees moved first and the national leadership then took up the strike, though the sources do not explain the gap between them.

The committee's membership was its most striking feature. According to the list drawn from the Peel report, it brought together the leaders of rival clans and parties. Amin al-Husseini was president. Raghib al-Nashashibi represented the National Defence Party, Jamal al-Husseini the Palestine Arab Party, Yaqub al-Ghusayn the Youth Congress, Abd al-Latif Salah the National Bloc and Husayn al-Khalidi the Reform Party. Awni Abd al-Hadi of the Istiqlal was general secretary and Ahmed Hilmi Pasha treasurer. The coalition did not last long: Raghib al-Nashashibi left soon after it was formed. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_wiki_ahc]

The familiar description of the revolt as a spontaneous, leaderless outburst with no lasting result does not fit this record. The strike had a dated origin, a named committee with named officers, a written list of demands and a structure of local committees. Yet the opposite simplification fails too. A named leadership establishes an organized origin and an overall direction. It does not mean that every participant in three years of fighting took orders from al-Husseini, and the sources do not make that claim. The revolt's consequences, as this section and the next show, were large.

The general strike lasted about six months. Its end is recorded in a British document. On 12 October 1936 the General Officer Commanding issued a Special Order announcing that the strike and armed rebellion had been "called off unconditionally by the Higher Arab Committee." [cite:rs_rd4_ar_cojs_gocorder] The rulers of neighbouring Arab states had mediated its end. Different sources name them as the rulers of Transjordan, Iraq, Saudi Arabia and Yemen. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hcc] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots] Historians weigh the causes differently. Some sources stress the rulers' appeal, together with British concessions and the threat of martial law. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hcc] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots] Others also point to economic pressure, especially the approaching citrus harvest. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] The two explanations are compatible, and the evidence does not let us rank them.

The same Special Order gave the British army's own count of the cost of the strike period. It recorded 25 British soldiers killed and 120 wounded, 7 British police killed and 40 wounded, and 9 Palestine police killed and 64 wounded. It put the total cost of the rebellion so far at no fewer than 300 dead and 1,300 wounded. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_cojs_gocorder] This is the figure of one side, issued at the moment that side declared the rebellion over, and it should be read as such.

London's political answer was already in motion. A royal commission had been appointed in August 1936, during the strike, to investigate the causes of the revolt. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] [cite:wiki_peel_commission] Its report of July 1937 proposed partition, and its reception is told in [scene:s07a6|the Peel Commission].

Peel's proposal was followed by the revolt's second phase. On 26 September 1937, four gunmen described as followers of al-Qassam killed Lewis Andrews, District Commissioner for Galilee, as he left a service at Christ Church in Nazareth. His police bodyguard died of his wounds. Within 24 hours police had detained about 100 Palestinian Arabs. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_andrews] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked]

Britain then moved against the political leadership. It outlawed the Arab Higher Committee, around 30 September 1937, stripped al-Husseini of his offices, arrested members of the committee and deported several of them to the Seychelles. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_andrews] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_wiki_ahc] The sequence of al-Husseini's flight is told slightly differently in different sources. One account has him taking sanctuary in the Dome of the Rock on 17 July 1937 to avoid arrest, and then losing his positions after the district commissioner's killing in September. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] The research sources place his refuge in the Haram al-Sharif and his escape in the autumn of 1937. They disagree on where he went: Lebanon, according to Unpacked, or Syria, according to the Jewish Virtual Library. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_andrews] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] The account that sends him to Lebanon continues to Iraq, and historians document that he went on directing the revolt from exile. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] By Unpacked's account, he authorized attacks from abroad on Britons, on Jews, and on Palestinians considered collaborators. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] The record establishes that he continued to give direction. It does not itemize every order he sent.

With the leadership gone, the revolt moved into the countryside. Rebel bands set up their own administration there and taxed the peasants. The uprising peaked in the autumn of 1938, when rebels held large parts of the country, and was suppressed by the summer of 1939. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert]

Britain sent about 20,000 troops. Its measures included house demolitions, executions, the outlawing of the Arab Higher Committee, arrests and expulsions of activists, indefinite detention, collective fines on whole neighbourhoods, and the disarming of Palestinians. Matthew Hughes's 2019 study of the campaign groups these methods into eight categories. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert] The instruments mattered because of where they fell. Collective fines landed on neighbourhoods, not only on fighters, and indefinite detention held activists without a fixed term.

Executions give one measure of the campaign. Between 108 and 122 Palestinian Arabs were hanged in the 1930s. The reviewer of Hughes's study compares this with the 1,090 Kenyans hanged during the Mau Mau emergency of the 1950s. Two Jews were hanged. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes]

The revolt was also a civil conflict. Raghib al-Nashashibi, a former mayor of Jerusalem, favoured compromise with the British; the Mufti opposed it. Their rivalry produced hundreds of killings of Palestinians by Palestinians. The Nashashibi-aligned "peace bands" were raised with British and Jewish military and financial help to fight the rebels. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hughes_peacebands]

Hughes's study of the peace bands supplies the detail. By March 1938 Fakhri Nashashibi was organizing them from Lebanon. Fakhri 'Abd al-Hadi, a former rebel commander, returned to Palestine on 21 September 1938 with backing from the British consul in Damascus, Gilbert MacKereth. The money is documented. Britain paid band members £P6 a month. The insurgents paid their own men between 30 shillings and £P4. Both men were later killed: Husseini-backed gunmen killed Fakhri Nashashibi in Iraq in 1941, and 'Abd al-Hadi was murdered in 1943. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hughes_peacebands_full] The 1941 killing belongs to al-Husseini's exile, which continued into an alliance with Nazi Germany from 1941: he met Hitler and recruited for the Waffen-SS, a record set out in [scene:rd4_mufti_war|the Mufti's war]. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] It is his own documented record. The overwhelming majority of Palestinian Arabs had no part in it and no say over it.

The money ran both ways. The rebel bands taxed the villages they held. Britain paid the bands that fought them. And the general strike itself pushed the Yishuv toward a more self-reliant economy. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hughes_peacebands_full]

Jewish attacks on Arab civilians are part of the same record. In November 1937 the Irgun abandoned the policy of restraint and killed ten Arab civilians on the day later called "Black Sunday." After an attack on an Arab bus in April 1938, the British hanged Shlomo Ben-Yosef in June 1938. He was one of the two Jews hanged during the revolt. By Unpacked's count, at least 250 Palestinian Arabs had died at Irgun hands by the summer of 1939. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] The Irgun was formed in 1931, and its turn to reprisal belongs to the pattern the opening of this section described. Like the rebels' attacks on Jewish civilians, it is named here for what it was.

How many people died is the most contested question about the revolt. The figures come from different sources, count different things, and should be set out side by side.

For Arab deaths, the most detailed figure comes from Walid Khalidi's analysis of British statistics. He counts 3,832 Palestinian Arabs killed by the British and 1,200 killed in internal strife, 5,032 in all, with 14,760 wounded. Official British figures are lower and divided differently. They give over 2,000 Arabs killed in combat, 108 hanged and 961 killed by "gang and terrorist activities." Reference works round the total to about 5,000. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hcc] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert] The Jewish Virtual Library estimates roughly 5,000 dead, 15,000 wounded and 5,600 imprisoned. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots] Middle East Eye gives about 5,000 killed, 15,000–20,000 wounded and 5,600 imprisoned. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_mee_strike] At the high end, Unpacked (Noam Weissman, 2024), drawing on Oren Kessler, gives 5,000–8,000 Palestinian Arab dead and says about 20 percent of Palestinian Arab men were jailed at some point. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] The summary published by the Palestinian church committee states that over 10 percent of Palestinian Arab men aged 20 to 60 were killed, wounded, imprisoned or exiled. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hcc]

For Jewish and British deaths, the scholarly and reference figures cluster closely, and they are the record: roughly 400 to 415 Jews and 100 to 200 British. Ori Yehudai gives about 415 Jews and 100 British. The Jewish Virtual Library gives 415 Jewish deaths for 1936–39. Fred Strickert gives nearly 400 Jews and 200 Britons. Hughes's reviewer writes of "hundreds" of Jews. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_jvl_riots] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_ebsco_strickert] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] Much higher Jewish and British figures, attributed to the US Holocaust Memorial Museum, also circulate. They could not be verified, they conflict with every other source, and they are not used here.

The revolt also moved people. According to the review of Hughes's study, about 40,000 Christian Arabs and others left for Syria and Lebanon during the revolt. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes]

How to characterize the revolt is itself in dispute, and each reading rests on real evidence. In the Palestinian national reading, as Middle East Eye puts it, the revolt was "the most sustained armed resistance" against the Mandate, a national uprising crushed by brutal British repression. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_mee_strike] The demolitions, collective fines, hangings and detentions described above are its evidence. A critical reading, given by Unpacked, stresses that the rebels also turned on Palestinians, extorting and killing those they deemed disloyal, with leaders encouraging it. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_unpacked] Its evidence is the intra-Palestinian killing and al-Husseini's reported authorization of attacks on supposed collaborators.

Hughes's scholarship takes both seriously. He concludes that the peace bands "never won the war for the British." Collaboration, he finds, was driven more by survival, personal gain and feuds than by political conviction, and "Palestinian collaboration in the 1930s was too partial to damn the Arab revolt." [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hughes_peacebands_full] On that reading the internal violence was real and the uprising was still a national one. Neither fact cancels the other.

On the outcome, historians agree. The revolt failed militarily. The Palestinian leadership was left weak, divided and in exile. Military cooperation between Britain and the Zionist movement deepened, a story taken up in [scene:br_s01|Wingate's Night Squads and the Palmach]. And the general strike pushed the Yishuv toward a more self-reliant economy. [cite:rs_rd4_ar_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_shindler_hughes] [cite:rs_rd4_ar_hcc] The ledger of these years shows the sequence at a glance: the revolt in 1936, Peel's partition and transfer proposal in 1937 with the Arab Higher Committee objecting, and in 1939 the White Paper's limit on immigration, which the Zionist movement opposed.

Much remains unsettled. The death toll is the clearest gap: the Arab figures range from British official counts to 8,000, and the sourced Jewish and British figures run from about 400 to 415 and from 100 to 200, beside much higher figures that could not be verified. The sources do not settle the exact date of al-Husseini's flight or its first destination, or how much of the second phase he actually directed from abroad. Nor do they give firm totals for detentions or demolitions. The leadership that had called the strike in 1936 ended the revolt divided and in exile, just as London turned from partition to a policy of its own.
