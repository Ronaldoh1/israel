Who Coordinates What Gets Posted

A common assumption holds that pro-Israel content on social media is organic grassroots enthusiasm, and that any suggestion of coordination is conspiracy thinking. The opposite assumption, that every pro-Israel post is centrally directed, is just as common in other circles. The record for the years from 2009 to 2025 supports neither. It names a pollster and the organization that hired him, government ministries and the sums they spent, consulting firms and the contracts they registered with the United States Justice Department, the instructions sent to paid influencers, and a meeting attended by a head of government. It also documents an Israel-aligned campus organization caught running fake pages, and influence networks aimed against Israel. What follows sets out each piece in turn, with the weight each piece of evidence can bear.

A point of method comes first. Knowing where a talking point came from is one step; checking it against the record is another. A talking point that was written, tested and paid for is not false for that reason. Many are sincerely held by people who were never paid to repeat them, and some are accurate. The origin of a claim is not a verdict on it. That is why the coordination documented here sits beside, rather than replaces, the graded catalog of claims elsewhere in this library. The contracts tell the reader who chose a message and paid to spread it. They do not tell the reader whether the message is true.

Where the talking points come from. In 2009 The Israel Project commissioned the pollster Frank Luntz to test which language would win Americans' support.[cite:wiki_tip] The result, the Global Language Dictionary, was an internal document marked not for distribution. It was leaked to Newsweek.[cite:archive_luntz] Its rules read as a manual of persuasion rather than a statement of belief. Advocates were told not to pretend that Israel was without fault, and to concede a point before making their own. They were told to repeat the phrase "Iran-backed Hamas." They were told that "no matter what you're asked," they should "bridge to a productive pro-Israel message," and that Israel should be framed in "the language of America: democracy, freedom, security, and peace."[cite:luntz_pdf] The guide's own polling found that settlements were unpopular with the American public, even among Israel's supporters, and so it advised advocates to turn conversations toward peace.[cite:luntz_pdf]

The guide matters for two reasons. First, it shows that phrases a reader may meet as spontaneous opinion were, in at least this documented case, selected by survey for their effect on an American audience. Second, its own findings undercut the idea that support for Israel in the United States meant support for every Israeli policy: the polling located a weak point, settlements, and the advice was to steer around it rather than defend it. An earlier section traces how some of the guide's phrases still circulate [scene:s00a|how the story is taught]. The guide itself does not show that any given statement online came from it.

STOIC: a ministry, a firm, and fake accounts. The most concrete case of covert activity concerns STOIC, a political consulting and marketing firm based in Tel Aviv. According to the New York Times, which cited four Israeli officials and documents, Israel's Ministry of Diaspora Affairs allocated about $2 million in October 2023 and hired STOIC to run the campaign.[cite:toi_stoic] The Times reported that the operation ran fake accounts and three fake news sites aimed at members of the United States Congress, especially Black Democrats such as Hakeem Jeffries and Raphael Warnock, urging them to keep funding Israel's military.[cite:toi_stoic] [cite:opb_stoic] Hundreds of the accounts posed as Americans.[cite:opb_stoic] NPR's account of the same operation describes AI-generated content aimed at younger, progressive Americans, carrying pro-Israel messaging on the Gaza war.[cite:npr_stoic_ai_influence]

The operation was first identified by FakeReporter, an Israeli watchdog group that tracks misinformation. It was an Israeli organization, not a foreign critic, that found it, and it tied five websites to the operation.[cite:npr_stoic_ai_influence] The New York Times confirmed the reporting independently. The platforms then acted. Meta removed 510 Facebook accounts and 32 Instagram accounts that had posed as Jewish students, Black Americans and "concerned citizens."[cite:cyberscoop_stoic] OpenAI called STOIC a "for-hire Israeli threat actor."[cite:cyberscoop_stoic] Meta stated that it had deleted the accounts before they gained traction with real users, and both companies said the campaign won little engagement.[cite:npr_stoic_ai_influence] [cite:cyberscoop_stoic]

The reports do not agree in every detail, and the differences are worth setting out rather than smoothing over.

The central fact is disputed by the party named in it. The Ministry of Diaspora Affairs denied involvement.[cite:toi_stoic] Against that denial stands the Times's sourcing to four Israeli officials and documents, the watchdog's findings, and the two platforms' own enforcement actions. Criticism also came from an Israeli former diplomat: Michael Oren, a former ambassador, called the campaign damaging interference.[cite:toi_stoic] STOIC did not respond to interview requests.[cite:npr_stoic_ai_influence] The record therefore shows a covert operation with a named firm, a specific sum and a ministry identified by officials as its funder, caught by a watchdog and removed by the platforms. It does not show that the operation was the only one of its kind, or the largest, and the companies' own statements indicate it failed to reach many real people.

The budget behind the open campaign. STOIC was covert. The larger effort that followed was conducted largely in the open, under American disclosure law. In December 2024, during coalition negotiations, Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar secured an allocation of $150 million for global public diplomacy, hasbara in Hebrew. According to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, that was about twenty times previous hasbara budgets, and the ministry received the funds from May 2025.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara] An earlier section records a separate figure for 2026, a public-diplomacy budget of $730 million, and the history of the word hasbara.[cite:nuclearnews_730m_budget] The two figures belong to different years and come from different sources; this book does not have a basis for breaking either down line by line.

The paper trail: the foreign-agent filings. The details of how that money was spent in the United States became public through filings with the Justice Department under the Foreign Agents Registration Act, known as FARA. Every figure in the paragraphs that follow exists because that law compelled its disclosure.

The first track was run by Bridges Partners LLC, a Washington-based firm. It worked for Israel's Ministry of Foreign Affairs through an intermediary, Havas Media Group Germany.[cite:honestreporting_bridges_partners_fara] Its filings show it was hired to manage an influencer network code-named the "Esther Project," from June to November 2025, under a contract of up to $900,000.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara] [cite:wiki_esther_project_israel] The contract was itemized: $60,000 upfront for recruitment; $140,000 as five or six influencers began posting; up to $250,000 a month for influencer fees and production; and $50,000 for wrap-up.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara] HonestReporting's reading of the same filings found roughly $900,000 invoiced for an "Influencer Campaign," of which roughly $552,946 remained for payments to the influencers themselves once administrative and production costs were taken out.[cite:honestreporting_bridges_partners_fara] The campaign expected 75 to 90 posts. Dividing the money by the posts, analysts arrived at roughly $6,100 to $7,400 per post.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara] [cite:wiki_esther_project_israel] That calculation is the source of the figure of about $7,000 per post that circulates widely.[cite:globeistan_7000_per_post] [cite:honestreporting_bridges_partners_fara]

The second track was larger. Brad Parscale, formerly Donald Trump's campaign manager, registered his firm, Clock Tower X LLC, as a foreign agent for Israel under a reported $6 million contract for "strategic communications." It promised 100 ads or pieces of content a month, plus 5,000 variations, an output the Jewish Telegraphic Agency notes would likely require automated tools.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara] [cite:globeistan_7000_per_post] A related trip in 2025 was partly funded by a foreign-ministry contract worth $86,000.[cite:jta_esther_project_fara]

The money can be followed from its source to its effects, as far as the filings and the complaint they prompted allow.

What coordination looked like from the inside. A payment shows that someone was hired. It does not show what they were told to say. That second layer was documented by an investigation in Time magazine, which found private group chats in which influencers taking part in a pro-Israel campaign were guided on the specific language to use in posts on X, Instagram and TikTok, and were paid up to $4,250 per post.[cite:meo_israel_influencer_backfire] A separate case shows the mechanism from the side of someone who said no. A Jewish influencer reported receiving a confidential brief from a different campaign, offering $1,500 to promote specific talking points under a non-disclosure agreement. He declined to sign it.[cite:meo_israel_influencer_backfire] Refusal is part of the record too: the offer was made, and it was not universally accepted.

The effort reached the top of the Israeli government. In September 2025, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu met several American social media influencers in New York, specifically to enlist them in amplifying pro-Israel messaging in the United States.[cite:meo_israel_influencer_backfire] This was not a campaign run by a communications office several steps removed from political leadership; the head of government took part himself. The meeting's occurrence is documented. What was said in it, and whether every influencer present agreed to take part, is not in the public record these sources draw on.

The several payment figures in these reports, $1,500, up to $4,250, and roughly $6,100 to $7,400 per post, come from different campaigns and different documents. They should not be read as one price list, and the sources do not say whether the chats Time examined belonged to the Bridges Partners campaign.

The complaint and the open question. The firms registered. The influencers, as far as the filings show, did not. Public Citizen filed a formal complaint with the Justice Department's FARA Unit. It alleged that approximately 14 to 18 US-based influencers paid through the Bridges Partners contract were acting as unregistered agents of Israel's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, and it asked the department to require disclosure of their names and contracts.[cite:citizen_fara_complaint] The sources available to this book do not record a ruling on the complaint. Whether individual influencers paid through a registered firm must register themselves is the legal question it raises, and it remains open. There is also a naming question. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency noted that it is unclear whether the "Esther Project" has any link to the Heritage Foundation's similarly named Project Esther, which a later volume of this library examines.[cite:wiki_esther_project_israel] [cite:citizen_fara_complaint]

The disclosure itself says something about how the law works. The same statute that a later volume of this library shows going unapplied to AIPAC did apply to these contracts, which is the only reason the campaign is documented in such detail. Openly registered persuasion and covert fake accounts are different things, and the record contains both.

A campus organization and a takedown. The record also contains a case where a platform acted against an Israel-aligned group on its own authority. The Israel on Campus Coalition (ICC) is the main convening organization of pro-Israel campus groups. Two claims about it carry different weight and should be kept apart. The first is reported, and less independently verified: that ICC gathered information on pro-Palestinian students through informants inside Jewish and pro-Israel student organizations, and passed it to Israel's Ministry of Strategic Affairs.[cite:wiki_israel_campus_coalition] The second is more concretely documented. ICC secretly ran misleading Facebook pages and advertisements aimed at the Palestinian-American poet Remi Kanazi, and in 2018 Facebook itself removed the pages, citing its own policies against misrepresentation.[cite:wiki_israel_campus_coalition] That is a platform's enforcement action, not only an outside accusation. Neither claim shows that such tactics are unique to ICC among advocacy organizations, or that every campus group affiliated with it engaged in them; what is documented is attributed to its national organization.

The other direction. An account that documented only one side's influence operations would not be a fair account. Operations aimed against Israel are documented in the same record. An Iran-linked network ran fake profiles, bots and groups across WhatsApp, X, Telegram, Facebook, TikTok and Instagram. It spread disinformation, impersonated journalists and rabbis, and worked specifically to stoke tensions among Israelis themselves, including content aimed at Israel's LGBTQ+ community and at ultra-Orthodox Jews.[cite:calcalist_iran_social_media_network] A 2025 report by an independent research organization, titled "Conflict Amplified: Disinformation and Hate in the Israel-Hamas War," documented disinformation and coordinated hate campaigns from several sides of the conflict, not from one alone.[cite:isd_conflict_amplified_2025] These sources establish that both kinds of operation exist. They give no basis for saying that the Iran-linked operations and the Israeli-funded ones were of comparable size or reach, in either direction. How the platforms themselves policed this content is the subject of the next section [scene:s00a2|the platforms].

What the record shows, and what it leaves open. Taken together, the documents establish a funded and coordinated messaging apparatus. Its parts can be named: a pollster's tested vocabulary; a ministry's covert contract, which the ministry denies; a foreign ministry's budget and the registered firms it paid; private instructions to paid influencers; and a prime minister's personal recruitment. That is the familiar claim of pure organic enthusiasm answered by the record. The same record does not support the opposite claim, that every pro-Israel or pro-Palestinian post is centrally directed. Unpaid supporters exist alongside the paid campaigns, one influencer turned the offer down, and influence operations run against Israel as well.

Several questions remain unsettled. The Ministry of Diaspora Affairs' denial of the STOIC campaign stands against the New York Times's sources, and the published accounts differ on its target and its number of websites. The breakdown of the $150 million beyond the single Bridges Partners contract is not public in these sources, nor is the outcome of Public Citizen's complaint. What was said in the September 2025 meeting is unknown. The informant claim against ICC is reported but less independently verified. No source here measures the relative scale of the Israeli-funded and Iran-linked operations, or how many people any of them persuaded. The firms' names, the sums and the dates are documented. How much effect the money had is not.
