The Information War: The Bergson Group

It is tempting to imagine that American opinion about Palestine and about the catastrophe overtaking Europe's Jews formed on its own, from the news alone. The record of the war years shows something more deliberate. Alongside the established Zionist organizations, a delegation from the Irgun ran the most aggressive public campaign of the era.

Its leader was Hillel Kook, a nephew of Palestine's first Ashkenazi chief rabbi and a member of the Irgun. He arrived in the United States in 1940 and worked under an assumed name, "Peter Bergson" [cite:w_kook]. Over the following years he built a succession of committees, each with long lists of prominent sponsors drawn from Hollywood, Congress and the press: the Committee for a Jewish Army in 1941, the Emergency Committee to Save the Jewish People of Europe in 1943, and later the Hebrew Committee of National Liberation. The design was to move public opinion quickly and to do it outside the mainstream Zionist organizations [cite:w_kook].

The committees' signature tool was the full-page newspaper advertisement. They bought space in major papers and filled it with stark headlines about the destruction of European Jewry and demands for action. Paid space had a practical advantage: it bypassed editorial gatekeeping, putting the message in front of officials and the public on the same morning [cite:w_kook].

The group also staged events on a large scale. On 9 March 1943 a memorial pageant called We Will Never Die, written by Ben Hecht, produced by Billy Rose, directed by Moss Hart and with music by Kurt Weill, played twice in one night at Madison Square Garden before about 40,000 people. It then toured, including a performance in Washington attended by Eleanor Roosevelt, justices and members of Congress [cite:w_wwnd]. Seven months later, on 6 October 1943, about 400 Orthodox rabbis, in a march organized with the Bergson group, walked to the Capitol and the White House to plead for rescue. President Roosevelt did not receive them [cite:w_rabbismarch].

What did this pressure achieve? The group promoted a rescue resolution in Congress, and that congressional pressure, together with a Treasury Department report accusing the State Department of obstructing rescue, preceded Roosevelt's creation of the War Refugee Board by Executive Order 9417 in January 1944. The Board's rescue work is credited with saving many thousands of lives, though estimates of the number vary [cite:w_wrb]. The sequence is documented; how much weight to give each strand of pressure is a matter of judgment, and the record supports saying that the campaign was one of the forces that came before the decision, not that it alone produced it.

The Bergson group had enemies on more than one side. Mainstream Zionist leaders, including Rabbi Stephen Wise, opposed it as a rival. The FBI watched it as a foreign-linked operation, a story told in [scene:fa_s01|Foreign Agents Before There Was a State].

The campaign also changed its target. After 1944 the same machinery that had demanded rescue turned to campaigning for a Hebrew state and to supporting the Irgun's revolt against Britain. The Bergson operation thus shows both faces of the era's information war: a genuine rescue campaign during the Holocaust, and then a statehood campaign run with the same tools. Neither face fits the picture of opinion forming spontaneously. Both were organized and backed by named sponsors.
