Language and Ancestry: Two Different Questions

People sometimes ask which Jews are "Semitic Jews" and which are not. The question runs two separate things together. "Semitic" is the name of a family of languages, as [scene:s00b|the section on the word] sets out. Where a community's ancestors came from is a question for history and genetics. The answers to the two questions do not line up, and neither answers a third question: who is Jewish. Jewish law answers that one. A Jew is someone born to a Jewish mother or someone who has converted, and the movements differ on the details. [cite:fw_mjl_whoisjew] This section takes the first two questions in turn, then places every community from the map in the previous section on a single grid.

Start with language. Hebrew was the language of prayer and study, and for many centuries almost no Jewish community spoke it at home; everyday speech followed the region. [scene:ch01_four_words|Four words] Some of those everyday languages were Semitic. The Jews of Baghdad spoke Judeo-Arabic, Yemen's Jews spoke Judeo-Yemeni Arabic, Kurdish Jews spoke a Neo-Aramaic language, and Morocco's older Jewish communities spoke Judeo-Arabic or Judeo-Berber. [cite:jl_wiki_iraq] [cite:jl_wiki_judeo_yemeni] [cite:jl_ej_kurdistan] [cite:jl_wiki_toshavim] The Beta Israel of Ethiopia mostly spoke Amharic, or Tigrinya in Tigray, both of them Semitic languages, and they prayed in Ge'ez, another. [cite:jl_jvl_ethiopian] Many more of the languages were not Semitic. Ashkenazi Jews spoke Yiddish, a Germanic language, and Sephardi Jews spoke Judeo-Spanish, a Romance one. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] [cite:jl_jlp_judezmo] The Jews of Iran, Bukhara and the eastern Caucasus spoke Iranian languages: Judeo-Persian, Bukhori and Juhuri. [cite:jl_jlp_judeo_persian] [cite:jl_ela_bukhori] [cite:jl_ela_juhuri] Georgia's Jews spoke a Jewish form of Georgian and Greece's Romaniote Jews a Jewish Greek. [cite:jl_jlp_judeo_georgian] [cite:jl_wiki_yevanic] The Bene Israel spoke Marathi and the Jews of Kerala spoke Jewish Malayalam. [cite:jl_ej_bene_israel] [cite:jl_jlp_malayalam]

Ancestry is a separate question, and the most complete answer comes from a genome-wide study published in Nature in 2010 by Doron Behar and colleagues. They genotyped 121 people from 14 Jewish communities. [cite:an_behar2010_pdf] Most of the Jewish samples formed a tight cluster lying over the Druze and Cypriot samples, and the authors traced the origins of most diaspora communities to the Levant. [cite:an_behar2010_pubmed] Within that cluster they found two subgroups. One held the Ashkenazi, Moroccan and Sephardi Jews. The other held the Middle Eastern communities, Iranian and Iraqi Jews, and the Caucasus communities of Georgia and Azerbaijan. Yemenite Jews formed a compact cluster of their own, closest to Bedouin and Saudi samples. The ancestry component typical of Europeans was larger in Ashkenazi, Moroccan and Sephardi Jews than in the Middle Eastern communities. The paper gives no percentages for it. [cite:an_behar2010_pdf]

Two groups did not fit that pattern. The Beta Israel of Ethiopia and the Jews of India, both the Bene Israel and the Cochin Jews, clustered with the non-Jewish populations living around them, although the Bene Israel showed a clear link to the Levant on the male line. [cite:an_behar2010_pubmed] A 2016 study of the Bene Israel alone found both substantial Jewish and substantial Indian ancestry. The mixing happened within the last thousand years, the Jewish side came mostly through men, and the local side mostly through women. [cite:an_waldman2016]

Put side by side, the two answers show why the word "Semitic" cannot sort Jews by descent. The Beta Israel spoke Semitic languages, yet their genomes sit closest to their Ethiopian neighbours. More precisely, they sit closest to the Semitic-speaking Ethiopians rather than the Cushitic-speaking ones. [cite:an_behar2010_pdf] Ashkenazi Jews spoke a Germanic language, yet they carry Middle Eastern ancestry. The grid below places each community twice: down the side, by what the record shows about its ancestry; across the top, by whether its everyday language was Semitic.

The bottom three rows hold communities that the genetic studies cited here did not cover, or covered without settling anything. The Jews of Kaifeng in China most likely descend from Persian Jews who arrived in the twelfth century, and Israel requires their descendants to convert formally. [cite:jl_iranica_kaifeng] [cite:jl_wiki_kaifeng] The Abayudaya of Uganda began practising Judaism in 1919 as a religious movement; they do not claim descent from ancient Israel, and since 2002 their conversions have been overseen by the Conservative movement. [cite:jl_ijn_abayudaya] The Bnei Menashe of northeast India and the Lemba of southern Africa claim descent from ancient Israel. For the Bnei Menashe the claim is not established: one test was never published, and a second was not peer-reviewed. [cite:jl_eastmojo_bnei_menashe] Studies of the Lemba from 1996 and 2000 reported Middle Eastern markers on the male line. A 2014 reassessment found that such markers do not reliably predict Jewish ancestry, and most Lemba today are Christian. [cite:jl_ency_lemba] [cite:jl_tofanelli_2014]

The grid has limits, and they are marked on it. Communities drawn with an outline (Syrian, Kurdish, Bukharan, Italian and Romaniote Jews among them) are placed by their documented history alone, because no genome study of them was consulted for this book. The 2010 study is one study, though a large one, and work of this kind keeps being refined. The origins of the Beta Israel are still argued among historians: one view holds that they emerged as a group in Ethiopia between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries, another that they arrived far earlier. [cite:jl_kaplan_beta_israel] [cite:jl_mjl_ethiopian]

What the grid does not say matters as much as what it does. It does not rank Jewish communities, and it does not make any of them more or less Jewish. Every community on it includes converts and their descendants, and under Jewish law a convert is fully Jewish. [cite:fw_mjl_whoisjew] Israel's own institutions recognised the Beta Israel as Jews in 1973 and 1975, and declared the Bene Israel "Jews in every respect" in 1964. [cite:jl_jvl_ethiopian] [cite:jl_ej_bene_israel] Nor does ancestry decide a modern political question. The same genetic research finds that Palestinians and Jews of the Levant share substantial common ancestry, a point taken up in [scene:s00b|the section on "Semitic"]. Whether people speak a Semitic language, where a community's ancestors came from, and who is Jewish are three separate questions, and each has its own answer.
