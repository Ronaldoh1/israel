Where Palestinians Went: A Diaspora Older Than the Flight of 1948

Two Palestinian migrations are often run together. The first began in the 1870s, under Ottoman rule, and carried families to Latin America. The second was the flight of 1948, which carried refugees to neighbouring Arab lands. A common assumption holds that Latin America's Palestinian communities are made up of 1948 refugees who were welcomed with open doors. The familiar version ends there. The record contradicts both halves of it.

The emigration to the Americas began in the 1870s. Most of the early migrants were Christian families from Bethlehem, Beit Jala and Beit Sahour. Because they travelled on Ottoman papers, they were long called "Turcos." [cite:palquest_sv]

Chile holds the largest Palestinian community outside the Arab world, with estimates commonly running into the hundreds of thousands. In Santiago, the football club Palestino was founded in 1920; as Club Deportivo Palestino it plays in the national first division. [cite:w_palchile] [cite:w_palestino]

El Salvador's community is estimated at roughly 70,000 to 100,000 people, overwhelmingly of Bethlehem origin. It is described as the third-largest in Latin America, after Chile and Honduras. By the same account, El Salvador's Bethlehem-descended population is several times larger than Bethlehem's own population today. [cite:palquest_sv]

The doors were not open. In 1921 El Salvador declared Arabs and Chinese to be "pernicious" races, a documented act of exclusion. The country's elites looked down on the "Turcos" as peddlers. The community built its standing in commerce in spite of that discrimination. [cite:palquest_sv]

In time it reached the top of national politics. El Salvador has had two presidents of Palestinian descent. Antonio Saca won in 2004, defeating another candidate of Palestinian descent, Schafik Handal. Nayib Bukele won in 2019. His paternal grandparents emigrated from Jerusalem and Bethlehem in the early twentieth century, and his father became a well-known imam. [cite:palquest_sv] [cite:jweekly_bukele] Bukele himself is pro-Israel, a reminder that descent does not decide a politician's position. [cite:jweekly_bukele]

The refugees of 1948 were a different population, with a different date of departure and a different legal status. Roughly 700,000 people displaced that year went mainly to Jordan, including the West Bank, and to Lebanon, Syria and the Gaza Strip. There, from 1950, they were registered by UNRWA, the UN relief agency for the refugees described in [scene:ud_s01|the section on human rights].

Keeping the two apart matters for both histories. To treat the Ottoman-era emigrants as refugees of 1948 erases what they actually did: they left under an empire, met formal discrimination, and built communities that eventually produced heads of state. To treat the refugees of 1948 as part of an older, settled diaspora erases what happened to them: a displacement that remains unresolved. The two migrations began in the same land. They did not begin for the same reason.
