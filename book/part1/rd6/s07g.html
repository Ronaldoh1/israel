From AZEC to AIPAC: The American Organization After Statehood

A common account holds that the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, AIPAC, appeared suddenly in the 1950s as a new and independent lobbying group with no institutional past. The record shows something less abrupt and more interesting. The wartime body that had organized American Zionist political work did not dissolve when Israel was founded. It changed its name, spun off a separate lobbying committee, came under a federal foreign-agent order, and saw its lobbying work reorganized. Each of those steps is dated, and each concerned a specific organization. Keeping them apart is the only way to read the story accurately, because the most serious episode in it, the Justice Department's foreign-agent order of 1962, was aimed at one successor body at one time, not at the whole line of descent.

The starting point is the American Zionist Emergency Council, AZEC, which Abba Hillel Silver had built in 1943 as the political arm of the American movement during the war. In 1949, a year after statehood, it was renamed the American Zionist Council, the AZC, and registered as a tax-exempt nonprofit. [cite:azec_powerbase] This was a continuation, not a new founding: the same body under a new name and a new tax status. What the record cited here does not give is a full account of who led the Council after the change or how its internal structure shifted; it documents the continuity itself, not every change that came with it.

The Council's money raised a question that would follow it for more than a decade: where did it come from? That question explains the next step.

In 1953–54 Isaiah "Si" Kenen founded a new body, the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs, AZCPA. It was a separate entity, not tax-exempt, and not the same organization as the American Zionist Council, which had existed since 1949 under different leadership. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:wiki_american_zionist_council] According to these sources, Kenen created it because word had spread in Washington that the Eisenhower administration suspected Israel of funding the AZC and might force the Council to register as a foreign agent. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:wiki_american_zionist_council] Kenen had earlier worked in Israel's own foreign ministry and brought that experience of government relations to the new committee. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:britannica_aipac] In 1959 the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs was renamed the American Israel Public Affairs Committee. [cite:britannica_aipac] The fear that prompted its creation, a foreign-agent finding against the Council, was the same fear that came true against the Council itself nine years later.

The same years saw the first such order against an individual in this network. In 1952 the hosiery manufacturer and Truman donor Abraham Feinberg was ordered to register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act for publishing a bimonthly, Israel Speaks; he closed the publication rather than comply. That case is told with Feinberg's career in [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg]. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file]

The order against the Council came on 21 November 1962. The Internal Security Section of the Justice Department, under Attorney General Robert Kennedy, ordered the American Zionist Council to register as an agent of a foreign principal under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. The department alleged that the Council had received more than $5 million from the Jewish Agency over the preceding eight years. [cite:azc_fara_doj] The mechanism was the one Kenen had feared: the department's position was that money flowing from the Jewish Agency made the Council an agent of that foreign principal.

Two points of precision matter here. First, the order was directed at the 1949 successor, the American Zionist Council, seventeen years after AZEC's founding in 1943. It is not a finding about AZEC's activities from 1943 to 1949 under its original name, and it should not be read back across the whole lineage as one continuous violation. [cite:azc_fara_doj] Second, it was an allegation contained in an order, not a court judgment. The eight years it covered ran back to roughly the time Kenen had set up his separate committee, which is consistent with the account of why he did so, though the sources do not draw that connection themselves.

The Justice Department later withdrew its demand that the Council register. In 1963 the Council's lobbying functions were separated from it and placed in a restructured American Israel Public Affairs Committee, which operated solely on private American donations and on that basis asserted that it was not a foreign agent. [cite:aipac_wiki] The restructuring was deliberate and had a stated purpose: a funding structure that could support a claim of non-foreign-agency status from then on. [cite:aipac_wiki]

The sources describe AIPAC's beginning in two ways, and the difference is worth stating rather than smoothing over. One account dates AIPAC's emergence to 1963, when the Council's lobbying functions were spun off into it after the foreign-agent order. [cite:aipac_wiki] The other dates its founding to 1953–54, when Kenen created the American Zionist Committee for Public Affairs, and its present name to 1959. [cite:cjh_kenen_papers] [cite:britannica_aipac] Read closely, the two accounts refer to different things. The earlier date marks the creation of a distinct committee and the later name change; the 1963 date marks the moment the American Zionist Council's own lobbying work was moved into that committee and the committee was restructured to rest on American donations alone. Both are true of their own subject. What neither account in the material supplies is a full picture of how the Council and Kenen's committee related to each other between 1954 and 1963: who directed which lobbying, and how money moved between them, if it did.

Either way, the claim that AIPAC had no earlier institutional history does not survive the record. Its lineage runs through Kenen's 1953–54 committee and, from 1963, through the lobbying work of the Council that descended from the wartime AZEC. The 1953 founding date also coincides with the Anglo-American coup in Iran; the two are paired in [scene:s11|another chapter], which states plainly that no operational link between them is documented.

The gaps should be stated as plainly as the findings. The material does not say why the Justice Department withdrew its 1962 demand, or exactly when. It does not establish whether the $5 million allegation was ever tested, and it does not assess whether the 1963 restructuring fully answered the questions the order had raised; it documents what changed and the reason given for the change, not a legal judgment of its adequacy. Nor does it trace AZEC's place in the wider American network beyond these institutional steps; that network, including the 1962 finding as it concerned the Council rather than AZEC, is mapped in [scene:nw_s02|the network of the American build-up].
