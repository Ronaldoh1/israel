Who Actually Ran Palestine: The Seven High Commissioners

It is easy to write of the Mandate as if "the British government," a single faceless machine, had run it. In practice, Palestine was administered day to day by a succession of seven appointed High Commissioners, with acting commissioners filling the gaps between them. Each made decisions that can be dated and traced. Their tenures began in these years and ran on to 1948. They are set out together here because the office they held was one of the structures built at the Mandate's outset.

The first was Herbert Samuel, appointed in 1920. A British Cabinet minister and an advocate of Zionism, Samuel had written a memorandum in 1915 that influenced the Balfour Declaration itself. He was the first Jewish governor of Palestine since Roman times. He served until 1925, and the administrative and legal foundations he laid framed the work of every later administration. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel] [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

His successor, Field Marshal Herbert Plumer, came to the post from command of the British Expeditionary Force's Second Army and served from 1925 to 1928. He was named without consultation with the World Zionist Organization, yet he was regarded as committed to fairness toward Zionist aspirations. When he died in 1932, a Jewish Telegraphic Agency obituary described his tenure as "the only period when complete tranquility existed in Palestine." [cite:jta_plumer_obituary] [cite:toi_diaries_1929_officials] That is a contemporary judgment of relative calm, not a claim that his three years were free of tension.

Sir John Chancellor followed, from December 1928 to September 1931. His tenure opened amid what was described as "tremendous tension," beginning with a dispute at the Western Wall on Yom Kippur 1928. It is remembered above all for the Hebron Massacre of August 24, 1929. Yet Chancellor was not in the country when it happened. He was on home leave in England during the critical weeks of July and August 1929. Harry Luke served as acting High Commissioner throughout, and Chancellor returned to Jerusalem only on August 29, five days after the killings. [cite:toi_diaries_1929_officials] [cite:encyclopedia_hc_palestine] His absence during the acute crisis, and his responsibility as head of the administration whose policies shaped it, are separate facts. Both stand.

Arthur Wauchope held the office from 1931 to 1938, through years of rising Jewish immigration and the outbreak of the Arab Revolt of 1936 to 1939. Historians generally regard him as more sympathetic to Zionist settlement than his successors, and his approach has been called relatively conciliatory. He resigned as the revolt intensified. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

Harold MacMichael, who served from 1938 to 1944, governed Palestine as the 1939 White Paper was put into effect and through the early years of Jewish paramilitary resistance. In 1944 Lehi, also known as the Stern Gang, ambushed his motorcade in an attempt to kill him. He survived, wounded; his wife was seriously injured. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

The shortest tenure, and the one most often left out of popular accounts, belonged to Field Marshal John Vereker, 6th Viscount Gort. He served from November 1944 to November 1945 and retired because of ill health. He died the following year, in 1946. [cite:wiki_john_vereker_gort] His brevity in office is itself the notable fact; the record does not attribute major policy shifts to him.

The last was Alan Cunningham, from 1945 to 1948. He presided over the most violent period of Jewish paramilitary resistance, including the bombing of the King David Hotel, and over the chaotic British withdrawal from Palestine in May 1948. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

Naming all seven matters because twenty-eight years of rule did not run on a single setting. Each administration made its own choices within the broader policy framework: how firmly to enforce immigration limits, how to respond to violence coming from more than one direction, even whether to be present or on leave when a crisis broke. [cite:encyclopedia_hc_palestine] No one man's character explains the Mandate's outcome; the individuals worked inside a much larger structure. But that structure was carried out by people, and the record of who was in charge, and when, belongs in any accurate account.
