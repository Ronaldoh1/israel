## Prelude · The Board Before the Game (1694–1896)

**Summary.** What actually existed before political Zionism did. The Prelude opens in the City of London and the founding of the Bank of England in 1694, when 1,268 subscribers lent the Crown £1.2 million at 8% to fight France. It follows the wars of 1789–1815, the Ottoman order that governed Palestine without internal borders, the London house of Rothschild and the 1846 pamphlet behind its most famous legend, the papal loans, the Protestant restoration lobby, the Ottoman reforms and the Land Code of 1858 that made deeds sellable, the religious debate over a Jewish state, the thinkers before Herzl, the Jews already in Palestine, the Ottoman bans of 1882, Edmond de Rothschild's colonies and his refusal of Herzl, every branch of the family checked, Baron de Hirsch's Argentine colonies, and Dreyfus. It ends with the board as it stood in 1896.

**Script.**

Every story about Israel starts somewhere. Most start in 1948, or 1917, or with Herzl in 1896. This book starts in 1694, in London, with a loan.

On 27 July 1694, a royal charter created the Bank of England. It was a company through which 1,268 private subscribers lent the English Crown £1.2 million at 8% to fight a war against France. From that day, a state could borrow on a regular footing, and the lenders who served it could become very powerful. The Bank sits in the City of London, a financial district of about one square mile with privileges older than Magna Carta. At its 2013 elections, about 9,000 residents were outvoted by a business vote some 32,000 strong.

Why start there? Because the question this part asks is simple: before Zionism existed, who could actually act on Palestine, and with what tools? Money is one of those tools.

Palestine at that time was a set of Ottoman districts. The Ottoman Empire had governed the Arab Middle East since 1516, with no internal borders. Its people were mostly Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians, with old Jewish communities in Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias.

Then come the Rothschilds, and the book is careful here. The London house was founded by Nathan Mayer Rothschild, who lived from 1777 to 1836. The most famous story about him, that he made a killing on the news of Waterloo, can be traced to a pamphlet published in 1846, ten years after his death, written to stir hostility toward Jews. So the book separates the documented bank from the legend. It also shows the family lending to the Pope himself in the 1830s, and how the family keeps its wealth private: in 2023, its holding company bought out the last outside shares of its Paris bank and took it off the stock exchange.

Christians come next. Long before Zionism, British Protestants lobbied for a Jewish "restoration" to the land, as prophecy. And the Ottomans changed the rules of the ground itself. The Land Code of 1858 decided who could hold a deed and who could buy it. That made land sellable, often by distant owners, over the heads of the farmers working it.

Inside Judaism there was an older debate: could people bring about the return themselves, or was that forbidden until the Messiah? That argument still runs today. Then, from the 1830s to the 1880s, a handful of thinkers turned prayer into a program: rabbis like Kalischer and Alkalai, and then Pinsker after the pogroms of 1881. In 1836 Kalischer even wrote to Amschel Rothschild in Frankfurt, a man he'd never met, asking him to help settle the land.

Here's what many people don't expect. From June 1882, the Ottoman government forbade foreign Jews to enter and buy land. And the Arabs of Palestine objected from the start. The colonies grew through the gaps in those rules.

Their patron was Edmond de Rothschild in Paris. He rescued the failing colonies from 1883 and poured money into them, reported in 1934 at over $60 million. That's about $2 billion in today's dollars. But when Herzl came to him in 1896 asking him to back a political movement, Edmond said no. The book checks every branch of the family: Paris, London, Vienna, Frankfurt, Naples. Outside Edmond, the documented gifts before 1948 are small, about $1 million in today's dollars.

He wasn't the only financier either. Baron de Hirsch founded a company in London in 1891 to settle Jews in Argentina. By 1896 about 6,757 colonists lived there.

And then a French court convicted a Jewish officer, Alfred Dreyfus, of treason, on forged evidence. Herzl watched. In 1896 he published Der Judenstaat, The Jewish State. That's where the Prelude ends: the board is set, and the game is about to begin.

## Stage One · Conceive (1896–1917)

**Summary.** How, in twenty-one years, a pamphlet became a working structure. Stage One covers Ottoman Palestine as it was, the Basel Congress of 1897 and its opponents, the Zionist Organization's machinery, Herzl's search for any power that would grant a charter, and the movement's early knowledge of the Arab population. It follows the movement's bank (the Jewish Colonial Trust, 1899), its land fund (the JNF, 1901), the Anglo-Palestine Company (1902), the East Africa offer of 1903 and its rejection in 1905, the Second Aliyah, the Palestine Office, the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, who bought land from whom, the newspaper Filastin, the American machine of Hadassah, Brandeis and the ZOA, the war years, NILI and the Jewish Legion. It ends with five dates, all before 1922, that test the claim that the state grew out of the Holocaust.

**Script.**

In 1896 a journalist in Vienna named Theodor Herzl published a pamphlet called The Jewish State. It weighed two places for a Jewish homeland: Palestine and Argentina. Twenty-one years later there was a bank, a land fund, an office in Jaffa, and a promise from the British Empire. This stage asks how that happened, who built it, with whose money, and against whom.

Start with the country Herzl named first. It had no border and no government of its own. It was Ottoman land, run from Jerusalem, and most of its people were Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians. From the 1880s the Ottomans restricted Jewish immigration and land purchase, though unevenly.

The movement has an exact birthday: the First Zionist Congress, in Basel, Switzerland, from 29 to 31 August 1897. Herzl convened it. And it had opponents from day one: German rabbis, the Jewish socialist Bund, and the thinker Ahad Ha'am.

Within two years the Zionist Organization had real machinery. On 20 March 1899 it incorporated a bank in London, the Jewish Colonial Trust, owned a pound at a time by small shareholders. In 1901 it founded the Jewish National Fund, to buy land and never resell it. In 1902 the bank set up the Anglo-Palestine Company inside Palestine, with about £50,000 in capital. That company later became Bank Leumi.

Herzl wanted a charter, legal backing from a great power. He tried the Ottoman Sultan, the German Kaiser, Britain, anyone who would sign. In 1903, after the Kishinev massacre in Russia, Britain offered land in East Africa, and the Sixth Zionist Congress voted to investigate it. Two years later, in 1905, the Seventh Congress rejected it. Palestine was chosen, by the movement's own vote, against a real alternative.

Did the founders think Palestine was empty? The record says no. In 1899 Yusuf al-Khalidi, the former mayor of Jerusalem, wrote asking that the movement leave Palestine in peace. Herzl answered him. They knew people lived there.

Then the people came. The Second Aliyah, from 1904 to 1914, was small beside the more than 2.5 million Jews who left Eastern Europe in those decades, most of them for America. But it founded the institutions of the later state. By 1908 the movement had a Palestine Office in Jaffa, a land company and named purchasing agents. Its first big land test came in the Jezreel Valley, and an Ottoman governor tried to block the al-Fula sale in 1910. Palestinians resisted from the first large purchase.

And there was a Palestinian public. A newspaper called Filastin, "Palestine," began publishing in Jaffa in 1911.

Across the ocean, an American machine took shape: Hadassah, the women's Zionist organization, in 1912; the lawyer Louis Brandeis; the Zionist Organization of America. And in 1909 Oxford University Press published the Scofield Reference Bible, whose notes taught millions of American Christians to read modern events as prophecy.

Then war. When the Ottomans entered the First World War, Palestine became a front. During it, a Jewish spy ring called NILI worked for the British, and Jewish soldiers served in the Zion Mule Corps and the Jewish Legion. So the Balfour Declaration wasn't simply a gift; there were services rendered.

The stage ends with a test. Many people believe the demand for a Jewish state grew out of the Holocaust. That's a claim about order, and the calendar can check it. Herzl's pamphlet in 1896. The Basel Program in 1897. The Balfour Declaration in 1917. The Mandate in 1922. All before Hitler took power in 1933. The Holocaust shaped what came later. But the plan came first.

## Stage Two · Secure (1917–1922)

**Summary.** How a British letter with no legal force was made secure within five years. Stage Two sets the three wartime promises for one land side by side (Hussein–McMahon, Sykes–Picot and Balfour) and asks why the letter went to Walter Rothschild. It follows Allenby's martial law in Jerusalem, the Faisal–Weizmann Agreement of January 1919 and its condition, the Paris peace conference and the shelved King–Crane report, the legal route by which a letter became international law, the Palestinian national movement's congresses, the Third Aliyah, the first riots and the founding of the Haganah, the Keren Hayesod, the Mufti, Cairo 1921 and Transjordan, the Churchill White Paper, the Mandate's text, the first American campaigns, Congress's 1922 endorsement, the council that never sat, and the High Commissioners.

**Script.**

On 2 November 1917, Britain made a promise about a country it didn't yet govern. Arthur Balfour, the Foreign Secretary, signed a letter favouring "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine. When he signed it, Palestine was still Ottoman. British forces took Beersheba on 31 October. Jerusalem surrendered only on 9 December.

The letter mentioned the "existing non-Jewish communities," roughly 700,000 Muslim and Christian Arabs, about 90% of the people. They weren't asked. And the letter promised them civil and religious rights, not political ones.

It wasn't Britain's only promise. In letters of 1915 and 1916, Sir Henry McMahon pledged British support for Arab independence to Sharif Hussein of Mecca. In May 1916, the Sykes–Picot Agreement divided the same lands with France. Three promises, one land.

Why did Britain care? It started with a canal. The Suez Canal opened in 1869. Britain bought Egypt's shares in 1875 and occupied Egypt in 1882. Palestine guarded the canal's approach.

Then the address. The letter went to "Lord Rothschild" at 148 Piccadilly: Lionel Walter Rothschild. Its wording began with a draft formula Walter sent Balfour on 18 July 1917, after months of drafting with Zionist leaders. He was president of the English Zionist Federation. The final text was negotiated and written by others. The book checks whether Walter paid for that place and finds no documented money from him. What's documented is his name, his title and his role.

Then a short agreement many people quote. On 3 January 1919, in London, Emir Faisal and Chaim Weizmann signed a deal in which Faisal accepted Jewish immigration and the Balfour Declaration. But Faisal added a condition in his own hand: it held only if the Arabs got their independence. They didn't. In 1920 France crushed Faisal's kingdom in Syria at Maysalun. So the agreement people cite as Arab consent was conditional, and the condition failed.

At the Paris peace conference in 1919, an American commission, King–Crane, recommended against the Zionist program after asking people in the region what they wanted. Its report was shelved.

So how did a letter become law? Through the League of Nations. The Mandate for Palestine, approved in 1922, wrote the Balfour promise into its text and created a Jewish Agency to work with the government. It named no equivalent body for the Arab majority.

Palestinians organized. National congresses met from 1919, sent delegations and demanded elections. Britain offered a Legislative Council in 1922, with ten officials and twelve elected members, but under rules that ruled out ending the national home. Arabs boycotted it, and it never sat.

Violence came early. In April 1920, during the Nabi Musa festival in Jerusalem, riots broke out. Riots came again in Jaffa in May 1921. Between those two outbreaks the Yishuv founded the Haganah, its defence organization. British inquiries named the causes plainly: Arab fears of being displaced and of the national home.

The movement built its money machine too. On 24 December 1920, in London, it founded the Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund. In 1921 Weizmann and Albert Einstein toured America raising money for it.

Britain also built Muslim institutions. Amin al-Husseini became Mufti of Jerusalem in May 1921 and head of the Supreme Muslim Council in 1922. At the Cairo conference of March 1921, Britain chose Abdullah to rule Transjordan, which was later exempted from the national-home clauses. The Churchill White Paper of June 1922 narrowed the promise: a national home in Palestine, not Palestine as a national home.

And America signed on early. On 21 September 1922, President Harding signed a joint resolution of Congress favouring a Jewish national home in Palestine, twenty-six years before 1948.

In five years, a letter became a legal order, with a British government in Jerusalem to carry it out.

## Stage Three · Build (1922–1936)

**Summary.** What was built in the fourteen years after the Mandate, by whom and on what terms, and what was left unbuilt. Stage Three follows the Histadrut and "Hebrew labour," the Rutenberg electricity concession, the censuses of 1922 and 1931, the Mandate's completion in law, the Revisionists, the murder of Jacob de Haan, the Fourth and Fifth Aliyot, the enlarged Jewish Agency of 1929 that brought in non-Zionist money, the Hebrew University and Brit Shalom, the Mandate economy, the Western Wall riots of August 1929, Palestinian politics from congresses to parties, the fall of Weimar, the Passfield White Paper and its reversal, land and labour, the armed groups, the American Palestine Committee, the Jews of Baghdad and Constantine, the Haavara transfer agreement, the Arlosoroff murder, the Nuremberg Laws, al-Qassam, and Britain's last offer before the revolt.

**Script.**

At the end of 1922, the promise of 1917 was law. But on the ground, the Jewish community was small. Britain's first census that year counted 757,182 people in Palestine. 83,794 were Jews, about 11%. This stage asks what got built in the next fourteen years, and what didn't.

The answer is institutions, not speeches. Start with labour. In December 1920 in Haifa, two labour parties founded the Histadrut, the General Federation of Hebrew Workers. It was open only to Jews. It had about 8,400 members in 1922, in a country with roughly 50,000 Arab workers. It ran its own bank, its own building company, and it controlled the Haganah, which then had only a few hundred volunteers. Its slogan was "Hebrew labour": Jewish employers should hire Jewish workers. That built a separate Jewish economy, and it also shut Arab workers out of it.

Then electricity. On 12 September 1921 the Mandate government signed a concession with Pinhas Rutenberg to electrify the country. His grid followed the map of Jewish settlement. That's a power line in both senses.

The numbers moved fast. By the second census, in 1931, Jews were 17% of the population, Muslims 73%, Christians 9%. Immigration rose with the Fourth Aliyah of the mid-1920s and the Fifth Aliyah of the 1930s. America's immigration quotas of 1924, by one account, turned many Jewish emigrants toward Palestine.

The movement needed bigger money, so it widened its circle. In 1929, at Zurich, the Jewish Agency was enlarged to take in non-Zionists: wealthy American Jews who would fund building in Palestine without signing up to a state.

Not everyone was on board. Inside the movement, Ze'ev Jabotinsky's Revisionists demanded more, faster, on both banks of the Jordan. Outside it, an Orthodox Jewish community that had lived in the land for generations opposed Zionism. Its spokesman, Jacob de Haan, was shot dead in Jerusalem on 30 June 1924. On 1 April 1925 Arthur Balfour opened the Hebrew University on Mount Scopus, and the same year a small group called Brit Shalom argued for one country shared by both peoples.

Then violence. In August 1929, a dispute over the Western Wall in Jerusalem spread into riots. In Hebron and Safed, Jews were massacred. British investigators again pointed to Arab fears over land and immigration. In 1930 the Passfield White Paper proposed limiting immigration and land sales. Under Zionist pressure, Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald's letter of 1931 explained it away. A policy adopted, then reversed in a year.

And now Germany. The Weimar Republic collapsed, and Hitler took power in 1933. On 25 August 1933, Zionist bodies signed a deal with the Nazi government's economics ministry: the Haavara, or transfer agreement. It let German Jews move part of their capital to Palestine as German goods. It was fiercely debated, because Jews elsewhere were boycotting Germany. On 16 June 1933, the labour leader Chaim Arlosoroff, who had pushed the deal, was murdered on the beach at Tel Aviv. On 15 September 1935 the Nuremberg Laws wrote Nazi persecution into statute.

Palestinians kept organizing: from congresses to parties. A Haifa preacher, Shaykh 'Izz al-Din al-Qassam, took a small armed band into the hills, and on 20 November 1935 British police killed him near Jenin. Five weeks later Britain made one last political offer, a legislative council. It went nowhere.

So what did fourteen years build? A Jewish economy, a labour federation with a bank and a militia, a power grid, a university, and a pipeline of money. What was left unbuilt? Any shared government, and any Palestinian institution Britain would recognize. Both facts matter for 1948.

## Stage Four · Pivot to America (1936–1945)

**Summary.** How the centre of the Palestine question moved, in under a decade, from a British administration in Jerusalem to Washington. Stage Four covers the Arab Revolt of 1936–39, the Peel Commission's first partition map and its "exchange of population," partition dropped in 1938–39, Évian and Bermuda and the closing doors, Britain training the Jewish fighters who would later fight it, Aliyah Bet's clandestine fleet, Kristallnacht, the Vatican's authority and Pius XII's wartime record, the White Paper of 1939 and the ships it turned away, Lehi's offer to Germany and the killing of Lord Moyne, the Mufti in Berlin, the Bergson Group, the Foreign Agents Registration Act, Wannsee, Biltmore's call for a commonwealth, how the six million are counted, the American Zionist Emergency Council, the 1944 platforms, Roosevelt's hidden decline and the choice of Truman, Ibn Saud at Great Bitter Lake, and Labour's record.

**Script.**

In 1936, Palestine was a British question, decided in London and Jerusalem. By 1945, it was an American one. This stage tells how the centre moved across the Atlantic.

It begins with a revolt. On 15 April 1936, two Jews were killed near Tulkarm. Within ten days a general strike was called, and local committees united into the Arab Higher Committee. The Arab Revolt ran until 1939, and Britain crushed it.

Britain's answer was a royal commission. The Peel Commission, appointed in August 1936, reported in July 1937. It drew the first partition map: a small Jewish state, a larger Arab area joined to Transjordan. And beside the map it recommended something else: an "exchange of population," meaning moving Arabs out of the Jewish state. That's where the idea of transfer enters the official record. Both sides refused the plan, and by 1939 Britain dropped partition.

Now Europe. In the summer of 1938, delegates from 32 countries met at Évian in France to discuss Jews fleeing the Nazis. Almost none would take more. The doors were closing. Four months later, on the night of 9 to 10 November 1938, Kristallnacht: synagogues burned and Jewish shops smashed across Germany, Austria and the Sudetenland.

Here's a fact many people don't know. In that same summer of 1938, Britain was training Jewish fighters in Palestine, in units like the Special Night Squads, to fight the Arab revolt. The army of 1948 wasn't entirely self-made. Britain trained some of the men who would later fight it. At the same time, the Haganah ran a clandestine fleet, Aliyah Bet, bringing immigrants in past British limits.

Then Britain turned. The White Paper of May 1939 capped Jewish immigration at 75,000 over five years and promised an independent Palestine with an Arab majority. For Jews trying to escape Europe, the limit had a cost, and the book follows the ships that were turned away.

The war years also hold an uncomfortable record. A document of 11 January 1941, found in German embassy files in Ankara, records an offer by the Zionist group Lehi to fight alongside Germany against Britain. In November 1944 Lehi killed the British minister Lord Moyne in Cairo. On the other side, the Mufti, Hajj Amin al-Husseini, went into exile and ended up in Berlin, working with the Nazi regime. The book tells both, without softening either.

On 20 January 1942, at Wannsee, fifteen officials met for about ninety minutes to coordinate the murder of Europe's Jews. And four months later, in May 1942, at the Biltmore Hotel in New York, the Zionist movement changed its goal from a "national home" to a Jewish commonwealth. The Holocaust and the demand for a state ran side by side from then on. The book also explains how the six million are known: historians like Raul Hilberg and Lucy Dawidowicz, who arrived at 5,933,900 from birth and death records, and the perpetrators' own paperwork.

Now America. In 1938 Congress passed the Foreign Agents Registration Act, requiring people who work for foreign principals to register. In 1940 an Irgun member, Hillel Kook, arrived and worked as "Peter Bergson," running full-page ads for rescue. In August 1943 the main American Zionist groups formed the American Zionist Emergency Council to coordinate their political work. By 1944, both party platforms backed a Jewish commonwealth.

And then the presidency. On 28 March 1944 a Navy cardiologist found serious heart disease in Franklin Roosevelt. That year the Democrats replaced Vice President Henry Wallace with Harry Truman. On 14 February 1945, on his way home from Yalta, Roosevelt met King Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia aboard the USS Quincy, and on 5 April he wrote promising no decision on Palestine without consulting the Arabs. A week later, Roosevelt was dead. Truman was president.

## Stage Five · Execute (1945–1948)

**Summary.** The three years in which the plan became a state. Stage Five follows Truman's arrival and his own words, the July 1945 meeting in Rudolf Sonneborn's New York apartment that founded the arms network, Bevin's Palestine policy, the Anglo-American Committee, the Jewish Resistance Movement, Black Sabbath and the King David Hotel bombing, the Jewish Agency's partition map, the White House, Britain's handover to the UN, Moscow's early yes, UNSCOP and the Arab boycott, the warnings of the State Department, the Pentagon and the new CIA, the Americans who objected, Resolution 181, who spoke for the Palestinian Arabs, acceptance and rejection, the Jews of the Arab world after the vote, Golda Meyerson's 1948 tour, the arms pipelines including Czechoslovakia, Plan Dalet, the trusteeship retreat, the massacres and how the record changed, the fall of the mixed towns, King Abdullah's talks, independence and the Nakba, recognition, the Arab states' war, the military balance, one army from three militias, and the refugees.

**Script.**

In the summer of 1945, the war in Europe was over and the full scale of the Holocaust was becoming public. Palestine was still British. Three years later, there was a State of Israel and some 700,000 Palestinian refugees. This stage shows how, step by step.

Start in Washington. Harry Truman became president on 12 April 1945, after just eighty-two days as vice president, with no foreign-policy experience. One week before he died, Roosevelt had promised Ibn Saud in writing that nothing hostile to the Arabs would be done without consultation. Truman inherited that promise.

The same July, in New York, David Ben-Gurion met a group of wealthy American Jews in the apartment of Rudolf Sonneborn. They built an arms network. A common belief says Israel's weapons came only after May 1948. In fact the network was buying and shipping machinery and arms from 1945.

Britain had a new Labour government and a Foreign Secretary, Ernest Bevin, who wanted out without a war with the Arab world. He set up an Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry in November 1945. Meanwhile, the three Jewish armed groups, the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi, joined forces against Britain. On 29 June 1946, Britain struck back in Operation Agatha, the "Black Sabbath," arresting the Yishuv's leaders. On 22 July 1946, the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, the British headquarters. 91 people were killed: British, Arab and Jewish.

In 1947 Britain handed the problem to the United Nations. A special session opened on 28 April. And here's a surprise: Moscow said yes first. In May 1947, the Soviet delegate Andrei Gromyko told the UN the Soviet Union would support a Jewish state, or partition. A UN committee, UNSCOP, toured the country that summer. The Arab Higher Committee boycotted it, leaving an empty chair.

Inside the US government, the warnings were loud. On 22 September 1947 Loy Henderson, the State Department's top Middle East official, wrote that partition was "not workable." The Pentagon and the brand-new CIA warned too, about oil, the Soviets and war. Some Americans objected publicly, like the American Council for Judaism, founded in 1942. Truman overruled them.

On 29 November 1947, the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 181, partition, after a pause and intense lobbying of small states. The Jewish Agency accepted. The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected it. The book warns against the one-line summary, "Israel accepted, the Arabs rejected and started the war," and shows the two escalations in their fuller form.

A civil war followed. Money was the Agency's first shortage, so in early 1948 Golda Meyerson, later Golda Meir, toured America and raised about $50 million for arms. Weapons came through two pipes: the American network and a government, Czechoslovakia, with Soviet consent.

On 10 March 1948, the Haganah issued Plan Dalet, its operational plan for taking and holding territory. Historians still argue over what it meant for Arab villages, and the book sets out that argument. Then Washington wobbled: in March 1948 the US proposed a UN trusteeship instead of partition.

April brought massacres. At Deir Yassin, Irgun and Lehi fighters killed villagers. Days later, Arab fighters ambushed a Hadassah medical convoy and killed doctors and nurses. Tantura came in May. The book shows how the record of each changed as new evidence surfaced. The mixed towns, Haifa and Jaffa, fell, and their Arab populations fled or were driven out.

On Friday 14 May 1948, at four in the afternoon, Ben-Gurion read the declaration of independence in the Tel Aviv Museum. The United States recognized the state eleven minutes later, by Truman's decision alone, not by Congress. The Mandate ended at midnight. On 15 May, Arab armies crossed in. That same war was, for Palestinians, the Nakba.

## Stage Six · Consolidate and Account (1948–1950s)

**Summary.** What became of the war's facts once the shooting stopped, and the accounts the founding left. Stage Six covers the UN mediator Count Bernadotte's two plans, Truman's retreat and threat not kept, the donor Abraham Feinberg, the barring of refugees' return, who would govern Arab Palestine, the Arabs who stayed under military government, the Absentees' Property Law, Resolution 194 and the refugee count, the Rhodes armistices, the deferred constitution, Syria's offer of 1949, Lausanne, UN admission and Jerusalem, where the refugees went, the Jews of the Arab world after 1948, mass immigration, the Yemenite children affair, the Law of Return and the Nationality Law, AZEC to AIPAC, paying for the new state, how the flight was explained, how the case was made to Americans, what money did to policy, the full ledger of 1899–1949, the defence budgets, the American network, seven founding narratives checked, and the whole sequence from a bank to a state.

**Script.**

On 20 May 1948, six days after Israel declared independence, the UN appointed a mediator: Count Folke Bernadotte, head of the Swedish Red Cross. The war he was sent to stop was still raging. This stage asks what happened once the shooting stopped, and what accounts the founding left to settle.

Bernadotte proposed two plans that summer, including the return of refugees. In September 1948 he was assassinated in Jerusalem by the Jewish group Lehi. His plans died with him.

At the end of 1948, inside the lines Israel held, its statisticians counted 716,700 Jews and 156,000 non-Jews. Beyond those lines, some 700,000 Palestinians were refugees. On 11 December 1948 the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 194, by 35 votes to 15 with 8 abstentions. Its eleventh paragraph said refugees wishing to return and live in peace should be allowed to. Israel barred their return, and the book shows how that line hardened from 1948 to 1956. Truman pressed Israel on refugees and territory, then didn't follow through. The book traces one donor in that story, Abraham Feinberg, whose name is missing from most popular accounts.

The war ended without peace. In 1949 the Rhodes armistices drew lines that were not borders. Syria's new military ruler, Husni al-Za'im, offered to resettle refugees in return for peace. The offer went nowhere, and he was overthrown within months. At Lausanne in 1949 the UN tried to settle everything at once, and the two sides' sequences never met. Israel joined the UN in May 1949, while the question of Jerusalem stayed open.

Inside Israel, the Arabs who stayed became citizens but lived under military government until 1966. The Absentees' Property Law of 1950 turned the property of those who fled, and even of those who stayed but had been away from home, into state-held land.

Then the ingathering. In its first years, Israel took in hundreds of thousands of immigrants: Holocaust survivors from Europe and Jews from Iraq, Yemen, Libya and elsewhere. Many lived in transit camps. The gravest charge from those camps is the Yemenite children affair: infants who disappeared, and families who were told they had died. The book follows that record carefully. And the Knesset wrote down who belonged: the Law of Return in 1950 and the Nationality Law in 1952. Then the mass fund drives, the American appeals, the Haavara transfer, and private and immigrant capital, which the map now counts. Some hold up, some don't, and some hold in part. The other lays the whole sequence out step by step: fifty-one years from a bank to a state. A state set running before it was declared.

## From the Land · The Same Road, Seen From the Land (1882–1949)

**Summary.** The same road walked from the fields beside it, through the eyes of the Arabs of Palestine, Muslim and Christian, told from their own documents and keeping the same rules: their leaders' failures and the violence some of them did are part of the record too. It covers the first warnings of 1899–1914, including Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi's letter to Herzl, the two promises of 1915–1923 of which only one was made to them, the land sold from under tenants by mostly absentee landowners in 1910–1933, the road from petition to revolt in 1929–1939, partition and the catastrophe of 1947–1949, and Rashid Khalidi's account, The Hundred Years' War on Palestine.

**Script.**

Every stage of this book follows the road that led to the state: the congresses, the funds, the declarations, the votes. But that road ran through a country that already had people in it. This part walks the same years from where they stood, using their own words wherever the record keeps them.

The first warning reached Herzl in his own lifetime. Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi had been mayor of Jerusalem and had sat for the city in the first Ottoman parliament. On 1 March 1899 he wrote to Zadoc Kahn, the chief rabbi of France, asking him to pass a letter to Herzl. He said the Zionist idea was natural and just in principle, but that Palestine was inhabited and part of the Ottoman Empire, and he asked: in the name of God, let Palestine be left alone. Herzl wrote back. He promised the Arabs would benefit. He didn't answer the question of whose country it would be.

The warnings kept coming, in newspapers like Filastin and Al-Karmil, in the Ottoman parliament, and from a governor who tried to stop a land sale at al-Fula in 1910.

Then the war, and two British promises. Only one of them was made to the Arabs. In 1915 and 1916 Sir Henry McMahon promised Sharif Hussein of Mecca British support for Arab independence if the Arabs rose against the Ottomans. They did, in the Arab Revolt of 1916 to 1918. Then came the Balfour Declaration, promising the same land as a national home for someone else. Palestinians held congresses, sent delegations to London, and asked again and again for an elected government. Britain said no, because an elected government with an Arab majority would have stopped the national home.

The land is the heart of the Palestinian experience of this period, and it's harder than either familiar story. Britain did not hand the land to Zionist buyers. It was bought, lawfully, mostly from landowners who didn't live on it, many of them Arab families in the towns or in Beirut and Damascus. But the farmers who worked that land, often for generations, had no deed. When the land was sold, the tenants were evicted. Hebrew-labour rules then kept them from working on it. So the law was followed, and families still lost their livelihoods. Some fought eviction through the courts for years.

Then violence, and here the book keeps the same rules. In August 1929, a dispute at the Western Wall ended in killings in Jerusalem, Safed and above all Hebron, where Jews were massacred on 24 August. That's part of this record too. From 1936 to 1939 came the Arab Revolt against British rule and Zionist immigration. Britain crushed it, executed leaders and exiled others. And the Mufti, Hajj Amin al-Husseini, chose the wrong side of a world war and worked with Nazi Germany. None of that is hidden here.

Then partition. When the question went to the United Nations in 1947, the people who lived there had no vote. The Mandate's figures counted about 1.3 million Arabs and 630,000 Jews. Jews were about a third of the population, and the plan gave the Jewish state about 55 to 56% of the land. In the war that followed, 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians lost their homes. They call it the Nakba, the catastrophe.

The part closes with one book: Rashid Khalidi's The Hundred Years' War on Palestine, from 2020. Khalidi taught at Columbia, and his great-great-uncle was the Yusuf al-Khalidi who wrote to Herzl in 1899. It's history and family memoir at once.

Read from the land, the road to 1948 is a story of warnings heard and set aside. The people of Palestine said in writing in 1899 what they feared. They said it in their newspapers, their parliament, their congresses and their courts. The record holds their divisions and the violence some of them did. What it doesn't hold is a people who failed to see what was coming. They saw it first.

**Script.**

The state was declared on 14 May 1948. The road this book follows ends there. What the founding set in motion did not. This stage follows it to today, one chain at a time.

The refugees. In the war of 1947 to 1949, roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians were displaced. In December 1948 the UN resolved that refugees wishing to return and live at peace should be permitted to. That question still stands. Today about 5.9 million Palestinians are registered refugees.

The war of 1967. On 5 June 1967, after Egypt closed the Straits of Tiran, Israel struck first. In six days it took Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan. Israeli historians still argue over whether its survival was at stake. On 22 November the UN Security Council passed Resolution 242, stressing that territory can't be acquired by war. That war made an occupation, and it's now nearly sixty years old.

Lebanon. After the PLO was expelled from Jordan, southern Lebanon became its base. Israel invaded Lebanon on 6 June 1982 and drove the PLO to Tunis. In September, Phalangist militiamen massacred hundreds, by some counts thousands, at the Sabra and Shatila camps, which Israeli troops had surrounded. Israel's own inquiry found its defence minister indirectly responsible. Hezbollah formed during that occupation.

Hamas. The Muslim Brotherhood opened branches in Gaza in the 1940s. After 1967 Ahmed Yassin rebuilt it there as a network of mosques and charities. Israel licensed it in 1979 and largely left it alone while fighting the PLO. Some Israeli officials later called that a mistake; others say Israel never funded or armed it. Hamas was founded in 1987, with the First Intifada.

The peace plans. Madrid in 1991. The Oslo Accords: mutual recognition in 1993, and the West Bank cut into Areas A, B and C in 1995, with every hard question left for later. Prime Minister Rabin was assassinated by an Israeli opponent of the deal. The final status was never settled.

The patron. From 1949 the patron was the United States. From October 2023 to September 2025, US military aid to Israel came to $21.7 billion.

The 1979 revolution ended it within weeks: Israel's mission in Tehran was handed to the PLO. That chain didn't start with Israel's founding, and the book says so.

## Appendix Two · The Other Homelands (1891–1948)

**Summary.** The roads not taken. Between 1891 and 1948 eight other lands were proposed, offered or studied as homes for Jews: Argentina, the northern Sinai (El Arish), British East Africa, Birobidzhan in the Soviet Far East, Madagascar, Alaska, Sosúa in the Dominican Republic, and Suriname. Each is told from its own documents and measured on six of the things a state needed. The finding: by the time the choice was made there was no empty land with a willing ruler anywhere, and none of the eight was an offer of a state.

**Script.**

Here's a question that follows every account of 1948: with the whole world to choose from, why Palestine, a small land where people already lived? This appendix follows the roads that weren't taken. Eight of them.

First, the world as it was. In 1870 Europeans controlled about a tenth of Africa, all of it near the coasts. By 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent. By the time the movement went looking, almost every piece of land already had a ruler.

One: Argentina. In his 1896 pamphlet, Herzl named two possible lands, Argentina and Palestine. Baron de Hirsch's company settled Jews on farms in Argentina from 1891, and for a time it moved more people onto more land than anything in Palestine. About 6,757 colonists lived there by 1896. But Argentina offered land, not sovereignty. You could farm, as an Argentine, not govern yourself.

Two: El Arish, in the northern Sinai. In 1902 Herzl met Britain's Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, and the northern Sinai was studied for an autonomous Jewish settlement under British sponsorship. It failed on water: Egypt wouldn't release enough Nile water to irrigate it. Sovereignty, but no water.

Three: East Africa. After the Kishinev pogrom of 1903, Chamberlain offered about 16,300 square kilometres of the Uasin Gishu plateau, in today's Kenya. It was the only alternative a great power formally put on the table. It nearly split the movement. In 1905 the Zionist Congress rejected it.

Four: Birobidzhan. One government did give Jews a territory of their own, bigger than Mandatory Palestine: the Soviet Union, in its Far East. A decree of 28 March 1928 set the land aside, and in 1934 it became the Jewish Autonomous Region, with Yiddish as its official language. It covers about 36,271 square kilometres. But it was Soviet, under Stalin, and few Jews went.

Five: Madagascar. It's often named as an alternative homeland, but on the record it never was one. The idea came from antisemites, first Paul de Lagarde in 1878, and later the Nazis. No Jewish organization is documented as seeking it.

Six: Alaska. In November 1938, US Interior Secretary Harold Ickes proposed settling European refugees in Alaska, outside the immigration quotas. His department named four sites, including Baranof Island. Congress didn't pass it.

Seven: Sosúa, in the Dominican Republic. At the Évian conference of July 1938, the dictator Rafael Trujillo's government was the only one to offer to take large numbers, 50,000 to 100,000 Jews. His reported motives were to repair his image after his army's massacre of Haitians, and to "whiten" the population. A few hundred came.

Eight: Suriname. In 1947 the Freeland League reached a preliminary agreement, and on 27 June 1947 Suriname's Legislative Council voted to accept a plan to settle 30,000 homeless European Jews. Then it was withdrawn.

So what do the eight show? Not that Palestine was the easy choice. Where land was plentiful, like Argentina, Alaska and the Dominican Republic, the ruler offered refuge or citizenship, never self-rule. Where self-rule was possible, the land couldn't support it, or it came under Stalin. Every offer of land came with someone else's sovereignty attached, and every offer of sovereignty came with someone else's land. The movement chose the one land it was willing to go to, the one a great power would back. And the people already there paid for that choice.

## Appendix Three · The Architecture in Code

**Summary.** The architecture of the road to 1948 in two other languages: code and diagrams. The appendix uses five techniques from Apple's Objective-C runtime as a vocabulary for what institutions did: +load (installed before anyone could object), method swizzling (a "home" that ran as a state), NSProxy (the Jewish Agency in between), objc_msgSend (one bank for the movement's money) and inheritance without override (each line built on the last). It runs them as one program, then draws the institutions as an entity diagram and the whole system on one page, and ends with what the code does not claim. Every fact in the code comes from the stages, and every block is followed by plain language.

**Script.**

I spent twenty years writing software. So here's a different way to see the road to 1948: as code. Not because it was a secret program, but because the language of software has names for things institutions do.

The code is Objective-C, the language Apple's software was written in for three decades. Its runtime lets a program change its own behaviour while it runs. Five of its techniques map onto five mechanisms on the road to 1948. And every block of code in the book is followed by the same thing in plain language.

One: plus load. In Objective-C, a class's load method runs the moment the program starts, before any of the program's own code runs a single line. Nothing written later can stop it. On the road to 1948, the program was installed before the people it concerned were asked: the Basel Program in 1897, the Balfour letter in 1917, the Mandate in 1922. By the time the people of Palestine could object, the class was already loaded.

Two: method swizzling. Swizzling swaps the code behind a method while its name stays the same. Every caller still asks for the same thing and gets something different. The public word, from Basel in 1897 to Balfour in 1917, was a "home." The program behind it, in Herzl's own title from 1896, was The Jewish State. The label stayed "home." The implementation became a state.

Three: NSProxy. A proxy is an object that isn't the real thing. It stands in for another object and forwards every message to it. Under Article 4 of the Mandate, the Jewish Agency was the recognized body standing between the British government and the movement. Talk to the Agency, and you were really talking to the Zionist Organization behind it. That was the Agency itself, from 1929.

Four: objc_msgSend. In Objective-C, every message between objects passes through one function. Whoever controls that function controls every call. The movement's money had a similar gateway: the Jewish Colonial Trust from 1899, and its Palestine arm from 1902, the Anglo-Palestine Bank. The Haavara transfer from Germany in the 1930s also ran through it. One bank, routing the movement's money.

Five: inheritance without override. A subclass inherits everything from the class above it. If it overrides nothing, every limit the parent set still stands on paper, and none of them gets applied. Balfour in 1917 promised to protect the rights of the "existing non-Jewish communities." The Mandate in 1922 inherited that line. The partition plan of 1947 gave about 55 to 56% of the land to the Jewish state. Each new line inherited the protections of the last without ever applying them.

Then the appendix runs all five as one program, with the network map's own test results as the output. It draws the institutions as an entity diagram: the powers on the left, the British cabinet, the Mandate government, the UN and the American government; the movement on the right; and the state, in red, at the end. And it shows the whole system on one page, in layers: what went in, the institutions that carried it, the decisions that made it law, and what came out in 1948 and after.

Now the most important part, and the appendix says it plainly. The code is a vocabulary, not a finding. It doesn't claim the institutions were literally software, that a hidden program ran beneath them, or that the people in them coordinated in secret. Each step it names was public: Herzl published his title, the Basel Program and the Balfour letter were printed, the Mandate's articles were law. That's the point. Nothing here was hidden. It was all in the open, in published documents. The code just lets you see the shape.
