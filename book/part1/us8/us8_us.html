The United States: Civil-Rights Law and the Racial Gaps It Left

American equality law rests on a constitutional amendment and a cluster of statutes from the 1960s. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) guarantees "the equal protection of the laws". The Civil Rights Act of 1964 bars discrimination in employment and in federally funded programmes. The Voting Rights Act followed in 1965. In September 1965 Executive Order 11246 required federal contractors not to discriminate by race, colour, religion, sex or national origin, and to "take affirmative action" in hiring. [cite:us8_wiki_eo11246] [cite:us8_wiki_sffa] [cite:us8_wiki_shelby] Unlike Israel, the United States has no law that defines the state as belonging to one group. Its gaps grew instead out of laws and practices that sorted people by race in housing, credit, schooling and voting, which the 1960s statutes outlawed. The record since then has two strands: how far the measured gaps closed, and how the legal tools built to close them have been narrowed since 2013.

Poverty and income: a long fall, then a plateau

The official poverty rate for Black Americans peaked at 41.8 per cent in 1966. It fell to 21.2 per cent in 2017, then a record low. The Census Bureau changed how it counted race after 2002, so the long series has a break. [cite:us8_pf_1966] The rate reached its lowest yet, 17.1 per cent, in 2022, rose to 18.4 per cent in 2024 and fell to 17.3 per cent in 2025. That was still 2.2 times the rate for non-Hispanic white Americans. On the Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts taxes, benefits and living costs, the Black rate was 20.1 per cent in 2025 (11.3 per cent in 2021). Among children the 2025 official rates were 24.1 per cent for Black children and 8.5 per cent for non-Hispanic white children. [cite:us8_jbhe_2025] [cite:us8a_jbhe] For the whole population, official poverty was 10.2 per cent in 2025. The Hispanic rate, 13.9 per cent, and the child rate, 13.4 per cent, were the lowest on record. [cite:us8_census_2025] Median household income reached a record $87,460. The Black median rose 4.8 per cent to $59,980, about 69 per cent of the national median by this book's arithmetic. [cite:us8_census_2025] [cite:us8_be_income]

Wealth: the widest gap, and it widened again

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF), taken every three years since 1989, measures family net worth by race. Read by the Economic Policy Institute, it shows a median of $143,563 for white families and $8,552 for Black families in 1989, a ratio of about 17 to 1. By 2007 the figures were $211,726 and $25,923, about 8 to 1, and by 2019 $189,100 and $24,100, again about 8 to 1. [cite:us8_epi_wealth] The Fed's own note on the 2019 survey said the typical white family held "eight times the wealth of the typical Black family". [cite:us8_fed_2019note] The 2025 survey, published on 9 October 2026, shows the gap widening again. In 2025 dollars, median net worth for white non-Hispanic families rose 6 per cent between 2022 and 2025, to $332,100. For Black families it fell 25 per cent, to $36,800. Hispanic families' median rose 18 per cent, to $79,600. [cite:us8_scf2025] [cite:us8_fed_scf_pr] The white-to-Black ratio was therefore about 9 to 1 in 2025, by this book's arithmetic. The EPI series and the Fed's 2025 table use different price years, so ratios are compared here, not dollar amounts.

Home equity is the main store of wealth for most American families, and in 2019 the Fed found that 46 per cent of white families under 35 owned a home, against 17 per cent of young Black families. [cite:us8_fed_2019note] Israel has no survey that measures household wealth by population group in this way, so the wealth comparison runs on the American side only.

Mobility, health and policing

The economists Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Maggie Jones and Sonya Porter found that, holding parental income constant, Black boys grew up to earn less than white boys in 99 per cent of census tracts; among women there was no such gap. Parents' marital status, education and wealth explained very little of the gap, and differences in ability did not explain it. The gaps were smallest in low-poverty neighbourhoods with less racial bias among white residents, but fewer than 5 per cent of Black children grew up in such places. [cite:us8_oi_race] [cite:us8a_oi] The same pattern runs through other systems this book measures. In 2023 the infant mortality rate was 10.93 per 1,000 for Black infants and 4.48 for white infants. [cite:us16_cdc_imr2023] By the count of Mapping Police Violence, a research project that tracks police killings, police killed Black Americans at 2.6 times the rate of white Americans in 2025. [cite:us4_mpv_2025] Black Americans' homicide victimisation rate averaged about eight times the white rate from 1976 to 2025. [cite:us5_ccj_trends] [scene:us4_policing|Part Four: policing] [scene:us16_outcomes|Part Sixteen: health outcomes]

The legal tools since 2013

Between 2013 and 2026 the Supreme Court and the executive narrowed the main legal tools of the 1960s. In Shelby County v. Holder (25 June 2013, 5–4) the Court struck down the formula that decided which states had to clear changes to voting rules with Washington in advance, ending that requirement unless Congress wrote a new formula. [cite:us8_wiki_shelby] In Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (29 June 2023, 6–2) it held race-conscious university admissions unconstitutional. [cite:us8_wiki_sffa] On 21 January 2025 President Trump's Executive Order 14173 revoked Johnson's 1965 order on federal contractors. The new order said that "Illegal DEI and DEIA policies" violate federal civil-rights law. [cite:us8_govinfo_eo14173] [cite:us8_wiki_eo11246] In Louisiana v. Callais, decided 6–3 at the end of April 2026, the Court struck down Louisiana's second majority-Black congressional district and held that the Voting Rights Act can justify drawing districts by race only in narrow cases. Justice Elena Kagan, dissenting, called it the "latest chapter in the majority's now-completed demolition of the Voting Rights Act". [cite:us8_ncc_callais] The executive order presents itself as enforcing the 1960s laws as written; the dissent in Callais read the same period as the dismantling of their remedies.
