The Split That Made the Israeli Right

Zionism is sometimes described as a single, unified political movement until the founding of Israel produced its later party divisions. The institutional record shows otherwise. The movement's foundational split between right and left predates the state by twenty-three years, and it can be traced through three dated organizational breaks: Betar in 1923, the Revisionist secession in 1925, and the New Zionist Organization in 1935. The line that runs from those breaks leads, merger by merger, to the party that has dominated Israeli politics for much of the past five decades.

The man at the center of it was Ze'ev Jabotinsky, a Russian-Jewish writer and Zionist leader [cite:wiki_jabotinsky]. His positions on territory help explain why the split he led was so sharp and so lasting. Testifying before the Peel Commission in 1937, he argued against any partition of Palestine and insisted on Jewish rights to the entire Mandate territory, including Transjordan. He died of a heart attack in 1940 while visiting Betar in the United States [cite:wiki_jabotinsky].

Betar came first. Jabotinsky founded it in 1923 as a disciplined, militarized youth movement emphasizing hadar, or pride, and training in armed self-defense. It spread through Poland, the Baltic states, Palestine and the Americas. By 1934 the broader Revisionist movement claimed some 600,000 members [cite:wiki_betar]. That figure is a claimed membership, and claims of this kind and scale include a range of engagement levels. Even so, it describes a mass, international organization, not a fringe.

The formal break followed in April 1925. In Paris, Jabotinsky founded the World Union of Zionist-Revisionists, known as Hatzohar, which seceded from the World Zionist Organization led by Chaim Weizmann [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism]. The secession came with a stated platform: the transformation of Palestine and Transjordan into a Jewish commonwealth within the British Commonwealth. That stood in direct opposition to the gradualist strategy of the mainstream movement [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism]. The platform was the organization's own founding position; it does not tell us that every member endorsed every detail.

The 1925 secession still left some room for a relationship with the mainstream. The second break, a decade later, closed it. In 1935, after the Zionist Executive formally rejected his platform, Jabotinsky resigned from the World Zionist Organization altogether and founded the New Zionist Organization [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization]. It conducted its own political and fundraising activity, parallel to and in competition with the mainstream institutions, among them the Jewish Agency and AZEC [cite:wiki_hatzohar].

From this current came the armed organizations that would operate alongside, and often against, the Haganah and the Jewish Agency: the Irgun and Lehi, whose story is told in [scene:s07e2|the paramilitary line]. From it, too, came a political party. Herut was founded on June 15, 1948 by Menachem Begin, a former Irgun commander, as a successor to the Revisionist paramilitary tradition. It was framed at the time as a challenge to Hatzohar, which was itself succeeded by and merged into Herut after Israel's establishment [cite:wiki_herut] [cite:wiki_hatzohar]. Herut merged with the Liberal Party in 1965 to form Gahal [cite:wiki_gahal]. In 1973 Gahal became the founding core of Likud, the party that has led or co-led Israeli governments for most of the past five decades, including under Netanyahu [cite:wiki_likud].

Political lineages are often asserted loosely, as one party being the "spiritual heir" of another. This one is a dated chain of foundings and mergers, traceable name by name. It is a lineage of institutions, not a claim that today's Likud holds Jabotinsky's original positions; parties change a great deal over a century. But the mainstream bodies, the World Zionist Organization under Weizmann, the Jewish Agency and AZEC, were never the only political current in Zionism. This was the other one, and it was organized, international and in open competition by the mid-1930s.
