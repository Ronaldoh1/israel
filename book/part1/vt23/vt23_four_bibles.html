Four Bibles: 66, 73, About 76 and 81 Books

Ask four Christians how many books are in the Bible and the answers can be 66, 73, around 76 or 81, depending on whether the person is Protestant, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox or a member of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. [cite:vt23_wiki_ot] [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] The New Testament is almost the same in all four: 27 books, though the Ethiopian church's own count adds eight books of church order. Nearly all the difference sits in the Old Testament, and specifically in the books that the Greek-speaking churches read but the Hebrew Bible does not contain. This section sets the four lists side by side and names who fixed each, when, and with what authority. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon]

Protestant: 66

The Protestant Old Testament has 39 books, the same contents as the Hebrew Bible arranged and counted differently, and the New Testament has 27. [cite:vt23_wiki_ot] The list did not come from one act. In his September Testament of 1522 Martin Luther placed Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation at the end as disputed books, and called James an "epistle of straw" with, in his view, no evangelical character. [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon] His full Bible of 1534 printed Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, the Maccabees and the additions to Esther and Daniel in a separate section as "Apocrypha", books not equal to scripture but "useful and good to read". [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible] Luther was following Jerome's Hebrew-based list rather than inventing a new one. [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon] [scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|Jerome's doubts]

The Church of England's Articles allowed the same books to be read "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not to establish doctrine. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero] The Reformed Westminster Confession of 1646 went further: the Apocrypha, "not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the canon of the Scripture". [cite:vt14_wcf_ch1] The British and Foreign Bible Society's decision in the 1820s to stop funding Bibles that printed them is what made the slim 66-book Bible the common Protestant edition. [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible] Lutheran confessions never formally defined a canon at all. [cite:vt23_wiki_lutherscanon]

Catholic: 73

The Catholic Old Testament has 46 books and the New Testament 27. [cite:vt23_wiki_ot] The seven additional Old Testament books are Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1 and 2 Maccabees, together with longer forms of Esther and Daniel. These are the books the African councils of 393 to 419 had kept and Jerome had doubted. [cite:vt23_na_carthage] [cite:vt23_ccel_jerome_solomon] The Council of Florence listed them in 1442, and the Council of Trent made the list binding under anathema on 8 April 1546, by 24 votes to 15 with 16 abstentions. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] [cite:vt14_trent_s4] Three books that some Greek and Latin Bibles had carried, the Prayer of Manasseh and the books known in Latin as 3 and 4 Esdras, were not on Trent's list and went into an appendix of the official Latin Bible. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero]

Eastern Orthodox: about 76, not fixed by any single council

The Eastern Orthodox churches read the Old Testament in the Greek Septuagint and keep the Catholic deuterocanon plus further books: 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151 and the Prayer of Manasseh, with 4 Maccabees often printed in an appendix. Russian Bibles also include the book Catholic Bibles relegate as 4 Esdras. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon] The Old Testament therefore runs to as many as 49 books, about 76 in all with the New Testament, and the exact count depends on the edition and how books are divided. [cite:vt23_wiki_ot] [cite:vt23_owiki_otcanon]

No ecumenical council has defined the Orthodox canon. The Council in Trullo of 692 endorsed several earlier lists, including Athanasius's, Laodicea's and Carthage's, which "do not agree" with one another; Pope Sergius I refused to accept that council. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon] The Synod of Jerusalem of 1672 issued a list close to Trent's, but it was a regional synod, not binding on all Orthodox churches, even though Greek Bibles follow it. [cite:vt23_owiki_otcanon] Orthodox practice is the clearest case in this part of a church that lives with a canon whose edges are settled by use rather than by definition.

Ethiopian Tewahedo: 81

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church counts 81 books, and it reaches that number in two ways. The church's own count, given on its website and taken from the traditional commentary on its law book, the Fetha Negest, is 46 Old Testament and 35 New Testament books: the 27 familiar New Testament books plus eight books of church order. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] [cite:vt27_cowley1974] A narrower reckoning, printed in the Ge'ez–Amharic and Amharic Bibles issued under Emperor Haile Selassie, is 54 Old Testament and 27 New Testament books, also 81. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The narrower canon contains the whole Hebrew Bible, the Catholic deuterocanon except 1 and 2 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, 3 and 4 Ezra, and all 27 New Testament books, together with four books no other major church treats as scripture: 1 Enoch, Jubilees, the three books of Meqabyan, which share a name with the Maccabees but are different works, and the Paralipomena of Jeremiah, also called 4 Baruch. [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] The broader canon counts fewer Old Testament books by grouping some together, yet holds more distinct works: nine that the narrower list lacks, a medieval history called the Josippon and eight books of church order (the four books of the Sinodos, two books of the Covenant, an Ethiopic Clement and the Didascalia). It is a different arrangement of 81, not 81 plus nine. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The number 81 is older than either list: the Fetha Negest's heading says the books are 81, though its list as written totals 73, and the commentary tradition filled the gap. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] The detail is in [scene:vt27_church_and_canon|Part Twenty-Seven].

The Ethiopian church was never under Rome's jurisdiction, and its canon was not shaped by the Latin lists in the previous section. Where the two meet, they disagree: the Gelasian Decree, read in the West, rejected "Little Genesis", the Book of Jubilees, which Ethiopia kept and which about fifteen scrolls found at Qumran show was widely used by the community there. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt23_wiki_jubilees] How Enoch dropped out of Western use while Ethiopia preserved it is the subject of Part Twenty-Seven. [scene:vt27_church_and_canon|The Ethiopian church and its canon] [scene:vt27_how_the_west_lost_enoch|How the West lost Enoch]

The New Testament's quieter differences

The 27 books of the New Testament are in all four Bibles today, but even that core was not universal. The Syriac Peshitta, the Bible of the Syriac-speaking churches, has a New Testament of 22 books, without 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation. [cite:vt23_wiki_bibcanon] The Armenian church added Revelation only around 1200. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] These are the same books Origen and Eusebius had marked as doubtful in the third and fourth centuries, which shows how long the disputes they recorded lasted in churches outside the Latin and Greek mainstream. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt23_na_eus_he6]

What the four lists show

The comparison bears on Ron's thesis in two ways. First, there is no single "the Bible" that one body fixed for all Christians: the four largest traditions use different lists today, and only one of them, the Catholic, defined its list as dogma. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] [cite:vt23_owiki_otcanon] Second, the Roman list is not the shortest. Trent's 73 books are seven more than the Protestant Bible, and the books Protestants removed to an appendix and then dropped, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the Maccabees, are ones Rome kept. The books every one of the four leaves out, from the Gospel of Thomas to the Gospel of Judas, were set aside centuries earlier by the bishops and scholars named in the previous section.
