This volume, The Cold War Architecture, covers the years from the autumn of 1949 to the eve of June 1967. It picks up where Volume II's last stage left off: with armistice lines that were, by their own text, still not borders, a state admitted to the United Nations without recognized frontiers, and a refugee question that the conference at Lausanne had failed to settle, as told in [scene:rd6_armistices|the armistices of 1949]. Four threads run through the eighteen years that follow. Israel turned from a declared policy of belonging to neither camp to an open alignment with the West. The great powers tried to bind the region into Cold War alliances and to ration the arms that flowed into it, and the states of the region joined, refused or played off those schemes. Israel built a nuclear capacity in secret, and Washington tried to learn what was being built and to limit it. And in Washington a lobby for Israel, opened by one man in these first years, grew into a permanent institution. The volume follows these threads in six stages and ends on 4 June 1967; the war that began the next day belongs to Volume IV.

In the autumn of 1949 Israel's foreign policy fitted in a single clause. The government's Basic Principles of 9 March 1949 promised friendship "with all freedom-loving states, and in particular with the United States and the Soviet Union." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] There was reason for both halves. Washington and Moscow had each voted for partition in 1947; the Soviet Union had recognized the state, and the Eastern bloc had been its main arms supplier in 1948. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The United States had granted de jure recognition on 31 January 1949 and, in a secret note that May, warned that "a revision of its attitude toward Israel has become unavoidable." [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] Israel's secret services were months old, the successors of a Haganah intelligence service dissolved on 30 June 1948. [cite:rs_cw1_jvl_isa] On the borders, the Syrian armistice had created demilitarized zones without saying who owned them, and Egypt would soon argue that its armistice with Israel had not ended the war. Further east, Iran's parliament had approved a development plan to be paid for from oil, and the oil question dominated its elections. [cite:rs_cw1_loc_iran_study]

This stage asks how, in four years, the states of the region came to take sides between Washington and Moscow, and what taking sides secured and cost each of them. For Israel the answer runs from non-identification, through the vote on Korea in July 1950, to what Avi Shlaim calls, by the end of 1953, "an explicit pro-Western position." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] For Egypt it lies in a refusal, and for Iran in a coup. The stage also asks what became of the people the alignment did not consult: the Palestinian refugees, whose future passed from a failed conference to a new UN agency and a fund.

The cast is wide. In Israel, David Ben-Gurion and Moshe Sharett set the course, Abba Eban argued it at the United Nations and in Washington, and Reuven Shiloah and Isser Harel built the intelligence services. In Washington, Truman, Acheson and George McGhee gave way in 1953 to Eisenhower and the brothers John Foster and Allen Dulles. Britain's Churchill and Herbert Morrison, Stalin's government and Andrei Vyshinsky, Egypt's Mahmoud Fawzi, Syria's delegates at the Security Council, and Iran's Mohammad Mossadeq, the Shah and General Zahedi all acted on the same map. The UN acted through the Conciliation Commission and UNRWA. American advocates, I. L. Kenen above all, with Jacob Blaustein and Abraham Feinberg, pressed for aid, while the American Council for Judaism opposed them. And a new Palestinian generation, Yasir Arafat in Cairo and George Habash in Amman, began to organize. [cite:rs_cw1_jta_kenen_obit] [cite:rs_cw1_ej_acj] [cite:rs_cw1_palquest_arafat] [cite:rs_cw1_palquest_habash]

The sections follow the alignment roughly as it was made. They begin with non-identification and its test in the recognition of China, the founding of the secret services, the Tripartite Declaration of May 1950 and [scene:cw1_korea|the Korean decision]. Iran's oil crisis then opens the eastern front of the story. The Huleh crisis of 1951 brings the Syrian front to the Security Council. Three American sections follow: the first grant aid, the lobby that fought for it, and the first ties with American intelligence. Egypt's closure of the Canal to Israel-bound trade and the Middle East Command show the West courting the state that was blocking Israel's trade. The refugee question after Lausanne, the American campaign for "under God" in the Pledge, Moscow's turn from patron to rupture, and the Dulles line of 1953 bring the story to its end. The stage closes in Iran, with [scene:s11|the coup of August 1953] and the question of whether Israel took part in it or only gained from it.
