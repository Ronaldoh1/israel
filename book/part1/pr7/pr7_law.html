What the Law Says

Under the laws of war, a journalist is a civilian. Article 79 of the First Additional Protocol to the Geneva Conventions says journalists "are considered civilians and must be respected and protected," a rule the International Committee of the Red Cross treats as binding in every kind of armed conflict. A journalist loses that protection "only if, and for such time as, they directly participate in hostilities." Reporting, expressing opinions or spreading information does not make a journalist, or a newsroom, a lawful target, however hostile the reporting. [cite:pr_icrc_journalists]

Israel's Argument

Israel's position is that it does not target journalists. "The IDF has never, and will never, deliberately target journalists," the army wrote in February 2025, in reply to the Committee to Protect Journalists. It said it takes all feasible measures to reduce harm to civilians, including journalists, but that remaining in a combat zone carries risk, and that Hamas hides its command posts in civilian places. It named people it said were fighters presenting themselves as journalists: Mustafa Thuraya (spelled "Thuria" by the army), listed in a captured document as a Hamas squad deputy commander; Hamza Dahdouh, an Islamic Jihad operative "disguised" as an Al Jazeera cameraman; Ismail Abu Omar, who it said filmed himself in Kibbutz Nir Oz on 7 October; and Ismail al-Ghoul. It said it "cannot disclose the intelligence" proving these affiliations without exposing its sources. [cite:pr_idf_cpj2025] Three weeks into the war it had written to Reuters and AFP that "we cannot guarantee your employees' safety," because Hamas operated "in the vicinity of journalists and civilians." [cite:pr_cm_idfletter]

Where the Counters Agreed

Israel's claims were not all rejected. The Committee to Protect Journalists removed from its list people its research found had engaged in combat, eight in a review covering 2023 to April 2025 and 19 more in June 2026; the International Federation of Journalists removed eight names and added fourteen in July 2026, raising its count from 235 to 241. [cite:gz_cpj_2026] [cite:pr_wiki_killing] In the cases set out in Part Six, the army's evidence was disputed or contradicted by independent reporting. [scene:pr6_cases|The named]

The Legitimization Cell

In August 2025, after the killing of Anas al-Sharif, the Israeli magazine +972 reported, from three intelligence sources, that the army had set up a unit after 7 October, called the Legitimization Cell, to find intelligence that could be declassified to link Gaza journalists to armed groups. Its purpose, the sources said, was not security but to answer international criticism and protect support abroad, including American arms supplies. One source described "a push to find one journalist who might not be so innocent." Arab News's account of the report carries no army response. [cite:pr_an_legitcell]

Before This War

The Committee to Protect Journalists has documented the same pattern in earlier wars: Yaser Murtaja, killed in 2018, whom a defence minister called a Hamas officer without evidence, and whom the Washington Post found had been vetted for an American aid grant; Hussam Salama and Mahmoud al-Kumi, killed in 2012, whom Human Rights Watch found no proof were militants; Hamid Shihab, killed in 2014; and Mohamed Abu Halima, killed in 2004. "Smear campaigns endanger journalists and erode public trust in the media," its programme director said. [cite:pr_cpj_smear2024]

The organizations' answer is the title of this part, in the words of Reporters Without Borders: "Journalists are not terrorists and the mere publication of these documents doesn't constitute proof of their affiliation." [cite:pr_wiki_killing]
