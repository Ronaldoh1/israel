Kabbalah is the inner tradition of Judaism: a body of teaching about the hidden life of God, the structure of creation and the path of the soul back to its source. Its name means "tradition", that which is received, and for most of its history it was received the way its name implies: from a teacher, by a few, under conditions. The Mishnah forbade expounding the account of God's chariot even to a single student unless he was wise and understood it himself. [cite:et_wiki_merkabah] Later rabbis set an age: forty. [cite:kb_ou_age40]

That makes Kabbalah a natural subject for this shelf, which follows who held sacred knowledge and how. But it is a different case from those before it. Here the gate was held not by an empire or a church over others, but by a community's own teachers over their own students, and the reasons they gave, the danger of the knowledge itself, were tested in history when a mystical messiah swept the Jewish world in 1666 and then converted to Islam. [cite:kb_brit_sabbatai] This book follows the gate as it tightened after that disaster and opened again with Hasidism. [cite:kb_ou_age40] [cite:kb_brit_devequt]

It also follows Kabbalah outside its community: borrowed by Christian scholars who hoped to use it to convert Jews, sold in red string by a Los Angeles centre under federal investigation, and turned into the English word "cabal", which conspiracy theories now use for the opposite of what the record shows. [cite:kb_wiki_xkab] [cite:kb_herald_kc] [cite:kb_wo_cabal] The book names institutions and documented people throughout. It treats Kabbalah as a tradition with a history, not as a code for any people. The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads four of its central teachings as maps of the inner life.
