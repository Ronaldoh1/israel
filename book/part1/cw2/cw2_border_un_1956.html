The Lake, the Council and the Secretary-General, December 1955–October 1956

By the end of 1955 the pattern of the border had been set for two years: an attack across the line, an Israeli military reply on a larger scale, a complaint to the Mixed Armistice Commission and then to the Security Council, and a resolution of censure. The last year of this stage tested whether that pattern could be broken by the United Nations itself. It began with the heaviest of the raids on the Syrian front, brought the Council's sharpest language yet and a personal mission by the Secretary-General, and ended, in October 1956, with Israeli forces again crossing the line at night.

On the night of 11–12 December 1955 Israeli regular forces attacked Syrian army positions on the eastern side of Lake Tiberias. Syria complained to the Security Council on 13 December. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_kinneret] The operation, which Avi Shlaim names Olive Leaves, was in his account authorized by Ben-Gurion alone. Ben-Gurion had returned as defence minister in February 1955 and as prime minister on 3 November 1955; Moshe Sharett, his predecessor as prime minister, was now foreign minister, and he was in Washington asking for arms. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_bg_portfolio] [cite:rs_cw2_pmo_govts] Shlaim quotes Sharett's jibe that "Ben-Gurion the defence minister consulted with Ben-Gurion the foreign minister," and Abba Eban's verdict that "the short-term objective had triumphed unduly over our long-term aims." [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_kinneret] The timing was the injury. As the section on the arms race tells, the answer Sharett had come to Washington for was put off in the days after the raid, and in the end refused ([scene:cw2_czech_arms|the arms race]). [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_arms]

Israel's case was the lake itself. UNTSO's Chief of Staff reported to the Council on 15 and 30 December 1955 that Israel linked the attack to Syrian fire on Israeli fishing boats and on the police escorts that accompanied them. He did not dismiss the provocation. He weighed it, and found "a striking disparity between the scale of retaliation and the provocation." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_kinneret] The word retaliation was the UN's own here: the dispute was not over whether the raid answered something, but over whether the answer bore any proportion to it.

Inside Israel's government the reaction ran along the line that had divided it since 1953, told in [scene:cw2_sharett_ben_gurion|the leadership split]. The American Embassy in Tel Aviv reported, in a telegram received on 3 January 1956, that Sharett did not condemn the government's action but appeared to regard it "as a severe blunder." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_tlv_kinneret] It was a precise position. The foreign minister would not break with the cabinet in public, and did not defend the decision either.

The Council's answer came on 19 January 1956. Resolution 111 was adopted unanimously at the 715th meeting, on a draft put forward jointly by the United States, Britain and France. It condemned the attack as "a flagrant violation" of the cease-fire, of the Israel–Syria Armistice Agreement and of the Charter. It took up Israel's case directly and rejected it: Syrian interference with Israeli activities on the lake, the resolution held, "in no way justifies the Israel action." And it closed with a warning that went beyond any earlier text. If Israel did not comply, the Council would "have to consider what further measures under the Charter are required." [cite:rs_cw2_un_res111] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_kinneret]

That last sentence threatened more than it imposed: it named no measure and set no deadline. The resolution was the third Council censure of an Israeli raid in this stage, after Resolution 101 on Qibya in November 1953 and Resolution 106 on Gaza in March 1955, and it was the one that answered Israel's stated justification most directly. [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res106] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res111]

Censure had not changed the pattern, and in the spring the Council tried a different instrument: the Secretary-General in person. On 4 April 1956 it asked Dag Hammarskjöld, unanimously, to survey how far the parties were complying with the armistice agreements. He travelled in the region from 10 April to 3 May 1956 and reported on 9 May. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] What he brought back was a set of assurances, and their form shows the limit of what he could obtain. Each government undertook to observe the cease-fire "unconditionally", provided the other side complied, and each reserved its right of self-defence under Article 51 of the Charter. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] An unconditional pledge that was conditional on the other party's conduct, with self-defence reserved, left every government the judge of when it was released from it. It was what the governments would sign.

The Council adopted the Secretary-General's approach on 4 June 1956, again unanimously. It endorsed his view that full compliance with the armistice agreements "represents a stage which has to be passed" before progress could be made on the main issues, and it declared that the freedom of movement of UN observers "must be respected." [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] That the Council had to say so in a resolution suggests how the observers' work had been going: the body charged with establishing the facts on the line needed the Council's backing to reach them. The first point set the order of things: no settlement of the refugee, border or recognition questions until the armistice itself was kept.

The summer showed how much the assurances held. In mid-August 1956 mines and attacks near the Gaza Strip killed four Israeli citizens and wounded eight. The next day an Egyptian car carrying medical personnel was ambushed in territory under Egyptian control, and nine Egyptians were killed. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] The two incidents sit side by side in the UN's own annual record, a day apart, and the sequence shows the logic both governments had reserved to themselves in May: an attack, and an answer, each claiming the other side's breach as its release. The Gaza front by then had its own history of escalation, from the raid of February 1955 through the Egyptian-organized fida'i units, whose commander, Mustafa Hafez, had been killed by Israeli intelligence in July 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza]

On the Jordanian line, the UN Yearbook lists incidents at Sheikh Abd el Aziz and Mount Scopus on 24–25 July 1956 and at Sharafi near Husan on 25–26 September. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] Then, on the night of 10–11 October 1956, Israeli forces demolished the police post at Qalqilya, in Jordan, with explosives. The Yearbook says the attack inflicted "heavy casualties". It gives no figure. [cite:rs_cw2_unyb1956] This book gives none either, because no figure from the sources used here supports one.

The case for the Israeli government across these ten months rested on what it faced: fire on its fishermen on the lake, mines and ambushes on the Gaza line that killed civilians, and a UN machinery that recorded the attacks but could not stop them. It had accepted the Secretary-General's cease-fire on the same reciprocal terms as its neighbours, and it held that each raid answered a breach. The case made by the Council was put in the Council's own words: that the scale of the replies bore no relation to the provocations, as UNTSO reported of the lake, and that no interference by Syria could justify what was done there. Both cases were made at the time, by the governments and bodies charged with the line, and both stand in the record.

Several things the record used here does not settle. It gives no casualty figures for the Lake Tiberias raid or for Qalqilya. It does not say, for the August killings near Gaza, which incident each government regarded as the first breach. And it does not show that Resolution 111's warning of "further measures" was ever followed by any. Two weeks after Qalqilya the Jordanian elections of 21 October 1956 brought Sulayman Nabulsi, an admirer of Nasser, to the premiership. [cite:rs_cw2_loc_jordan] The killings at Kafr Qasim and the Sinai campaign that followed belong to Stage Three.
