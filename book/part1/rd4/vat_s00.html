What the Vatican Actually Controls

Before turning to the wartime conduct of the Catholic Church, it helps to be exact about what the Church's authority covers. A common assumption holds that the Vatican has, or claims, authority over Christian scripture and doctrine in general, Catholic and Protestant alike. It does not, and it has not since the sixteenth century.

The Scofield Reference Bible, discussed elsewhere in this book as a foundational document of American Christian Zionism, illustrates the point. It was never submitted to Rome for approval, and Rome was never in a position to demand its retraction. That was not an oversight. It reflects the actual structure of Church authority.

That authority is called the magisterium: the Church's teaching office, exercised through the Pope and the bishops. It binds Catholics, which means Catholic doctrine, Catholic clergy and Catholic religious publications. It has never claimed, and has no mechanism to exercise, binding authority over people or institutions outside the Catholic Church. [cite:catholic_imprimatur_explained] One of its instruments is the imprimatur, "let it be printed," an approval process applied to Catholic religious publications such as catechisms, devotional works and translations intended for Catholic use. Protestant publishers never sought it and never needed it. That included Oxford University Press, which published the Scofield Bible.

The reason for this boundary lies in the Reformation. Its central premise was the rejection of Rome's authority to bind believers' consciences. The principle of sola scriptura held that scripture, not the Church hierarchy, was the final authority. Evangelical Protestants who use the Scofield Bible therefore stand, by their own tradition's founding principle, entirely outside any claim Rome could make on their beliefs. To ask why the Vatican did not stop Scofield is a little like asking why one country's supreme court did not strike down another country's law. The jurisdiction was never there.

That answer, however, is too easy if it ends there. The Vatican speaks constantly on matters over which it has no binding authority at all: climate policy, nuclear weapons, immigration law, the birth-control policies of secular states. [cite:wiki_pius_xii_holocaust] The authority to bind and the capacity to speak publicly are two different things, and the Church exercises the second routinely in areas where it has none of the first.

So the fair question is not why Rome failed to force a retraction it could never have forced. It is why, having already ruled against premillennial dispensationalism internally in the 1940s, as discussed elsewhere in this book, the Church never used its considerable public voice to say so loudly, as it does on many other subjects outside its jurisdiction. That is a question about the choice to speak or to stay silent, not about legal power.

The same question, silence against action, was tested at far higher stakes in the same decade. During the Second World War the Church's leadership faced not a disputed reading of prophecy but the destruction of Europe's Jews. How it responded, and how historians have judged that response, is the subject of the next section, on [scene:vat_s00b|Pius XII and the Holocaust].
