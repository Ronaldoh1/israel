Refugees, 1948

The war of 1948 brought Palestinian refugees into Lebanon. The camps set up for them held an estimated 100,000 people when they first arrived; by 1956 the UN agency for Palestine refugees, UNRWA, ran seventeen official camps in the country. [cite:hz1_bpb_refugees] [cite:hz1_bpb_refugees] Lebanon did not give them citizenship. Their status has stayed precarious: Palestinians in Lebanon are barred from most professions and may not own property, and 452,669 were registered with UNRWA in January 2015, though the number actually living in the country is believed to be lower. [cite:hz1_bpb_refugees] For the war and the flight itself, see Gaza, Documented. [scene:gz1_refugees|The refugees of 1948]

Raids and Reprisals, 1968–1969

In the late 1960s Palestinian guerrilla groups, the fedayeen, began attacking Israel from southern Lebanon, and Israel answered with airstrikes and raids on Lebanese territory. [cite:hz1_olj_cairo] The best-known early reprisal hit the Lebanese state itself rather than the guerrillas. Overnight on 28 December 1968, 64 Israeli commandos led by Rafael Eitan, later Israel's chief of staff, landed at Beirut's international airport and destroyed twelve Middle East Airlines aircraft, by Al Majalla's count. Israel's stated reason was an attack on an Israeli airliner by the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine, which was based in Beirut. [cite:hz1_majalla_airport] The UN Security Council, unanimously, condemned Israel "for its premeditated military action in violation of its obligations under the Charter" and said Lebanon was entitled to redress. [cite:hz1_unscr262]

The raids put the Lebanese government between two pressures. Arab governments pressed it to let the Palestinians fight; many Lebanese wanted the state to reassert control over its own territory, and through 1969 the Lebanese army clashed with the guerrillas. President Charles Helou turned to Egypt's president, Gamal Abdel Nasser, to broker a settlement. [cite:hz1_olj_cairo]

The Cairo Agreement

The settlement was signed in Cairo on 3 November 1969 by the commander of the Lebanese army, General Emile Bustani, and the chairman of the Palestine Liberation Organization, Yasser Arafat, with Egyptian ministers present. [cite:hz1_cairo_text] Its unofficial text, published by the Institute for Palestine Studies, recognised the right of Palestinians in Lebanon to join "the Palestinian Revolution through armed struggle", provided they respected Lebanese sovereignty; gave the guerrillas crossing points and observation posts along the frontier and a right of passage to the Arqub district in the south-east; and placed control of arms in the camps with Palestinian "Armed Struggle" posts. Clause 15 read: "This agreement shall remain top secret." [cite:hz1_cairo_text]

It did not stay secret. An unofficial version appeared in the Beirut daily An-Nahar on 20 April 1970. [cite:hz1_wiki_cairo] Its practical effect was to move sixteen UNRWA camps, home to about 300,000 refugees, out of the jurisdiction of the Lebanese army's intelligence bureau and under the PLO's Armed Struggle Command, and to let the guerrillas establish control over much of the border area. [cite:hz1_wiki_cairo] The Lebanese state had, in writing, licensed a foreign armed movement to wage war on a neighbour from its soil. Lebanon's parliament annulled the agreement on 21 May 1987, long after it had ceased to matter. [cite:hz1_wiki_cairo]

Black September and the Move to Lebanon

The PLO's centre of gravity was then not Lebanon but Jordan, where its groups had built a rival authority to King Hussein's. On 17 September 1970 the Jordanian army launched an offensive to retake the territory they held; a ceasefire was signed in Cairo on 27 September. [cite:hz1_afp_blacksept] The death toll is disputed. Jordan put it at about 3,000, fighters, soldiers and civilians together; the PLO spoke of tens of thousands, and Arafat of 25,000; other estimates of Palestinians killed run from about 2,000 to 3,400. [cite:hz1_afp_blacksept] [cite:hz1_wiki_blacksept] Fighting resumed in 1971, and in July 1971 the Jordanian government drove the remaining fighters out. They went to Lebanon. [cite:hz1_afp_blacksept] [cite:hz1_wiki_blacksept]

From then on the PLO's leadership, its fighters and its institutions were in Beirut and the south, under the terms of the Cairo Agreement. In effect, the PLO established a state within a state. [cite:hz1_wiki_cairo] The south, Lebanon's poorest region and the home of most of its Shia, became the front line.

Attacks Across the Border

Guerrilla groups based in Lebanon carried out some of the deadliest attacks on Israeli civilians of the decade. On 11 April 1974 three gunmen who had crossed from Lebanon killed 18 people, eight of them children, in an apartment building in Kiryat Shmona; the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine – General Command is named as responsible. Two days later Israeli forces raided villages in southern Lebanon. [cite:hz1_wiki_kiryat] On 15 May 1974 members of the Democratic Front for the Liberation of Palestine, also crossing from Lebanon, seized a school in Ma'alot; 25 hostages were killed, 22 of them children, when an Israeli unit stormed the building and the gunmen opened fire and threw grenades. [cite:hz1_wiki_1974]

The two governments explained these years differently. Israel's position, as its defence establishment put it in 1979, was that its operations in the south were meant to "deter the Palestinian terrorists from carrying out operations against Israeli targets". [cite:hz1_jta_cluster] The Cairo Agreement stated the PLO's position: armed struggle was a right of the Palestinians in Lebanon. [cite:hz1_cairo_text] Lebanon's government, which had signed that right away under pressure, also bore the reprisals, and in March 1978 it told the Security Council that it had no connection with the Palestinian operation that preceded Israel's invasion. [cite:hz1_unifil_bg]

Civil War, 1975

Lebanon's civil war began on 13 April 1975. That morning gunmen fired on a church in the Christian suburb of Ain el-Rummaneh, killing four people; hours later Phalangist militiamen killed about 30 Palestinians travelling through the suburb on a bus. [cite:hz1_wiki_civilwar] The fighting that followed set the Lebanese Front, mainly Maronite parties including the Phalange (Kataeb), against the Lebanese National Movement, an alliance of leftist and Muslim parties allied with the PLO. [cite:hz1_wiki_civilwar] The war's causes were Lebanese and Palestinian: the confessional settlement of 1943 and the armed Palestinian presence licensed in 1969. Foreign states then joined. On 1 June 1976 about 12,000 Syrian troops entered Lebanon and fought the Palestinian and leftist militias; Israel, from 1976, backed Christian militias against the PLO. [cite:hz1_wiki_civilwar] [cite:hz1_wiki_freeleb] By its end in 1990 the war had killed about 120,000 people and displaced nearly a million. [cite:hz1_wiki_civilwar]
