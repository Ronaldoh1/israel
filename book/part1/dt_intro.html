This volume answers a claim. In April 2009 the investor Peter Thiel wrote that he no longer believed "that freedom and democracy are compatible." [cite:de1_cato_thiel] In January 2025 the writer Curtis Yarvin, who had blogged for years as Mencius Moldbug, told the New York Times that he did not "believe in actually voting at all." [cite:de1_audit_nyt_voting] The body of writing around them, which the British philosopher Nick Land named the Dark Enlightenment, proposes to replace elected government with a state run like a company, under a chief executive answerable to its owners, in which residents have "no voice" and the freedom to leave in its place. [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Its companion volume, The Dark Enlightenment Architecture, reads those texts closely and tests their cure; this book tests their premise, that American democracy has been tried and has failed. [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|The diagnosis, steelmanned]

The book accepts much of the evidence that premise rests on. Trust in the federal government to do what is right most of the time fell from 77 per cent in 1964 to 17 per cent in 2025. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] The measuring instruments built to grade democracies now grade the United States lower than at any point in their own records: the Economist Intelligence Unit has called it a "flawed democracy" since 2016, and the V-Dem Institute moved it in 2025 from "liberal democracy" to "electoral democracy", the lowest score in the G7. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] Where the book parts company with Thiel and Yarvin is on what that evidence shows. A system can perform badly because democracy cannot work, or because the system was never made fully democratic in the first place. Those are different claims, and they call for opposite remedies.

The second claim can be tested piece by piece, because the places where the American system departs from majority rule are written down. A president is chosen by electors allotted state by state, and in 2000 and 2016 the candidate with fewer votes nationwide won. [cite:dt2_brit_splits] House districts are drawn by state governments, and in June 2019 the Supreme Court held, 5–4, that claims of partisan gerrymandering are beyond the reach of the federal courts. [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] In January 2010 the Supreme Court held, 5–4, that corporations and unions may spend without limit on independent political advocacy, while upholding the disclosure rules by 8–1. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] And the First Amendment restrains the government, not the private companies that now carry much of the country's political speech: in the Court's words in 2019, the Free Speech Clause "prohibits only governmental abridgment of speech." [cite:dt5_lii_halleck] Each of these is a design fact, documented in a constitution, a statute or a ruling. Whether the country is better or worse off because of any of them is a separate question, and the book keeps the two apart.

How the book tests the claim

Part One sets out what Thiel, Yarvin and Land actually said, and where the record supports their account of the symptoms. Parts Two to Five map the four departures from majority rule: the Electoral College; primaries and gerrymanders; money; and the asymmetry of speech rights between corporations and citizens. Parts Six and Seven examine the remedies that are already being tried somewhere in the country, from ranked-choice ballots in New York and Alaska to public financing in New York City and Seattle, each with its evidence, its critics and its legal route, whether a city charter, a state ballot measure, a statute, a constitutional amendment or a court. Part Eight holds Thiel's and Yarvin's argument to the same standard as everything else, and Part Nine sets out what each reform would take and what is due to be decided, beginning with the measures on the ballot on 3 November 2026.

Every part ends with a passage headed "The test", which asks whether the claim that democracy has failed survives that part's facts, and grades the answer in three registers. Documented means a primary record or independent reporting establishes it. Probable means the record makes it likely without proving it. Not supported means the record does not show it, or runs against it. The same passage says where the record cuts against this book's own thesis, because a reform agenda is a claim too, and it gets the same treatment. One rule applies throughout: every party, donor, lobby and viewpoint is held to the same standard, and no reform is presented without the case against it. The book's argument, in short, is that the critics have described a real failure and misnamed it. Whether that argument holds is for the record in the parts that follow to decide.
