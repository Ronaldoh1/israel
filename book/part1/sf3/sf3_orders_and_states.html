The Order That Became a Dynasty, and the Lodges That Were Closed

The Safavid dynasty, which ruled Iran from 1501 to 1736, descended from Shaykh Safi al-Din of Ardabil, who lived from 1253 to 1334 and led the Safaviyya Sufi order. [cite:sf_brit_safavid] The order was "originally Sunni", following the Shafiʿi school of law, but it moved gradually toward Shiʿism, and by the time of Shaykh Junayd it was explicitly Shiʿi. [cite:sf_brit_safavid] With the backing of the Qizilbash, Turkmen and other heterodox tribes, the order's young leader Ismaʿil took Tabriz, and in July 1501 he was enthroned as shah. Within about ten years he had conquered most of Iran. [cite:sf_brit_safavid] He then made Shiʿism the state religion and enforced its creed and prayers in the mosques, although most of the territory was Sunni. [cite:sf_brit_safavid]

The Safavid story is the reverse of al-Hallaj's. There, a mystic was killed by the state; here, a mystical order became the state, and its creed was imposed on a population that did not share it. An inner path that began with ascetics in wool ended, in this case, as the religion of an empire, enforced in its mosques.

In the Ottoman Empire the orders stood beside the throne rather than on it. The Bektashi order gained political weight through its relations with the Janissaries, the empire's standing army, and the head of the Mevlevi order invested the Ottoman sultan with the sword. [cite:sf_brit_orders] Sufi lodges were part of the fabric of Ottoman life, and that is why the republic that replaced the empire treated them as part of the old order.

On 30 November 1925, as part of Mustafa Kemal's reforms, the Turkish Republic passed a law that made all tekkes and zawiyas, the Sufi lodges, illegal. [cite:sf_wiki_akbaba] At the Akbaba lodge in Istanbul, for example, the leader had been active until then; the law effectively ended the lodge's activity. [cite:sf_wiki_akbaba] In Britannica's words, in Turkey the orders "have been banned since 1925", although many people still keep the mystical tradition. [cite:sf_brit_orders]

The power of the orders cut both ways. Their networks of loyalty, their chains of masters and their hold on ordinary people made them useful allies of a throne, and, when they chose, a path to one. The same features made them a target for a state that wanted no rival centres of loyalty. The Safavids used an order to take power; the Turkish Republic closed the orders to secure power. In both cases the inner path was treated as what it had also become: an institution.
