In the summer of 1945 the war in Europe was over, and the full scale of the Holocaust was becoming documented and public. [cite:sonneborn_toi] Palestine was still a British Mandate, and Britain had a new government: Clement Attlee formed his Labour cabinet on 26 July 1945, with Ernest Bevin as Foreign Secretary. [cite:rs_rd5_britannica_bevin] In Washington, Harry Truman had been President since 12 April, when Franklin Roosevelt died one week after putting in writing his assurance to Ibn Saud that nothing would be done hostile to the Arabs without consultation. Truman had been Vice President for eighty-two days and arrived with no foreign-policy experience. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The Arab League, founded that March, stood for a "unitary and independent Palestinian state." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] The Arab Higher Committee, the Palestinian Arabs' leading body, had not functioned for years and would be re-established only in 1946, by a decision of the Arab League; the man who would chair it, Hajj Amin al-Husayni, was being held by France. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_jps_1990] [cite:rs_rd5_dipc_husayni] In New York, David Ben-Gurion had already reached a judgment of his own: that the Arab states would go to war, and that the Haganah had neither the weapons nor the money to survive it. [cite:sonneborn_toi]

Three years later the Mandate was gone, a Jewish state had been proclaimed and recognized, and some 700,000 Palestinian Arabs had become refugees. This stage asks how that happened in so short a time: who decided each step, through what instrument, with whose money and whose arms, and who objected. It asks with equal weight what the Palestinian Arab leadership and the Arab states chose to do, and what they were unable to do. The answer is not one decision but a chain of them, taken in London, Washington, Lake Success, Cairo, Amman, Prague and Tel Aviv, and it ends in a war that produced, in the same months, a state for one people and what the other calls the Nakba, the "catastrophe."

The cast is the widest of any stage. In London stood Bevin, Attlee and the Colonial Secretary Arthur Creech Jones. In Washington the President's political advisers, Clark Clifford and David Niles, and his old business partner Eddie Jacobson, faced the Secretary of State George Marshall, the State Department's Loy Henderson and Robert Lovett, the Defense Secretary James Forrestal, the Joint Chiefs and a newly created CIA. The Jewish Agency worked through Ben-Gurion, Chaim Weizmann, Moshe Shertok and Golda Meyerson, and through American supporters such as Rudolf Sonneborn, Henry Montor and Abba Hillel Silver; the Irgun under Menachem Begin, and Lehi, fought on their own account. On the Arab side were the Arab Higher Committee under the Mufti and Jamal Husseini, the commanders Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni and Fawzi al-Qawuqji, the Arab League, and King Abdullah of Transjordan with his British-officered Arab Legion. Andrei Gromyko spoke for Moscow. Dissenters, among them the American Council for Judaism, Judah Magnes and Hannah Arendt, objected throughout and were outweighed.

The sections follow the chain roughly in the order it was forged. They begin with the change of president and the men around him, Truman's own recorded words, and the July 1945 meeting in [scene:s07d|Sonneborn's apartment] that founded an arms network on American soil. Bevin's policy, the Anglo-American Committee and the Jewish armed campaign of 1945–46 lead to Operation Agatha and the King David Hotel. The Jewish Agency's early turn to partition, the White House's inner channels and Britain's decision to hand Palestine to the United Nations set the stage for the Soviet turn, the UN special committee, the written warnings of State, the Pentagon and the CIA, and the dissenters at home. Resolution 181 is followed by the Arab League's control of Palestinian decision-making, the civil war that began on 1 December 1947, the violence against Jews in Arab cities, and the money and arms of early 1948. Plan Dalet, Washington's retreat to trusteeship, Deir Yassin, the fall of the mixed towns and Abdullah's double course lead to independence and the Nakba, recognition, the Arab states' war and [scene:s10b|the actual military balance]. The stage closes with the making of one army and with [scene:rd5_refugees|the refugees], whose return the new government had refused by June 1948.
