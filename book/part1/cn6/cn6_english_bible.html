The English Bible: Wycliffe's Bones and Tyndale's Stake

The fight over who could read the Bible came to England a century and a half after Toulouse, and it was fought over the same principle: whether the scriptures could be put into the language of the people without the permission of the clergy. It produced one of the strangest punishments in the history of the church, the burning of a man decades after his death.

John Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian, attacked the wealth and political power of the church of his day. In his work On Civil Dominion of 1376 he wrote that "England belongs to no pope. The pope is but a man, subject to sin." [cite:cn_ht_wycliffe] His followers produced the first complete translation of the Bible into English, issued from 1382, and the number of copies that survive shows how widely it was valued. [cite:cn_ht_wycliffe] His followers carried his books and his ideas through England.

The church's response came in two stages. The first was a set of resolutions adopted by the Oxford University convocation in 1407 after disturbances among Wycliffe's followers, extended by a council of the province of Canterbury in 1408 and in force there by 1409. They are known as the Constitutions of Oxford, or of Arundel, after Thomas Arundel, archbishop of Canterbury. [cite:cn_wiki_oxford] Their seventh article provides that no one should make a new translation of scripture into English "in the form of a book, booklet or tract" without prior permission. [cite:cn_wiki_oxford] Historians disagree about how far it went. Some have read it as outlawing the possession of English scripture; the text does not say that, and Thomas More read it as requiring a lay person to obtain the bishop's permission. [cite:cn_wiki_oxford] Either way, the decision over who could read the Bible in English was placed in the hands of the bishops.

The second stage was posthumous. On 4 May 1415 the Council of Constance, the council that also condemned the Czech reformer Jan Hus, declared Wycliffe a heretic. [cite:cn_ht_wycliffe] He had died decades earlier, so the sentence was carried out on his body. In 1428 his remains were dug up from the churchyard at Lutterworth and burned, and the ashes were thrown into the River Swift. [cite:cn_ht_wycliffe] A council could not reach a dead man's soul. It could make sure that no grave in an English churchyard honoured the first translator of the English Bible.

The printing press changed the contest, because a printed book could be made faster than it could be found and burned. William Tyndale, an English scholar who had become convinced, in Britannica's words, "that the Bible alone should determine the practices and doctrines of the church", could not get permission to translate in England and went to the Continent. His New Testament was printed at Cologne in 1525, and two more editions followed at Worms the same year. [cite:cn_brit_tyndale] The first copies were smuggled into England in 1526, "where they were at once proscribed". [cite:cn_brit_tyndale] Tyndale was captured in Antwerp before he could finish the Old Testament. Condemned for heresy, he was strangled and then burned at the stake at Vilvoorde, near Brussels, on 6 October 1536. [cite:cn_brit_tyndale]

His words outlived him. Tyndale's English became the model for English Bibles for nearly four hundred years, beginning with the King James Version of 1611. [cite:cn_brit_tyndale] Millions of people who have never heard his name quote him every time they read the Bible in English. The institution that executed him for translating it did not, in the end, prevent the translation. It ensured that the translator paid for it with his life, and that his work entered England as contraband.

The English case shows the second element of the canon's power in its plainest form. Fixing the list of books had settled which texts were scripture. Controlling the language of those texts settled who could read them without a priest. In 1229 at Toulouse and in 1407 at Oxford the answer was the same: the clergy, and those the clergy licensed. Wycliffe's bones and Tyndale's stake were the price of disagreeing.
