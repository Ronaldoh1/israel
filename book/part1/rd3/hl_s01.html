The Histadrut and "Hebrew Labor"

Labor Zionism is often remembered as a socialist workers' movement, committed to class solidarity across national lines. The institution at its center tells a more complicated story. The Histadrut was a trade union, but it was also a builder of enterprises, a training ground for the state's leaders, and, from its founding, an organization open to Jews only.

It was founded in Haifa in December 1920 as the General Federation of Hebrew Workers in the Land of Israel, uniting the labor Zionist parties Ahdut HaAvoda and Hapoel Hatzair [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Membership was about 4,400 in 1920 and about 8,400 in 1922. A Zionist historian's estimate puts the number of Arab workers in Palestine at the time at roughly 50,000 [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss]. Those workers were outside the federation by design: membership was restricted to Jews [cite:wiki_histadrut].

The decisive figure was David Ben-Gurion, elected secretary at the end of 1921. In the historian Zeev Sternhell's account, Ben-Gurion turned the Histadrut into a national instrument for realizing Zionism rather than a conventional class union [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Its campaign in the labor market was "Hebrew labor," avoda ivrit: pressing Jewish employers, especially citrus growers and builders, to hire only Jewish workers [cite:wiki_histadrut]. This was separation in the labor market well before there was any separation in law. Critics, including the BDS movement, describe the result as national exclusivism in place of worker solidarity, and characterize the pre-state Histadrut as a national-exclusivist rather than a socialist body [cite:bds_histadrut_critique]. That is the critics' characterization.

The Histadrut did not only organize workers. It owned the places they worked. It built the construction and public-works firm Solel Boneh; the Workers' Bank, Bank Hapoalim, in association with the World Zionist Organization; the Kupat Holim health fund, on which many Israelis later depended for insurance; and industrial holdings that later became Koor Industries [cite:wiki_histadrut]. By 1989 it employed about 280,000 workers [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss]. A union that ran a bank, a construction company and a health system was, in practice, a parallel economy.

It was also a school for government. Ben-Gurion went on to chair the Jewish Agency from 1935 and became prime minister in 1948. Two later prime ministers, Levi Eshkol and Golda Meir, also rose through the Histadrut [cite:wiki_histadrut]. Israel's first prime ministers came from its ranks.

The exclusion of Arab workers outlasted the Mandate. Arab workers were admitted to full membership only in 1959, eleven years after statehood. By 1983 about 170,000 of the federation's 1.6 million members were Arab [cite:wiki_histadrut] [cite:marxists_histadrut_union_boss]. The institution's own leaders were not silent about its origins. Pinhas Lavon, its secretary-general in 1960, described its founders as young people seeking work in a country "which rejected its inhabitants."

Seen alongside the other institutions of the period, the Histadrut supplied one layer of a larger structure. The Jewish National Fund held land that could not be resold to non-Jews. The Haganah supplied arms. The Histadrut organized a Jews-only labor market with its own bank and its own health system. Together they meant that the pre-state Yishuv had built separate economic institutions decades before 1948. The familiar picture of Labor Zionism as cross-national socialist solidarity sits uneasily beside an institution that admitted Arab workers only in 1959.
