The Land's Other Communities

A common shorthand presents the conflict as Jews against Muslims, and Palestinians as uniformly Muslim. The land's population has never fitted that frame. Alongside its Jewish and Muslim Arab majorities it holds Druze, whose faith took shape in the Middle Ages and accepts no converts; Bedouin, whose relationship with the state turns on land; Circassians, Muslims from the Caucasus who serve in Israel's army; Samaritans, a community of a few hundred who trace their descent to ancient Israel; and Palestinian Christians, members of some of the oldest Christian communities on earth. Each stands in a different relationship to the State of Israel, and those differences are documented in law, in census figures and in the record of demolitions and conscription. The legal situations of Palestinians in general, and the numbers of Arab citizens of Israel as a whole, are set out earlier in this volume [scene:pr_s02|who the Palestinians are in law].

The Druze. The Druze faith took shape in the Middle Ages and has accepted no converts since the eleventh century [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_druze]. According to Minority Rights Group, about 150,000 Druze live in Israel proper, in eighteen villages, and about 24,000 to 25,000 more in the Golan Heights [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_druze]. Central Bureau of Statistics figures reported in April 2024 give a slightly higher total for Israel, about 152,000, against 14,500 in 1949; the largest Druze localities at the end of 2022 were Daliyat al-Karmel and Yarka, each with more than 17,000 people [cite:rs_ch01_tps_druze].

The Druze relationship with the state was set early. Druze in Galilee and on Mount Carmel fought alongside the Jewish forces in 1948. In 1956 Druze men were included in military conscription, and in 1961 separate Druze religious courts were established [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_druze] [cite:rs_ch01_tps_druze]. The Golan Druze followed a different course. After Israel captured the Golan Heights in 1967 it offered citizenship to the Druze living there, and, according to the same April 2024 report, most refused [cite:rs_ch01_tps_druze]. The sources used here do not say how many have since accepted, or whether Golan Druze who did not take citizenship are subject to the draft.

The Circassians. Israel's Circassians are Muslims whose origins lie in the Caucasus. They live mainly in two Galilee villages: Kfar Kama, with about 2,000 people of the Shapsug group, and Rehaniya, with about 1,000 of the Abadzakh group. According to a community-sourced account, they were settled there at the Ottoman Sultan's request in the late nineteenth century, and Circassian men serve in the army and the police [cite:rs_ch01_mjl_circassians]. That account rests on a single source drawn from a Circassian foundation, and it does not settle when and why the families left the Caucasus; this book does not go beyond what it states.

The Negev Bedouin. The largest of these communities, and the one whose position is most contested, is the Bedouin of the Negev. About 92,000 Bedouin lived in the Negev before 1948. After the war about 11,000 remained inside Israel, from 19 of the original 95 tribes, according to Minority Rights Group and the Israeli-Arab legal center Adalah [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin] [cite:rs_ch01_almonitor_bedouin]. Those who stayed were concentrated in an area known as the siyag, Arabic for "fence," of about 1.5 million dunams, against some 13 million dunams they had used before [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin].

The legal mechanism that followed ran through two statutes. The Land Acquisition Law of 1953 and the Planning and Building Law of 1965 registered much of the Bedouin's land as state land [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. From the 1960s the state built seven planned townships, beginning with Tel Sheva, Rahat and Kseifa [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. Villages outside the townships and outside the state's plans were left "unrecognized": without legal standing as settlements, and with their buildings treated in official policy as illegal construction on state land. Israel does not recognize the Bedouin as an indigenous people [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. Just over 300,000 Bedouin live in Israel today, about 3% of the population [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin].

Recognition has moved slowly. The Association for Civil Rights in Israel counted 46 unrecognized Bedouin villages in the Negev, of which the state had recognized 11 by about 2011, leaving 35. The same organization recorded 275 buildings demolished in 2010 and 1,000 in 2011 [cite:rs_ch01_acri_bedouin]. The demolitions have continued. In May 2024 Israeli authorities demolished about 47 to 50 homes in the unrecognized village of Wadi al-Khalil; National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir called the buildings illegal constructions and the demolition "an important step" [cite:rs_ch01_almonitor_bedouin] [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin].

Even the number of people affected is disputed. Minority Rights Group puts the population of the unrecognized villages at about 100,000 people in roughly 35 villages [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. Adalah, as reported by Al-Monitor in May 2024, puts it at about half of some 300,000 Negev Bedouin, which is about 150,000 [cite:rs_ch01_almonitor_bedouin]. An ACRI fact sheet from about 2011 gave about 70,000 [cite:rs_ch01_acri_bedouin]. The figures come from different years and partly from advocacy organizations, and the sources do not explain the gap between them. Note also that Adalah's base of some 300,000 refers to the Negev Bedouin, while Minority Rights Group's figure of just over 300,000 refers to the Bedouin of Israel as a whole.

Behind the numbers lies a deeper disagreement over what the villages are. The state and its ministers treat construction in unrecognized villages as illegal building on state land, which is the basis on which demolition orders are issued and on which Ben-Gvir defended Wadi al-Khalil [cite:rs_ch01_almonitor_bedouin] [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. On that view, the law is the 1953 and 1965 statutes, and the land is registered to the state. Bedouin and rights groups answer that the villages either predate the state or were placed where they stand by the state itself, and that 35 of them meet every Central Bureau of Statistics test for a settlement except the minister's declaration [cite:rs_ch01_acri_bedouin] [cite:rs_ch01_mrg_bedouin]. On that view, the Bedouin are a dispossessed indigenous people rather than squatters. One reading of the dispute is that the two sides differ less over what the statutes say than over whether the allocation of land they made was just.

The Samaritans. The smallest of these communities has the longest claimed history. The Samaritans hold that they descend from Israelites who escaped the Assyrian deportation after 722/721 BCE. They keep their own version of the Pentateuch and hold Mount Gerizim sacred; their temple there was destroyed by John Hyrcanus in 128 BCE [cite:rs_ch01_ency_samaritans]. By 1917 the community had fallen to a low of 146 people. In January 2003 it numbered 654: 349 in Holon, inside Israel, and 305 at Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim near Nablus [cite:rs_ch01_ency_samaritans]. The Oslo arrangements placed the Nablus community under Palestinian administration, so that a single community of a few hundred people now lives on both sides of the lines described at the start of this volume [cite:rs_ch01_ency_samaritans].

Palestinian Christians. Palestinians include one of the world's oldest Christian communities. In the early twentieth century Christians were about 12% of Palestine's population. According to the 2017 census, fewer than 50,000 live in the West Bank and East Jerusalem today, between 47,000 and 50,000, or about 1% of the population; most are concentrated in Bethlehem, Ramallah and East Jerusalem [cite:aljazeera_christians_2025] [cite:noonpost_christians]. Gaza's Christians fell from about 3,000 in the early 2000s to about 1,000 before October 2023, and to a few hundred since [cite:aljazeera_christians_2025] [cite:noonpost_christians]. The decline is attributed to war, displacement and economic hardship [cite:aljazeera_christians_2025].

The war since October 2023 reached them directly. On 17 July 2025 an Israeli tank shell struck the compound of the Holy Family Church, Gaza's only Catholic parish, where about 600 Christians and Muslims had taken shelter. Three people were killed and the parish priest was wounded [cite:persecution_holyfamily]. Pope Leo XIV said he was deeply saddened and called for a ceasefire. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said that Israel "deeply regrets" that "stray ammunition" had hit the church, and that the incident was being investigated [cite:persecution_holyfamily] [cite:lorient_pope_holyfamily]. The same month, in the West Bank, arsonists targeted the area of the ancient church of St George in Taybeh, the last all-Christian town there, in what the reporting describes as settler arson [cite:aljazeera_christians_2025]. Munther Isaac, the Lutheran pastor in Bethlehem, has warned that Palestinian Christians risk disappearing from the Holy Land [cite:aljazeera_christians_2025]. Palestinian Christians live under the same occupation and the same war as their Muslim neighbors, and their presence alone is enough to show that "Jews against Muslims" does not describe the conflict. Their fuller story belongs to a later volume [scene:pc_s03|Palestinian Christians, 1900s–2025].

What the record does not settle. Several questions remain open on the sources used here. How many people live in the Negev's unrecognized villages is disputed, with estimates from about 70,000 to about 150,000, and no source explains the gap. Whether those villages are illegal construction on state land or the homes of a dispossessed indigenous people is a question of how the land was allocated after 1948, on which the state and the Bedouin and their advocates disagree. The Druze figures differ slightly between Minority Rights Group's 150,000 and the Central Bureau of Statistics' 152,000. The Circassian account rests on one community-derived source and does not establish when or why the families left the Caucasus. The fate of the Golan Druze offer of citizenship after the first refusals is not covered here. And the figures for Palestinian Christians are drawn from news reporting that cites the 2017 census rather than from the census itself.
