Herzl's Diary: A Note, Not a Programme

The founder of political Zionism left no map. Theodor Herzl's pamphlet Der Judenstaat (1896) weighed Palestine against Argentina, and the programme adopted at the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897 named Palestine without drawing a line around it; both are discussed in [scene:gi1_versions|Part One] and in [scene:s04|the founding of the Zionist Organization]. What Herzl did leave was a diary, kept from 1895 until his death in 1904 and later published in German and in English. Two entries in it bear on borders. Both are real. Neither became the policy of the movement he led, and the second has been misreported in two opposite directions: as proof of a plan to rule from the Nile to the Euphrates, and as a fabrication.

Hechler's maps, 1896

The first entry records someone else's vision. William Hechler, an Anglican clergyman serving as chaplain to the British embassy in Vienna, was a Christian believer in the Jews' return and one of Herzl's earliest patrons. On a train journey, Herzl wrote, Hechler "unfolded his maps of Palestine and instructed me for hours on end." The entry continues: "The northern frontier ought to be the mountains facing Cappadocia; the southern, the Suez Canal. The slogan to be circulated: 'The Palestine of David and Solomon!'" The passage is in the first volume of the English edition of the diaries (1960, p. 342), as quoted in the encyclopaedic literature; this book has not checked it against the printed edition. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] The Palestinian historian H. S. Haddad, writing in the Journal of Palestine Studies in 1974, drew on it to argue that Hechler "gave Herzl the biblical definitions of the boundaries." [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael]

The entry shows what a Christian Restorationist told Herzl and what Herzl wrote down. It does not show that Herzl adopted the frontier, and nothing in his public writings or in the Zionist Organization's programmes of his lifetime repeats it. Haddad's reading, that Herzl took his idea of the state's extent from the biblical kingdom, is an argument built on this entry and should be read as one. The Christian side of the story belongs to [scene:gi11_christian|Part Eleven].

The 1898 note

The second entry is the one quoted most often. In 1898, the year the German Kaiser toured the Ottoman Empire and met Herzl in Jerusalem, Herzl noted a plan to ask the Ottoman sultan for a territory running from the frontier of Egypt to the Euphrates. The scholar who documented it is the same one most often cited for having debunked it. Daniel Pipes, writing in the Middle East Quarterly in 1994, stated: "In 1898, Herzl planned to ask the Ottoman sultan for a territory stretching from the Egyptian frontier to the Euphrates." His footnote gives the source as the German critical edition of the diaries, Zionistisches Tagebücher, 1895–1899, edited by Johannes Wachten, Chaya Harel and others (Berlin: Ullstein, 1983), volume 2, page 650. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny]

Pipes's verdict was narrow. "Herzl did not in fact request the Nile-to-Euphrates region from the Ottoman king or anyone else," he wrote, and he added that "four years later he spoke of settling Jews in Mesopotamia," a different and much smaller idea. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] His article's target was not the diary but the use made of it: the claim, made by Arab and Iranian leaders, that the State of Israel was pursuing a programme to rule from the Nile to the Euphrates, with evidence that included an inscription said to hang over the Knesset, the blue stripes of the flag and the map supposedly stamped on the 10-agora coin. Pipes showed that no one had seen the inscription, that the stripes come from the Jewish prayer shawl and that the coin copies one struck in 37 BCE. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] Those are taken up in [scene:gi10_myths|Part Ten].

Two later accounts in this library went further than Pipes and called the Herzl item itself a fabrication: the Road to 1948 volume states that the line "is not in his writings" and traces it to a 1994 misattribution ([scene:s04|the founding of the Zionist Organization]), and Book Four of the Israel Architecture series described it as "not authentic." Both cite, directly or indirectly, the article that confirms the diary note. On the record available here, that is an error. The note is authentic; what was never true is that Herzl put it to the sultan or that it became anyone's programme. [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny]

The exact wording is less certain than its existence. A version widely quoted in English reads "The area of the Jewish State stretches: 'From the Brook of Egypt to the Euphrates'", attributed to the second volume of the English edition (commonly cited as p. 711). This book could not check that wording or its context against the printed text, including whether the passage records Herzl's own plan or a draft prepared for him. It is therefore not quoted here as Herzl's words. "Brook of Egypt", if that is the phrase, echoes the biblical "river of Egypt" of Genesis 15:18, which many readers take to be the Wadi el-Arish in northern Sinai rather than the Nile; [scene:gi1_phrase|Part One] discusses the difference.

What Herzl actually asked for

The requests Herzl did make were for a charter, and they were limited by what any government would give. He sought from Sultan Abdul Hamid II a charter for Jewish settlement in Palestine; when that failed he turned to Britain, proposing in 1902 a settlement at El-Arish in Sinai and, in 1903, considering the British offer of land in East Africa. The El-Arish scheme died when Egypt's British administration refused to spare Nile water; the East Africa offer split the Sixth Zionist Congress and was rejected in 1905. That sequence is told in [scene:hz_s01|Herzl and the sultan]. A man who considered Argentina, Sinai, East Africa and Mesopotamia within eight years did not hold a fixed territorial programme, and his diary note of 1898 is better read as one of several openings he tried, none of which was put to the sultan in that form.

The finding for this volume is therefore two-sided. A founder of the movement did write down, once, a territorial idea on the scale of the broadest biblical text. That is a fact, and it should not be denied in order to answer those who misuse it. It is also a fact that he did not pursue it, that the organisation he founded never adopted it, and that the next statement of borders the Zionist Organization actually put before the world, at Paris in 1919, was far narrower. That statement is the subject of [scene:gi2_map1919|the next section].
