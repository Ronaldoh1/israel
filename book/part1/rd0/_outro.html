By the eve of the First Zionist Congress in 1897, much was already in place. The hope of return had moved from prayer to program. Two rabbis had argued that human settlement could begin redemption. Moses Hess and Leon Pinsker had made it a political proposal. The Lovers of Zion had met at Kattowitz in 1884, the student group BILU had sent its pioneers, and in 1890 Nathan Birnbaum had given the idea its name: "Zionism."

The pressures behind it were documented too: the pogroms after 1881, the May Laws of 1882 that restricted Jewish life in Russia, and the Dreyfus Affair, which convinced Theodor Herzl that assimilation could not guarantee safety. Yet most of those fleeing Russia chose America, not Palestine. Alternatives had been tried at scale, from Baron de Hirsch's Argentine colonies to Edmond de Rothschild's thirty settlements in Palestine, and neither was a statehood project. Opposition was present from the start, from the socialist Bund, Reform Judaism, the cultural Zionists and Orthodox rabbis.

Around all of this stood older structures: a British state whose Parliament seated the City's Remembrancer, its official lobbyist; banking houses bound to governments and the papacy by contract; a Protestant restorationist lobby; and a Vatican that would refuse. Political Zionism entered that board. It did not build it.
