Ottoman Palestine: Named, Counted and Governed

From 1516 to 1917 the Ottoman Empire governed most of the Arab Middle East as provinces of a single state. Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem and Beirut were provincial cities of the same empire, and people and goods moved between them without passports. There were no international borders between what are now Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, Jordan and Israel and the Palestinian territories, because none of those states yet existed. Iran stood outside this order altogether: it was never Ottoman, and it kept its own dynasty, its own language and a Shia majority.

Inside the empire, law was organized by religion as much as by territory. Under what is called the millet system, recognized religious communities, Muslims, Christians of several churches and Jews, administered their own religious law, their own courts and their own schooling, and governed marriage and inheritance under their own leaders.[cite:w_ottoman_millet] It was not a golden age. Non-Muslims paid special taxes and held a lesser legal status; the empire taxed heavily, conscripted men, and put down revolts by force, and periodic violence was part of its record. The point of describing it is not that the order was just. The point is that it was not the map of rival states that the twentieth century produced. Almost every border that matters in today's conflicts was drawn in a six-year window, between 1916 and 1922, by two European powers, Britain and France.

Two claims about this period circulate widely, and they point in opposite directions. The first holds that before Zionism "Palestine" was an empty, undifferentiated stretch of Ottoman desert with no population or administrative identity of its own, and that the place became real only when the British Mandate invented it. The second holds that the Ottomans occupied Palestine much as settlers later would. The administrative record answers both, and it begins with a technical point that the first claim's defenders are right about.

The Ottomans generally named their provinces after their capital cities. No single Ottoman administrative unit was ever officially called "Palestine" on a map.[cite:wiki_ottoman_palestine] That is accurate, and it is not a concession invented to soften the argument. It is the strongest form of the claim that Palestine "never existed," and it deserves a precise answer rather than a denial. The answer is that a naming convention is a different thing from the absence of a place. The regional name was far older than the Ottomans. Rome designated its province Syria Palaestina in the second century CE, and the name persisted through Byzantine, Crusader and Mamluk rule. Throughout the Ottoman centuries it stayed in standing use in European maps, in travel writing, in religious texts and in Arabic geographic writing, including local Arabic-language sources.[cite:wiki_ottoman_palestine] (The longer history of the name, from the ancient writers onward, is told in the section before this one.) An American comparison helps. There was never a colonial charter for a place officially named "New England" in the way there was a Massachusetts Bay Colony. Massachusetts, Connecticut and Rhode Island were nonetheless real, governed and populated, and "New England" was the regional name in continuous use for the whole of them. Whether a province bore a name and whether the place existed are two different questions.

The technicality is effective as a talking point precisely because it is true. It lets a speaker correct a listener, "Palestine wasn't an official Ottoman province," while implying something much larger: that the place, its people and its government were not real. The record supports the narrow claim and contradicts the broad one. Both statements hold at once.

What the Ottomans did create was an administrative status for this region that was higher than that of most districts in the empire. Ordinarily an Ottoman district, a sanjak, sat inside a larger province, a vilayet, and reported upward through the provincial governor. Jerusalem was taken out of that chain. After an initial separation in 1864, the district of Jerusalem was raised in 1872 to the status of a mutasarrifate: it was governed by a mutasarrif who answered directly to Constantinople, bypassing the provincial governor altogether.[cite:wiki_mutasarrifate_jerusalem] That arrangement was reserved for a small number of especially significant districts across the empire, and Jerusalem held it because of the region's religious and strategic importance, both to the Ottomans themselves and to the Western powers. It ran without interruption until the British occupation in 1917, a span of 45 years.[cite:wiki_mutasarrifate_jerusalem] In plain terms, Jerusalem was one of the places important enough that the capital insisted on governing it directly, with no regional manager in between.

The status needs to be read for what it was. It reflected religious and strategic significance inside an Ottoman imperial framework. It does not show that the Ottomans thought of the territory in the national categories of the twentieth century, Arab, Palestinian or Zionist, and it anticipated none of the politics that came later.[cite:wiki_mutasarrifate_jerusalem]

By 1887–88 the territory that later became Mandate Palestine was organized in three units. In the south lay the independent Sanjak of Jerusalem, the mutasarrifate. In the north lay the Sanjaks of Nablus and Acre, both attached administratively to the larger Vilayet of Beirut. Taken together, these three districts map closely onto the later boundary of the British Mandate.[cite:ips_ottoman_palestine] Over four centuries the arrangement shifted. Across the whole Ottoman period, Palestine is described as having been governed chiefly as the district of Jerusalem together with parts of the provinces of Beirut and Damascus, and from 1831 to 1840 it passed out of Ottoman hands into Egyptian rule before returning. The boundaries were not identical in every detail from decade to decade; administrative lines moved at the margins, as they do in most territories. The continuity that matters is that a bounded, governed region existed, and that its outline was not an arbitrary line drawn later by the British.[cite:ips_ottoman_palestine]

The three districts were inhabited. Their combined population was approximately 600,000 by the late nineteenth century, roughly a decade before political Zionism existed as an organized movement.[cite:ips_ottoman_palestine] The figure is an approximation and is offered as one. Nineteenth-century imperial counting was real but imperfect, and "roughly 600,000" claims no precision to the individual. Other counts from the period give lower totals: a census of 1871–72, discussed in [scene:s00i|the section on peoples and identity], and the reconstructions from Ottoman registers set out in the next section, which left out foreign nationals. What all of them show is a counted population, not one that appeared from nothing. The counting continued across the transition from Ottoman to British administration between 1917 and 1922, which relabeled one continuous population rather than introducing a new one.[cite:ips_ottoman_palestine]

That population lived in named places. The administrative record itself names the towns governed under this system, among them Jerusalem, Gaza, Jaffa, Hebron, Nablus, Haifa, Acre, Safed and Nazareth. All nine are populated cities today. An 1885 British encyclopedia entry on the region gives a more granular count from the same period: 753 distinct named places, with more than 54,000 houses, meaning families, across the Ottoman districts.[cite:britannica_history_palestine] That entry is a contemporary, independent count, not a later reconstruction. It does not show that the settlement pattern was fixed. Populations and settlements shifted over four centuries of Ottoman rule, as they do everywhere. It does show a named and inhabited land.

Who were those people? Most were Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians. Alongside them lived long-established Jewish communities in Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias, whose life is the subject of the next section. Most were Ottoman subjects, though many Jewish immigrants were foreign nationals.

This bears on the second claim, that the Ottomans occupied Palestine in the way later settlers did. The precise term for Ottoman rule is imperial administration. The empire ruled through governors, garrisons and taxation, over a population that stayed what it had been. It did not replace that population with its own. That is the distinction between an empire and a settler project, and the Ottoman record falls on the imperial side of it. The same record answers the "empty backwater" claim from the other direction: an empire does not post a governor answering directly to its capital over a district with no one in it.

The Ottoman Land Code of 1858 is told in the next volume, in [scene:ot_s01|the Ottoman Land Code].

Ottoman rule ended in war. British forces captured Jerusalem in December 1917, and the Mutasarrifate's long run ended with the occupation. The legal end came six years later. The Republic of Turkey, founded in 1923, renounced all rights over the former Arab provinces in the Treaty of Lausanne that year. Modern Turkey is therefore the empire's successor state, not a claimant to the land.[cite:w_lausanne]

The Arab majority that the Ottomans governed was still the majority when British rule ended. British Mandate figures for 1947 counted about 1.3 million Arabs and 630,000 Jews in Palestine. On 29 November 1947 the United Nations adopted Resolution 181, which proposed a Jewish state on 56% of the land and an Arab state on 44%, with Jerusalem under international administration, at a time when Jewish ownership amounted to approximately 7% of the land. The Jewish Agency accepted the plan and the Arab League rejected it. In the war of 1948 approximately 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled, the event Palestinians call the Nakba, "the catastrophe." From the 1980s, Israeli scholars working in newly declassified Israeli military archives, among them Benny Morris, Ilan Pappé, Avi Shlaim and Simha Flapan, documented that the refugee crisis involved both flight from war and systematic expulsions, complicating the earlier official Israeli account that all the refugees left voluntarily. Morris and Pappé draw different moral conclusions from the same evidence, but both agree that expulsions occurred. The overall picture is documented; the breakdown of each village's depopulation, as expulsion or as flight, is not settled and varies by place and by historian. That argument is followed in [scene:hd_s01|how historians argue about 1948].

Several things remain open. The figure of about 600,000 is an approximation, and the period's counts do not agree with one another; how far apart they are, and why, depends on whom each count included. The administrative lines of the three districts moved over the decades, and how closely they matched the Mandate's border at any given date is a matter of degree. And the Mutasarrifate's elevated status tells us how Constantinople valued Jerusalem, not how the people who lived in the district understood themselves. That last question, of identity, is a separate one, and the record on it has to be read on its own terms.
