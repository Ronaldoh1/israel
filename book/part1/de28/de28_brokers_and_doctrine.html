Buy It, Don't Warrant It: The Law Around the Stack

The vendors in the previous section sell to ICE because the law lets ICE buy. This section sets out the legal doctrine that makes the purchases possible, what the intelligence community's own advisers said about it, what Congress has and has not done, and then the other half of the system, the tools by which agencies identify people who have not been charged with anything: administrative subpoenas, social-media vetting and the policy a federal court found unconstitutional in 2025.

The doctrine: commercially available information

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and generally requires a warrant, a judge's order based on probable cause, before the government searches a person's private things. Under the third-party doctrine, set by the Supreme Court in the 1970s, records a person hands to a business, such as bank records or the numbers dialled from a phone, lose that protection. In Carpenter v. United States (2018) the Court refused to extend the doctrine to historical phone-location records, and on 29 June 2026, in Chatrie v. United States, it held that obtaining even two hours of a person's location history from Google was a search. Both rulings concern data the government compels a company to hand over; neither addresses data the government simply buys. [cite:ow9_cornell_tpd] [cite:ow9_pw_chatrie] The library's account of these cases is in [scene:ow9_ice|The One-Way Mirror] and [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Six].

Into that gap falls "commercially available information," the government's term for data anyone can buy: location trails from apps, people-search profiles, licence-plate reads, social-media archives. Agencies have taken the view that buying it is not a search. An internal ICE legal analysis concluded that Webloc's phone-location data "can be queried without a warrant," and in March 2026 the FBI director told the Senate: "We do purchase commercially available information that is consistent with the Constitution and the laws." [cite:de25_404_webloc] [cite:de25_tc_patel] That position has not been tested in the Supreme Court.

What the intelligence community's own advisers said

The clearest official description of the stakes came from inside government. A senior advisory group to the Director of National Intelligence reported in January 2022, in a document declassified on 12 June 2023, that intelligence agencies were acquiring "a significant amount" of commercially available information, and that such data "can reveal sensitive and intimate information about individuals." [cite:de28_lawfare_cai] It could be used, the report said, to "cause harm to an individual's reputation, emotional well-being, or physical safety," and it now covered nearly everyone, supplying details that once would have required a targeted investigation to obtain. [cite:de28_fortune_cai] It recommended cataloguing what agencies bought and writing standards for its use; it did not recommend a warrant requirement. [cite:de28_lawfare_cai]

DHS's own inspector general found in September 2023 that ICE, Customs and Border Protection and the Secret Service had bought and used phone-location data without completing the privacy impact assessments that the E-Government Act of 2002 requires, and recommended that ICE stop using it until they were approved. [cite:de28_oig_23_61] ICE's return to the market through Penlink in 2025 drew a letter from seventy members of Congress in March 2026 asking the inspector general to investigate; ICE said it "does not purchase illegal information." [cite:de28_reg_wyden70]

What Congress did

The House of Representatives passed the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act on 17 April 2024, by 219 votes to 199, with members of both parties in the majority and over the Biden White House's opposition. It would have required a court order before law-enforcement and intelligence agencies buy Americans' data. The Senate did not take it up and it died with that Congress. [cite:de28_pogo_4th] [cite:ow9_cs_4thact] On 14 April 2026 Representative Warren Davidson, an Ohio Republican, offered an amendment to the bill reauthorising Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act that would "prohibit the federal government from purchasing Americans' private information and data from third-party brokers." [cite:de28_davidson_2026] No reauthorisation was enacted; Section 702 lapsed on 12 June 2026, while collection continued under existing court certifications, as [scene:us10_us|US vs Israel] describes. [cite:us10_eff_702lapse] As of October 2026 no federal statute requires a warrant before an agency buys data it could not compel without one. Montana became the first state to pass such a rule, for its own agencies, in May 2025; it does not bind federal ones ([scene:de26_counterweights|Part Twenty-Eight]). [cite:de28_reason_montana]

Unmasking critics: the administrative subpoena

An administrative subpoena is a demand for records that an agency can issue on its own authority, without a judge. It cannot reach the content of messages, but it can reach a user's name, address, IP address and session times. [cite:de28_eff_letter] Three documented cases show how DHS used it in 2025. On 31 March 2025 Homeland Security Investigations subpoenaed Google for the account of Amandla Thomas-Johnson, a Cornell doctoral student on a visa, citing a provision on documents relating to a person's right to enter or reside in the country; Google complied without first telling him and produced basic subscriber information. [cite:de28_int_thomasjohnson] In early September 2025 DHS asked Meta for the "names, email addresses, and phone numbers" behind six Instagram accounts that had named a Border Patrol agent during raids in Los Angeles; a magistrate judge ordered Meta not to comply, and DHS withdrew the demands before any ruling. [cite:de28_int_meta] [cite:de28_eff_letter] And a Philadelphia-area man who emailed a DHS lawyer urging "common sense and decency" in an Afghan asylum case was the subject of a subpoena to Google issued four hours later; two DHS agents and a local police officer came to his home to question him, before Google had produced anything. The ACLU moved to quash it in February 2026 and announced on 10 February that DHS had withdrawn it. [cite:de28_aclu_doe]

The scale is unknown. Google reported 28,622 subpoenas of all kinds in the first half of 2025 and Meta 14,520, but neither breaks them out by agency. On 10 February 2026 the Electronic Frontier Foundation and the ACLU of Northern California asked ten companies to require a court order before complying with DHS subpoenas, to notify users early and to resist gag orders. [cite:de28_eff_letter]

Speech as a ground: vetting and AAUP v. Rubio

For non-citizens, speech has become a formal factor in decisions. On 9 April 2025 US Citizenship and Immigration Services said it would screen applicants' social media for antisemitic activity and support for named terrorist groups as grounds to deny green cards and student benefits; the Council on American-Islamic Relations and J Street, a group that calls itself pro-Israel and pro-peace, both said it threatened free speech. [cite:de28_npr_uscis] A State Department cable of 18 June 2025 told consular officers to require student-visa applicants to make their social media public and to look for "hostile attitudes toward our citizens, culture, government, institutions, or founding principles." [cite:de28_reuters_visa]

The test case for where this leads was AAUP v. Rubio. At the trial in July 2025, Peter Hatch of DHS's intelligence office testified that a "Tiger Team" of analysts had produced more than a hundred reports on foreign students and academics, drawing names from a list of about 5,000 compiled by Canary Mission, a privately run website that profiles campus activists; he said he had never before been asked for reports on foreign students. [cite:de28_crimson_hatch] On 30 September 2025 Judge William Young found the government's policy unconstitutional and said it had "chilled the speech of non-citizen academics"; in January 2026 he vacated it, presumed retaliation in any immigration action against the plaintiff groups' members, and stayed their removal. The government appealed to the First Circuit, which had not ruled as of October 2026. [cite:de28_ch_aaup] The domestic-terrorism directive that names ideas as indicators is in [scene:de30_nspm7_and_detachment|Part Thirty-Two].

If it were connected

Documented: each link from a post to a doorstep exists separately; in the Philadelphia case a critical email led within hours to a subpoena and within weeks to agents at the door, though no record shows that the address came from a broker or a platform. [cite:de28_aclu_doe] Possible, with no document: that subpoena returns and vetting flags are loaded into the same case systems as broker data, and beyond that a standing rule that runs every flagged post through people-search and location data automatically; either would need only an internal procedure, not a new law. What stands in the way is narrow: a judge who blocked one subpoena, withdrawals of others once challenged, a vacated policy under appeal, and no statute.
