The Yemenite Children Affair

The young state's treatment of its own immigrants is part of the founding record too, and one episode in particular has been contested inside Israel for decades. A common view holds that Israel's founding generation treated all Jewish immigrants equally. The record of the transit camps, and of the children who disappeared from them, complicates that view.

Jews who arrived from Arab and Muslim countries after 1948 were often placed in transit camps, the ma'abarot, shantytown settlements for new arrivals. According to the Times of Israel, these immigrants were largely sidelined by the European-born leadership of the ruling Labor party. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021]

It was in these camps and in the clinics serving them that the affair began. Families, mostly from Yemen but also from the Balkans, North Africa and elsewhere, say that between 1948 and 1954 their infants were taken from camps and clinics. Doctors told the parents that the children had died, but refused to give them bodies or death certificates. The families allege that the babies were given to Ashkenazi families in Israel and abroad. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] The children who vanished in this way are counted in the hundreds.

The state investigated more than once. Three official inquiries, the first in 1967, concluded that most of the children had died of disease. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] That conclusion has not ended the dispute. The newspaper Israel Hayom later published documents that it said indicated possible organized involvement by state bodies. [cite:israelhayom_yemenite] Those documents, as described, point to a possibility rather than an established finding, and the official inquiries and the families' accounts remain in conflict.

The government's formal response came in February 2021. The cabinet approved NIS 162 million, about $50 million, in compensation for the families, with about 1,050 families eligible. Its decision stated that the government "regrets" the events and recognizes the suffering of the families. [cite:tps_yemenite_2021] [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] Prime Minister Netanyahu described the affair as among the most painful in the state's history and asked that it be taught in schools. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021]

The families' organizations were not satisfied. They said the decision lacked the essential element, an official apology and an acceptance of responsibility. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] The distinction between regret and responsibility is the heart of the matter: the compensation acknowledged suffering without the state accepting responsibility for what had happened.

The affair belongs in an account of 1948 and its aftermath for a reason that goes beyond the individual cases. It is documented evidence of a hierarchy among Jews inside the new state: an Ashkenazi establishment and Mizrahi immigrants placed in camps under its administration. Readers raised in Israel often learn this part of the record late. It sits alongside the laws governing the state's Arab citizens, described in [scene:ap_s01|the Absentees' Property Law], as a reminder that consolidating the state meant deciding how different groups of its people would be treated, and that those decisions were not uniform.
