22 November 1963: A Question of Timing

On 22 November 1963 President Kennedy was killed in Dallas. At that moment two disputes between his administration and Israel's institutions were unresolved: the Justice Department's order that the American Zionist Council register as a foreign agent, and the arrangement for American scientists to visit the reactor at Dimona. Two simple accounts of what followed are in circulation. One holds that nothing in American policy toward Israel changed, and that Johnson carried on exactly as Kennedy had set things. The other holds that both disputes collapsed on the day Kennedy died. The record supports neither in that simple form. This section sets out where each dispute stood that week, what the documents show happened to each afterwards, and what the record says, and does not say, about any connection between them and the President's death. The date enters this history as a fact of timing. Nothing in the documents reached for this book makes it more than that.

The foreign-agents order, November 1963. A year after the Justice Department's order of November 1962, the American Zionist Council had not registered, by the account of nearly every source; a single 2016 version of a Wikipedia article says otherwise, and that dispute is set out in [scene:s11a|the FARA order]. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_azc_2016] The last active step toward enforcement, as Grant F. Smith of IRmep, an advocate of registration, reports it, came in October 1963, when the FBI offered to help enforce the order as evidence of foreign control accumulated. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] [cite:antiwar_where_did_aipac_come_from] The Senate inquiry that had examined the Jewish Agency's payments to the Council in the summer was still formally open: its mandate ran to 31 January 1964, when its report was due, and its published record was still being compiled in late 1963, with a sworn letter dated 21 October 1963 printed in its final part. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26] Smith also reports that, before November, a Wall Street Journal story described Justice Department officials weighing the risk of offending Jewish opinion, which prompted letters to Congress and to Robert F. Kennedy urging enforcement. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] On his account, then, the order stood that week with an offer of help from the FBI behind it and hesitation inside the department that issued it.

Dimona, November 1963. The inspection question had been settled in principle three months earlier, in the exchange told in Eshkol's answer. Levi Eshkol's reply of 19 August 1963 accepted regular visits in principle but not the twice-yearly interval Kennedy had asked for; Kennedy's reply of 26 August assumed "basic agreement on regular visits"; and a memorandum of 16 September recorded Israel's agreement to a "thorough" visit, including the "plutonium separation plant," before the reactor's activation. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] In the week of 22 November that visit had not yet been held. Its terms, and the point Eshkol had declined, had been fixed while Kennedy was alive.

The assassination. President John F. Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas, Texas, on 22 November 1963. Two official bodies inquired into the killing and its causes: the Warren Commission, whose investigation of 1964 concluded that Lee Harvey Oswald acted alone, and the House Select Committee on Assassinations in 1979. Neither identified Israeli nuclear policy, Dimona or the dispute with the American Zionist Council as a factor in the assassination. [cite:wiki_warren_commission]

Dimona after November. The visit promised for before criticality did not take place before it. By the Israeli account the reactor went critical on 26 December 1963; the Hebrew website of the Israel Atomic Energy Commission gives July 1963 for the start of operations. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The first visit under the 1963 arrangement was held on 18 January 1964. William Burr and Avner Cohen, the editors of the declassified Kennedy–Ben-Gurion–Eshkol letters, write that it "may have been delayed because of Kennedy's assassination." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That is the only connection the editors draw between the assassination and Dimona, and they put it as a possibility about a delay of weeks, not as a finding about policy. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The demand itself continued. Cohen and Burr write that Johnson, like Kennedy, pressed for inspections to verify Eshkol's pledge that Dimona was for peaceful purposes. [cite:rs_cw5_cns_duplicity] The State Department's own historians record that the Johnson administration kept insisting on the visits begun under Kennedy, and that the visitors found no evidence of a weapons program while suspicions continued. [cite:rs_cw5_frus6468_summary] How hard Johnson pressed is a matter of reading. The journalist Seymour Hersh, in The Samson Option (1991), describes Johnson as personally opposed to Israel acquiring nuclear weapons, holding that "a nuclear Israel was unacceptable," but pursuing the point with markedly less sustained pressure than Kennedy had applied through 1963. The National Security Archive's 2020 release of records from 1964–65 presents visits that satisfied Washington enough to keep the wider relationship in place without the unrestricted access Kennedy's letters had demanded. [cite:nsarchive_duplicity_1964_65] The visits continued every year through the decade; the sources count them as six and as seven. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_majalla] By January 1965 the outside consultant Henry Kissinger was privately telling American diplomats of his near-certainty that Israel had begun weapons development. [cite:nsarchive_duplicity_1964_65] What those visits saw and missed belongs to the years after this stage, and is told in [scene:s11d|Johnson's inspections].

The foreign-agents order after November. Smith writes that after 22 November 1963 the effort to enforce the order against the American Zionist Council "gradually falls apart." He cites no document recording a decision to drop it. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast] The final outcome is not in doubt, whatever its cause: the Council was never ultimately compelled to register under the Act. Where the sources date a restructuring of its lobbying function to early 1963, that restructuring stood, unchallenged to completion by any further federal enforcement action. [cite:antiwar_where_did_aipac_come_from] On the account Volume II drew on, the registration pressure dissolved after the assassination, which is a correlation of timing and not proof of cause or of anyone's intent, and it did not resume under later administrations at comparable intensity until decades later. [cite:crustnews_aipac_history] Smith states elsewhere that the Justice Department kept the order secret until 2008 and has never tried to enforce it since, and he calls the effort "serious, but fizzled out." [cite:rs_cw5_smith_ipa_2022] Kobby Barda, writing in Modern Judaism in 2026, explains the same outcome differently. He argues that the matter had become moot: the lobby had separated from the Jewish Agency's money, the Agency's subscription purchases having ended in 1962, and operated lawfully as a domestic lobby, so there was nothing left to enforce. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The two explanations differ on why enforcement stopped. They agree on what the record lacks: neither cites a Johnson administration document recording a decision.

Two different kinds of claim. Claims that join these disputes to 22 November 1963 are of two kinds, and they need to be kept apart, because the evidence for each is different.

The first kind is a claim about consequences: that Kennedy's death changed what happened to the two disputes afterwards. The editors' remark about the January visit is one such claim, made cautiously. Smith's "gradually falls apart" places a change after the date. Philip Giraldi, writing in The American Conservative in 2017, says that the Council "was pressured" by Kennedy's Justice Department in 1962 and 1963, and that "Kennedy's death stopped that effort." [cite:rs_cw5_giraldi_2017] Said Arikat, in a news analysis for Al-Quds of 30 May 2026, writes that after the assassination the pressure "steadily lost momentum" under Johnson and was "never meaningfully revived." [cite:rs_cw5_arikat_2026] Giraldi's and Arikat's statements assert a sequence. Neither cites a document of a decision, or a source attributing the change to the assassination. [cite:rs_cw5_giraldi_2017] [cite:rs_cw5_arikat_2026] These are claims about the effect of a change of president on policy, and claims of that kind can in principle be tested against the record. On the record reached here, the Dimona demand continued under Johnson, and the end of the foreign-agents effort is described, by Smith and Barda alike, without any document of a decision.

The second kind is a claim about causes: that the disputes over Dimona or the foreign-agents order had some part in the killing itself. Such claims exist. Barda writes that recent conspiracy claims allege that AIPAC was involved in the assassination. He calls the theory "baseless," and says that a review of the released files found "no evidence whatsoever" linking AIPAC to it. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] His only named source for the spread of these claims is a report of the Combat Antisemitism Movement, dated 6 March 2025, on JFK conspiracy theorists and bot activity; he names no individual who makes them. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] Barda places the 1963 Fulbright hearing "in the shadow of" these claims, and identifies Smith as the leading researcher on the foreign-agents question; he does not attribute any assassination claim to Smith. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] Neither does this book attribute one to Smith, Giraldi or Arikat: the passages of theirs reached here make statements about what happened to policy after 22 November, not about why Kennedy was killed.

No source reached for this book presents evidence for a claim of the second kind. The two official inquiries named above did not identify either dispute as a factor. [cite:wiki_warren_commission] The editors of the Dimona documents connect the assassination to Dimona only through the possible delay of a single visit. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Avner Cohen, whose work is considered the standard academic reference on Israel's nuclear history, and Seymour Hersh both document the Kennedy–Israel nuclear dispute in detail without asserting or implying a connection to the assassination. Claims that the killing was connected to Dimona or to the lobby are therefore reported here as what they are: claimed, and unsupported by any evidence in the record. Barda's evidence that they spread comes from a report by the Combat Antisemitism Movement; this book reports them only as claims, and extends them to no people and no community.

Testing the timing claim. The method in the panel above asks two questions of any claim, and a third about what has been left out. Applied to the claim of consequence, it gives a divided answer. Is the fact true? The dated facts are. Kennedy died on 22 November 1963; the last enforcement step Smith reports came in October; the first Dimona visit came in January 1964; the order was not enforced to completion. Does it prove the conclusion? No. A sequence of dates does not by itself show that one event produced another, and for the foreign-agents order the record holds no document of a decision to stop, while Barda offers a different explanation that does not depend on the date at all. What is left out? For Dimona, the fact that the terms Kennedy was unable to obtain, a visit every six months, had already been declined in August, while he was alive, and the fact that Johnson went on demanding visits. For the foreign-agents order, Smith's own report of hesitation inside the Justice Department before November. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_cns_duplicity] [cite:rs_cw5_smith_podcast]

Applied to the claim of cause, the method stops at the second question. The first fact, the coincidence of dates, is true. Nothing in the record carries it to the conclusion, and the official inquiries and the historians of the period give no support to it. [cite:wiki_warren_commission] [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026]

What changed under Johnson. Set against the two simple accounts this section began with, the record shows something between them. Policy did not continue "exactly" as before: the foreign-agents order was never enforced to completion, and Hersh describes less sustained pressure on Dimona, and the 2020 release describes visits without the unrestricted access Kennedy had asked for. Nor did both disputes collapse on one day. The Dimona demand continued, the visits began in January 1964 and went on for the rest of the decade, and the end of the foreign-agents effort came without a recorded date or decision. How Johnson inherited and handled Kennedy's two lines of policy toward Israel and Egypt is the opening subject of the next stage, in [scene:cw6_johnson_eshkol|Johnson and Eshkol].

The record leaves open, on the foreign-agents order, who in the Justice Department decided, or allowed, that it would not be enforced, and when; no document of that decision is cited by Smith or by Barda, or was found in this book's research. On Dimona, whether the January visit was in fact delayed by the assassination is put by the editors only as a possibility, and the date the reactor began operating is itself given two ways. The details of the two official inquiries are taken here from a single reference summary and have not been re-verified for this book. What the record does show is plain. There is no document, official finding or historical study among the sources reached that connects Kennedy's death to Dimona or to the foreign-agents dispute as a cause. The record is silent on any such link, and this book says so in those words. It shows two real, independently documented policy disputes and one real, independently investigated assassination, close together in time. It does not show, and no credible investigation or historian has shown, that these threads are causally connected. Setting documented facts side by side is not the same as claiming that they caused each other, and this book states that distinction directly rather than leaving it to inference.
