The Map: Israel, the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem

Most confusion about this conflict starts with geography. Four names recur through every volume of this book: Israel, the West Bank, Gaza and East Jerusalem. In everyday speech, and in a good deal of public argument, they are used as though they were one place under different names, or as though the differences among them were a technicality. They are not. Each is a specific territory with its own history since 1948, and each lives today under its own legal regime. When later chapters say "the West Bank," "Area C," "Gaza" or "East Jerusalem," they mean a particular piece of ground under a particular rule, and most of the conflict's arguments turn out to be arguments about where these lines run and who governs on either side of them.

The first line is the one from which the others are measured. Israel, in the sense used throughout this book, is the state within the 1949 armistice lines, the boundary commonly called the "Green Line." The West Bank is the territory east of that line. Jordan held it from 1948 to 1967, and Israel has occupied it since 1967. What "occupation" means in international law, and who has found what about it, is the subject of [scene:pr_s03|the section on the three contested legal words]; here the word describes the status the record assigns to the territory.

The internal map of the West Bank was drawn in 1995, by the interim agreement known as Oslo II. It divided the territory into three zones, as a temporary arrangement pending a final agreement.[cite:wiki_oslo_areas] Area A, about 18 percent of the West Bank, was placed under Palestinian Authority civil and security control. It contains the major cities: Ramallah, Bethlehem, Nablus, Jenin, Tulkarem, Qalqilya, Jericho and most of Hebron. Area B, about 22 percent, came under Palestinian civil control with joint security control. Area C, about 61 percent, remained under full Israeli control.[cite:anera_areas_abc] Areas A and B together hold some 2.8 million Palestinians.

The mechanism matters as much as the percentages. The accord divided authority by function, not only by territory: in Area A the Palestinian Authority held both civil and security powers; in Area B it held civil powers while security was shared; in Area C Israel held both. The zones were meant to be a stage, with the divisions transferred gradually to the Palestinians.[cite:wiki_oslo_areas] They never were. Since 2002 the Israeli army has entered Area A routinely, so that even the zone of fullest Palestinian control is not closed to Israeli forces. An arrangement written as transitional became, in practice, the lasting shape of the territory.

Area C is where the land is. Every Israeli settlement outside East Jerusalem lies within it. About 60 percent of Area C is designated as firing zones, military land, state land or nature reserves, and Palestinian building permits there are rarely granted.[cite:wiki_area_c] [cite:anera_areas_abc] The result is a territory in which most Palestinians live in the smaller zones, while most of the open land, and all of the settlements outside Jerusalem, sit in the zone under Israeli control. When later chapters discuss settlement growth or the prospects of a Palestinian state, the ground at stake is mostly Area C. In all, about 737,000 Israeli settlers live in East Jerusalem and the West Bank.

Gaza is a strip of land on the Mediterranean coast with a different history. Egypt held it from 1948 to 1967, and Israel occupied it from 1967. In 2005 Israel withdrew its settlers from Gaza but kept control of its borders, airspace and coast.[cite:jerusalemstory_area_a] [cite:minorityrights_palestine] The distinction between removing settlers and removing control runs through every later argument about Gaza's legal status, and the record supports both halves of it: the settlers left, and the borders, airspace and coast stayed under Israeli control.

East Jerusalem is the fourth place, and the one whose status is most directly disputed between Israeli and international law. Israel occupied it in 1967 and then annexed it, a step that has not been recognized internationally. Roughly 360,000 Palestinians live there.[cite:jerusalemstory_area_a] [cite:minorityrights_palestine]

How East Jerusalem's status came to be set in two contradictory ways is a story of three dates. A familiar claim holds that everyone recognizes Jerusalem as Israel's capital; its mirror image holds that no one should. Neither is what the record shows. In July 1980 the Knesset passed a Basic Law declaring "Jerusalem, complete and united," the capital of Israel. The law covered East Jerusalem, captured in 1967. In Israeli law, that is where the matter has stood since.

The international response came within weeks. On 20 August 1980 the United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 478 by 14 votes to none, with the United States abstaining. The resolution censured the Israeli law "in the strongest terms," declared measures altering Jerusalem's status "null and void," and called on states that had diplomatic missions in the city to withdraw them.[cite:unsc478_text] [cite:un_photo_478] After Costa Rica and El Salvador moved their embassies out in 2006, no country kept one in Jerusalem.[cite:wiki_unsc478]

The change came from Washington. Following President Trump's announcement in December 2017, the United States opened its embassy in Jerusalem on 14 May 2018, the first embassy in the city since 2006.[cite:wiki_unsc478] Most countries still keep their embassies in Tel Aviv. Under the Oslo Accords, the city's final status was designated as a matter for negotiation between the parties.

The two positions, set side by side, are each accurately stated on their own terms. Israel holds that Jerusalem is its capital under its own law, and the talking point that Jerusalem is Israel's undivided capital describes that law correctly. The Security Council declared that law null and void, and most of the world does not recognize the annexation. Both facts are documented. What the record does not contain is a single legal answer accepted by both systems, and the Oslo framework, by leaving the city's final status to negotiation, put off that answer rather than giving it.

The same is true of the map as a whole. The divisions of 1995 were written as temporary, and the record shows that they have lasted three decades without the transfer they anticipated or the final agreement that was to replace them. What the permanent lines will be, if any are ever agreed, the record cannot say. What it can say is where the lines are now and who governs on each side of them. Who lives inside them, and under which of these regimes, is the subject of [scene:pr_s02|the next section].
