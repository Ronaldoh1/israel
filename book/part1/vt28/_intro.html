Part Twenty-Eight moves from the ancient texts to the medieval movement in which Rome first met organised dualist Christianity, and to the institutions it built in response. The first section sets out what the Good Men, whom their enemies named Cathars, taught and practised, as far as their own surviving ritual and their prosecutors' records agree: two principles, souls trapped in matter, one sacrament given by the laying on of hands, a clergy of men and women who swore no oaths and took no tithes, and a sharp critique of the Roman clergy and its sacraments. The second follows the Albigensian Crusade from the murder of a papal legate in 1208 through the sack of Béziers in 1209 to the burning at Montségur in 1244, testing the famous quotations and death tolls against their sources. The third traces how popes, councils and kings built a standing procedure against heresy between 1184 and 1252, including the authorisation of torture, and what its registers show of the last Good Men, who were gone by the 1320s.
