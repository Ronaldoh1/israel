The Lobby and the Law: Lobbying, Foreign Agents and the 1963 Question

Part One listed the laws. This part turns to how they passed: the organized advocacy around Congress, the money that entered elections, and the votes themselves. It begins with the law that governs advocacy, because the central argument about the best-known pro-Israel organization, the American Israel Public Affairs Committee, is a legal one. American law treats a citizen lobbying for a foreign-policy cause very differently from an agent acting for a foreign government, and the question of which side of that line AIPAC stands on has been argued since 1962. The Follow the Money volume of this library tells AIPAC's institutional history in [scene:pm2_origins|from a one-man office to the largest foreign-policy lobby]; this section sets out only the legal frame and the documented record of how it has been applied.

Two disclosure regimes. The Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938 requires registration by anyone who acts "at the order, request, or under the direction or control" of a foreign principal and engages in political activity for it. Lobbyists registered under the Lobbying Disclosure Act are exempt from FARA, but the exemption does not apply where a foreign government or foreign political party is the principal beneficiary. [cite:ow2_doj_faq] The difference matters. A FARA registrant must disclose its foreign principal, its activities and its payments in detail and label its informational materials; a domestic lobby discloses only its quarterly lobbying spending and the issues it lobbied on.

The 1962 order and the 1963 inquiry. AIPAC's founder, Isaiah L. Kenen, formed his committee in 1953 as a body separate from the American Zionist Council, partly because of concern about foreign-agent registration; it took the AIPAC name in 1959 by most accounts. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The concern was tested in the early 1960s. On 21 November 1962 the Justice Department ordered the American Zionist Council, not AIPAC, to register as a foreign agent, alleging that it had received more than $5 million from the Jewish Agency, a body already registered under FARA; the Senate record carries a figure of $5,100,001.02 for 1955–1962. [cite:jta_fulbright_jewish_agency_hearing] The Senate Foreign Relations Committee under J. William Fulbright examined the payments in 1963, as part of a wider inquiry into the agents of foreign principals. Kobby Barda's 2026 study of the hearing record dates the funding inquiry to 2 August 1963 and reports testimony that the Agency paid $38,000 for subscriptions to Kenen's Near East Report through the Council; Kenen stated in an affidavit that the Council's purchases never exceeded 23 per cent of the newsletter's copies. Barda gives the Council's total receipts from the Agency as $1.5 million and concludes that the separation between the Agency and Kenen was "absolute," which is his interpretation. [cite:ow2_barda_2026] The two totals, $5.1 million and $1.5 million, have not been reconciled in any source consulted. The full account of the order and the hearing is in [scene:s11a|the order of 21 November 1962] and [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the Senate].

The position since. AIPAC has never registered under FARA. Its position is that it is an American organization, registered as a lobby and funded by private donations, that receives no financial assistance from Israel or any other foreign group. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] Fulbright in the 1970s and the former CIA officer Victor Marchetti in the 1980s argued that it should register anyway. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] On the documents consulted, no public record shows the Justice Department ordering AIPAC itself to register, and no court has found it to be acting at the direction or control of the Israeli government. The argument for registration rests on structure and coordination; the argument against rests on the statutory test of direction or control, which requires more than shared aims.

The weakness of FARA enforcement in general is documented and should be kept in view. A Justice Department Inspector General audit published on 7 September 2016 found that 62 per cent of new registrants had filed late and half of the six-monthly supplemental statements were late, called the compliance rates "unacceptable," and stressed the need for "a comprehensive Department enforcement strategy on FARA." [cite:ow2_pogo_oig2016] Non-registration by an American advocacy group is therefore not, on its own, evidence of special treatment; the Act was weakly enforced across the board.

What AIPAC is, and spends. AIPAC is a 501(c)(4) social-welfare organization; donations to it are not tax-deductible, and its Forms 990 report revenue of $156.4 million in FY2024 and $122.5 million in FY2025. [cite:pm_pp_aipac990] Its lobbying disclosures are modest by comparison: about $3.5 million in 2018, roughly 147th among lobbying organizations that year, and about $3.3 million in 2024 and almost $3.8 million in 2025. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] [cite:pm_cjn_lobby2025] The gap between a small lobbying line and a large budget is examined in [scene:pm2_lobbying|the lobbying]: lobbying disclosures record what an organization pays lobbyists, not the time its members spend meeting their own representatives, and they do not record electoral money at all. AIPAC states its aim as persuading "the U.S. government to enact specific policies" that support the relationship, and it counted among its achievements of 2023–24 an emergency package of $14.3 billion and what it called the largest funding package in Israel's history. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024]

The legal frame, then, is clear even where its application is argued. AIPAC lobbies as a domestic organization; the foreign-agent question was raised in 1962 against a different body and has not been resolved against AIPAC in any proceeding consulted.
