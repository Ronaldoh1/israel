Who Recognizes a Nation?

The United States recognized Israel on the day it was declared. The decision is sometimes remembered as an act of Congress, as if the House and Senate had voted the new state into American favour. They did not. Recognition came in a short press release from the White House, issued on the authority of one office, and Congress had no vote on it. How that power came to rest with one office, how Truman used it, and how other governments answered the same question in 1948 and 1949 together show what recognition did and did not decide.

The constitutional basis is a single clause. Article II, Section 3 provides that the President "shall receive Ambassadors and other public Ministers." [cite:const_art2s3] In Zivotofsky v. Kerry (2015) the Supreme Court held that the clause gives the President the exclusive power to recognize foreign sovereigns. [cite:scotus_zivotofsky] The ruling came sixty-seven years after 1948, so it does not describe a rule that anyone cited at the time. It states the law as it now stands, and it confirms that the power Truman used was his alone.

He used it on 14 May 1948. Eleven minutes after Israel's proclamation, he announced de facto recognition in a short press release. [cite:truman_recognition] De facto recognition accepted the new government as the authority actually in control. Full legal recognition, de jure, followed on 31 January 1949. [cite:jvl_dejure_1949] In one account in this library, the second step came only after Truman's election victory. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] Two accounts in this library give the time of the first announcement as 6:11 p.m. Washington time. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The Declaration had been read in Tel Aviv at four in the afternoon, and the Mandate expired at midnight. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] The sources do not say whether the eleven minutes are counted from the reading or from the moment the state came into being at the Mandate's end. The figure is exact; its starting point is not stated.

The speed was arranged. Clark Clifford, the President's counsel, managed the timing against the objection of the State Department, which was not told in advance. The American delegation at the United Nations was caught out too: at that moment it was still pressing the trusteeship proposal that Washington had put forward in March as an alternative to partition. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The decision came after Eddie Jacobson, Truman's old business partner, had brought Chaim Weizmann to see him, and after a November 1947 memorandum had analysed "The Politics of 1948 and the Jews." [cite:jvl_dejure_1949] [cite:jvl_truman_index] Recognition went against the written advice of the Secretary of State, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Secretary of Defense and the CIA. The CIA's estimate ORE-55, dated 28 November 1947, the day before the partition vote, had predicted armed hostilities if partition passed and warned that American commercial and strategic interests would be put in danger. [cite:cia_ore55] One account in this library goes further. It describes declassified CIA assessments predicting that recognition would immediately produce war with the Arab states, and calls the eleven minutes the fastest diplomatic recognition in American history. Those claims are that account's own and are not repeated in ORE-55's summary. The warnings and the advisers who overrode them are traced in [scene:ore_s01|the CIA's estimate].

Why Truman acted as he did is argued in the opening sections of this chapter. One reading stresses the domestic calculation that Clifford's memorandum set out. Another stresses Truman's own repeated statements of humanitarian conviction. The record holds evidence for both. The constitutional point holds whatever his motive. Because the power to recognize was the President's alone, the months of argument over partition, trusteeship, oil and strategy ended in one decision by one man. The State Department, the Pentagon and the new intelligence agency could advise. Advocates could press. Only the President could decide.

Congress's part was real but indirect. It could pass resolutions of support, it controlled appropriations, and the Senate confirmed ambassadors, including the first American ambassador to Israel. None of these powers decided whether the United States would recognize Israel. They shaped what recognition would mean in practice afterwards.

Other governments answered the question differently, and on different grounds. The Soviet Union went further than Washington, and almost as fast. On 17 May 1948, three days after independence, it became the first state to recognize Israel de jure, while the American recognition was still de facto. [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] It was the same course Moscow had followed since Andrei Gromyko's speech at the United Nations in May 1947, and analysts at the time and since have read its motive as removing British influence from the region. That is their reading, not a stated Soviet aim. [cite:washinst_who_saved_israel] The convergence had limits. In December 1948 the Soviet bloc voted against Resolution 194 on the return of refugees. [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] The fuller Soviet story is told in [scene:so_s01|Moscow said yes first].

Britain, the outgoing Mandatory power, came slowly. It had abstained in the partition vote of 29 November 1947. In May 1948 Ernest Bevin told the House of Commons that "there are, after all, no Arabs in the House." Britain recognized Israel in January 1949; the historian Raphael Langham describes Bevin's acceptance as "grudging." [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit] The government that had issued the Balfour Declaration and held the Mandate accepted the state that emerged from it only after eight months.

Some governments declined altogether. In 1947, according to secondary reports, Ben-Gurion sent a secret telegram or telephone message to Muhammad Ali Jinnah, the founder of Pakistan, proposing that Pakistan recognize the Jewish state when it was established. Jinnah's reply was reportedly negative. [cite:timesofisrael_blog_secret_diplomacy] [cite:infogalactic_israel_pakistan_relations] The account rests on secondary reporting, not on a surviving primary document, and should be read with that limit. According to the sources used here, Pakistan has never recognized Israel and has no diplomatic relations with it. The later record of unacknowledged contacts between the two belongs to a later volume.

The Holy See withheld recognition for longer than almost any other major power. Its documented reasons were three: Catholic doctrine on the place of the Jewish people in salvation history, the unresolved status of the holy sites of Jerusalem, and concern for the Christian and Muslim communities of Palestine under the new state. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] Its opposition was active. In 1949 the Vatican pressed at the United Nations to make Israel's admission conditional on two commitments: a return of Palestinian refugees and formal guarantees for the Christian and Muslim holy sites, above all in Jerusalem. Ben-Gurion is recorded as saying that year: "There is a major religion in the world, which has to settle with us a historical reckoning." The campaign failed. Israel was admitted to the United Nations in May 1949 without the guarantees the Vatican had sought. The Holy See did not establish diplomatic relations until the Fundamental Agreement of 30 December 1993, forty-five years after independence. [cite:wiki_fundamental_agreement] How that change came about is told in [scene:vat_s01|the Vatican's long refusal].

Set side by side, these answers show what recognition was in 1948. It was a decision each government took alone, through whatever office its own constitution named, and for its own reasons. In Washington that office was the presidency, and the reasons were contested inside the government itself. In Moscow, on the analysts' reading, the reason was Britain. In London it was reluctant acceptance of a settled fact, and in Rome theology and the holy places. None of these decisions created the state, which existed by its own proclamation. What recognition decided was who would deal with it, and on what terms.

Some questions remain open. The press release and the date of de jure recognition are documented. The exact moment from which the eleven minutes are counted is not stated in the sources used here. The approach to Jinnah is known only from secondary accounts. The details of the Vatican's 1949 campaign at the United Nations, beyond its two demands and its failure, are not documented in this book's sources. How much any single adviser or argument weighed with Truman remains, as the earlier sections show, a matter of interpretation.
