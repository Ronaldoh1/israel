The Actual Military Balance of 1948

Two popular stories about the military balance of the 1948 war circulate side by side. The first, the more familiar, holds that Israel was a tiny, vastly outnumbered nation that defeated overwhelming Arab armies through sheer will. The second, told in reply, holds that Israel won only because international sympathy, not real capability, tipped the scales. The documented military record supports neither. It shows a balance that changed during the war, in stages, and that ended close to the reverse of where the underdog story begins.

The most useful single document was written while the war was still being fought. A US government report dated July 1, 1948, which the sources describe in one place as a CIA report and in another as a State Department report, put Israeli forces at roughly 90,000 in total, with about 35,000 used for active operations. The same report stated that the Arab armies were stronger than Israel in artillery and tanks at that point, while fielding a wide variety of small arms. [cite:frus1948_military_situation] A report written before anyone knew the outcome carries a kind of credibility that a later reconstruction cannot fully match. Its limit is that it is a snapshot of one moment, not an account of the whole war.

Historians describe that whole war in two distinct phases. From the invasion on May 15 until the first truce in June 1948, documented accounts describe the Israeli forces as initially inferior in both numbers and armament. The early disadvantage was real, and the record does not dismiss it. After the truce, new troop enrollments and arms supplies, including the Czech shipments described earlier in this stage ([scene:cz_s01|the Czech arms]), allowed Israel to seize the initiative decisively. One historical anthology states that for most of the war's remaining duration, excluding that initial period, "the Jews were superior in personnel, equipment, logistics and organization." [cite:jvl_israel_studies_1948]

By the later stages the reversal was plain in the numbers. Documented accounts state that Israel's army outnumbered the combined Arab armies by roughly 2 to 1. The Arab states reinforced their own forces over the course of the war, but historians document that they never matched the pace of Israeli growth in personnel and armament. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] That final advantage does not mean the outcome was inevitable or easy. It means that the war's ending looked very different from its opening weeks.

Why, then, has the image of overwhelming odds proved so durable? Part of the answer lies in its sources. Many of the most widely repeated figures for the "tiny, outnumbered Israel" account trace back to estimates from John Glubb, commander of Jordan's Arab Legion during the war. Historians who have worked on the documented order of battle note that, as the commander of one of the fighting armies, Glubb had a direct and plausible incentive to exaggerate Israeli numbers and play down Arab ones. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] This is a reason for caution, not proof of deliberate falsification. It is a caution rarely mentioned when his figures are cited as neutral fact.

Set against the full record, both popular versions fail. The underdog story holds only for the war's opening weeks, and even there it leans on one interested commander's disputed figures. The sympathy story fares no better. It has to ignore a dated military reversal, the arms deliveries that accompanied it and a final 2 to 1 advantage that owed nothing to international goodwill and a great deal to logistics, organization and weapons that arrived and were used.
