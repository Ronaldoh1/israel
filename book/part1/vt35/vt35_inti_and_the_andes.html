Inti and the Andes

The Inca state was organised around the sun. Inti, the sun, was the most important deity of its official religion, and the empire's highest priest, the Willaq Umu, was a priest of the sun, usually a brother of the ruler and probably the second most powerful person in the empire. [cite:vt35_wiki_willaqumu] When the Spanish took Cusco, the sun was therefore not one god among many to them: it was the religion of the state they were replacing. This section sets out what happened to that religion's temple, its festival, its sacred image and its priests, who did each thing, and what survived. The campaigns of the bishops against Andean religion generally, the "extirpation of idolatries" of the seventeenth century, are told in the previous part. [scene:vt34_the_americas|The Inquisitions in the Americas]

The Coricancha: gold for a ransom, a convent on the walls

The Coricancha in Cusco was the temple of Inti. Its walls were covered in sheets of gold and its courtyard held golden statues. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] The first act against it was not religious at all. In 1533, when Francisco Pizarro's company held the ruler Atahualpa prisoner at Cajamarca and demanded a ransom in gold for his life, most of the gold was collected from the Coricancha and melted down. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] Atahualpa was killed anyway. The actor here was a band of conquistadors after treasure, and what they took was metal; that the metal was a temple's was, to them, beside the point.

The second act was religious. Spanish colonists, and then the Dominican friars who still hold it, built the church and convent of Santo Domingo directly on the Inca walls. Construction took most of a century, and the building that stands now went up after an earthquake in 1650 destroyed the first convent. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] The choice of site followed a pattern the conquerors used across the Americas and the Romans had used before them: put the new sanctuary on the old one. Four rooms of the Inca temple still stand inside the convent, because their fitted stonework outlasted the earthquakes that brought down the Spanish building above. The Dominican order still manages the complex today, jointly with Peru's Ministry of Culture. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha]

Inti Raymi: banned, then rebuilt

Inti Raymi, "the festival of the sun", was the Inca new year at the June solstice, credited to the ruler Pachacuti and described in the early seventeenth century by the mestizo chronicler Inca Garcilaso de la Vega, son of a conquistador and an Inca noblewoman. [cite:vt35_wiki_intiraymi] [cite:vt35_infobae_inti] The last Inti Raymi held in the presence of an Inca ruler is commonly dated to 1535. [cite:vt35_wiki_intiraymi] Its prohibition is usually credited to Viceroy Francisco de Toledo, the crown's governor of Peru, who is said to have banned ceremonies tied to the Inca cult, Inti Raymi among them, in 1572 as part of a programme of evangelisation; other accounts name "the Spanish colonists and their Catholic priests" more generally. [cite:vt35_infobae_inti] [cite:vt35_wiki_intiraymi] No decree naming the festival has been identified, so the firm statement is narrower: the state festival ended with the Inca state, and its open celebration ceased under Spanish rule, the clergy opposing it, until the twentieth century.

The festival people attend in Cusco today is a reconstruction. In 1944 Humberto Vidal Unda and Faustino Espinoza Navarro staged a theatrical Inti Raymi with Indigenous actors, drawing largely on Garcilaso's description, and it has been performed at the fortress of Sacsayhuamán every 24 June since. [cite:vt35_infobae_inti] [cite:vt35_wiki_intiraymi] What was suppressed was a state rite; what returned, four centuries later, was a civic and Indigenous-heritage performance built from a colonial chronicle.

Corpus Christi on the solstice

The Church's answer to the sun festival was a festival of its own. Corpus Christi, the feast of the body of Christ in the Eucharist, falls in late May or June and so lands near the solstice; in Cusco it became the great public festival of the colonial city, held at the season of the Inca solstice celebration of Inti Raymi. [cite:vt35_smarthistory_corpus] The art historian Carolyn Dean, in the standard study of the Cusco festival, describes it as staging "the triumph of Christian orthodoxy over the imperial Inka patron (the Sun)". [cite:vt35_dean_muse] In the procession the host was carried in a monstrance, a vessel of gold rays round a white disc, through the streets of the former Inca capital.

Dean's study is also the main evidence that Andean people were not only spectators of this replacement. In the surviving colonial series of Corpus Christi canvases from Cusco (eighteen were made, sixteen survive), Inca nobles march in the procession wearing their own regalia, including a garment with a sun face on its front, marking them as Andean nobility within the Christian city. [cite:vt35_smarthistory_corpus] [cite:vt35_dean_muse] The festival was a symbol of conquest; it was also the stage on which the descendants of the Inca kept their rank visible.

The Punchao and the last Inca

The holiest image of the Inca sun cult was the Punchao, a gold figure that held the remains of the hearts of dead Inca kings. The Inca rulers who held out in the Vilcabamba region after the conquest kept it with them. In 1572 Viceroy Toledo sent an expedition into Vilcabamba; it captured the last ruler, Túpac Amaru, and seized the Punchao along with the mummified bodies of two earlier rulers. Túpac Amaru was executed in Cusco on 24 September 1572. [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru] The execution was not a clerical act: numerous Catholic clergy, convinced of his innocence, knelt to plead that he be sent to Spain for trial, and Philip II is reported to have disapproved of it afterwards. [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru]

The Punchao's fate is disputed. Some accounts say the seized sacred objects were destroyed. [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru] The historian Catherine Julien traced a different trail: Toledo took the image to Spain when he returned in 1581; after his death in 1582 the Council of the Indies seized items from his estate; and a gold idol appears in the inventory made after Philip II's death in 1598, possibly kept in the royal treasure house in Madrid into the early 1600s. [cite:vt35_julien_punchao] If Julien is right, the sun of the Inca ended in a king's treasury, as a trophy, rather than in a bonfire.

Taki Onqoy: the huacas' rebellion, 1560s

The best-documented religious resistance of the period was not a sun cult. Taki Onqoy, "the dancing sickness", arose in the 1560s around Huamanga (modern Ayacucho). Its leaders taught that the huacas, the sacred places and beings of the Andes, had revived in order to defeat "the God of the Christians" and would send disease on the Spaniards; followers were to reject Spanish food, clothing and religion. [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki] [cite:vt35_wiki_taki] The priest Luis de Olvera later claimed to have been the first to detect and "correct" it, in 1564, and to have destroyed some 700 huacas and more than 8,000 idols. [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki]

The suppression was led by the cleric Cristóbal de Albornoz, acting as an official visitor with authority to investigate and punish. Penalties included public shaming, shaving of the head, whipping, forced labour and exile; leaders were taken to Cusco to renounce their beliefs in public, women participants were confined in convents and local lords were fined. [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki] [cite:vt35_wiki_taki] Historians disagree about the movement's size. The historian Pedro Guibovich, following Rafael Varón, argues that Albornoz, who built his career on the campaign, exaggerated its spread in his own record of services, and that it was a local phenomenon confined to parts of present-day Ayacucho, Huancavelica and Apurímac. [cite:vt35_guibovich_taki]

The sun inside Andean Christianity

Andean religion was not simply erased and replaced. Much of it continued inside Catholic forms that Andean communities made their own. The clearest living example is the pilgrimage to the sanctuary of the Lord of Qoyllurit'i in the Cusco region, inscribed by UNESCO in 2011. In UNESCO's description it "combines elements from Catholicism and worship of pre-Hispanic nature deities"; it begins fifty-eight days after Easter, involves about a hundred distinct dances, and ends with a procession that carries the sacred images to the village of Tayancani "to greet the first rays of sunlight". [cite:vt35_unesco_qoyllur] The pilgrims are Catholic; the mountains, the snow and the rising sun are honoured as well.

The record therefore shows three things at once. Crown officials and conquistadors stripped the sun's temple, seized its image and killed its last royal patron; clergy built on its walls, set a Christian feast on its festival and punished those who called the old powers back; and Andean people carried the sun into the new religion and, in the twentieth century, brought its festival back into public. Who "suppressed the sun" in Peru was, in turn, a band of soldiers, a viceroy, a religious order and a set of clerical visitors, each acting for their own reasons.
