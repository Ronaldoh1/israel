Herzl in Search of a Patron, 1898–1903

It is often assumed that the Zionist movement always aimed at Palestine through Britain, and Britain alone. Herzl's own diplomacy in the years after Basel tells a different story. His goal was a charter, legal backing for settlement from a great power, and in the space of five years he approached Germany, the Ottoman Empire, Britain and Russia in turn, offering each whatever it seemed to value.

The first approach was to Germany. Through William Hechler, a Christian restorationist, Herzl met Kaiser Wilhelm II three times in 1898: in Istanbul, at the Rothschild-funded colony of Mikveh Israel, and in Jerusalem.[cite:wiki_kaiser_levant_1898] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] The Kaiser raised the matter with the Sultan. The request failed, and the Kaiser's interest faded.

Herzl then went to the source of authority over Palestine itself. On 17 May 1901 Sultan Abdul Hamid II received him. Herzl offered the help of Jewish finance with the Ottoman Empire's chronic debts in exchange for a charter. No charter was granted.[cite:avnery_herzl_sultan] [cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] The offer is revealing of the method: the Empire's debts were chronic, and finance was what Herzl proposed to supply.

With Britain the offer changed shape. In October 1902 Herzl proposed to the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, a Jewish settlement at El-Arish in the Sinai, presented as a buffer protecting British interests at the Suez Canal. Britain tentatively agreed. In 1903, however, Lord Cromer, the British authority in Cairo, vetoed the scheme, saying the Nile water needed for irrigation could not be spared.[cite:unu_water_elarish] [cite:wiki_leopold_kessler] Here the inducement was strategic rather than financial, and it foundered on a practical question of water.

The most controversial approach came in August 1903. Months after the Kishinev pogrom, and without consulting Russia's own Zionists, Herzl twice met the Russian Interior Minister, Vyacheslav von Plehve, a man widely regarded as responsible for the atmosphere that had produced Kishinev. The meetings yielded a letter in which the Tsar's government agreed to press the Sultan for a charter and to allow Zionist societies to operate in Russia.[cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] Russian Zionists criticized the meetings sharply.

Weeks later, Britain's offer of territory in East Africa split the Sixth Zionist Congress, a crisis that is taken up in [scene:s04e|the East Africa offer].

Seen together, these episodes show the founding strategy of political Zionism. It was not attached to any one power. It sought a charter from whichever power would grant one, and it adjusted its offer to each: finance for the indebted Ottomans, a strategic buffer for Britain at Suez, and emigration in the Russian case. Germany, the Ottoman Empire, Britain and Russia were all approached within five years. Britain's eventual sponsorship in 1917 was the strategy succeeding, not its starting point.

The sequence also shows how little these early efforts produced on their own terms. The Kaiser's interest faded, the Sultan granted nothing, Cairo vetoed El-Arish, and the Russian letter brought a backlash within the movement. What persisted was the method itself, and the organization that could carry it forward after Herzl's death in 1904.
