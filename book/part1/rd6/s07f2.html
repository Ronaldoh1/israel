Abraham Feinberg: A Donor, a Campaign, and a Decision

Abraham Feinberg's name is missing from most popular accounts of 1948. The usual explanation of Truman's support for Israel rests on personal conviction and on political calculation about Jewish voters in general. In that account, financial need never changed a specific decision. The record contains one case in which it did, and Feinberg stands at its centre.

Abraham "Abe" Feinberg was born in 1908 and raised in the Bronx, and he made his fortune in hosiery. From 1945 until 1968 he played a significant role, mostly behind the scenes, in relations between the United States and Israel. He was a confidant and key financier to three presidents: Truman, Kennedy and Johnson. He was also one of the original seventeen members of the Sonneborn Institute, the arms-buying network formed in 1945. He died in 1998. [cite:emory_feinberg_thesis] [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb]

The 1948 campaign brought him to the centre. Truman's campaign was broke, and Thomas Dewey was widely expected to make him a one-term president. Truman needed $100,000, a very large sum in 1948, to pay for a whistle-stop tour through the Midwest. Feinberg and the jewelry magnate Ed Kaufmann led the fundraising that produced that amount at that moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] Feinberg later summed up his path to influence in his own words: "My path to power was cooperation in terms of what they needed — campaign money." [cite:prnewswire_feinberg_fbi]

The historian John Judis ties that money to a specific decision. In mid-1948 Truman wanted Israel to stop seizing territory by force beyond the UN partition lines, and he was personally "disgusted" by Israel's refugee policy. He reportedly said that Jews had turned their own story on its head by denying Palestinians the right of return. Judis documents that Truman abandoned these impulses in part because he needed the $100,000 from Feinberg and Kaufmann for the tour at that exact moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman]

The claim should be stated with its limits. It is a historian's sourced account, not an inference from general goodwill. It names a donor, a sum, a moment and a policy objection that was dropped. It also says "in part." Judis does not argue that money was the only reason Truman stopped pressing. The wider record of the period, examined in [scene:mn_s02|the question of money and politicians], shows other documented forces behind American policy that involved no payment at all. What the Feinberg case establishes, on Judis's account, is that financial dependence did, at least once, change a specific decision.

Feinberg's role did not end with the campaign. In 1952 US authorities ordered him to register as an agent of a foreign principal over Israel Speaks, a publication he ran. He shut it down rather than register under the Foreign Agents Registration Act, the law requiring agents of foreign principals to register. [cite:israellobby_feinberg_fbi] According to Avner Cohen's history Israel and the Bomb, from 1958 Ben-Gurion secretly made Feinberg his chief coordinator for raising private American money for Israel's clandestine nuclear weapons program. He told his staff simply to "call Abe." [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb]

Read together, these episodes describe something more lasting than a single campaign gift. Feinberg was a fixture across three administrations, connected to the arms network of 1945, to the campaign of 1948, and to the nuclear program from 1958. Truman's recognition of the state, eleven minutes after its declaration, sits inside this same wider financial relationship. On the record, this is not a story about money in the abstract. It is a named donor, a precise figure and a dated decision, documented as such.
