One Officer, One Account: The Liaison to 1967

The oldest of the agency ties described in this book was never written into a statute, and no vote of Congress created it. It began as an arrangement between two intelligence services, managed for its first two decades by one American officer, and it has been publicly acknowledged by both sides since the 1970s. How it began is told in detail, with the disagreements between the sources, in [scene:cw1_cia_liaison|the first ties with American intelligence] and [scene:cw2_angleton|the Angleton channel]. This section draws the outline needed for the rest of the volume: who held the account, what passed through it, and what each side received from it up to the war of June 1967.

According to the journalists Yossi Melman and Dan Raviv, the turn came in 1951, when David Ben-Gurion, on a visit to the United States, met Walter Bedell Smith, Truman's Director of Central Intelligence, and "convinced U.S. intelligence to give Israel a try." [cite:ow3_tablet] After that meeting, they write, James Jesus Angleton "was put in charge of the U.S. side", with Amos Manor and Teddy Kollek as his Israeli counterparts. [cite:ow3_tablet] No written agreement from that year has been published, and the sources disagree about when the arrangement became formal. Jeff McConnell, writing in 1986, said Angleton had exercised formal authority over the CIA's liaison with Israel from the early 1950s, how early being in dispute. [cite:ow3_merip]

The clearest description of the arrangement comes from inside the agency. David Robarge, the CIA's Chief Historian, wrote in the agency's journal in 2009 that Angleton became head of the new Counterintelligence Staff in 1954, and that his "control of that country's account at CIA, an administrative anomaly, was one of the foundations of his influence." [cite:ow3_robarge] Robarge added that Angleton "ardently supported Israel" and "saw Israel as a bastion against the Soviet Union." [cite:ow3_robarge] McConnell gave the structural detail: the Israel desk sat under Angleton's counterintelligence staff rather than in the section that handled the rest of the Middle East. [cite:ow3_merip] The consequence is the first asymmetry in this book, and it is an American one. The officer responsible for assessing whether a foreign service was working against the United States was the same officer who managed that service as a partner.

What came through the channel. The relationship's most famous product was the text of Nikita Khrushchev's secret speech of February 1956. Shammai Siskind, writing in the Middle East Quarterly in 2020, describes the route: a Polish journalist, Victor Grayevski, borrowed a copy in Warsaw; a Shin Bet agent, Yaakov Barmor, photographed it; Ben-Gurion sent it by diplomatic courier to Israel's intelligence liaison in Washington, Izzy Dorot; and only Angleton was to see it. [cite:ow3_meq_siskind] The text reached Allen Dulles by mid-April 1956, and the CIA gave it to the New York Times in early June. [cite:ow3_meq_siskind] Melman and Raviv call it "the great turning point" of the relationship. [cite:ow3_tablet]

The speech was not the only return. Israel's immigrants from the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, Melman and Raviv write, made it "an indispensable source" on Cold War questions. [cite:ow3_tablet] Siskind lists hardware and data that followed: a MiG-21 flown to Israel by a defecting Iraqi pilot in 1963 and later lent to the United States; a Soviet P-12 radar removed from Egypt in a 1969 raid and handed to Washington; an Egyptian SA-6 missile battery captured in October 1973 and shared with the United States; and, in the early 1970s, detailed information on the structure and deployment of much of the Soviet strategic missile force. [cite:ow3_meq_siskind] These are claims made by a writer sympathetic to the relationship, but they describe specific transfers, and they are the strongest documented answer to the question of what the United States received.

What Israel received. The sources for the 1950s and 1960s say less about the American side of the ledger. The critical account that dates the liaison to 1951 describes an exchange of American intelligence on the Arab states for high-value Israeli material, and that account is discussed with its weaknesses in [scene:cw1_cia_liaison|the section on the liaison's origins]. Later NSA documents, covered in Part Four, describe the American side of the signals relationship in much more concrete terms. For the CIA channel in its first two decades, the public record does not contain an inventory of what was given.

The war of 1967. The channel's most consequential use in this period was a conversation, not a document. On 1 June 1967, four days before the war began, Meir Amit, head of the Mossad, met Defense Secretary Robert McNamara in Washington, with the Deputy Director of Central Intelligence, Admiral Rufus Taylor, taking notes. [cite:ow3_frus_amit] Amit said the Egyptian blockade of the Straits of Tiran was "window dressing" for a larger threat, that "time is very much against Israel", and that Israel could expect about 4,000 casualties in Sinai if it held air superiority. He stressed that his remarks were "entirely informal, off the record," and McNamara said he would convey them to the President. [cite:ow3_frus_amit] What was decided afterwards, and how the American response was read in Jerusalem, is told in [scene:cw6_tiran_june4|the last days before the war]. The memorandum establishes the mechanism: Israel's foreign intelligence chief could put a case directly to the Secretary of Defense, in the presence of the CIA's second-ranking officer, without passing through the State Department.

The other side of the ledger. The same years produced the first American suspicions. In 1965 an audit found highly enriched uranium missing from the NUMEC plant at Apollo, Pennsylvania, a case told in [scene:fis_s07a|the NUMEC shortage] and taken up in the next section. And McConnell records that, in the mid-1950s, Angleton is generally credited with the effort to end covert operations by each service against the other. [cite:ow3_merip] Whether such an understanding existed, and whether it held, is disputed; the next section shows that the CIA's own analysts later reported Israeli collection inside the United States.

What the record does not settle. No written liaison agreement for these years has been published. The date of the no-spying understanding is uncertain, and so is whether it held. The public record does not list what American intelligence was given to Israel in return for the speech and the Soviet hardware. And the account of Angleton's alleged disclosures to Israel of CIA sources in Arab countries, which circulates in later writing, rests on no document cited in this book and is not repeated here as fact.
