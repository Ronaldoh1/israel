Dreyfus and Herzl, 1894–1896

The familiar account holds that Zionism arose from ancient scripture. The organized political movement in fact crystallized in response to a specific, documented episode of modern European antisemitism, and it was contested within Jewish life from the same moment.

The episode was the Dreyfus Affair. In December 1894 Captain Alfred Dreyfus, an Alsatian-born Jewish officer in the French army, was convicted of treason on forged evidence. The key document, the "bordereau," supposedly proved espionage for Germany; it was later shown to have been written by Ferdinand Esterhazy, whom the army's General Staff protected once his guilt became clear. Dreyfus was sent to Devil's Island. Émile Zola's open letter "J'accuse!", published in L'Aurore in January 1898, forced the frame-up into public view, and Dreyfus was fully rehabilitated in 1906, twelve years after his conviction. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] The Affair split French society into Dreyfusards and anti-Dreyfusards. It was not a case of a whole nation acting as one: Zola and many others who exposed the injustice were French and not Jewish.

Where it happened mattered as much as what happened. France had emancipated its Jews in 1791, the first European state to do so. A century later, the same republic of universal rights produced the most publicized antisemitic show trial of the nineteenth century, with crowds in the streets chanting against the Jews. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] French Jews had built substantial civic and professional lives in the intervening century, which is why the Affair felt like a rupture rather than more of the same.

Theodor Herzl (1860–1904) was there to see it. Born in Pest to a German-speaking, assimilated Jewish family, he studied law in Vienna, took a doctorate in 1884, and turned to journalism and playwriting. In 1891 he became Paris correspondent of the Neue Freie Presse. On December 22, 1894, he attended Dreyfus's public degradation ceremony and heard the crowd; the cry is variously rendered as "Death to the Jews" and "À bas les Juifs!" ("Down with the Jews!"). He recorded the moment.

The common telling compresses what followed. Herzl's thinking did not crystallize overnight. The sources describe his diaries as showing years of development between the degradation of December 1894 and Der Judenstaat in February 1896, though by those same dates the interval was a little over a year. During 1895 he weighed mass conversion, social integration through political engagement and, increasingly, territorial concentration. What Dreyfus gave him was a diagnosis: if an assimilated officer could be convicted on forged evidence in emancipated Paris, then assimilation did not produce safety, and no European Jewish community was structurally secure. If safety could not be had inside European societies, it would have to be built outside them.

The result was Der Judenstaat, published in Vienna in February 1896. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] It reads as political engineering, not theology. It proposes a "Society of Jews" for political leadership and a "Jewish Company" for economic development, a seven-hour workday, a flag and a bank. It treats "Jew" as a national category and includes irreligious Jews on equal terms. It makes no messianic claim. It also shops for territory, weighing Argentina seriously alongside Palestine. At its founding moment, political Zionism was territorially agnostic. Between 1896 and 1903 Herzl negotiated over Palestine with the Ottoman Sultan, who refused to transfer sovereignty, and over Cyprus, El-Arish in Sinai and, in August 1903, a British offer in East Africa known as the Uganda scheme. The Russian delegation revolted against Uganda, and the Seventh Zionist Congress of 1905, after Herzl's death, rejected it. Palestine became fixed through the movement's own later decisions, not by the founding text. That openness did not make Palestine arbitrary; specific religious, historical and communal reasons drew the movement there.

Herzl knew Palestine was inhabited. A diary entry dated June 12, 1895, published only in the posthumous Complete Diaries edited by Raphael Patai (1960), speaks of trying to "spirit the penniless population across the border" and says expropriation and "the removal of the poor must be carried out discreetly and circumspectly." The passage is disputed. Some scholars argue it has been misread or referred to non-Palestinian populations; Benny Morris and Nur Masalha treat it as evidence of early awareness that the project would entail population transfer. This book's own interpretive reading, which is a reading and not a documented finding, sees later Israeli law, from the Law of Return (1950) to the 2018 Nation-State Law, as carrying forward the national-particular design of Herzl's text.

After the Basel Congress of 1897 Herzl wrote in his diary: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State. Perhaps in five years, certainly in fifty, everyone will see it." [cite:herzl_diaries] He died in 1904, aged 44. The State of Israel was declared fifty-one years after Basel.

Opposition was present from the beginning, and from four separate directions. The Jewish Labor Bund, founded in October 1897, rejected Zionism in favor of Jewish autonomy within Eastern Europe through socialist politics. [cite:wiki_bund] Reform Judaism had rejected the return framework in 1885. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] Ahad Ha'am proposed a cultural rather than political Zionism. [cite:wiki_ahad_haam] Orthodox anti-Zionist rabbis, whose lineage runs to today's Neturei Karta, held that return could come only through divine redemption. [cite:neturei_karta] These critiques did not agree with one another, but together they show that political Zionism was one Jewish answer among several, not the voice of Judaism as a whole.
