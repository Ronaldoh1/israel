Trent: The Farnese Pope Fixes the List

The Council of Trent made the Catholic canon of scripture an article of faith on 8 April 1546. For the first time in the history of the Western church, doubting a book on the list was not a scholarly opinion but a cause for anathema. The council was convened by Pope Paul III, and his own career is a summary of everything this book has traced: the papacy as a family enterprise, entered through connection and used for the advancement of kin.

Paul III was born Alessandro Farnese in 1468, of a baronial family of Lazio; his mother was a Caetani. [cite:cn_wiki_paul3] He was made a cardinal in 1493 by Alexander VI, the Borgia pope. His sister Giulia was reportedly Alexander's mistress and may have helped secure the appointment, and Romans mocked the young cardinal as the "Borgia brother-in-law". [cite:cn_wiki_paul3] He had four children with his mistress Silvia Ruffini, among them Pier Luigi. When he was elected pope in 1534, his efforts, in the words of his Wikipedia biography, "were distracted by nepotism to advance the power and fortunes of his family". Shortly after his election he made two grandsons cardinals: Alessandro Farnese, aged fourteen, and Guido Ascanio Sforza, aged sixteen. [cite:cn_wiki_paul3] In 1545 he planned to obtain the duchies of Parma and Piacenza for his son Pier Luigi by exchanging them for papal territories; Pier Luigi was assassinated at Piacenza in 1547. [cite:cn_wiki_paul3]

The same pope laid the foundations of the Counter-Reformation. On 27 September 1540 he approved the Society of Jesus; in 1542 he instituted or reorganised the Congregation of the Holy Office of the Inquisition; and on 15 March 1545, under the bull Laetare Hierusalem, he convoked the council at Trent, under pressure from the Emperor Charles V. [cite:cn_wiki_paul3] In April 1546 the council's fourth session took up scripture. Its decree listed the books of the Old and New Testaments, seventy-three in all, and declared anathema anyone who did not accept them "in their entirety and with all their parts" as sacred and canonical. [cite:vt14_trent_s4] The vote was 24 in favour, 15 against and 16 abstaining. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] The same session ordered that the Latin Vulgate, Jerome's translation commissioned by Damasus in 382, be held "as authentic". [cite:vt14_trent_s4] [cite:cn_brit_vulgate]

A canon made dogma needed an official text, and the attempt to produce one became an embarrassment that the institution covered with a small fiction. Sixtus V, pope from 1585, was dissatisfied with the edition his commission prepared and edited the Vulgate himself with a few helpers. It was published in 1590, preceded by a bull, Aeternus ille, which reportedly threatened excommunication for any alteration: "not even the smallest particle should be altered, added or removed". [cite:cn_wiki_sixtine] The edition was full of errors; two whole verses and part of a third were missing from the Book of Numbers. [cite:cn_wiki_sixtine] Sixtus died on 27 August 1590, and soon afterwards the College of Cardinals halted the sale of his Bible and ordered copies destroyed. Some scholars think the bull was never formally promulgated. [cite:cn_wiki_sixtine]

The replacement, the Clementine Vulgate of November 1592, was published under Sixtus's name. Its preface, written by the Jesuit cardinal Robert Bellarmine, ascribed all the imperfections of the earlier edition to errors of the press. [cite:cn_wiki_sixtine] Bellarmine knew better. In a letter of 1602 he wrote that Sixtus had put "himself and the whole Church" in danger by correcting the Bible according to his own judgment. [cite:cn_wiki_sixtine] The official Latin Bible of the Catholic Church for the next four centuries thus began with a pope's personal edition that his successors withdrew, and a preface that blamed the printers to protect the papacy's standing.

Trent's achievement was real and lasting: it gave Catholics a fixed list and a fixed text, and both endured into the twentieth century. The Vulgate was replaced as the official Latin text only by the Nova Vulgata, promulgated by John Paul II in 1979. [cite:cn_brit_vulgate] But the council that fixed the list was summoned by a pope who made a fourteen-year-old grandson a cardinal, and the text it declared authentic was corrected, recalled and re-issued under a polite untruth. The canon's final Catholic form came out of the same institution, and the same habits, as everything before it. Part Eight follows the instrument the Counter-Reformation built to enforce it: the list not of books to be read, but of books to be banned.
