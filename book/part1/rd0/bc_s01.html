The Square Mile: Privilege Without Sovereignty

This volume opens not in Palestine, and not in any ministry or synagogue, but in a financial district of roughly one square mile in the middle of London. It is run by the oldest municipal corporation in Britain, the City of London Corporation. The Corporation governs through a Lord Mayor and a Court of Common Council, and it keeps its own police force. Its privileges predate Magna Carta, and when Parliament has legislated since, it has routinely preserved them by express exception written into the later Acts. [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation]

A claim widely repeated online holds that the City is a sovereign city-state, beyond the authority of Parliament and the Crown, and that the monarch must ask the Lord Mayor's permission before entering it. The documents do not bear that out. What they show is in some ways more instructive: three privileges that are real, unusual and on the record, and none of which amounts to statehood. They are worth taking one at a time.

The business vote. In Corporation elections, businesses are allocated votes alongside the people who live there. At the City elections of April 2013, the roughly 9,000 resident votes were outnumbered by a business vote some 32,000 strong. Those figures come from the Green Party, which campaigns to end the arrangement, and they should be read as a campaigner's figures. The same Green Party statement cited a Bloomberg survey that questioned how far the business vote represented the workers it is supposed to speak for. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation] The arrangement is unique in the United Kingdom. In practical terms it means that the firms based in the district, rather than its residents alone, choose much of its government.

The Remembrancer. The second privilege places the Corporation inside Parliament itself. The City Remembrancer is a Corporation official who sits in the House of Commons, facing the Speaker's chair, the only person who is not a member of the House permitted on its floor. The office reviews parliamentary legislation in order to protect the Corporation's interests, and it does so with a budget reported at about £6 million and a team of in-house lawyers. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:wiki_city_remembrancer] The author Nicholas Shaxson has called the office "the world's oldest institutional lobbyist." In 2013 the Green MP Caroline Lucas formally asked the Speaker to remove the Remembrancer from the floor of the House. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation] The sources used here record the request; they do not record what came of it.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because it is the heart of the matter. A lobbyist ordinarily stands outside a legislature and seeks to be heard. The Remembrancer is seated inside one and reads bills as they pass with a single client's interests in mind. Read together, the business vote and the Remembrancer can be seen to reinforce each other: the firms help choose the Corporation, and the Corporation keeps watch on the law that governs them. Who could not act in the same way is equally plain. No other local authority, and no group of residents anywhere else in the country, has a comparable seat.

Temple Bar. The third privilege is ceremonial, and it is the one the legend rests on. When the monarch enters the City on a state occasion, the Lord Mayor meets the sovereign at Temple Bar and presents the City's sword. An account published in 1869 described the custom as the Mayor "requiring" the sovereign to ask leave to enter. [cite:wiki_temple_bar] The record shows a courtesy, not a legal requirement. The sovereign's carriage has passed Temple Bar without stopping, as the BBC's Huw Edwards noted on air during a Jubilee, and Parliament's authority over the City is a matter of statute, not of the Lord Mayor's leave. [cite:metabunk_city_sovereign_debunk] [cite:wiki_temple_bar]

Laid side by side, the claim and the record separate cleanly.

The test that matters is whether the City's privileges amount to sovereignty, and they do not. The Corporation is a local authority, not a state. It has less autonomy than the Crown Dependencies of Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, each of which has its own legislature. Parliament can legislate for the City, and does. [cite:metabunk_city_sovereign_debunk] [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation] The privileges survive because Parliament keeps choosing to preserve them, which is the opposite of standing above Parliament.

The Corporation's own description of itself is more revealing than the legend. It has said that it acts "rather like a trade body" for finance and business. [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation] That is the documented finding, and it is the more serious one, precisely because it is true. A sovereign enclave would be a curiosity of constitutional law. A trade body for finance with its own electorate of firms and an official seated in the Commons is a working arrangement of influence, open to examination, and examined here on those terms.

What the record leaves open should be said as plainly. The vote figures come from a party that wants the business vote abolished, and the Remembrancer's budget is a reported figure, not one drawn from published accounts in the sources used here. Nor do these sources measure how often the Remembrancer's work changed the text of a bill. The influence is structural and documented; its effect on any particular law is not established here.

That is why the story starts in the Square Mile. The City matters to this history not as a hidden sovereign but as something older and better documented: a long-standing arrangement by which finance held a formal place inside the legislature of the state that would later issue [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration]. Its firms could vote, and its interests were read into bills before they passed. The banking houses that appear in the sections that follow did their business inside this structure.
