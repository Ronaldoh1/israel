Alaska: A Refuge Within Reach, 1938–1940

As the refugee crisis grew in 1938, an American cabinet secretary proposed a refuge on American soil, outside the quotas that kept most refugees out.

In November 1938 Interior Secretary Harold Ickes proposed settling European refugees, especially Jews from Germany and Austria, in Alaska; his department's report of 1939-40 named four sites, including Baranof Island and the Matanuska-Susitna Valley. Because Alaska was a territory, settlers could have entered outside the normal immigration quotas. [cite:a_wp_slattery]

Senator William King and Representative Franck Havenner introduced a bill. Skagway, Petersburg and Seward endorsed the plan; some opponents called the refugees "Trojan horses". [cite:a_wp_slattery]

Most American Jewish leaders opposed it; Rabbi Stephen Wise said it would give a "wrong and hurtful impression". Roosevelt would accept only 10,000 settlers a year for five years, no more than 10 percent of them Jews, never spoke of it in public, and it died. [cite:a_wp_slattery]

The likely path. At most a few thousand refugees settled as Americans in a territory that became a state of the Union in 1959. A refuge, never a homeland.

The cost. None in violence; its cost was the refugees it did not save.
