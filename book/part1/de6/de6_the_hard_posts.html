The Hard Posts

Five passages from Unqualified Reservations account for most of what is said about Yarvin's character, by critics and defenders alike. Each is set out here in the same way: the date and title of the post, a short quotation in his own words, the context in which he wrote it as far as it can be established, his own later account where he has given one, and what his critics say. The quotations are kept short and exact. They are neither softened nor sharpened, and where a widely repeated version of a passage is not what he wrote, the section says so.

"Why I am not a white nationalist," 22 November 2007

The title is a denial, and the denial is real. "I am not a white nationalist," Yarvin wrote, "because I don't find white nationalism useful or effective." [cite:de6_le_contra] The grounds he gave were practical and epistemic rather than moral: he did not find it effective, and he wrote that it "distorts reality." [cite:de6_audit_le] In the same essay he wrote: "It should be obvious that, although I am not a white nationalist, I am not exactly allergic to the stuff," and described it as "the most marginalized and socially excluded belief system in the history of the world." [cite:de6_land_ebook] He listed white-nationalist blogs for readers, described sites such as VDARE as "thoughtful, well-written commentary" and wrote of feeling "the urge to defend" the ideology, while declining to link to what he called "out-and-out racist blogs" with their "racial epithets, anti-Semitism." [cite:de6_audit_mm] [cite:de6_audit_land] Nick Land, an admirer, read the essay as rejecting white nationalism "on grounds that owe nothing to conventional reflexes." [cite:de6_audit_land]

In January 2025 David Marchese of the New York Times read him a line from his own writing, "It is strategically barren," and answered that the trouble with white nationalism is that it is racist; Yarvin did not take up the point. [cite:de6_audit_nyt] Critics take the essay as the clearest statement of his sympathy: Media Matters titled its account of his 2021 Fox Nation appearance with the phrase "self-described defender of white nationalism," and the journalist Corey Pein wrote that his doctrine was "steeped in pseudoscientific racism." [cite:de6_mm_2021] [cite:de2_pein2017] The text supports a plain description: he rejected the label and white nationalism as a political project as ineffective and as distorting, not as wrong, and wrote with open sympathy for its arguments about race. Both halves belong in any honest summary, and this book prints them together.

"Patchwork: A Positive Vision (Part 1)," 13 November 2008

The passage that circulates online as the "Fris Corp" quotation is real. In the first instalment of Patchwork, discussing what a profit-seeking sovereign corporation running a city such as San Francisco would do with its surplus residents, those who cost more than they pay, Yarvin wrote: "Sell their slums out from under them, obviously;" then "demo everything, spray for roaches, rodents, and pit bulls," "smooth the rubble out with a bulldozer or two, and possibly a little aerial bombing," and "erect new residential districts suitable for Russian oligarchs." These people, he added, "can of course emigrate to any other realm in the Patchwork," and then conceded the catch himself: "This presumes, however, that said realm is willing to accept them." [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de6_audit_le] The scholar Vincent Lê, who quotes the passage with its source, reads it as leaving such residents with no realm willing to take them. [cite:de6_audit_le]

The passage is written in a mocking register, and it describes a hypothetical, not a plan for any real city. It is nevertheless a description, by its author, of how his preferred kind of sovereign would treat poor residents: dispossession and demolition, with bombing offered in passing. No later account by Yarvin explaining or withdrawing it is on record. [cite:de6_le_contra]

"Patchwork 2: Profit strategies for our new corporate overlords," 20 November 2008

A week later he returned to the same residents. He raised converting them "into biodiesel, which can help power the Muni buses," San Francisco's public buses, and then said he was "just kidding." [cite:de6_tnr_duran] The post did not stop there. "Our goal, in short, is a humane alternative to genocide," he wrote, and the best humane alternative he offered was to "virtualize" such people: to hold them in "permanent solitary confinement," connected to an "immersive virtual-reality interface." [cite:de6_tnr_duran] [cite:de2_tnr] Ava Kofman, in The New Yorker, reports that the post itself framed the aim as reaching the same result as mass murder without its moral stigma. [cite:de6_audit_kofman]

The disclaimer is inside the post itself, and it attaches to the biodiesel line. The stated goal and the confinement proposal that follow it are not marked as jokes. The text supports saying, in plain words, that Yarvin proposed the indefinite solitary confinement of people a sovereign corporation found unproductive, and presented it as preferable to killing them. Defenders call the post satire; the reading remains disputed, and [scene:de6_claims_vs_record|the last section] grades it as such. [cite:de6_tnr_duran] Whether that confinement amounts to anything that exists today is examined, rung by rung, in [scene:de35_the_consequence_ladder|Part Thirty-Five].

"Why Carlyle Matters," July 2009

In July 2009 Yarvin published two essays on the Victorian writer Thomas Carlyle, whose case for strong rule he credited with his turn away from democracy; they were later collected, with a third, as Moldbug on Carlyle, a 2010 compilation now held by Princeton University Library. [cite:de3_princeton_carlyle] [cite:de6_princeton] [cite:de3_tait] In "Why Carlyle Matters" he wrote: "Not all humans are born the same, of course," and "the innate character and intelligence of some is more suited to mastery than slavery. For others, it is more suited to slavery." "A person makes a good slave if he is loyal, patient, and not exceptionally bright or stubborn." He attributed what he called "this broad pattern of observation" to heredity: it was "most parsimoniously explained by genetic differences." [cite:de6_mm_2021] [cite:de3_audit_mm]

These lines are in Yarvin's own voice, quoted in a block from the post, not lines of Carlyle's that he was reporting. The paragraph that precedes them in the original, which would show how he introduced the passage and what "pattern" he meant, could not be verified, and the reading here goes no further than the quoted words. What those words say is plain enough: that fitness to be a master or a slave is, for some people, innate and hereditary. That is a defence of natural hierarchy in its oldest form. In 2016, during the dispute over his invitation to the LambdaConf programming conference, he wrote that he was not an "outspoken advocate for slavery," a racist, a sexist or a fascist, putting the first phrase in quotation marks as his accusers' words. [cite:de6_audit_wiki] In 2025, pressed by Marchese on his writing that the Civil War had not made "anyone's life more pleasant, including that of freed slaves," he said that abolition "was a necessary step to get through that period." When Marchese called his own position on slavery "very morally clear," Yarvin replied that the interviewer was responding "in this kind of cartoonish way." [cite:de6_audit_nyt]

A sentence often attributed to him, that "some races are better suited for slavery than others," does not appear in the post. It is a paraphrase that has circulated as a quotation, and it is wrong as a quotation: the verified text speaks of individuals and of heredity, not of races by name. The correction does not make the passage milder; it makes the record accurate. [cite:de6_mm_2021] [cite:de3_mm]

"Right-wing terrorism as folk activism," 23 July 2011

On 22 July 2011 Anders Behring Breivik killed seventy-seven people in Norway, with a bomb in Oslo and a shooting at a political youth summer camp. [cite:de6_mm_2021] The next day Yarvin published a post about the attack. He called Breivik a terrorist, "ABB is a terrorist. Nelson Mandela is a terrorist," and argued that no one who condoned "Che, Stalin, Mao, or any other leftist murderer" had standing to demand that the right disown "a rightist who didn't even make triple digits"; he made the point with a vulgar line aimed at such critics. [cite:de6_mm_2021] [cite:de6_audit_mm] He also wrote: "At least he shot communists, not Muslims. He gored the matador and not the cape." [cite:de5_pein_baffler] [cite:de6_mm_2021] The journalist James Pogue, in Vanity Fair in 2022, summarised the post's objection: the killings were condemned "not on the grounds that terrorism is wrong but because the killings wouldn't do anything effective to overthrow" the Norwegian government. [cite:de6_audit_wiki]

A widely repeated claim says that Yarvin endorsed Breivik. The post does not; it calls him a terrorist. Its offence is different and is on the page: it judged a massacre at a youth camp by its usefulness, and used it to argue that Mandela deserved the same verdict. In 2025, asked about the post, Yarvin held the same equivalence and put it more soberly: "I prefer to condemn them both," since both, he said, killed innocent people. [cite:de6_nyt_transcript]

Read together, the five posts show a consistent habit: a provocation stated flatly, a qualification or a joke, and an argument that continues after the joke. They show contempt for democratic equality, sympathy for race science, and a willingness to describe the coercion of unwanted people, sometimes in comic dress, as administration. They do not show a call to violence by his readers, and in 2009 he described the reaction's task as persuasion. [cite:de6_le_contra] How his public statements about these texts compare with the texts themselves is the subject of the last section.
