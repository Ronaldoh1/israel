Part Ten follows the promise that a working life ends in a secure retirement. It begins with the private pension: the 1963 Studebaker collapse that led to federal pension insurance in 1974, the tax clause of 1978 that became the 401(k), and the four decades in which individual accounts replaced guaranteed pensions for most private-sector workers, with what that change did to who is covered, how much they hold, what fees take, and where the risk of running short now sits. It then turns to Social Security, the part of the retirement system that has most nearly done what it promised, setting out how it is built and financed, how far it reduced poverty in old age, how its rules were changed in 1983, why the cap on taxed wages now reaches less of the nation's pay, and what the 2034 trust-fund date does and does not mean. It ends with old age as it is lived now, measured by two official poverty rates, an index of what basic living actually costs older people, the savings older households hold, and the growing number of Americans still working past 65.
