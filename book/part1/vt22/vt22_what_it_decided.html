What Nicaea Decided: A Creed Under an Emperor's Eye

The Council of Nicaea did not decide which books were in the Bible, and it did not invent belief in the divinity of Jesus Christ; the next section tests both claims. What it did decide is large enough on its own. In the summer of 325, with the emperor present, the assembled bishops adopted a single written definition of how the Son relates to the Father, wrote into it a philosophical term that appears nowhere in the scriptures, attached curses to the opposing view, and saw the emperor exile those who would not sign and order the losing side's books burned. It also fixed a common rule for Easter, settled a schism in Egypt and issued twenty canons on church order. This section sets out each decision from the council's own texts and the letters of the men who were there. [cite:vt22_canons] [cite:vt22_socrates1]

The question on trial

Arius's position, as his opponents recorded it and as the council condemned it, was that "there was a time when the Son was not": the Son was begotten by the Father before time, but he was not eternal alongside the Father and was a creature, however exalted. [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] Arius did not deny that Christ was divine in some sense; his surviving fragments call the Son God, but a God who had a beginning. [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] His opponents, led by Alexander of Alexandria and his young deacon Athanasius, held that a Son who had a beginning could not save, because only God can join humanity to God. The dispute was therefore not whether Christ was divine, but what kind of divine and on whose terms.

The creed and the word homoousios

The creed the council adopted begins "We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible", and says of the Son that he is "of one substance" with the Father, in Greek homoousion. It ends with anathemas against anyone who says "that there was a time when the Son of God was not", that he was created, changeable, or of another substance than the Father. [cite:vt22_canons] The Holy Spirit receives one line. The creed is short and polemical: it was written to exclude Arius, not to summarise all Christian belief.

The decisive word came, by the account of one bishop in the room, from the emperor. Eusebius of Caesarea, a moderate who had been sympathetic to Arius, wrote to his own church explaining why he had signed. He said he had offered a creed from his home church, and that "our most pious emperor himself was the first to admit that they were perfectly correct", urging all to subscribe "with the insertion, however, of that single word homoousios". The emperor then explained that the word did not imply anything bodily, and on that reading Eusebius accepted it, "having regard to peace". [cite:vt22_socrates1] Eusebius had reason to present his signature as the emperor's wish rather than his own surrender, and other accounts credit Hosius of Corduba and Alexander with promoting the word. What is not in doubt is that Constantine and Hosius backed it. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea]

The term was not new. Valentinian Christians had used it in the second century, and a council at Antioch around 268 had condemned it in the case of Paul of Samosata, which is one reason many eastern bishops distrusted it. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] Its meaning was disputed for the next fifty years. That a contested philosophical word, absent from scripture, became the test of orthodoxy at an emperor's urging is the council's most consequential finding, and the record supports it directly.

Signatures, exile and the burning order

The council's own synodal letter, preserved by Socrates, reports that Theonas of Marmarica and Secundus of Ptolemais refused to sign and "suffered the same condemnation" as Arius. [cite:vt22_socrates1] Socrates himself names five bishops who objected to homoousios: Eusebius of Nicomedia, Theognis of Nicaea, Maris of Chalcedon, Theonas and Secundus. [cite:vt22_socrates1] The two accounts can be reconciled. Eusebius of Nicomedia, by other ancient testimony, signed the creed after long resistance but kept contact with Arius's party and was exiled a few months after the council, with Theognis; he was restored about 329. [cite:vt22_wiki_eusnic] Arius, Theonas and Secundus were excommunicated and sent into exile in Illyria. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea] The penalty for refusing a church definition was imposed by the state.

The emperor then wrote "to the bishops and people". He ordered that Arius and his followers be called "Porphyrians", after a pagan philosopher who had written against Christianity, and he commanded that "if any treatise composed by Arius should be discovered, let it be consigned to the flames"; anyone found hiding one faced death. [cite:vt22_socrates1] This is the one documented order to burn books in the Nicene story. It concerned Arius's own writings, not any gospel. Historians accept that burning took place to some degree, but no execution under the order is recorded. [cite:vt22_wiki_arius] Its effect on the record is visible: Arius's Thalia survives only in fragments quoted by his opponents, chiefly Athanasius. [cite:vt22_wiki_arius]

Easter, and the language used about Jews

The council's second task was the date of Easter. Some eastern churches calculated it from the Jewish reckoning of Passover; Rome and Alexandria did not. The synodal letter says those "who have heretofore kept this festival when the Jews did" would now conform to Rome and Alexandria. [cite:vt22_socrates1] The council set uniformity as the rule but, as far as the record shows, did not prescribe a method of calculation. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea]

Constantine's letter announcing the Easter ruling to the churches justified it in hostile terms. It speaks of "the detestable Jewish crowd" and of "that nation of parricides who slew their Lord". [cite:vt22_vc3] Those phrases are the emperor's, written to make a calendar decision into a mark of separation from Judaism, and they became part of a long tradition of Christian anti-Jewish polemic. The Catholic Church formally rejected the collective charge behind them at the Second Vatican Council. Nostra Aetate, proclaimed on 28 October 1965, states that Christ's passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive", nor against Jews today, and "decries hatred, persecutions, displays of anti-Semitism, directed against Jews at any time and by anyone". [cite:vt22_nostra]

The Melitians and the twenty canons

The synodal letter also dealt with Melitius of Lycopolis, an Egyptian bishop who had ordained clergy in rivalry with Alexandria. He was allowed to "remain in his own city but exercise no authority either to ordain or nominate for ordination". [cite:vt22_socrates1] The settlement did not hold, and the Melitians later allied with Arius's supporters against Athanasius. [cite:vt22_wiki_nicaea]

The twenty canons are rules of church order, and several had long consequences. Canon 6 confirmed that "the ancient customs in Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis prevail", giving the bishop of Alexandria authority over those provinces, "since the like is customary for the Bishop of Rome also", and kept Antioch's privileges. Canon 7 gave the bishop of Jerusalem, then called Aelia, a place of honour while preserving the rights of his metropolitan. [cite:vt22_canons] This is the first time a general council recognised that some bishops held authority over whole regions: the start of the system later called the patriarchates, in which Rome was one great see among several. Fifty-six years later the Council of Constantinople gave Constantinople "the prerogative of honour after the Bishop of Rome" because it was "New Rome". [cite:vt22_wiki_const381] Other canons set terms for receiving clergy of the Novatian church, whom the council called Cathari ("the pure"), unrelated to the medieval Cathars, and required followers of Paul of Samosata to be rebaptised. Canon 20 ordered that prayer on Sundays and at Pentecost be made standing. None of the twenty names a book of scripture. [cite:vt22_canons]

What happened next

Nicaea did not end the dispute. Constantine became more lenient toward the exiles within a few years. [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] A synod at Jerusalem restored Arius to communion in 336, and he died in Constantinople that year before he could be received; Socrates presented the death as divine judgement, and some modern scholars suspect poison. [cite:vt22_wiki_arius] The bishop who baptised Constantine on his deathbed in May 337 was Eusebius of Nicomedia, the exile of 325. [cite:vt22_wiki_eusnic] Constantine's son Constantius II favoured Arius's heirs, and after the twin councils of Rimini and Seleucia in 359, Jerome wrote that the world "awoke with a groan to find itself Arian". [cite:vt22_wiki_arianism] The Nicene position was restored only when Theodosius I convened about 150 eastern bishops at Constantinople in 381, which reaffirmed and expanded the creed. [cite:vt22_wiki_const381]

The documented finding is therefore precise. Nicaea did not create Christianity, but it created a new way of deciding what Christianity was: a written test of belief, framed in disputed philosophical language, adopted in the emperor's presence, and enforced by exile and the burning of books. That method outlived Constantine and every one of the bishops in the room.
