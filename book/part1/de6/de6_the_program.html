The Program: From Formalism to RAGE

Yarvin's political writing is long, digressive and ironic, and it is easy to quote in pieces that make it sound either harmless or monstrous. Read in order, it has a clear structure: a diagnosis of who really rules, a design for a different kind of state, and a route from one to the other. This section sets out each, defines its terms in his own words where possible, and notes where the vocabulary changed between the pseudonymous blog of 2007–2014 and the interviews he gave under his own name in 2025. The hardest passages, which arise where the design meets people it has no use for, are the subject of [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|the next section].

The diagnosis: formalism and the Cathedral

The first post of Unqualified Reservations, "A formalist manifesto" of 23 April 2007, began from property. Political conflict, Yarvin argued, comes from disagreement about who owns what, and violence is "anything that breaks the rule, or replaces it with a different rule." His remedy was to stop arguing about who ought to hold power and to record who actually does: "We need a rule that tells us whose wallet is whose." "Formalism says: let's figure out exactly who has what, now, and give them a little fancy certificate." [cite:de2_le] [cite:de6_le_contra] Formalism, in short, is the proposal that real power should be made official, so that it can be owned, priced and held to account as property rather than hidden behind elections.

Applied to the United States, the formalist question was where real power sat, and his answer was the term for which he is best known. "The Cathedral," in Yarvin's usage, is the set of prestigious institutions, chiefly the universities and the established press, together with the permanent civil service that takes its cues from them, which he argued set the bounds of respectable opinion and policy without being elected. [cite:de6_pein_2014] [cite:de1_tablet] He has insisted that it is not a conspiracy: the institutions behave, as Siegel summarised it, "as if they were a single organizational structure" because of shared incentives, not coordination. [cite:de1_tablet] In a later interview he defined it in one line: "The cathedral is essentially the reification of the legitimate sources of information." [cite:de6_wbur] Nick Land, who popularised the term in 2012, credited it to him: "Moldbug calls it the Cathedral." [cite:de6_audit_land] Its creed, in Yarvin's account, is "Universalism," a secular descendant of New England Protestantism. [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|Part Seven] weighs how much of the diagnosis survives scrutiny; [scene:de8_tests_it_fails|Part Eight] shows why critics find it unfalsifiable.

The design: neocameralism, the CEO-monarch and Patchwork

If power cannot be abolished, Yarvin's next step went, it should be owned. He called his model "neocameralism," after the cameralism of eighteenth-century Prussia, in which the state's finances were managed as a royal estate. Land's summary of Moldbug's definition is the clearest: "To a neocameralist, a state is a business which owns a country." Ownership is divided into shares, "each share has one vote, and the shareholders elect a board, which hires and fires managers," and the residents are customers. [cite:de6_land_ebook] [cite:de6_audit_land] Yarvin made the point with a fictional country: "Fnargland is a business. Like any business, it has no reason at all to alienate its customers." [cite:de6_le_contra] A passage Land reproduces without naming its source says that neocameralism "has never been tried" and that its nearest equivalents were Frederick the Great's Prussia and "Hong Kong, Singapore and Dubai." [cite:de6_land_ebook] [cite:de1_audit_land_unattrib]

At the head of such a state sits a chief executive with full authority, the figure Yarvin's "Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives" of 2008, by Kofman's count about 120,000 words, called a "CEO-monarch." [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] He has not changed the model's core. In May 2025 he told CNN that the monarch "should operate like a startup CEO," answerable to a board rather than to voters. [cite:de6_cnn_2025] [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin] Kofman reported the safeguard he proposes against a bad ruler: a board, kept secret, that can select and recall the chief executive and holds cryptographic keys able to disable the government's weapons. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] The weakness of that safeguard is examined in [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight].

Patchwork, the series that began on 13 November 2008, scaled the design down and multiplied it. Large states would give way to a patchwork of small ones, "each governed by its own joint-stock corporation without regard to the residents' opinions," competing for residents who would have a single remedy: "If residents don't like their government, they can and should move." [cite:de2_le] This is the idea of exit in its purest form. The economist Albert Hirschman had distinguished "exit," leaving an organisation one dislikes, from "voice," trying to change it from within; Yarvin's design abolishes voice and relies on exit alone, the formula Land compressed to "No voice, free exit." [cite:de6_audit_land] Kofman reported that he still describes the end state as statelets "like Singapore or Dubai." [cite:de6_kofman_nuso]

The route: RAGE

Patchwork imagined new states; the route Yarvin proposed for the existing one was a takeover. In 2012, at a technology "unconference" called BIL, he gave a talk on how to reboot the American government. Corey Pein, writing in 2014, recorded its central lines: "If Americans want to change their government, they're going to have to get over their dictator phobia"; what the country needed was "a national CEO" or "what's called a dictator"; "There's really no other solution." Yarvin even proposed two, one for Republican states and one for Democratic ones. [cite:de6_pein_2014] The first step of the takeover was to "Retire All Government Employees," which he abbreviated RAGE. [cite:de6_tnr_duran] The acronym has only ever meant that; a version that expands it as "Random Acts of Government Efficiency" circulates online and is wrong. The idea travelled quickly: an online petition to the White House drafted later by the programmer Justine Tunney took its first demand, to retire all government employees, verbatim from the talk. [cite:de6_pein_2014]

How a leader would take that power was left vague in the early texts, and the vagueness matters. In his 2009 "Gentle Introduction" series Yarvin described the reaction's task as persuasion: "All the Reaction must do is convince reasonable, educated men and women of good will." Power, once won, would be held by a party "only as long as it takes to hire a qualified administrator." [cite:de6_le_contra] In his 2025 interviews the route ran through an election. A president, he told the New York Times, would take the kind of power he believed Franklin Roosevelt had demanded in 1933, which he paraphrased as "give me absolute power or I'll take it anyway." [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] The civil service would be wound down "like a company that goes out of business, but sort of more so," its former officials treated as ordinary people who had served a bad regime. A new regime "can't be" about bayoneting professors, he said; "Winning means these are your people now." [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] CNN reported a harder note from 2022: the image of loyal "ninjas" who would "seize all points of power, without respect for paper protections." [cite:de6_cnn_2025]

What changed, and what did not

Between 2012 and 2025 the words softened and the design did not. The 2012 talk asked Americans to get over their "dictator phobia"; the 2025 interviews spoke of national chief executives, "monarch" as a "neutral term," and a democracy that is not bad but "very weak." [cite:de6_pein_2014] [cite:de1_nyt_interview] The single executive with full authority, accountable to a board rather than to voters, and the retirement of the civil service, appear in both. Whether that change of vocabulary is a reversal or a repackaging is a judgement [scene:de6_claims_vs_record|the last section of this part] sets out with his own explanation beside it.

One more distinction belongs here because it is so often lost. In 2025 the federal government cut its workforce sharply and claimed broad new powers over the civil service, and many commentators described this as RAGE carried out. The resemblance is real; provenance is another matter. No published document shows a line of descent from the 2012 talk to any order or plan of 2025; the only reported link is an anonymous adviser's remark that the people designing the cuts had read him. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] Rhyme is not provenance. [scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|Part Thirty-Two] sets RAGE beside Schedule F and its successors clause by clause, and [scene:de33_two_tests|Part Thirty-Five] asks what the cuts actually achieved.
