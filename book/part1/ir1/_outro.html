Part One established that the alliance was real, sustained and deliberately hidden. Iran voted against partition in 1947 but recognised Israel de facto on 6 March 1950, never de jure; Mosaddegh's government closed the Jerusalem consulate in 1951 without withdrawing that recognition. [cite:ir1_wiki_embtehran] [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] The coup of August 1953, which the CIA's own history says was "carried out under CIA direction," restored the Shah, under whom the relationship grew; historians agree on the CIA's role and disagree on how decisive it was. [cite:ir1_afp_cia2013] [cite:ir1_iranica_coup] [cite:ir1_takeyh_fa] Through Ben-Gurion's periphery policy and the Trident arrangement, Israel's intelligence service worked with SAVAK from 1957, and historians report that Mossad helped found it; Iran was a route out of Iraq for its Jews; Israeli exports to Iran passed $100 million a year by 1977, and in that year the two governments signed oil-for-arms contracts including a missile project, Flower. [cite:ir1_alpher_fa] [cite:ir1_irb_savak] [cite:ir1_wiki_ezra] [cite:ir1_wiki_flower] Iran did not join the Arab oil embargo of 1973, though Egypt's blockade of the Red Sea cut the tanker route to Eilat during the war. [cite:ir1_wiki_1973oil] [cite:ir1_thompson_energy] The two governments built the Eilat–Ashkelon pipeline together in 1968; since the revolution Israel has kept it, kept it secret and, citing its law on trading with an enemy, has not paid an arbitral award of about $1.1 billion that a Swiss court upheld in 2016. [cite:ir1_calcalist_2017] [cite:ir1_ih_swiss2016] [cite:ir1_toi_2016] Part Two follows the alliance through the revolution that ended it, and the secret arms trade that continued afterwards.
