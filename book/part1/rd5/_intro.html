When the Second World War ended in Europe in 1945, there was no Jewish state and no United Nations decision on Palestine. Within three years both existed. This part of the story covers that short interval, from the summer of 1945 to the founding year of 1948, and asks a simple question: how were the decisive pieces put in place?

The answer runs along several tracks at once. In a Manhattan apartment in July 1945, David Ben-Gurion set in motion an arms and supply network that would operate for three years outside US law. In Washington, a president who had been Vice President for only 82 days inherited the Palestine question. He made his choices amid White House advisers, an old friend's personal access, and an approaching election. In Palestine, Britain's mass raid of June 1946 was followed by the King David Hotel bombing. The bombing hardened British opinion toward getting out, and it helped push London to hand the problem to the United Nations. There, the Jewish Agency, the body that represented the Jewish community of Palestine, arrived with a prepared plan. The Arab Higher Committee, the leading Palestinian Arab body, boycotted the inquiry.

Throughout, warnings were written down. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Secretary of Defense, the new CIA and critics outside government all objected, in time and on the record. The pages that follow trace who acted, who objected, and why the objections did not prevail.
