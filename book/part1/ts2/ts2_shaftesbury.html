Shaftesbury, Palmerston and the Jerusalem Bishopric, 1838–1896

Restorationism became a matter of state through one man's family connections. Anthony Ashley-Cooper, known as Lord Ashley before he inherited the earldom, was an evangelical member of Parliament and a social reformer. On 10 June 1830 he married Lady Emily Cowper, the stepdaughter (and, his biographers' sources suggest, probably the natural daughter) of Lord Palmerston, the Foreign Secretary. [cite:ts2_wiki_shaftesbury] The outline is in [scene:bc_s05|Restoration before Zionism]. This section sets out the documents and the dates, and keeps two motives in view throughout: the prophetic hopes of Shaftesbury and the London Society, and the strategic calculations of the minister who acted on them.

A consul in Jerusalem, 1838–1839

The first concrete result was diplomatic. Shaftesbury helped persuade Palmerston to send a British consul to Jerusalem, and William Tanner Young, a protégé of Shaftesbury's, was appointed vice-consul in 1838 and arrived in 1839. [cite:ts2_wiki_shaftesbury] [cite:ts2_wiki_young] The timing was strategic as well as religious: since 1831 Syria, Palestine included, had been held by Muhammad Ali, the governor of Egypt, in rebellion against the Ottoman sultan, and Britain's position in the region was shifting. [cite:ts2_wiki_shaftesbury] From mid-1839 Young asked Palmerston for authority to extend British protection to Jews in the city. A modern study quoted in his biography suggests he tried to serve two masters, the London Society and the Foreign Office; the Scottish ministers Andrew Bonar and Robert Murray M'Cheyne, who met him, described him as moved by "a deep and enlightened attachment to the cause of God's ancient people". [cite:ts2_wiki_young] He was consul from 1841 to 1845.

In January 1839 Ashley carried the argument into print. Reviewing Lord Lindsay's travel letters in the Quarterly Review, he proposed that Jews be resettled in Palestine, the first such proposal by a major British politician, and he argued in economic terms that the land needed only "capital and skill". [cite:ts2_wiki_shaftesbury] Crome summarises the article as urging Britain to establish a Jewish homeland in Palestine. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm]

Palmerston's dispatch, 11 August 1840

The minister did not share the prophecy. In 1840, with the eastern crisis over Muhammad Ali at its height, Ashley pressed Palmerston to have Britain promote a Jewish presence in Palestine under Ottoman authority and British protection. Crome, drawing on Shaftesbury's diary, records that Ashley found himself "forced to argue from worldly reasons" because Palmerston had little interest in prophecy, and that Palmerston instructed his ambassador to raise the idea with the sultan without informing the cabinet. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm]

The instruction survives in the Foreign Office files (FO 78/390, No. 134), from Palmerston to Lord Ponsonby, the ambassador at Constantinople, dated 11 August 1840. It is written entirely in the language of interest. Palmerston reported that there existed among the Jews of Europe "a strong notion that the Time is approaching when their nation is to return to Palestine". He argued that if they returned "under the sanction and protection, and at the invitation of the Sultan", they would be "a check upon any future evil designs of Mehemet Ali or his Successor", and he added: "It is well known that the Jews of Europe possess great wealth." [cite:ts2_cojs_palmerston1840] The dispatch makes no appeal to scripture. It offers the sultan settlers and capital, and Britain a client population in a province contested with Egypt. The sultan did not view the proposal favourably, and nothing came of it. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm]

The Protestant bishop in Jerusalem, 1841

The second result was ecclesiastical. In 1841 King Frederick William IV of Prussia sent his envoy Christian Bunsen to London with a proposal for a joint Protestant bishopric in Jerusalem, which the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Bishop of London welcomed. The Act of Parliament that made it possible came into force on 5 October 1841. An endowment of £30,000 provided the bishop with £1,200 a year; Britain and Prussia were to nominate alternately, with a Canterbury veto over Prussian choices. [cite:ts2_wiki_bishopric] Crome credits Shaftesbury's work with Bunsen for the result, and records that the London Society partly funded it and that restorationists saw it as a means of preparing Jews for conversion. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm]

The choice of bishop made the purpose plain. Michael Solomon Alexander had been a rabbi in Plymouth before his conversion in 1825; he became a missionary of the London Society and professor of Hebrew and rabbinical literature at King's College London. He was consecrated on 7 November 1841, reached Jerusalem early in 1842, and died near Cairo on 23 November 1845. [cite:ts2_wiki_bishopric] Crome judges that the bishopric had little practical effect. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] It was contested at home from both sides: German Lutherans thought too much had been conceded, and the Tractarians of the Oxford Movement opposed it as a Protestant compromise; John Henry Newman counted it among the reasons for his move to Rome. Prussia ended the arrangement on 3 November 1886, after which the Church of England kept the see alone. [cite:ts2_wiki_bishopric] In Lewis's account, Shaftesbury's three aims, a consul, an Anglican church and a joint bishopric, had all been achieved by 1849. [cite:ts2_sandmel_lewis]

The long middle and the American handover

Shaftesbury became president of the London Society in 1848 and later helped found the Palestine Exploration Fund in 1865, but his influence in government waned from the mid-1850s. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] Lewis, who calls him the "leading Christian Zionist of the nineteenth century", traces a line from his advocacy to the British attitudes that helped produce the Balfour Declaration in 1917, more than thirty years after his death. [cite:ts2_sandmel_lewis] That line is an argument about a climate of opinion, not a documented chain of decisions, and it is tested where Balfour's own motives are examined, in [scene:ts6_balfour|Part Six] and [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration]. By the end of the century the centre of gravity was shifting to the United States. In March 1891 the Chicago evangelist William Blackstone presented a memorial to President Benjamin Harrison and Secretary of State James Blaine urging that Palestine be given back to the Jews. [cite:ts2_crome_cdamm] His petition, and the system of prophecy behind it, belong to the story told in [scene:ts3_darby|Part Three] and [scene:ts6_blackstone|Part Six].

Hechler and Herzl

The British strand had one more direct link to the Zionist movement, and it was personal. William Hechler, born in Benares in 1845, was chaplain to the British embassy in Vienna from 1885 to 1910. In 1884 he had published The Restoration of the Jews to Palestine, and from a reckoning of 1,260 years counted from a prophecy of the reign of the Caliph Umar (637–638) he expected a decisive event in 1897 or 1898. [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] Theodor Herzl's Der Judenstaat appeared in 1896. Herzl recorded their first meeting, on 10 March 1896, and was not wholly persuaded by his visitor: "I take him for a naïve visionary with a collector's enthusiasm." [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] Hechler was nonetheless useful. He had been tutor in the household of the Grand Duke of Baden and arranged Herzl's audience with the Grand Duke on 25 April 1896; he attended the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897. [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] The Jewish Telegraphic Agency's obituary in February 1931 said that he introduced Herzl to Wilhelm II and accompanied him when the Kaiser received him in Jerusalem, and that Herzl's diaries mention him more often than anyone else. [cite:ts2_jta_hechler1931] Hechler's own biography records a more indirect route to the Kaiser, through Philipp zu Eulenburg, after an early attempt failed. [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] The Kaiser episode and what it achieved are told in [scene:hz_s01|Herzl's search for a charter], and Hechler's maps of a biblical Palestine in [scene:gi2_herzl|The Greater Israel Architecture].

Hechler differed from the restorationists of the earlier sections on one point that matters for the rest of this volume. His biography records that he did not hold Jewish conversion to be a precondition for the return or for the Second Coming, and quotes him: "It is the duty of every Christian to love the Jews." [cite:ts2_wiki_hechler] With him the strand passed from a theology of conversion towards practical support for a Jewish political movement on that movement's terms. What it did not have, in 1840, 1841 or 1898, was the power to deliver anything: Palestine was the sultan's, and each request failed at Constantinople.
