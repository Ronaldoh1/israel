Who Decides in America

When a sentence about Israel or Palestine appears in an American public-school textbook, someone chose it. The question this section asks is who, and under what authority. The answer starts with a law that takes the national government out of the question entirely.

No one in Washington

Federal law, at 20 U.S.C. § 1232a, forbids any federal department or official to exercise "direction, supervision, or control" over the curriculum or the selection of textbooks in the nation's schools. [cite:usc_20_1232a] The Department of Education therefore does not review, approve or correct textbooks, on this subject or any other. That was a deliberate choice, made to keep schooling local. Its effect is that there is no national fact-check. Responsibility passes to fifty states, and from many of them to thousands of local school districts.

The states that buy in bulk

Some states approve textbooks centrally and others leave the choice to districts. The states that adopt centrally, and buy for millions of pupils at once, have the most leverage over publishers. Texas is the best-known example, and the reasons are structural. Its State Board of Education has fifteen members, each elected from a district of about 1.8 million people; in early 2026 ten were Republicans and five Democrats. The board sets the state's curriculum standards and approves the instructional materials used in every Texas public school. It also manages the Permanent School Fund, valued at $60.6 billion, which helps pay for those materials. [cite:pen_chron_sboe2026] In 2015 the board voted against having independent academic experts check textbooks for factual errors. [cite:tx_board_rejects_factcheck]

A common claim needs care here. It is often said that "Texas decides the textbooks for the whole country." The record is more mixed. Texas is one of about twenty states that approve lists of books centrally, and its market was second only to California's. An industry spokesman called its national sway "a bit of an urban myth," pointing to publishers' growing ability to tailor editions state by state. Others in the field disagreed: a consultant to publishers said a big Texas or California book is often reused as the base for other states' editions, changed but not rewritten "from the ground up." [cite:pen_edweek_texas2010] The fair reading is that a sentence written for Texas reviewers does not automatically reach a pupil in Oregon, but that Texas remains a buyer large enough that publishers write with its board in mind.

Other states rely on publishers to vouch for themselves. Virginia's review process has accepted publishers' own certification that their books are accurate. [cite:va_textbook_review] Where a state does no checking, and the federal government is barred from checking, the only people reading the drafts closely are the publishers' staff and whoever else takes the trouble to submit comments.

Who takes the trouble

For this subject, one group takes the trouble more systematically than any other. The Institute for Curriculum Services was founded in 2005 by the Jewish Community Relations Council of the Bay Area in San Francisco. By 2024 it described itself as a $2.8 million national programme that had trained more than 18,000 teachers and administrators in all fifty states and achieved 14,000 "tangible improvements to instructional materials and state standards." In July 2024 it became an independent nonprofit. [cite:pen_ejp_ics2024]

How that work reaches a state's textbook process is documented in Virginia, through records released under the state's freedom-of-information law. On 28 February 2018, the directors of three Jewish community relations councils, in Richmond, Tidewater and Greater Washington, wrote to the Virginia Department of Education about the textbooks then being considered for adoption. They said the Institute had reviewed the books at their request, endorsed its recommendations, asked the state to correct what they called "errors and misleading information regarding Judaism and Jewish history" before approval, and thanked Pearson and McGraw-Hill for earlier changes made on its advice. [cite:pen_jcrc_va_letter2018]

Some of the requested changes concerned the vocabulary of the conflict itself. According to the Virginia Coalition for Human Rights, which obtained and published the submissions, they included replacing "settlers" with "communities," "occupation" with "control of," "wall" with "security fence," and "militant" with "terrorist." In May 2018 fourteen Virginia academics, among them the former State Department official Lawrence Wilkerson and the scholar of American policy William Quandt, wrote to the publishers asking them not to adopt the recommendations without review by "a diverse panel of qualified and nonpartisan academic experts." [cite:pen_afp_vchr2018] The published record does not show how many of the changes the publishers made.

The other side of the argument, and the other direction

The Institute and the councils describe this work as correcting errors, and some of what they flag may be wrong in the books. A textbook can misstate Jewish history as easily as Palestinian history, and asking a publisher to fix a mistake is a legal and ordinary thing to do. The objection from the academics was not that a community group may comment. It was that one side's preferred vocabulary for a contested conflict was being presented as a factual correction, through a process the public cannot see.

Pressure also runs the other way, and through the courts as well as the review panels. Six taxpayers in Newton, Massachusetts, sued the city's public schools, saying the high-school history courses contained anti-Israel and biased material, and asked a court to order the material removed and the curriculum vetted by a court-appointed reviewer. They dropped the case in the summer of 2019 before any judge ruled. [cite:pen_newtonite_suit] Campaigns over ethnic-studies courses in California, discussed in the last section of this part, have drawn organized groups on both sides.

What the American system adds up to

The record does not show one group writing America's textbooks. It shows a system with no national checker, states that range from heavy review to none, publishers owned by investors the public cannot see, and an open door for anyone organized enough to submit comments. On this subject, one national network's work is documented in detail, and its requested edits reach the language of the conflict itself. The publishers' answers to those edits are not on any public record, and that absence, more than any single change, is the finding.
