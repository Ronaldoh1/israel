In 1896 Theodor Herzl (1860–1904), a Budapest-born journalist working in Vienna, published Der Judenstaat, a pamphlet that weighed Palestine against Argentina as the territory for a political answer to European antisemitism. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] The country he named first had no border and no government of its own. It was Ottoman land, administered since 1872 as the Mutasarrifate of Jerusalem, with the districts of Jaffa, Hebron, Beersheba and Gaza, and the sanjaks of Nablus and Acre to its north. [cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] Most of its people were Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians. Its Jews, about 7,000 in 1800 and about 27,000 in 1880 by the National Library of Israel's count, lived mostly in four holy cities, largely on charity from abroad, and had no program of statehood; since 1881 a new wave of immigrants had begun to change them. [cite:rs_rd1_nli_oldyishuv] From the 1880s the Ottoman government restricted Jewish immigration and land purchase. [cite:w_ottoman_land_code] In 1896 there was not yet a Zionist Organization, a movement bank, a national land fund or a Zionist office in Jaffa.

This stage asks how, in twenty-one years, a pamphlet became a working structure: who built it, with whose money, under which law, and against whom. It also tests a familiar claim, that the project of a Jewish state grew out of the catastrophe of the 1930s and 1940s. The dates in this stage stand against that claim. By 1905 the movement had weighed Argentina, a Sinai buffer at Suez and a British offer in East Africa, and it had chosen Palestine through its own contested votes. Herzl died in 1904 at 44, long before any of what he predicted came to pass. [cite:herzl_diaries]

The cast is wide, and it argued with itself. On the Zionist side stood Herzl and his successors David Wolffsohn and Otto Warburg; Chaim Weizmann and Nahum Sokolow, who led the movement from London in wartime; Arthur Ruppin and Yehoshua Hankin, who organized the buying of land; the young workers of the Second Aliyah; and, in the United States, Henrietta Szold and Louis Brandeis. Against Herzl, or beside him on other terms, stood Jewish opponents: the German "protest rabbis", the Bund, Ahad Ha'am, Edmond de Rothschild, American Reform rabbis, Agudat Israel and, in 1917, the official bodies of British Jewry. The powers Herzl approached for a charter were Abdul Hamid II, Kaiser Wilhelm II, Joseph Chamberlain, Vyacheslav von Plehve and Pius X. In Palestine and its region, Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi wrote to Herzl in 1899, the Nazareth governor Shukri al-Asali tried to block a land sale in 1910, Najib Nassar and the El-Issa cousins built an Arabic press that argued against Zionism, and Jerusalem's deputies raised it in the Ottoman parliament. The sellers were landowners, many of them absentee, such as the Sursock family of Beirut; the tenants who worked their land held no title to it.

The sections follow that record roughly in time. They begin with the Ottoman country and the dispute over how many people lived in it, then the Basel Congress of 1897 and its opponents, the machinery of the Zionist Organization, and Herzl's search for a charter. Herzl's exchange with al-Khalidi sets out what the founders knew of the people already there and what they proposed. The money follows: the Jewish Colonial Trust, the Jewish National Fund, and the Anglo-Palestine Company, founded in the same months that the Kishinev pogrom drove the movement to vote on East Africa. The rejection of that offer in 1905, the split it caused and a labelled thought experiment about it come next, then the Jewish currents for and against, the Second Aliyah and the Yemenite immigration, the Palestine Office, and the Scofield Reference Bible. The land mechanism is examined from the Ottoman sale of the Jezreel Valley in 1872 onward, beside the Arab press that reported it. The American organizations, the founding of the Federal Reserve and the claim attached to it, and the war years in Palestine lead to the services Jews rendered Britain and to [scene:s07a|what stood by 1917].
