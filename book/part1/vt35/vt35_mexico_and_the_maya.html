Mexico and the Maya

In central Mexico the sun had two faces. Tonatiuh was the sun of the daytime sky, called the Fifth Sun because the Mexica held that four earlier worlds, each with its own sun, had been destroyed before this one. [cite:vt35_wiki_tonatiuh] Huitzilopochtli was the patron god of the Mexica and of their capital, Tenochtitlan, a god of the sun and of war. [cite:vt35_wiki_huitz] Among the Maya, the sun god known in Yucatec and Lacandon as Kinich Ahau (k'inich is usually read "sun-eyed") appears in the codices as the figure scholars call God G, and the word k'inich served as a royal title in the Classic period. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayasun] This section sets out what the Spanish did to the temples, images and books of these suns, who did it, what the record says about the religion they were destroying, and what survived. The book-burnings of the Franciscan Diego de Landa, later bishop of Yucatán, and the work of the friars Zumárraga and Sahagún are told in the previous part and are only summarised here. [scene:vt34_the_americas|The Inquisitions in the Americas]

A sun that had to be fed

The record has to start with what the Mexica sun religion asked of its worshippers, because the conquerors used it to justify everything that followed and because leaving it out would misdescribe the religion. In the Mexica account, the present sun was born when the god Nanahuatzin threw himself into a fire at Teotihuacan, and it needed to be kept moving across the sky, its daily journey sustained by human sacrifice. [cite:vt35_wiki_tonatiuh] The myth of Huitzilopochtli's birth at Coatepec, where he defeats his sister Coyolxauhqui and his four hundred brothers, was read as the sunrise defeating the moon and stars, and offerings to him were understood as "sustenance for the Sun". [cite:vt35_wiki_huitz]

The scale of sacrifice is disputed, but archaeology has confirmed its reality. Excavations by Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) behind the cathedral had uncovered more than 600 skulls in the great skull rack, the huei tzompantli, by December 2020; it was built in stages between about 1486 and 1502. A reported figure of 20,000 prisoners sacrificed at the temple's rededication in 1487 is disputed, and archaeologists say they cannot tell how many of the dead were warriors. [cite:vt35_smith_tzompantli] The conquistador Andrés de Tapia described "tens of thousands of skulls"; that is a conqueror's estimate, not a count. [cite:vt35_smith_tzompantli]

The Templo Mayor and the cathedral

The Templo Mayor of Tenochtitlan carried two shrines on its summit, one to Tlaloc, the rain god, and the red southern shrine to Huitzilopochtli; the pyramid was understood as a re-creation of the hill of Coatepec. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor] [cite:vt35_wiki_huitz] The friar Toribio de Motolinía recorded that a feast was held when the sun stood in the middle of Huitzilopochtli's temple, "at the equinox"; this is the only known text from the period linking a Mesoamerican temple to an astronomical observation, and archaeoastronomers still debate what exactly it describes. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor]

The temple was almost totally destroyed by the Spanish in 1521, during and after the siege of the city by Hernán Cortés's army and its Indigenous allies. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor] The conquistadors then decided to build a church on the site, using the stones of the destroyed temple of Huitzilopochtli; the present Metropolitan Cathedral was begun in 1573 around that first church and finished in 1813, on top of the former sacred precinct. [cite:vt35_wiki_cathedral] The temple itself vanished under the colonial city until 21 February 1978, when workers for the electricity company struck the great carved disc of Coyolxauhqui; the excavation that followed, directed by Eduardo Matos Moctezuma from 1978 to 1982, brought the temple's foundations back into view. [cite:vt35_wiki_templomayor]

The image of Huitzilopochtli itself had a stranger history. One account says a soldier was about to destroy it when an Indigenous man rescued it; it resurfaced in the 1530s during an investigation by Bishop Juan de Zumárraga and was then lost again. [cite:vt35_wiki_huitz] Zumárraga, who held the powers of an apostolic inquisitor from 1535, never set up a formal tribunal, but he tried Don Carlos Ometochtzin, a lord of Texcoco, for returning to the old religion and handed him to the secular authorities, who burned him in 1539; the case is told in the previous part. [cite:vt35_wiki_zumarraga] [cite:vt34_wiki_ometochtzin] [scene:vt34_the_americas|The Inquisitions in the Americas]

The Sun Stone: buried by an archbishop

The great carved disc now called the Sun Stone, or Aztec calendar stone, was carved between about 1502 and 1521. Many read its central face as Tonatiuh; other scholars identify it as the earth monster Tlaltecuhtli or a lord of the night, and the stone is not a working calendar but a ritual monument that uses calendar signs, possibly a platform for sacrificial combat. [cite:vt35_wiki_sunstone] Around the face are the four earlier suns, with the present era, Four Movement, at the centre.

After the conquest the stone lay exposed near the Templo Mayor. According to the Dominican chronicler Diego Durán, Archbishop Alonso de Montúfar, who led the archdiocese of Mexico from 1551, ordered it buried so that "the memory of the ancient sacrifice that was made there would be lost". [cite:vt35_wiki_sunstone] It stayed underground until 17 December 1790, when it was found during repair works at the cathedral. What followed cuts against a simple story of suppression: the scholar Antonio de León y Gama asked the cathedral's canon not to have it buried again, and on 2 July 1791 it was mounted on the west tower of the cathedral, where it stayed, on public view, until 1885. It has been in the National Museum of Anthropology since 1964. [cite:vt35_wiki_sunstone] The archdiocese whose archbishop buried it in the sixteenth century let it stand on its cathedral in the eighteenth.

The Maya sky in four books

The Maya kept their astronomy in books of bark paper. Most were destroyed by Catholic priests during the conquest of Yucatán; Landa ordered many destroyed in July 1562 and wrote that because they contained "superstition and lies of the devil" he "burned them all". [cite:vt35_wiki_mayacodices] Four Maya codices survive: those now in Dresden, Madrid and Paris, and the Maya Codex of Mexico, judged authentic by INAH in 2018. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayacodices] What one surviving book shows is the measure of what the others probably held. About 65 per cent of the Dresden Codex is astronomical tables tracking eclipses, equinoxes, solstices and the cycles of Venus and Mars; its eclipse tables predicted solar eclipses accurately for thirty-three years in the eighth century, and eclipses are drawn as serpents swallowing the sun. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayacodices] Landa's own account also preserves a piece of the sun cult he opposed: he recorded that Kinich Ahau was patron of one of the four kinds of year in the fifty-two-year cycle. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayasun]

Central Mexican books survived in the same accidental way. The Codex Borgia, a pre-conquest manuscript of the 260-day divinatory calendar (the tonalpohualli) from the Puebla–Tlaxcala region, reached Europe in the colonial period, passed into the collection of the eighteenth-century Cardinal Stefano Borgia, went from him to the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith and in 1902 to the Vatican Apostolic Library, which has digitised it and made it public. Its final calendar page shows Tonatiuh receiving offerings. [cite:vt35_wiki_borgia] One of the most important surviving books of the Mexican sun religion is thus held, and published, by the Vatican.

What lived on

The public cult of the Mexica sun ended with the Mexica state, and human sacrifice ended with it; no one now argues for its return. Other parts of the old relation to the sun continued under Christian forms. The Totonac ceremony of the voladores, in which four men tied to ropes descend in spirals from a tall pole, was inscribed by UNESCO in 2009 as a fertility rite expressing "respect for and harmony with the natural and spiritual worlds". [cite:vt35_unesco_voladores] Among the Tzotzil Maya of San Juan Chamula in Chiapas, Christ and the sun are joined in a single figure, Our Father Sun/Christ, and the Virgin, the moon and the earth in a single Our Mother; the oral cosmology recorded by the anthropologist Gary Gossen opens with a text called "Of Our Father Sun in Heaven". [cite:vt35_everyculture_chamula] [cite:vt35_gossen_famsi] Among the Lacandon Maya, Kinich Ahau survives in a narrative in which he will one day descend and end the world. [cite:vt35_wiki_mayasun]

A widely repeated claim holds that colonial churches were deliberately aligned to the sun on the old festival dates, so that the Indigenous sun cult carried on in the masonry. The evidence is thin and mixed. A study of forty-one Jesuit mission churches in north-western New Spain found no clear solar pattern at all, with "surprisingly few" facing the eastern sunrise range, and its authors could not say why the churches face as they do. [cite:vt35_arxiv_jesuit_orient] The claim should be treated as open, and as unsupported for the region that has been measured.

In Mexico, then, the actors were an army that razed the temple in war, conquistadors who built on it, an archbishop who buried a monument to erase a memory, friars and a bishop who burned books, and a later colonial city and cathedral that put the same monument on display. The religion they suppressed was a sun cult that required human sacrifice, and its astronomy, calendars and histories were lost with it, except where a few books and many living communities kept them.
