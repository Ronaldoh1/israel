A smaller Jewish community remained in the land without a break, though for most of those centuries it was a minority. After crushing the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE), Rome killed, enslaved or expelled much of Judea's Jewish population, barred Jews from Jerusalem, rebuilt it as Aelia Capitolina and renamed the province Syria Palaestina [cite:wiki_syria_palaestina]. But a continuous Jewish presence stayed on, with Galilee as its religious center, where much classical Jewish literature was produced. In the 16th century Jewish communities took root in Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed, forming the pre-Zionist "Old Yishuv," a minority that fluctuated in size [cite:encyclo_israel_history].

The kernel of truth is that the Jewish population shrank sharply and the majority became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians, the ancestors of today's Palestinians, under Byzantine, Muslim, Crusader, Mamluk and Ottoman rule [cite:wiki_palestine_region]. Both histories are documented, and neither erases the other.
