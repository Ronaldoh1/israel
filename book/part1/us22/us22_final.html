The Final Score, Tested Five Ways

This section adds up the scores, tests the totals against the choices that produced them, answers directly the questions readers most often ask about the two systems, and gives this volume's final assessment. Every total below is this platform's synthesis of the published measures in the previous section, made by the rules stated in the first; none is a fact in the way a census count is, and a reader who weighs the areas differently can recompute from the same rows. [scene:us22_scores|The scores, area by area] [scene:us22_method|How this scorecard grades]

The totals

On services, the question the first scorecard put first, the new scorecard comes out close to where the old one did: Israel 3.2 and the United States 2.3 on the old 0–5 scale, a gap of about 1.0, against the first scorecard's 3.70 and 2.49, a gap of 1.21. The direction and size of that finding survive a method that publishes every row, scores housing and education on the figures rather than against them, and drops the contested bankruptcy estimate. Israel wins seven of the eleven areas: health, household debt and prices, housing, family and the safety net, public safety, wellbeing, and the economy and public finances. The United States wins three: work and wages, education, and equity inside each system. The eleventh, liberty and coercion, goes to whichever side the reader counts.

Five tests

The overall result holds in every test: under every weighting, every goalpost setting, every single area removed and with no goalposts at all, Israel's system scores higher, by between about 0.5 and 1.5 points of 10 on the scored methods, the smallest margin coming when only the three broad pillars are averaged in the everyone-governed view. The services result holds the same way. The liberty result does not. Counting everyone each state governs, the United States leads on liberty whichever single row is removed. Counting citizens only, Israel's narrow lead depends on two rows: remove the prisons row and the United States leads, 6.7 to 6.0, and remove the executions row and it leads 5.8 to 5.4. That is reported here as a tie.

The questions readers ask

Which country carries more public debt? The United States, and it is growing while Israel's has shrunk. American federal debt held by the public was 99 per cent of GDP in 2025, up from 26 per cent in 1980, and the Congressional Budget Office projects 120 per cent by 2036. Israel's general government debt was about 68 per cent of GDP in 2025, up from 61.9 per cent before the war but far below the roughly 163 per cent of the mid-1980s. [cite:us11_omb_debt] [cite:us11_cbo_outlook2026] [cite:us11_imf_debt] [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] [scene:us11_fiscal|Part Eleven: taxes and debt]

Whose households carry more debt? American households: 69.2 per cent of GDP in 2024 against 42.3 per cent in Israel. On top of that, Americans owed $1.651 trillion in student loans in 2026, with 9.3 million borrowers in default; Israel keeps university tuition low by regulation and publishes no comparable national student-debt series. [cite:us14_globes_mortgagegdp] [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] [cite:us17_ci_defaults] [cite:us14_huji_tuition]

Who spends more? It depends on who is spending, and on what. America spends far more per person on health, $13,473 in 2023 against $4,033, and almost twice as much per school student, $19,973 against $10,279; on health it gets shorter lives for the money, on schools higher scores. Israel's governments take more in tax, 29.8 per cent of GDP in 2023 against America's 27.7 per cent in 2022, and spend about two and a half times America's share of GDP on defence, 7.8 per cent against 3.1 per cent in 2025. American aid equals about 0.6 per cent of Israel's GDP, and the Congressional Research Service put it at about 14 per cent of Israel's defence budget. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:us16_wb_chepc] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_usa] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_isr] [cite:us11_oecd_rev_il] [cite:us11_dbn_rev_us] [cite:gi16_boi_2025] [cite:us19_sipri_fs2025] [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp]

Who works more? Israelis, by about 70 hours a year: 1,870 against 1,800 in 2025, down from 2,033 against 1,832 in 2000, so the gap has narrowed from about 200 hours. Israeli law guarantees 12 days of paid vacation and 15 weeks of paid leave after a birth; American federal law guarantees neither. Israelis also serve: most Jewish citizens are conscripted, and reservists averaged 233 days of service by mid-2026. [cite:us13_wiki_hours] [cite:us13_toi_vacation] [cite:us13_dol_vacation] [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] [cite:us19_taub_miluim]

Who is happier? Israelis, on the one survey that asks both populations the same question: 7.187 against 6.816 out of 10, eighth against twenty-third in the 2026 World Happiness Report. [cite:us22_whr26_hli]

Which state is more oppressive? Measured by how each treats its own citizens, neither clearly: the scores tie. The United States imprisons people at two and a half times Israel's rate and still executes them; Israel restricts religion by law, has had a state of emergency in force since 1948, censors its press through a military censor and ranks 116th of 180 for press freedom, against America's 64th. Measured by how each treats everyone it governs, Israel is the more coercive state on every row this volume could score: about a third of the people under its control cannot vote for its government and, in the West Bank, are tried in military courts, and it holds about 4,500 people without charge. The final assessment

For the people it treats as citizens, Israel's system delivers more of what households need than America's does, and the margin is wide: in coverage, life expectancy, infant survival, household debt, homelessness, family support and how people rate their lives, Israel scores higher, at about half America's health spending per head. The United States delivers higher pay, higher output per person, better schools on the 2025 results, and somewhat smaller gaps between groups on the measures this volume could compare. Taken together, on rules published in full and tested five ways, Israel's system scores about 6.2 of 10 and America's about 4.7.

That is a statement about systems, laws and governments, not peoples, and it has two limits a reader should keep in view. The first is coverage: Israel's citizens' figures leave out the 5.56 million Palestinians it governs without citizenship, and on liberty, the one area in which the people counted change the answer, counting them reverses the result. The second is cause. The scorecard measures what each system delivers; it does not show that American aid pays for any of Israel's advantages. The aid is military money, spent mostly in the United States, and weighs about 0.6 per cent of Israel's GDP; Israel's health law, wage floor and family benefits are paid for by Israeli taxes. [scene:us21_verdict|Part Twenty-One: the aid, the claims and what is open] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable]

What the record does and does not show. It shows each system's score on 31 to 33 shared measures, each from a named source, scored against published goalposts, and shows that the overall ranking survives every weighting tested. It does not show the result on measures neither country publishes comparably, among them police killings, firearm deaths, medical debt and house prices against income; one row, the American child-poverty rate on the OECD basis, awaits confirmation and is bounded above. Scores are as of October 2026, days before Israel votes on 27 October and the United States on 3 November, and several rows, from health coverage under America's 2025 budget law to Israel's wartime spending, are moving.
