The Jewish National Fund: Land Bought and Never Resold

A bank could lend money, but it could not by itself hold land for a nation. The idea of a fund that would do exactly that was older than the Jewish Colonial Trust. Hermann Schapira proposed a national land fund at the very first Zionist Congress, in Basel in 1897. He died in 1898 without seeing it established.[cite:jnf_wiki] For four years the proposal sat on the Congress agenda, debated and deferred, Congress after Congress.

It came to a head at the Fifth Zionist Congress in Basel in December 1901. With the delegates ready to disperse once more without a decision, Herzl stood before them and pushed the matter to a vote: "After striving for so many years to set up the fund, we do not want to disperse again without having done anything." The motion passed, and the Jewish National Fund was founded on 29 December 1901.[cite:jnf_wiki] Within a month it had its first director, Johann (Yona) Kremenetzky, who ran it from Vienna.[cite:jnf_wiki]

The delay might be read as resistance to the fund's purpose. The record does not show that. The sources describe repeated deferral, and the deferral appears to reflect ordinary institutional delay and competing priorities on a crowded Congress agenda, not organized opposition to a land fund as such. What the sequence does show is that the fund was not an automatic creation of the movement. It existed because its first proponent pressed for it and its leader forced a vote four years later.

The founding resolution was brief and absolute. As the sources give it: "The JNF shall be the eternal possession of the Jewish people. Its funds shall not be used except for the purchase of lands in Palestine and Syria."[cite:jnf_wiki] Two rules followed from it. The fund's money was restricted to a single purpose, the purchase of land, within a defined region. And whatever it bought became, in the resolution's language, the eternal possession of the Jewish people. The resolution as quoted applies that phrase to the fund itself; the sources' own summaries apply it to the land, and the practice that followed treated the land that way.

That practice was a deliberate structural choice, not an incidental one. Ordinary land changes hands again and again; a farmer buys it, his heirs sell it, a later buyer sells it on. The JNF built a rule to prevent that. From the start its land was removed from the market permanently. It was leased to settlers on long terms, not sold, so that no later transaction could reverse an acquisition once made.[cite:jnf_wiki] Land came into the fund and, by design, did not leave. That single rule is why the fund's totals, tracked across nearly half a century, only ever rose. The sources are careful on one point: the arrangement was not unique in kind. Permanent, inalienable land trusts exist in other places and other contexts. What is documented here is this particular, explicit rule, written into this fund's founding terms.

The money that paid for the land came, as with the Trust, from many small givers. The fund's best-known instrument was not designed at its headquarters. Haim Kleinman, a bank clerk in Nadvorna, in Galicia, put a blue box labeled "Keren Le'umit" in his own office and wrote to the movement's newspaper, Die Welt, proposing that the idea be taken up more widely. Kremenetzky adopted the suggestion, and the first mass-produced boxes were distributed in 1904.[cite:wiki_blue_box_jnf] By the interwar years roughly one million blue-and-white collection boxes sat in Jewish homes around the world.[cite:wiki_blue_box_jnf] The box made physical what the Trust's pound share had begun: a national project paid for in coins, household by household.

Kleinman himself was one of them. He visited Mandate Palestine in the 1930s and planned to settle there, but he perished in the Holocaust before he could do so.[cite:wiki_blue_box_jnf] The man whose idea paid for land across the diaspora never lived on it.

Who the box's donors were, and what each of them understood their coins to be for, is a different question. The mass adoption of the box is documented. The intentions of a million households are not, and the record cannot say whether every donor knew of, or would have endorsed, each later use to which the fund's money was put.

The fund's first land was not bought at all. In 1903 Isaac Leib Goldberg of Vilnius, a Russian Zionist leader, gave it 200 dunams east of Hadera, a parcel that later became an olive grove.[cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] From that gift the totals can be followed almost to the eve of statehood. By 1920 the fund held 22,363 dunams. By 1935 its holdings were about 89,500 acres, some 362 square kilometers, with 108 communities on them. By 1940 they reached 516,000 dunams, and by 1947–48 approximately 936,000 dunams.[cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] [cite:jnf_wiki]

The units in that series shift: the 1935 figure is reported in acres and square kilometers, the others in dunams. A dunam is about 1,000 square meters, so 362 square kilometers is roughly 362,000 dunams, which is how the library's land chart plots it. Read in a single unit, the series grows from 200 dunams to about 22,000, then to about 362,000, then 516,000, then about 936,000.

The chart makes the shape of the record plain. Growth was slow for two decades, then steep. The fund's first twenty years added about 22,000 dunams; the five years from 1935 to 1940 added well over 150,000. The record also places the fund within Jewish landholding as a whole. At the moment of statehood, total Jewish holdings in Palestine came to roughly 2 million dunams, about 7 percent of Mandate Palestine's roughly 26 million dunams, and the sources describe the JNF's share as more than half of that total.[cite:jnf_wiki] [cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] Taken at face value, the two rounded figures do not quite agree: 936,000 out of 2 million is a little under half. The fund's exact share depends on the precise total, which these sources give only as an approximation.

A common account holds that Zionist land buying before 1948 was sporadic and small. The record shows something different: a single institution, operating continuously for forty-seven years, under one rule, with its holdings rising in every period for which a figure exists. At the same time, the figure needs its context. Even at the end, all Jewish-owned land together amounted to about 7 percent of the country. The land fund was large within Jewish landholding and small within Palestine as a whole. Both facts are true, and a reader who hears only one of them will be misled.

These totals do not show that every dunam was acquired in the same way. The legal framework of the purchases, the sellers, and the tenants who lived on land that changed hands are treated in [scene:s04j|the land-purchase mechanism], which carries this section's numbers into the Ottoman and Mandate land system. The same JNF series appears again, with later figures beside it, in [scene:pm_s01|the ledger of the land's outcomes].

Several matters remain open. The record traces the fund's land but not, in these sources, its full annual income: how much the blue boxes collected in any given year is not documented here, and the library's funding ledger lists the JNF's income series among the totals still unavailable. The 1935 figure is given in a different unit from the rest, which makes the series harder to compare than it should be. And the precise share of Jewish-held land the fund owned in 1947–48 depends on a total that is reported only as roughly 2 million dunams.
