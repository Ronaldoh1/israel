The Exodus of 1941–1972 and Its Aftermath

Between 1948 and 1972, roughly 850,000 Jews left Arab and Muslim countries, from about two dozen states over some 24 years. The usual way of telling it is short: Jews were persecuted throughout Arab lands until Israel gave them refuge. That sentence contains real history, and it leaves out most of it. The documents show several causes working at once, each recorded on its own: violence and legal persecution by Arab governments, organized and named recruitment operations run by Israel and the Jewish Agency, and, in one specific and well-documented Egyptian case, sabotage carried out by Israeli military intelligence. They also show that arrival in Israel was not the end of the story. What happened to many of the newcomers in their first years is a second, separate story, in places a grave one, and Israel's own state commissions and independent researchers have investigated it without reaching full agreement.

This section follows that record in order: Iraq first, because its documentation is richest and its turning point came earliest; then the other airlifts and the Egyptian operation, briefly; then the transit camps, the disappearance of children from Yemenite and other Mizrahi families, and the mass irradiation of immigrant children for ringworm. The country-by-country figures, and the argument over the total, are set out in [scene:ch01_exodus_map|the next section].

Baghdad, June 1941. The turning point in Iraqi Jewish memory came seven years before Israel existed. Rashid Ali al-Gailani led a pro-Axis coup in Iraq. The British defeated it, and his government collapsed days before the violence began. Its fall left a vacuum in the capital and inflamed the nationalist and pro-Axis feeling it had stirred.[cite:wiki_farhud] On 1–2 June 1941, during the Jewish festival of Shavuot, crowds in Baghdad turned on the city's Jews. Iraqi soldiers and police who had backed the coup, together with the pro-Axis youth militias known as the Futuwwa, incited and led two days of killing, rape and looting. Civilian mobs and Bedouin from the city's outskirts joined them. British forces, victorious just outside the city, did not intervene.[cite:ushmm_farhud] [cite:wiki_farhud] The event is known as the Farhud. The vague word "riots" is sometimes still used for it. The record describes a targeted pogrom with members of the state's own security forces among the perpetrators.

The death toll is given differently in different sources, and the differences should be stated rather than smoothed over. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, the most authoritative single source on the event, gives 150 to 180 Jews killed, 600 injured, an undetermined number of women raped, and roughly 1,500 stores and homes looted.[cite:ushmm_farhud] An official commission of inquiry appointed by the Iraqi government recorded 128 killed by its own count, with independent estimates running as high as 180; the same account gives more than 1,000 injured and roughly 900 Jewish homes and more than 1,500 businesses looted or destroyed.[cite:ushmm_farhud] Some other sources put the dead at 500 or more.[cite:wiki_farhud] On any of these counts, the event marked the point after which Iraq's ancient Jewish community began its decline toward near-total emigration within a decade.[cite:ushmm_farhud]

The date matters for the argument as much as the toll. The Farhud took place in June 1941. Israel was declared in May 1948. Whatever role the pogrom later played in hastening departures, it cannot have been a reaction to the 1948 war or to the Nakba, the Palestinian displacement of that war. It does not show, on the other hand, that every later departure from Iraq was driven by the same forces. The departures of 1950 and 1951 had their own, additional causes.

Iraq at war, 1947–1949. After the United Nations voted for partition in November 1947, anti-Jewish measures in Iraq escalated. Pogroms gave way to systematic confiscation of property and to prosecutions on what one account calls trumped-up charges.[cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] On 15 May 1948, the day after Israel declared its independence, Iraq joined the Arab invasion. When the fighting ended, Iraq was the only Arab combatant that refused to sign an armistice with Israel, so that a formal state of war between the two has, technically, never ended.[cite:govil_aliyah] This is easy to lose inside the phrase "the 1948 war." For Iraq's Jews it meant that their state remained formally at war with the state that most of them would soon enter.

On 19 July 1948, Iraq made "Zionism" a crime punishable by up to seven years in prison. Because every Jew was presumed to be a Zionist, the law worked as a blanket criminalization of the whole community. The testimony of two Muslim witnesses was enough to convict, and there was little avenue for appeal.[cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] On 23 September 1948, Shafiq Ades, a prominent and well-connected Jewish businessman, was publicly hanged in Basra on charges of selling weapons to Israel and to the Iraqi Communist Party. His body was left hanging for hours as a public spectacle. The case is widely cited as the one that broke what remained of the community's sense of security: if a man of his standing was not safe, no one was.[cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] [cite:govil_aliyah]

The law of March 1950 and the airlift. On 2 March 1950, Iraq's parliament passed a law permitting Jews to leave on condition that they surrender their Iraqi citizenship.[cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] It was framed as permission. It worked, once people registered, as a mechanism for stripping them of nationality and then of property: a subsequent law required departing Jews to sell their property and liquidate their businesses before leaving, and the state denaturalized those who registered and stripped them of their assets.[cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] Economic loss was written into the exit itself.

Israel and the Jewish Agency organized the airlift that followed, Operation Ezra and Nehemiah. It was named for the biblical leaders of the return from the Babylonian exile, a deliberate echo of the community's own origins.[cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] Shlomo Hillel, an Iraqi-born agent of Mossad LeAliyah Bet, negotiated personally with the Iraqi authorities and helped organize the operation; he later described it from the inside in a memoir, Operation Babylon.[cite:wiki_shlomo_hillel] The aircraft were provided by the Near East Transport Company and El Al. One account gives the route as through Cyprus and Iran; another gives it as through Cyprus at first and later by direct flights from Baghdad to Lod.[cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] The cost was largely met by the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, at about $4 million.[cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] [cite:govil_aliyah]

The pace overwhelmed the planning. Israeli immigration authorities had prepared to receive about 300 people a day; at the peak, 1,400 arrived daily. By the end of 1951 more than 107,000 Iraqi Jews had been airlifted, and about 16,000 more had left by other routes.[cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] By early 1952, a community of some 2,800 years' standing had been reduced to about 6,000 people. Israeli government sources put its size before the airlift at about 135,000; another account gives roughly 130,000.[cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah] Summary accounts give the number airlifted as about 120,000 to 130,000.[cite:encyclopedia_ezra_nehemiah]

These facts establish a real, organized, named Israeli operation and a real Iraqi legal mechanism that made departure conditional on giving up citizenship and property. They do not, by themselves, say why so many Iraqi Jews chose to go. The Farhud, the criminal law of 1948, the hanging in Basra, the conditions of the 1950 law and the airlift's own active recruitment are all documented as contributing causes. None of them is the single cause.

The Baghdad bombings, 1950–1951. One part of this story is disputed, and the dispute is real. Between April 1950 and June 1951, at least five bombing incidents struck Jewish institutions and gathering places in Baghdad, among them the Masuda Shemtov synagogue. At least four Jews were killed and others injured.[cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] The attacks fell in exactly the months when the March 1950 law was driving mass registration for emigration. Iraq tried men for the bombings, and two of them, Basri and Shalom, were hanged in January 1952.[cite:meforum_exodus]

Two accounts of who was responsible have stood against each other ever since. The Iraqi government of the time, and later some historians, attributed the bombings to Zionist underground agents seeking to panic the community into leaving faster. Israeli officials have consistently and firmly denied any involvement, and have called the accusation an antisemitic fabrication meant to shift blame for a persecution the Iraqi state had itself created through years of pogroms, denaturalization and criminalization.[cite:wiki_ezra_nehemiah] A review of newly released Israeli, British and American archives, published by the Middle East Forum, found insufficient evidence to confirm Israeli involvement, while not closing the question entirely. The executions do not settle it either: the fairness and evidentiary basis of the trials, held amid intense anti-Zionist feeling, are themselves disputed.[cite:meforum_exodus]

What the record shows is real bombings, real deaths, and a real Iraqi prosecution that ended in executions. What it does not show is proof of who was responsible. Both the contemporary Iraqi accusation and the Israeli denial are documented positions. The evidence now available does not decide between them, and this book does not pretend that it does in either direction.

Yemen, Morocco and Egypt. Iraq's was one of three major named operations, each in a different country on a different timetable. Operation Magic Carpet airlifted approximately 48,000 to 49,000 Yemenite Jews to Israel in 1949–50. Operation Yachin, conducted in secret from 1961 to 1964, brought Moroccan Jews, later than the other two because Morocco's political timeline was different. Both, like Ezra and Nehemiah, were organized operations of the Israeli state and the Jewish Agency, not spontaneous flight, though the hardships in the countries of origin were also real and documented.[cite:wiki_idf_operations_list] Conditions and causes were not the same in all three countries, and they are not treated here as one undifferentiated movement; Yemen's and Morocco's own circumstances, including the payments made for Moroccan departures, are set out country by country in the next section.

Egypt supplies the one documented case of sabotage, and it touches this story because of whom it used. In July 1954, Israeli military intelligence, known as Aman, activated Unit 131, a cell of Egyptian Jews it had recruited and trained for the purpose. The cell placed incendiary devices at a post office, at American information-agency libraries in Cairo and Alexandria, and at British-owned cinemas. The intention was that the attacks would be blamed on Egyptian nationalists or communists and would persuade Britain to keep its troops in the Suez Canal Zone.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] Another summary of the same operation gives the intended scapegoats as Egyptian nationalists or the Muslim Brotherhood, and the aim as damaging Western confidence in Nasser's government.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The plan unraveled when a device ignited prematurely in an operative's pocket in Alexandria, and the cell was arrested.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

The people who paid were the local operatives, not the planners. At the Egyptian trial Moshe Marzouk and Shmuel Azar were sentenced to death and executed in January 1955. Max Bennett, an Israeli officer, took his own life in prison. Others received long sentences. Israel did not acknowledge the operatives for decades, and publicly honored the survivors in 2005.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The operation, later called Operation Susannah, is the earliest documented case of the Israeli state attacking a close ally's facilities under a false attribution.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] That it took place is not in dispute. Who ordered it is. Defense Minister Pinhas Lavon denied giving the order; the head of military intelligence, Binyamin Gibli, claimed that he had. Lavon resigned in 1955; one account describes him as forced out. The question was reopened in 1960 amid evidence that documents had been forged, split the ruling Mapai party, and contributed to Ben-Gurion's resignation in 1963. Who gave the order was never formally resolved.[cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The full affair is told in [scene:lv_s01|the Lavon Affair]. The record documents this pattern for Egypt only; it does not establish it for any other country in this account.

Two readings. The rescue reading holds that these were communities in danger, and that Israel brought them out. Its evidence is strong and specific: a pogrom in 1941 with soldiers and police among the killers, a 1948 law that made every Jew in Iraq a presumed criminal, a public hanging meant to be seen, an exit law that took citizenship and property together. The recruitment-and-sabotage reading holds that the Israeli state did not only receive these communities but actively worked to bring them, and in one place used local Jews in an operation that endangered them. Its evidence is also specific: named operations, a named agent negotiating with the Iraqi government, organized airlifts at a pace the reception system could not absorb, and the documented operation in Egypt. The disputed Baghdad bombings sit between the two readings, and the record cannot assign them to either. Neither reading cancels the other. Persecution, recruitment and, in one documented case, sabotage all operated, in proportions that differed from country to country.

Arrival: the ma'abarot. Being airlifted out of danger and being welcomed into equal opportunity were two different things. New arrivals, disproportionately Mizrahi, were housed in crowded transit camps called ma'abarot, at first in tents and later in basic shacks. These were spartan, temporary conditions, not immediate settlement in established communities or housing.[cite:honestreporting_forgotten_refugees] Ashkenazi immigrants arriving in the same period tended to be better educated on arrival and more likely to assimilate into the existing, largely Ashkenazi-run secular establishment. They also qualified for German war-reparations payments, which Mizrahi immigrants, who had never lived under Nazi occupation, could not receive.[cite:honestreporting_forgotten_refugees] The difference in starting conditions was structural, tied to reparations eligibility and to who already ran the institutions, not to prejudice alone. Individual lives varied widely. The pattern itself is documented.

The Yemenite Children Affair. Between 1948 and 1954, hundreds of infants and small children of Yemenite and other Mizrahi immigrant families disappeared from Israeli state hospitals and transit camps. Families were frequently told that their children had died, often without receiving a body, a death certificate or any information about burial.[cite:nytimes_disappeared_children] That basic pattern, of disappearance and inadequate explanation, is not in dispute.

What lay behind it is. A state inquiry, the Cohen-Kedmi Commission, investigated for nearly seven years, from 1995 to 2001. Its official finding was unequivocal: claims that the children had been deliberately kidnapped in a government-organized plot were not substantiated. It also acknowledged that the fates of 42 children remained unaccounted for even after its work.[cite:wiki_kedmi_commission] Against that finding stands serious, evidence-based dissent, not a fringe theory. The researcher Shoshana Madmoni-Gerber and hundreds of collected family testimonies support the argument that the disappearances reflect a real, coordinated pattern rather than isolated administrative failures.[cite:nytimes_disappeared_children] The activist Uzi Meshulam campaigned prominently for a fuller inquiry and was imprisoned in connection with his campaign, which is itself a disputed chapter in how the authorities handled the issue.[cite:wiki_uzi_meshulam]

Both positions are documented, and the people who have studied the primary evidence most closely still disagree. This book does not hold the commission's finding to be definitively right, nor the dissenting researchers and families. What is not in dispute is that children disappeared, that their families were given inadequate answers, and that what happened, and why, remains contested. The state's later decisions on the affair belong to a later period; they are taken up in [scene:mz_s02|a later chapter].

The Ringworm Affair. A separate scandal touched the same communities, and the two should not be confused. Through the 1950s, Israeli medical authorities treated scalp ringworm, a common condition at the time, by irradiating immigrant children with X-rays. The children treated were disproportionately Mizrahi and North African. One frequently cited figure puts the total in this campaign at roughly 27,000 children; estimates of the affected population run from about 20,000 to more than 100,000, the higher figures including comparable campaigns elsewhere.[cite:wiki_ringworm_affair]

Part of the motive was practical and is documented. Ringworm was grounds for exclusion under the immigration rules of the United States and other countries, which put direct institutional pressure on the authorities to treat and clear affected children so that their resettlement could proceed.[cite:wiki_ringworm_affair] Irradiation was a recognized treatment for ringworm elsewhere in the world in the same period, including among children who were not Mizrahi, and the record does not show that each individual decision was taken in bad faith. What it does show is the scale, the disproportionate effect on Mizrahi and North African children, and the immigration-driven pressure behind it. A 2003 Israeli documentary, The Ringworm Children, presented the episode as a serious, state-linked medical scandal, and used language as strong as "holocaust" to describe it. That choice of word is itself a measure of how gravely the episode is remembered in the communities it affected.[cite:wiki_ringworm_affair]

What left, and what remained. The headline figure of 850,000 is large and real. It is not the same claim as the end of Jewish life in the Middle East. It was a minority of the total Jewish population that had lived across the Muslim-majority world for more than a thousand years, in cities where Jewish communities were sometimes a third of the population or more. The presence thinned, in some cities dramatically: one account describes Baghdad's community of 140,000 as functionally gone today. But it did not vanish everywhere, and it did not go all at once or for one reason. The flattened version, "they all left because they were persecuted," survives because it is a simpler sentence than "a partial departure with several causes took place over 24 years, with real differences from country to country." It undersells how long the shared life lasted, and it oversells how complete the departure was.

The record leaves three questions open. It does not establish who planted the bombs in Baghdad in 1950 and 1951. It does not establish who gave the order for the Egyptian operation of 1954. And it does not establish what became of the children of Yemenite and other Mizrahi families whose fates even the state's own commission could not account for. Nor can it weigh, family by family, how much each departure owed to fear and how much to recruitment. The exact total of those who left, and whether they should be called refugees, are themselves argued over, as the next section shows.
