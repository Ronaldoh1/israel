Israel and the Coup in Iran: Participant or Beneficiary, 1947–1953

Two claims about Iran circulate together and are often answered together. The first holds that Iran has been a theocracy hostile to the West since time immemorial and never had a democratic government. The second holds that the coup of 1953 had nothing to do with Israel, or, in its mirror image, that Israel had a hand in it. The record answers the first claim directly and the second with a distinction. Before 1979 Iran was a constitutional monarchy with an elected parliament, the Majlis. In 1951 the Majlis made Mohammad Mossadeq prime minister and voted to nationalize the British-controlled Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. [cite:explaininghistory_1953_coup] [cite:kathryn_palmer_1953_coup] In August 1953 an operation run by the CIA and Britain's MI6 removed him and restored the Shah's power, a role the CIA formally acknowledged in documents released in 2013. [cite:nsarchive_cia_confirms_role] [cite:npr_declassified_1953_coup] The theocracy came in 1979, twenty-six years after the elected government was removed. On Israel, the finding of this section is plain: no documented Israeli role in the coup exists. Whether Israel benefited from it, and from when, is a separate question with a separate answer.

The oil crisis and the coup themselves are told elsewhere in this stage, the coup in [scene:s11|the account of August 1953]. This section follows a thread those accounts leave aside: what Iran's governments did about Israel from 1947 to 1953, and where the coup falls in that sequence.

Iran began on the other side. In 1947 it sat on the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine as one of three members that opposed partition and favoured a federal solution. On 29 November 1947 it voted against partition in the General Assembly. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] In the war of 1948 it showed tacit solidarity with the Arab states but avoided combat and resisted Arab demands for direct military support. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] The pattern of those first two years was a government that voted with the Arab states at the United Nations while keeping its own forces out of the fighting.

The first practical contact concerned Iran's own people. In March 1949 Iran sent an unofficial envoy, Abbas Sayqal, to handle the claims of Iranian residents of Palestine. In the same year it refused Israel's request to station a non-resident minister, based in Ankara, in Tehran. And on 11 May 1949 it voted against Israel's admission to the United Nations. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] Iran was willing to deal with the new state over property and residents, but not to receive its diplomats or to vote it into the world body.

Then, in March 1950, it recognized Israel, in the most limited form available. On 6 March 1950, while the Majlis was in recess, the government recognized Israel de facto, without a formal announcement. The Foreign Minister, Entezam, informed Abba Eban the next day, 7 March, and on 26 March 1950 Reza Safinia presented credentials as a "special envoy". [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] One reading of the timing and the form is that recognition while parliament was not sitting avoided a Majlis debate, and recognition without announcement a public one; and de facto recognition stopped short of official, de jure recognition. According to Eitay Mack, Iran avoided official recognition because of domestic and Arab pressure. [cite:rs_cw1_972_mack]

The next step under Mossadeq went the other way. On 7 July 1951, shortly after he took office, his government closed Iran's consulate in Jerusalem, citing "financial difficulties". It did not revoke the de facto recognition. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] [cite:mossadegh_org_israel_relations] The stated reason was financial, and the sources here give no other. Whether it was the whole reason, or a formula that let a nationalist government distance itself from Israel without breaking the recognition its predecessor had granted, the record cited here does not say. What it does show is the limit of the step: a consulate closed, a recognition left standing.

The first economic ties of 1953 complicate the simplest version of the "beneficiary" story. On 11 June 1953, before the coup and while Mossadeq was still prime minister, the national banks of the two countries signed an agreement opening a line of credit. In 1953 the Iranian–Israeli trading company IRIS was founded. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] The account this section draws on places IRIS after Mossadeq's fall, as the first sign of a reversal. [cite:iranica_israel_iran] [cite:mossadegh_org_israel_relations] The encyclopaedia entry dates it only to 1953, and it dates the credit line explicitly before the coup. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] On these sources, then, the first financial link between the two states was made under the government the coup removed, not under the one it installed.

Against that record the question of participation can be answered. No documented Israeli role in the coup exists. [cite:iranica_israel_iran] [cite:mossadegh_org_israel_relations] Israel was five years old in 1953; the operation was planned, paid for and run by the American and British services, as told in the account of the coup itself. Nothing in the sources gathered for this stage places an Israeli official, agency or payment in the planning or the events of August 1953. The claim that Israel took part is therefore not supported by these sources; it should not be confused with the separate, documented question of what Israel gained afterwards.

On that second question the evidence is real but later, and it needs dating. According to the encyclopaedia entry by David Menashri, Israel began buying Iranian oil in 1957, through foreign-flagged ships. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] Menashri dates the first intelligence contact to September 1957, when General Teymur Bakhtiar, head of the newly established SAVAK, met Israel's ambassador Yaacov Zur in Paris and offered an exchange of intelligence, and he dates Ben-Gurion's periphery concept to the late 1950s. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] Eitay Mack writes that Israel's Foreign Ministry files on relations with Iran, covering 1953 to 1979, have been declassified [cite:rs_cw1_972_mack], and the account behind this section reads those files as showing that Israel bought a significant portion, and in some years all, of its oil from the Shah's Iran. [cite:mag972_israel_shah]

The same account, and the account of the coup in this stage, go further: they state that the Mossad helped found and train the Shah's secret police, SAVAK, from 1957, and that the "Trident" intelligence pact began in 1958. [cite:globalsecurity_savak_cia_training] [cite:mag972_israel_shah] The sources assembled for this chapter do not establish that claim for 1957. What they document for that year is narrower: an offer to exchange intelligence, made by SAVAK's head to an Israeli ambassador in Paris in September. [cite:rs_cw1_iranica_israel] The fuller question of SAVAK, the periphery and the Trident belongs to the late 1950s and is examined, with its own sources, in [scene:cw4_iran|Stage Four's account of Iran and Israel].

The synthesis this section's source offers is that the coup served Washington and London directly and Israel indirectly: not a participant, but a beneficiary. On its account, the sequence ran from an elected government in 1951, through its removal by two foreign intelligence services in 1953, to a quarter century of the Shah's rule as an American ally and as Israel's main oil supplier and regional partner, and then to revolution in 1979. [cite:mag972_israel_shah] That is a judgment made with hindsight across twenty-six years, and its parts carry different weight. That the coup served American and British interests is the burden of the coup's own documented history. That Israel later gained from the Shah's Iran in oil and in partnership is supported by the dated evidence from 1957. That Israel's gains were a result of the coup, rather than of the Shah's own later choices and Israel's periphery policy, is an inference the sources here do not test. The record of 1950–53, with recognition granted before Mossadeq, a consulate closed under him, and a credit line signed in his last months in office, does not show a straight line from his fall to Israel's gain.

The record leaves several questions open. Why Mossadeq's government closed the consulate beyond the "financial difficulties" it cited is not documented here. Whether IRIS was founded before or after August 1953 is not given. How much of Iran's later oil trade with Israel is owed to the coup, as against the decisions of the late 1950s, cannot be settled from these sources, and the Mossad's role in SAVAK's founding is not established by them for 1957. Iran's own Jewish community, whose history runs beside this one, is the subject of [scene:ira_src_s16|a separate section on Iran's Jews].
