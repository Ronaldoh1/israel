"Antisemitism": A Word Named by the Haters

It is often said that "anti-Semitism" is a precise term: that the Semites are a people, and that the word names hatred of them. The word's own paper trail tells a more complicated story. A linguist named a family of languages in 1781. Race science turned that family into a "race" in the 1850s. A Jewish scholar first wrote "antisemitic" in 1860, to attack that race theory. Then, in 1879, a German agitator who was not Jewish made "antisemitism" the name of his own movement against Jews. The name is a misnomer. Its meaning is not.

The first step was a classification of grammar. In 1781 the Göttingen historian August Ludwig von Schlözer coined "Semitic" (semitisch) for a family of related languages, borrowing the name of Shem, son of Noah, from Genesis 10. That family today includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Amharic, Tigrinya and Maltese. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] It says nothing about blood, race or religion, and it has never belonged to one people; the family and its speakers are described in [scene:s00b|the root word].

The second step turned the language label into a ranking of human beings. The French scholar Ernest Renan's comparative history of the Semitic languages, published in 1855, treated their speakers, Hebrews and Arabs alike, as a "Semitic race" inferior to the "Aryans." [cite:wiki_renan] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] This was the race science of the era: a term invented to group grammars was converted into a judgment about peoples. Historians now regard "Semitic race" as a discredited category, which is why the word still causes confusion. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym]

The adjective came next, and it came from a Jewish scholar. In 1860 the bibliographer Moritz Steinschneider wrote of "antisemitic prejudices" (antisemitische Vorurteile) to rebut Renan's claim that Semitic peoples were inferior. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] In that first use the target of the prejudice was the whole invented "Semitic race," Hebrews and Arabs alike. Within twenty years, men who hated Jews in Germany would take the word over and narrow it to one people.

The man most associated with that narrowing was Wilhelm Marr (1819–1904), a German journalist and political agitator. He was a Lutheran, not a Jew as is sometimes claimed. [cite:jvl_marr] [cite:wiki_marr_1879] In the 1840s he had been involved in radical left politics; later in life he turned to German nationalist and anti-Jewish politics. In 1879 he published a pamphlet, Der Sieg des Judenthums über das Germanenthum ("The Victory of Judaism over Germandom"), which argued that Jews held a disproportionate and threatening influence over German cultural and economic life. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] The same year he founded the League of Antisemites (Antisemiten-Liga), the first German organization devoted to the alleged Jewish threat and one of the first to use the term as a self-description. It called for forcing Jews out of Germany. His "Antisemitic Pamphlets" of 1881 spread the word further. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] [cite:jvl_marr]

The choice of word was deliberate, and its purpose is documented. "Antisemitism" was a euphemism for Judenhass, Jew-hatred. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] Historians of the period, including Moshe Zimmermann in his biography Wilhelm Marr: The Patriarch of Anti-Semitism (1986), show that Marr borrowed the scientific-sounding "Semitic" from contemporary linguistics to give an old religious and cultural hatred a modern, secular, pseudo-scientific surface, at a time when openly religious Jew-hatred was becoming less acceptable in educated German circles. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] [cite:jvl_marr] The logic of the borrowed term did real work. If Jews were a foreign "Semitic race," then neither conversion nor centuries of life in Germany could make them German. [cite:jvl_marr] [cite:wiki_marr_1879] The word was coined by the people doing the hating, about the people being hated.

Nothing in Marr's own writing, or in the founding activity of his League, shows any concern with Arabic speakers, Ethiopian Christians, Assyrian communities or any other Semitic-language population. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] From its first day as the name of a movement, the word referred to one specific people, dressed in the borrowed language of a much broader linguistic category, and it kept that narrow sense in actual use through the century that followed. Zimmermann also reports a turn at the end of Marr's life: Marr renounced antisemitism in a text he never published. [cite:jvl_marr]

Jews had their own name for the hatred, and it said plainly who the target was. In 1882, after pogroms in the Russian Empire, the Odessa physician Leon Pinsker published Auto-Emancipation. He called the hatred "Judeophobia": an irrational fear of Jews, which he described as an inherited disease. [cite:jvl_pinsker] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] Pinsker concluded that Jews needed a homeland of their own, which makes the pamphlet one of the founding texts of Zionism. [cite:jvl_pinsker] The pogroms, the blood libel and the older history of the hatred that Marr renamed are set out in [scene:ch01_hatred|an old hatred, before and after its name].

The last step in the word's history came from the institutions most concerned with it. In April 2015 the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance recommended writing "antisemitism" without a hyphen. Its reason was that the hyphen "allows for the possibility of something called Semitism," lending weight to a discredited racial classification. [cite:ihra_spelling_2015] Style guides followed, and The New York Times dropped the hyphen in 2021. [cite:wjc_nyt_spelling] The spelling change is a small thing with a clear meaning: the bodies most invested in the word agree that its root is wrong.

What, then, does the word mean now? Two things are true together. The root is a misnomer: "Semitic" describes languages, Palestinians who speak Arabic are as Semitic as anyone, and many Jews have never spoken a Semitic language at home. And the meaning is settled by use: since the 1880s "antisemitism" has meant hostility to Jews, and nothing broader. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] [cite:ihra_spelling_2015] That cuts in both directions. The claim "Arabs are Semites too, so I cannot be antisemitic" does not hold, because words mean what they are used to mean, not what their parts suggest. Stretching the word to cover political criticism of a state does not hold automatically either; that dispute, and the competing definitions behind it, are documented separately in this book. The test is the target and the content of what is said, not the label.

The etymology settles what the word originally meant and how its meaning was fixed. It does not settle how any particular modern claim should be judged. Knowing that Marr chose the word to dignify Jew-hatred does not make criticism of Israeli state policy automatically legitimate, and it does not make such criticism automatically illegitimate. Those are separate questions. The record of the word answers only the first.
