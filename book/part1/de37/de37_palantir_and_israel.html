Palantir and Israel: What the Company Said, What Others Allege, What Is Shown

Of all the ties this book maps, the one between Palantir and Israel draws the most heat and the loosest claims. The library has already set out its core. [scene:ow9_israel|The One-Way Mirror] gives the January 2024 partnership with Israel's Ministry of Defense, the Israeli business press's account of what the company does and does not do, Palantir's written denials to a United Nations rapporteur, the separate case of Microsoft's cloud and Israel's signals-intelligence unit, and the Norwegian asset manager that sold its shares. [scene:ow12_ties|The documented business] gives the Epstein, Barak and Valar records. This section does not repeat them. It adds what has entered the record since, in the company's own words and in its critics', and it keeps three things apart throughout: what Palantir has said, what others allege, and what a document shows.

The partnership, in the company's words

The partnership was announced by the company, not uncovered. On 12 January 2024 Josh Harris, an executive vice president, told Bloomberg that after a meeting in Tel Aviv the day before, attended by Israeli defence officials, Peter Thiel and Alex Karp, "both parties have mutually agreed to harness Palantir's advanced technology in support of war-related missions." He said the partnership "aims to significantly aid the Israeli Ministry of Defense in addressing the current situation." No terms, value or product were disclosed, then or since. [cite:de37_bbg_partner] Palantir had already declared its position in public. In October 2023 it ran a full-page advertisement in The New York Times stating that it "stands with Israel," and on its February 2024 earnings call Karp said he was "exceedingly proud that after Oct. 7, within weeks, we are on the ground." [cite:de37_cnbc_karp]

The company has also given its reason in writing, and the reason is political, not religious or ethnic. In a letter of 21 April 2025 its director of privacy and civil liberties, Courtney Bowman, wrote: "Our work in Israel predates the terror attack, and is in line with our global support of US allies and liberal democracies." [cite:de37_bhrcc_pal] A month later, writing to the UN Special Rapporteur, the company said its work for the Ministry of Defense "only began in the wake of the attacks of 7 October 2023," its earlier Israeli customers having been civilian. [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] The two statements fit together: a civilian presence before the war, a defence contract after it. Karp's own account of the money is that Israel "is not a commercially relevant part of our business." [cite:ow9_fortune_karp] Israel is one of several governments the company serves in war. Karp has said Palantir is "responsible for most of the targeting in Ukraine," where it works without charge, and its contracts with Britain, NATO and the Pentagon are set out in [scene:de27_money_and_war|Part Twenty-Nine]. [cite:de27_time_ukraine]

What it is said to do, and what it denies

The heaviest allegation came from the United Nations. A report by the Special Rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territory, Francesca Albanese, presented to the Human Rights Council in July 2025 under the symbol A/HRC/59/23, said there were "reasonable grounds to believe Palantir has provided automatic predictive policing technology," along with "core defence infrastructure" and its AI Platform, "which allows real-time battlefield data integration for automated decision making." [cite:de37_mee_unreport] "Reasonable grounds to believe" is a rapporteur's standard of inquiry, not a court's finding; the report named many companies, and its specific basis for the Palantir paragraph is not given in the reporting on it.

Palantir's answers are on the record and specific. It denied supporting "predictive policing workflows in Israel." [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] On the two targeting systems most often attached to it, it wrote that claims of its involvement "are not true," that both are "independent of and pre-date" its partnership with the ministry, and that it has "no involvement with the 'Lavender' database used by the IDF." [cite:de37_bhrcc_pal] Lavender was first described in April 2024 by +972 Magazine and Local Call as an Israeli army system that marked tens of thousands of Palestinians as suspected militants, with brief human review; the army called such systems "auxiliary tools." [cite:hm_972_lavender] Nothing in that reporting names Palantir as its maker, and the only detailed Israeli account of Palantir's work, in the business daily Globes, said the army's signals-intelligence unit and the Shin Bet had examined Palantir's system and declined it, as the library sets out. [cite:ow9_globes_palantir]

One claim from a different direction is harder to dismiss and impossible, on the record, to confirm. Michael Steinberger's biography of Karp, The Philosopher in the Valley, published in late 2025, says the company's technology "was deployed by the Israelis during military operations in Lebanon in 2024" and "was also used in Operation Grim Beeper," the explosion of thousands of pagers carried by Hezbollah members on 17 September 2024, and that a team of engineers was sent from London. The book, as reported, does not say how the software was used or on what evidence the statement rests, and no response from Palantir was reported. [cite:de37_mee_steinberger] It is a single source, written by an author with access to the company, about an operation for which no official account exists. The book grades it as reported, with its content unexplained: it shows a claim of use in a war, not a mechanism.

Protest, divestment and the state's reply

Karp has met protest in person and answered it in political terms. At the Hill and Valley Forum in Washington in late April 2025, a protester told him that Palantir's technology was killing her family in Palestine. He answered, "Mostly terrorists that's true," and after she was escorted out said: "She believes I'm evil, and I believe she's an unwitting product of an evil force, Hamas." [cite:de37_natl_karp] The exchange records a chief executive's view of the war; it is not evidence of what his software does there, in either direction.

Two investor and customer stories are often told about Israel, and only one of them is about Israel. The divestment widely described as "Norway's" was by Storebrand, a private Norwegian asset manager, which sold a holding of about $24 million in October 2024 and named Palantir's sales to Israel as its reason; it was not Norway's state oil fund. [cite:ow9_globes_storebrand] The Australian supermarket chain Coles agreed in September 2026 to end its partnership with Palantir after a year-long campaign, and an Australian Palestine-solidarity group now lists the decision among the results of its campaign over Palantir's Israeli work. [cite:de37_nrfp_release] The campaign that claimed it, run by the group GetUp!, objected on grounds of workplace surveillance, and the trade reporting of the decision does not mention Israel; Coles said the software ran in its own environment and Palantir "does not control the data." [cite:de37_itnews_coles]

The rapporteur's report also drew a reply from the United States government. On 9 July 2025 Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced sanctions on Albanese under an executive order aimed at the International Criminal Court. His stated grounds included her engagement with the court and her "threatening letters to dozens of entities worldwide," among them "major American companies across finance, technology, defense, energy, and hospitality," and her recommendation that the court investigate those companies; the statement also accused her of antisemitism. [cite:de37_state_albanese] Those are the government's stated reasons, not findings. What the sanction meant for the person sanctioned, and how it bears on the use of financial tools against critics, is examined in [scene:de34_the_repurposing_test|Part Thirty-Six] and [scene:de35_the_consequence_engine|Part Thirty-Seven].

The network's other documented ties to Israel

Beyond Palantir the documented ties are few, and each is of a named kind. Thiel's Founders Fund invested in Carbyne, an Israeli-founded emergency-call company, in 2018; the reporting does not connect that investment to Ehud Barak's partnership or to Epstein's money in it, and the library sets out the full chain. [cite:ow10_ctech_barak] [cite:ow10_wiki_carbyne] Epstein's files show him trying on at least six occasions to bring Thiel together with Barak, and Thiel answering one proposal to meet in Saudi Arabia that he would "avoid the Middle East for the next decade or so." [cite:de14_jacobin] Valar, Thiel's fund, declined the one Barak-linked company Epstein pitched to it. [cite:ow12_wiki_epstein] Elon Musk visited Israel on 27 November 2023, toured Kfar Aza, a kibbutz attacked on 7 October, with Prime Minister Netanyahu, and reached an understanding with Israel's communications minister that Starlink terminals could operate in Israel and Gaza only "with the approval of the Israeli Ministry of Communications." [cite:de37_cbs_musk] That tie runs from a foreign government to an American company, as a licensing condition that government set. Nick Land's 2012 serial does not mention Israel at all. [cite:de4_land_de]

Put together, the record shows a customer relationship with a foreign defence ministry whose contents are not public, an American chief executive who supports that government's war in plain words and says why, a United Nations allegation that the company denies in part, one book's unexplained claim of use in Lebanon, and investors and customers who have left for reasons they stated. It is the same kind of record the book has kept for Palantir's work with the Pentagon, ICE and the British health service: contracts and statements, with the use itself largely out of view. What circulates beyond that record, and fails, is the subject of [scene:de37_what_is_not_supported|the next section].
