Immigration, 1923–1936: The Fourth and Fifth Aliyot

Between 1923 and 1936 the number of Jews entering Palestine each year rose and fell steeply. In 1925 more than 33,000 arrived; in 1928 barely 2,000. In 1935 more than 60,000 came, the highest figure of these years. The swings were not random. They followed pressures and decisions outside Palestine, in Poland and in Washington among them, and they passed through a British administration that counted the arrivals and set an allotment for those who came as workers. This section follows the two great waves of these years, the Fourth Aliyah of the mid-1920s and the Fifth Aliyah of the 1930s, and sets out what the official figures show, and where they disagree.

The Fourth Aliyah, 1924–1929. The Jewish Virtual Library, summarizing the standard account, puts the Fourth Aliyah at 82,000 Jews who came to Palestine between 1924 and 1929, of whom 23,000 left again [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah]. It attributes the wave to two pressures that operated together. One was in Poland: an economic crisis and anti-Jewish policies there pushed Jews to leave [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah]. The other was in the United States. The Jewish Virtual Library points to stiff American immigration quotas, and the Center for Israel Education ties the rise in immigration to Palestine directly to the US Immigration Act of 1924, which limited annual immigration from each country to 2 percent of its residents in the 1890 census [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah] [cite:rs_rd3_cie_1924_act].

Most of the Fourth Aliyah's immigrants were middle class, with modest capital [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah]. The town whose growth tracks the wave most visibly is Tel Aviv. It had about 15,000 inhabitants in 1922 and about 34,000 in 1925; by 1931 it had 45,564 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_telaviv].

The wave broke quickly. The Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine, suffered an economic crisis in 1926–1928 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah]. The annual figures show its scale. Recorded Jewish immigration, which stood at 33,801 in 1925, fell to 13,081 in 1926, 2,713 in 1927 and 2,178 in 1928 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]. Many of those who had arrived left: the 23,000 departures are part of the Fourth Aliyah's own total [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah].

The British inquiry that followed the violence of August 1929 drew a lesson from those years. The Shaw Commission, reporting in March 1930, criticized what it called "excessive" immigration in 1925–26, and quoted Sir John Campbell's view that the crisis of 1927–28 had come from immigrants arriving "in excess of the economic absorbing power of the country" [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest]. The phrase mattered. It tied the permitted level of immigration to an economic test, the country's capacity to absorb newcomers, and that test ran through the British arguments over immigration that followed. The argument that followed Shaw, through Hope Simpson and the White Paper of 1930, is told in [scene:pf_s01|From inquiry to reversal].

The Fifth Aliyah. The low point did not last long. Recorded immigration was 5,249 in 1929, 4,944 in 1930 and 4,075 in 1931, and then it climbed: 9,553 in 1932, 30,327 in 1933, 42,359 in 1934 and 61,854 in 1935, before falling back to 29,727 in 1936 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]. The Jewish Virtual Library dates the Fifth Aliyah from 1929 to 1939. It states that nearly 250,000 Jews arrived in that wave by 1940, of whom 20,000 later left; that 174,000 settled in the four years 1933–1936 alone; and that the Yishuv reached 450,000 by 1940 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fifth_aliyah].

The steepest climb began in 1933. The emigration of Jews from Nazi Germany in these years, and the Transfer Agreement that let some of them bring capital, are told with their own numbers in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement]. The sources used here do not break down the annual totals by country of origin, so they cannot say what share of the 1933–1936 arrivals came from Germany and what share from Poland and elsewhere.

Tel Aviv again shows the effect most plainly. A town of 45,564 in 1931 had about 120,000 people by 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_telaviv]. It received municipal status on 12 May 1934 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_telaviv].

How the gate worked. Not every immigrant entered on the same terms, and the 1935 peak shows how the categories fitted together. A State Department memorandum of 6 November 1936, written by Paul Alling for Assistant Secretary Moore, cites Palestine Government statistics for that year. Of the 61,854 Jews who entered in 1935, 14,653 came under the labour schedule and 13,076 were their dependants. Alling estimated that the remaining 44,000 entered in other categories, such as capital holders, professionals and skilled artisans [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d531]. The figures as reported do not reconcile exactly: 61,854 less the workers and their dependants leaves about 34,000, not 44,000, and the memorandum as summarized does not explain the difference. What the breakdown does show clearly is that in the peak year fewer than one immigrant in four entered as a worker on the schedule; the rest came as dependants or, on Alling's estimate, in categories defined by means or occupation.

The labour schedule was the part of immigration that came in fixed allotments. Alling lists them for half-year periods, and his figures show how they moved. The allotment was 5,500 for April–September 1933 and rose to 8,000 for April–September 1935. For October 1935–March 1936 it was cut to 3,250 [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d531].

Two series, and a gap between them. Readers who look up the annual figures for these years will find more than one set. The memorandum by J. Rives Childs of the State Department's Division of Near Eastern Affairs, dated 3 June 1939, gives recorded Jewish immigration year by year, compiled from the British returns [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]. A second series, printed by the Jewish Virtual Library from the historian Yehoshua Porath, gives higher figures for most years [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_porath_table]. The two are set side by side below.

The pattern of the differences is itself informative. For 1928 to 1931 the two series agree exactly. For the mid-1920s the Porath figures are a few hundred to about a thousand higher each year. For 1932 to 1935 they are higher by roughly three to seven thousand a year, with the widest gap in 1933, 37,337 against 30,327. In 1936 the order reverses, and the Porath figure is slightly lower [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_porath_table]. The sources do not explain the difference. It may reflect how arrivals who were not recorded at entry, or who registered later, were counted in one series and not the other, but that is a possibility, not a documented finding [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_porath_table]. This book uses the State Department figures as the recorded series and gives the Porath figures beside them where the difference matters.

A third figure does not fit cleanly with either. The Jewish Virtual Library's total of 82,000 for the Fourth Aliyah is higher than the sum of either annual series for 1924–1929, while its 174,000 settlers for 1933–1936 falls between the two series' totals for those years [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fourth_aliyah] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_fifth_aliyah] [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_porath_table]. The wave totals and the annual returns may rest on different definitions; the sources do not say.

Immigration and natural increase. The same 1939 memorandum carries a finding that bears on one of the most argued questions of the Mandate. It cites a Consul General's report dated 10 January 1939, that the Jewish population had risen sixfold since 1920, almost entirely through immigration, while the Arab population had risen by about 57 percent, almost entirely through natural increase [cite:rs_rd3_frus1939_d828]. Two populations grew in these years by two different mechanisms. The Jewish community grew because people came; the Arab population grew because births exceeded deaths.

The finding also explains why the level of Jewish immigration, and the Shaw Commission's test of the country's "absorbing power," carried so much weight: on the consul's account, immigration was the means by which the Jewish share of the population changed. The consul's report is one official observer's assessment, made in 1939. The question of Arab immigration into Palestine in the 1930s, its scale and its sources, has not been researched for this book, and the prose here makes no claim about it beyond what the consul wrote.

What the record leaves open. The documents establish the shape of these years clearly: a surge in 1925, a collapse in 1927–28, a slow recovery, and a steep climb from 1932 to the peak of 1935. They establish the main forces behind the first wave, Polish economic and political pressure and the narrowing of American immigration, and they establish that fewer than one in four immigrants in the peak year entered as a schedule worker. They do not settle the exact number who came in any given year, because the two published series differ and neither source explains why. They do not break down the 1930s arrivals by country, or say how many of the 1935 immigrants without a schedule place held capital and how many were professionals or artisans. And the 44,000 in Alling's memorandum does not square with the numbers it is drawn from. These are the limits within which any account of the Fourth and Fifth Aliyot, including this one, has to work.
