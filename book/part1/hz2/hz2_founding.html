The Guards in the Bekaa

The men who formed Hezbollah came from a community that had already organized. Lebanon's Shia were by then up to 32 percent of the population but held 21 percent of parliamentary seats, and many had moved from the south to the edges of Beirut. [cite:hz1_mrg_shia] Amal had been formed in 1975 as the armed wing of Musa al-Sadr's Movement of the Deprived. Sadr himself disappeared in Libya in August 1978. [cite:hz1_brit_amal] [cite:hz1_olj_sadr] Part One sets out that background. [scene:hz1_lebanon|Part One: Lebanon's divided state] What was new in 1982 was a foreign state with a stated policy of exporting its revolution, and an Israeli invasion that gave it the opening. On 21 March 1980 Ayatollah Khomeini had told Iranians: "We should try hard to export our revolution to the world." Iran's 1979 constitution gives its armed forces "the ideological mission of jihad in God's way". [cite:hz1_merip_khomeini] [cite:hz1_iran_const]

The base. In 1982 Iran set up a base in the Bekaa Valley, which Syria controlled. From there, by the standard account, its Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps founded, financed, trained and equipped Hezbollah. The Sheikh Abdullah barracks in Baalbek was believed to house the Guards training Lebanese recruits. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] A Belfer Center study published in July 2026 opens from the same premise: the party was "Founded in 1982 under Iranian Revolutionary Guard sponsorship". [cite:hz2_belfer_cohen] Published figures for the size of the Guards contingent differ by a factor of several. None of the sources verified for this part gives a number, so this part gives none.

Islamic Amal and the clerics. Hezbollah did not start as a single body. One of its building blocks was Islamic Amal, a faction that broke from Amal under Hussein Musawi. Accounts of the bombings below describe it as an autonomous part of the "embryonic" Hezbollah. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] Historians do not agree on whether Hezbollah existed as an organization by late 1983. The group announced itself only in 1985. [cite:hz2_wiki_barracks] The best-known cleric of the period, Sheikh Mohammed Hussein Fadlallah, later became a grand ayatollah and the top spiritual leader of Lebanon's Shia community. Lebanese and American intelligence officials named him as the man who blessed the 1983 suicide bombers. He denied any role until his death in 2010. [cite:hz2_wilson_fadlallah] American intelligence had a name for the network by 1984. A CIA note written after the September 1984 bombing of the US embassy annex referred to "Hizb Allah, operating with Iranian support under the cover name of Islamic Jihad". [cite:hz2_d1_levitt]

Iran's role in its own words. Iran's government has not formally acknowledged directing any of the attacks of this period. The fullest account from an Iranian official comes from Sayyed Issa Tabatabai, Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei's representative in Lebanon, in an interview with Iran's state news agency, IRNA. He said Hezbollah's efforts "started in [Lebanon's] Baalbek area", where Revolutionary Guards arrived. He said that for two years "[Hezbollah's] military base was located in my home", and that he "quickly went to Lebanon and provided what was needed". [cite:hz2_fox_tabatabai] The interview is known in English through a translation by the Middle East Media Research Institute (MEMRI). MEMRI says IRNA removed the passage in which Tabatabai acknowledged receiving Khomeini's ruling ordering attacks. MEMRI notes that Iran "has always vehemently denied any role in the bombings". [cite:hz2_fox_tabatabai] It is a single interview, reported in English through one translator. It shows how an Iranian official close to the Supreme Leader described Iran's founding role, but it is not a government admission.

What US courts found. The most detailed findings on Iran's role come from American civil suits brought by families of the Marines killed in October 1983. In Peterson v. Islamic Republic of Iran (2003), Judge Royce Lamberth found that Hezbollah "was formed under the auspices of the government of Iran". He found that it "received massive material and technical support from the Iranian government". [cite:hz2_anderson] Expert witnesses included Patrick Clawson, who testified that "Hezbollah is largely under Iranian orders", and Reuven Paz, who said it was "totally controlled by Iran". [cite:hz2_anderson] [cite:hz2_fain] These findings carry weight, but two limits apply. Iran did not appear to contest them, so the cases were decided by default. And the legal test was evidence "satisfactory to the court", which the court said means it may not simply accept unsupported allegations. [cite:hz2_anderson] Iran has called the rulings "baseless" and "politically motivated". [cite:hz2_jurist_2007]

Hezbollah's own account. The movement's founding document, read in February 1985, describes the Israeli invasion and the Western forces in Beirut as its enemies. It also declares obedience to "one leader, wise and just", meaning Khomeini. [cite:hz2_wiki_ideology] Both parts of that self-description matter. Part Three examines the argument over which mattered more, and labels it as argument. [scene:hz3_question|Part Three: Would Hezbollah exist?]
