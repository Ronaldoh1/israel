Who Knows You Travel

Book Five asks the same questions of every tool: what it collects, who uses it, where it has gone wrong, what the law allows, and what its worst use would look like. The last is the book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, in which immigration enforcement is the occasion for building a machine that could later be pointed at anyone; a tool's ability is a documented fact, and its use against critics needs a document of its own. The earlier parts followed data a person gives away without noticing, through phones, cars and brokers ([scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four], [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Six]). Travel is different. To board a plane a person must hand over a name, a birth date and a face, and must do it to the state. This section sets out what each system takes, how long it keeps it, and where in 2025 and 2026 the first of these records began to flow to immigration enforcement.

The booking: Secure Flight

Every domestic and international flight on a US airline, and every foreign airline's flight to or from the United States, passes through a Transportation Security Administration (TSA) programme called Secure Flight. Its final rule, published on 28 October 2008 and in force from 29 December 2008, moved the job of checking passengers against government watch lists from the airlines to TSA itself. Airlines must ask for a passenger's full name, date of birth and gender, and for a redress number or Known Traveler Number if the passenger has one; TSA compares them with two subsets of the government's terrorist screening database, the No Fly list (barred from boarding) and the Selectee list (extra screening), and may check larger lists "when security conditions warrant". [cite:de25b_fr_secureflight] The privacy assessment published before launch set the retention clock: a passenger who matched nothing is kept for seven days after the trip, a possible match for seven years, and a confirmed match for 99 years. The same document promised that Secure Flight "does not use algorithms to assign risk scores to individuals" and used no commercial data. [cite:de25b_pia_secureflight]

A second TSA programme did score travellers. Quiet Skies used an algorithm to review travel patterns, foreign connections and other government data, and sent federal air marshals to watch people who were on no FBI watch list and were suspected of nothing. On 5 June 2025 Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem ended it, calling it "a political rolodex of the Biden Administration"; the trigger was the case of Tulsi Gabbard, later Director of National Intelligence, who said she had been listed after criticising Vice President Kamala Harris. CNN reported that the reason for her listing was never made clear, and the Washington Times put the programme's cost at about $200 million a year. [cite:de25b_cnn_quietskies] [cite:de25b_wt_quietskies] The episode belongs in the record for both parties at once: a rules-based watch on unaccused citizens was in use under a Democratic administration, and a Republican one shut it down while accusing its predecessor of political targeting.

The international trip: manifests, booking records and a fifteen-year archive

For international flights two more streams reach the government before the plane moves. The Advance Passenger Information System (APIS) is the passenger manifest: since 19 February 2008 airlines have had to send US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) the details from each passenger's passport page, at the latest 30 minutes before the doors close, or passenger by passenger up to the moment they close, for flights leaving the United States as well as those arriving. A passenger who comes back "not-cleared" cannot be given a boarding pass. [cite:de25b_fr_apis] The second stream is the Passenger Name Record (PNR), the airline's own booking file. It is richer than the passport page. Under the 2012 agreement that governs transfers from European airlines, a PNR can include travel dates and itinerary, ticket details, addresses and telephone numbers, the payment method and card number, the travel agent, the seat and the baggage. The agreement lets the Department of Homeland Security keep it for up to five years in an active database, with names masked after six months, and then for up to ten more years in a "dormant" one. [cite:de25b_ep_pnr] Europe's data-protection supervisor called a fifteen-year maximum "clearly disproportionate" while the deal was being negotiated. [cite:de25b_pm_pnr]

The streams meet in CBP's Automated Targeting System (ATS), which compares travellers with law-enforcement and intelligence records using "risk-based rules". Its published privacy assessment lists what it takes in: the APIS manifest, visa-waiver (ESTA) and visa applications, arrival records, the student-visa system SEVIS, booking records and, for some flights, TSA's Secure Flight data. It states that "ATS is retaining ingested information consistent with its own 15-year retention period", even where the source system would have deleted it sooner. [cite:de25b_pia_ats] Records of this family have travelled further. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) documents from 2014 to 2022, released under the Freedom of Information Act and reported by the Guardian, show investigators planning in 2020 to search APIS and border-crossing data through Palantir's case system, and training on searching SEVIS. [cite:de24_guardian_icm]

The face

Congress first ordered a biometric record of foreign visitors' entries and exits after 2001. A final rule published on 27 October 2025 and in force from 26 December 2025 completed it: CBP may photograph every non-citizen on entry and exit at airports, land crossings and seaports. US citizens are outside the rule and take part only voluntarily. [cite:de25b_mit_entryexit] [cite:de26_bu_entryexit] The camera at the departure gate is covered in [scene:de26_faces_drones_satellites|the section on faces]. At the security checkpoint, TSA compares a live photograph with the photo on the traveller's ID. It had the cameras at 84 airports in May 2025 and planned for about 400. TSA says a traveller may decline "without recourse" and keep their place in line, and that images are deleted once identity is confirmed, except for members of its PreCheck Touchless ID service (24 hours after departure) and photos kept to test accuracy. [cite:de25b_siw_tsaface] A survey by the Algorithmic Justice League found 74 per cent of respondents had not been told facial recognition was in use. A bipartisan group led by Senator Jeff Merkley reintroduced the Traveler Privacy Protection Act, which would make the opt-out a matter of law rather than policy, in May 2025; as of October 2026 it has not been enacted. [cite:de25b_reg_tsaface]

The identity document changed too. REAL ID enforcement, required by a 2005 Act that followed a recommendation of the 9/11 Commission, began on 7 May 2025: only licences meeting the federal standard, or a passport, now get a traveller through security. [cite:de25b_tsa_realid] From 1 February 2026 a traveller without one can pay $45 for a TSA check called ConfirmID, which asks questions only the real person should be able to answer and lasts ten days. [cite:de25b_az_confirmid]

The private lane: CLEAR

One name needs separating first. Thomson Reuters CLEAR, the investigative database ICE pays for and which is described in [scene:de24_data_and_fusion|Part Twenty-Four], is a different company from CLEAR the airport service. The airport CLEAR is Clear Secure, Inc. It began in 2003 as Verified Identity Pass, shut down after bankruptcy in 2009, was bought in 2010 by the investor Caryn Seidman Becker and her partner Ken Cornick, relaunched in 2012 and listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 2021 under the ticker YOU. [cite:de25b_wiki_clear] Its annual report states plainly that "we are controlled by the Founder Post-IPO Members", the founders' holding entities. [cite:de25b_10k_2025] At the end of 2025 it counted 38.0 million members, of whom 7.6 million were active paying members of its airport lane, CLEAR+, at 60 airports, on revenue of $900.8 million. [cite:de25b_pr_clearfy25]

Its privacy notice, last updated on 26 June 2026, lists what it takes: "faceprints, fingerprints, iris scans", a selfie and a photo of the traveller's ID. For CLEAR+ it shares data "with the Transportation Security Administration"; it discloses to government "if required by law or legal process"; it may share data with third parties for advertising and marketing; and it says it "does not sell your biometrics or sensitive Personal Data". [cite:de25b_clear_privacy] Its ties to the state are commercial and regulatory: it works under TSA's Registered Traveler programme, holds a Homeland Security certification under the SAFETY Act, was picked by TSA in January 2020 to enrol people in PreCheck and did so at 340 shops by the end of 2025, and on 9 December 2025 announced a contract with the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to verify identities for Medicare.gov accounts from early 2026. [cite:de25b_10k_2023] [cite:de25b_pr_clearfy25] [cite:de25b_clear_cms] Its record has one documented failure: in July 2022 a passenger travelling under a false identity and carrying ammunition got through its lane before TSA stopped him; CLEAR called it "a single human error", and TSA responded by requiring its own officers to check CLEAR members' IDs. [cite:de25b_cbs_clear] No document shows CLEAR giving member biometrics to ICE.

The first join: Secure Flight to ICE

One of these systems has been joined to immigration enforcement, and the join is documented. The New York Times reported on 12 December 2025 that TSA sends ICE lists of air travellers' names several times a week, which ICE matches against people with final removal orders. A memorandum of agreement signed in May 2025, released in a records lawsuit brought by the group American Oversight, cites the Secure Flight rule as TSA's legal basis and says ICE will keep what it receives in its own repository; the list of shared data fields is almost entirely redacted. The acting TSA administrator had told Congress that TSA does not "send the information to ICE". [cite:de25b_ipt_tsaice] [cite:de25b_ao_moa] Reuters, reviewing internal ICE data, counted records on more than 31,000 travellers passed from January 2025 to February 2026 and more than 800 arrests; it noted that the regulation creating Secure Flight said it was not meant to track immigration offenders. [cite:de25b_reuters_tsaice]

Behind the passenger's sequence sits a stack of systems: airline feeds into two matching engines, an archive, and the decisions at the airport, with the 2025 branch to ICE running out of the TSA side.

If it were connected

Graded on the scale used across Book Five, for joins only. Secure Flight names to ICE: documented, by the May 2025 agreement and the Reuters count; onward into Palantir's systems: possible, since no record shows that path. [cite:de25b_ao_moa] [cite:de25b_reuters_tsaice] Student-visa and airline-manifest records searched through ICE's Palantir case system: documented in Homeland Security Investigations records from 2014 to 2022. [cite:de24_guardian_icm] The faces gathered at checkpoints and gates joined to DHS's next biometric system, HART, which is designed to be shared across agencies and foreign partners: possible, since HART was not yet operational in August 2024. [cite:de28_hart_pia] The airport company CLEAR's irises and faces to immigration enforcement: possible only through the legal process its notice allows, with no document showing it; it is a different company from Thomson Reuters' CLEAR people-search database, which ICE does buy. [cite:de25b_clear_privacy] Together these would let an agency know, from a name, when and where a person has flown; what stands in the way is mostly the purpose language of each system's rule, and the 2025 agreement put Secure Flight to a use its own rule said it was not meant for.
