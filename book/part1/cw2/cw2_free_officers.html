Egypt's Revolution, 1952–1956: The Free Officers, the Base and the Presidency

The stage that this chapter calls "Retaliate" opens on the border between Israel and its neighbours, but the power that would shape most of its turns sat in Cairo. In the four years between July 1952 and June 1956, Egypt changed from a monarchy with a British army on its soil into a republic led by a colonel who had removed the king, outmanoeuvred his own figurehead, negotiated the British out of the Canal Zone, banned the Muslim Brotherhood and asked Moscow for weapons. Each of those steps was taken by Egyptians, for Egyptian reasons, and each later reached Israel: through the arms race, through the fedayeen, through the Canal. The Egyptian story comes first, because the rest of the stage cannot be read without it.

Egypt came to 1952 without a peace with Israel. It had joined Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and Iraq in invading the newly declared State of Israel in May 1948, and the war had ended not with a peace treaty but with the armistice negotiated at Rhodes in 1949, whose line functioned as the de facto Egypt–Israel border along the edge of the Gaza Strip. [cite:wikipedia_1948_war] Inside that line Egypt held the Gaza Strip, which it governed from 1948 to 1967 as a military protectorate: direct administration, but never annexation, a distinction Egyptian officials maintained throughout. Why Egypt chose not to annex is not settled by the sources used here. Some accounts stress an Arab League political consensus against formal annexation; others stress Egyptian strategic caution. Volume II tells how that administration began. A common belief holds that Egypt has never had any administrative role in Gaza and that the Strip was always a purely Palestinian territory apart from Egypt. The record is the reverse: for nineteen years Egypt was the governing power there, and in 1952 it had already been so for four. Gaza under Egyptian rule is the place where several of this stage's later crises began, from the raid of February 1955 to the fedayeen units, and both are told later in this chapter.

The coup of 23 July 1952. The Free Officers carried out their coup in Cairo on 23 July 1952. Their leader was Colonel Gamal Abdel Nasser; their figurehead was Major General Muhammad Naguib. King Farouk was dethroned and ordered to leave the country. [cite:rs_cw2_ebsco_farouk] [cite:w_1952_egypt] HISTORY.com puts the number who acted with Nasser at 89 other Free Officers; that count comes from a popular reference source and is given here as its figure. [cite:rs_cw2_history_nasser]

What the officers wanted can be read most reliably from what they did, because the sources do not set out the movement's programme in its own words. In the months after the coup the new regime decreed agricultural land reform. In the summer of 1953 the monarchy gave way to a republic. Naguib served as prime minister from 1952 to 1954 and as president from 1953 to 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_ebsco_farouk] The exact date on which the republic was proclaimed, and the date Farouk actually left, are not given in those sources. The pattern of the acts is plain enough: an end to the crown, a reform of land holding, and, as the next two years showed, an end to the British military presence that the monarchy had lived with.

The coup belongs to what can be called a decade of coups in the region, running from Syria's three in 1949 to the fall of Iraq's monarchy in 1958. Syria's are told in Volume II, Iran's of August 1953 in Stage One, and the others in later stages. [cite:w_1952_egypt] One common explanation for that decade holds that the region's dictatorships and wars grew only out of local culture and religion. Egypt's case, as these sources document it, fits neither that account nor its mirror image. The officers who took power were Egyptian and acted on their own decision; but every major question they then faced, from the British base to the source of their weapons, ran through London, Washington and Moscow. The regime's character was shaped by both.

Nasser against Naguib, 1954. The figurehead did not stay one. In early 1954 a power struggle split the ruling council. By April 1954 Nasser had majority support, and the "April Decrees" removed Naguib from effective power; Nasser became prime minister. [cite:rs_cw2_ebsco_farouk] [cite:rs_cw2_history_nasser] Naguib kept the title of president for some months more, but the decisions of the rest of 1954 were Nasser's.

Asking Moscow, June 1954. The first of those decisions to reach beyond Egypt's borders was taken quietly. On 15 June 1954 Nasser received the Soviet ambassador, D. S. Solod. According to the Soviet record of the conversation, he told Solod that Britain had stopped arms shipments to Egypt and had blocked supplies from other European states, that Egypt could not meet "a hundredth of its army's needs," and he formally asked what the Soviet position would be on selling arms to Egypt. [cite:rs_cw2_wilson_solod] That request, more than a year before the Czech arms deal was announced, matters for one of the stage's central disputes: whether Israel's raid on Gaza in February 1955 pushed Egypt toward Soviet weapons, or whether Nasser was already looking. The deal itself and that argument are told in [scene:cw2_czech_arms|the arms race]. Here it is enough to note Egypt's own stated case: a Western supplier had cut it off.

The same summer an Israeli military intelligence network of Egyptian Jews began a sabotage campaign inside Egypt meant to damage Egypt's ties with the West during the base negotiations; its course and its consequences are told in [scene:lv_s01|Operation Susannah and the Lavon affair].

The British leave the Canal Zone. The negotiation that operation was aimed at produced Egypt's largest gain of the period. The Anglo-Egyptian Heads of Agreement were initialled on 27 July 1954, and the final agreement was signed on 19 October 1954. It abrogated the treaty of 1936. British troops would leave the Canal Zone. The base would be kept in working order by 1,200 civilian technicians, of whom up to 800 could come from outside Egypt. Britain could return only in the event of an attack on Egypt, on any other Arab state, or on Turkey; an attack on Iran or on Israel would not count. The agreement ran for seven years. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_suez_base] In the same document Egypt pledged to uphold "the principles of the 1888 Convention of Constantinople concerning the free use of the Suez Canal," and the accord recognized the Canal as "an integral part of Egypt." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_suez_base]

The terms show who could act and who could not. Britain kept a right of return and a working base, but only on triggers it had agreed with Egypt and only for seven years. Egypt gained the departure of foreign troops and a written recognition that the Canal was part of its territory, in exchange for a pledge on free passage drawn from an 1888 convention. Israel was not a party, and the agreement's trigger clause excluded it: an attack on Israel was not among the events that would bring British forces back. Whether the pledge on free use extended to ships bound for Israel was not answered by the text. The Security Council had already ruled against Egypt's restrictions on Israel-bound shipping in 1951, as Stage One tells, and the question was being tested again even as the agreement was concluded: the seizure of the Israeli ship Bat Galim at the end of September 1954 is told later in this chapter.

Manshiya Square and the Brotherhood, October–November 1954. Not every Egyptian accepted the bargain, and the regime's opponents were not only foreign. On 26 October 1954, a week after the signing, Nasser was speaking about the new treaty in Manshiya Square in Alexandria when Mahmoud Abdel-Latif, a young member of the Muslim Brotherhood, fired at him. Nasser kept speaking. [cite:rs_cw2_origins_mb] Who ordered the attempt is not established. The historians who wrote the account used here, in the Origins series of Ohio State and Miami universities, say plainly that it is not clear; the gunman's membership is documented, but the chain of command behind him, if there was one, is not.

The consequences are documented. The Brotherhood was banned. More than 1,000 of its members were arrested, a small number were executed, and the movement went underground. [cite:rs_cw2_origins_mb] The ban removed the Brotherhood from legal political life, and the use the regime made of the shooting was not limited to the Brotherhood.

Naguib was next. In mid-November 1954 the Revolutionary Command Council removed him "from all his functions"; the announcement was planned for 15 November. The Council accused him of "conniving with Muslim Brotherhood extremists." The American ambassador in Cairo, Caffery, reporting the move to Washington, noted that Naguib had opposed the Suez agreement. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_naguib] Two charges sat side by side: the Council's, that the president had been in league with the men behind the attempt on Nasser, and the ambassador's observation that he had also been against the treaty Nasser had just signed. The telegram does not resolve which weighed more, and the sources here do not either. What it does show is that the contest of early 1954 ended, after the shooting, with Naguib's complete removal.

The presidency, June 1956. The consolidation was completed at the ballot box, on terms that left no room for a contest. On 23 June 1956 Nasser, the only candidate, was elected Egypt's first president with 99.95 per cent of the vote, in a referendum that also approved a new constitution by 99.8 per cent. [cite:rs_cw2_history_nasser] The figures come from HISTORY.com and are given as it reports them. The man who had been the hidden leader of a junta in 1952 was, four years later, the sole holder of the state's highest office.

What that rise meant beyond Egypt can be stated more broadly: Nasser's ascent after 1952 made Egypt the leading voice of pan-Arabism and of non-aligned Third World politics through the 1950s and 1960s, the Arab world's central power and its most vocal opponent of Israel's existence. That is a broad characterization, and the documents in this section show its foundations rather than its full extent: a leader who had removed his rivals at home, ended a foreign military presence, and opened a channel to the Soviet bloc. A month after the presidential vote Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company, and a few months after that Britain, France and Israel invaded; that war and the pressure that reversed it belong to Stage Three and are told in [scene:egi_src_s02|the first war and the Suez crisis]. [cite:wikipedia_suez_crisis]

The record leaves several things unsettled. It does not establish who, if anyone, ordered Mahmoud Abdel-Latif to fire in Manshiya Square, and it therefore cannot say whether the Brotherhood's leadership bore responsibility for an attempt that cost the whole movement its legal existence. It does not show whether Naguib's removal turned more on the Brotherhood charge or on his opposition to the Suez agreement. It does not give the Free Officers' programme in their own words, the scale of the land reform, or the precise date the republic was proclaimed. And it does not explain why Egypt kept Gaza as an administered territory rather than annexing it. What it does establish is the sequence: by the middle of 1956 one Egyptian government, under one man, held every lever that the rest of this stage would test.
