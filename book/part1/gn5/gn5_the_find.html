The Find: A Jar, a Clan and an Oven

In December 1945 two brothers of the al-Samman clan, Muhammad and Khalifah Ali, were digging for fertiliser at the foot of the Jabal al-Tarif, a cliff on the east bank of the Nile across from the town of Nag Hammadi. They struck a sealed jar. Inside were leather-bound books written in Coptic, the last stage of the ancient Egyptian language, written in Greek letters. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:vt25_enc_nh] It was the largest discovery of early Christian writings outside the Bible ever made.

Almost everything that is told about the next few months comes from one source: interviews that the American scholar James M. Robinson conducted with Muhammad Ali and others from late 1974, nearly thirty years later. [cite:vt25_goodacre2013] In Robinson's account, Muhammad Ali hesitated to open the jar for fear that it held a jinn, broke it with his mattock, and divided the books among the men present. He was involved in a blood feud at the time, which, in his account, made him reluctant to draw attention to the find. His widowed mother, he said, burned some of the leaves in the family oven; Robinson linked those to the twelfth codex, of which little survives. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

The story has been widely repeated and seriously questioned. Robinson's versions of 1977, 1979 and 1981 differ on who was present, and in 1984 the scholars Rodolphe Kasser and Martin Krause called much of it "stories and fables". Mark Goodacre has argued that an earlier, vaguer account by the French scholar Jean Doresse is more reliable. [cite:vt25_goodacre2013] Muhammad Ali's brother denied some details, and some scholars suggest that the blood-feud story may have been a cover. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] What is not disputed is that the books were found near Nag Hammadi in late 1945, that they were divided, that some were damaged or destroyed, and that within months they were being sold.

They were sold because they were worth money, and everyone who handled them knew it. According to the account Roger Pearse summarises from Robinson, the books were divided among seven people. One, Raghib Andrawus, took a book to Cairo, where a Coptic physician, George Sobhi, alerted the Department of Antiquities. The authorities seized the book and agreed to pay three hundred Egyptian pounds: two hundred and fifty to Raghib, on condition that the remaining fifty went to the Coptic Museum. The book was entered in the museum's register on 4 October 1946. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] Other books passed through other hands. One was sold through a gold merchant; another went to a grain merchant, who sold it for enough to open a shop in Cairo. [cite:gn_pearse_nh]

None of this was unusual. Cairo had an active antiquities trade, with dealers' shops as grand as the one in the Shepheard's Hotel where some of the books were offered. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] Gnostic manuscripts had reached Western collections that way before: the Askew Codex was bought from a London bookseller in 1772, and the Berlin Codex with the Gospel of Mary, described in Part Six, was bought in Cairo in 1896. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex] What was unusual was the scale and the contents. The jar held twelve codices and part of a thirteenth, with fifty-two tractates, most of them works unknown for sixteen centuries except through their enemies' quotations. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

The finders did not know that. They knew the books were old and saleable. The next section follows the books into the hands of people who knew exactly what they were, and who paid, seized, smuggled and held them accordingly.
