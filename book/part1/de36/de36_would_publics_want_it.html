What Publics Say They Want

The texts claim that democracy is a failing system whose subjects would be better off as customers of a competent owner. One way to test the appeal of that claim is to ask people. No survey has asked whether respondents would prefer a state run as a joint-stock company with a chief executive answerable to shareholders, and none is likely to; the idea is too specific. What large cross-national surveys do ask about is the nearest thing in ordinary language: rule by a strong leader who does not have to answer to a parliament, courts or elections, and rule by the army. This section reports those answers. They measure openness to unchecked executive rule in general, which is a wider and vaguer thing than the texts' design, so they overstate its fit rather than understate it.

Twenty-four countries, 2023

The most comparable data come from the Pew Research Center, which in 2023 surveyed adults in 24 countries, from 20 February to 22 May abroad and from 20 to 26 March in the United States. [cite:de36_pew_short] Respondents rated several systems of government as good or bad. One was "a system in which a strong leader can make decisions without interference from parliament or the courts." Across the 24 countries, the median share calling that system good was 26 per cent. For comparison, the median for representative democracy, in which elected representatives decide what becomes law, was 77 per cent; for direct democracy, in which citizens vote on major issues, 70 per cent; for rule by experts, 58 per cent; and for military rule, 15 per cent. [cite:de36_pew_systems] Majorities rejected strong-leader rule in all but five of the countries. Since 2017, support for it had risen in 8 of the 22 countries asked both times and fallen in Hungary, Israel, Italy and Sweden. [cite:de36_pew_systems]

The country figures vary widely. In Sweden 5 per cent called a strong leader good, in France and the Netherlands 12, in Germany 16, in Israel 19, in Britain 31 and in the United States 26. The highest figures were in India (67), Kenya (52), Indonesia (51) and Mexico (50), with South Africa at 47, Nigeria at 43, Brazil at 36 and Argentina at 27. [cite:de36_pew_topline] The American figure needs a caution: Pew moved its American survey from telephone in 2017 to an online panel in 2023, so changes over time in the United States are not strictly comparable.

Who says yes

Pew also combined the strong-leader and military items to count those who favoured at least one of the two. The median across 24 countries was 31 per cent, highest in India at 85 per cent and lowest in Sweden at 8 per cent. In most of the 18 countries where respondents placed themselves on a left-right scale, people on the right were more likely to favour such systems; in South Korea, for example, 49 per cent on the right did, against 28 per cent on the left. Lower-income respondents were more supportive in both rich and middle-income countries: in Britain, 47 per cent of those below the median income, against 27 per cent of those above it. In the United States the young were the most open, 38 per cent of those under 30 against 26 per cent of those aged 65 and over. [cite:de36_pew_short]

Africa, Latin America and the American trend

Other regional surveys point the same way, with sharper edges. Afrobarometer's first flagship report, released on 18 July 2024 and based on 53,444 face-to-face interviews in 39 African countries, found that 66 per cent preferred democracy to any other system, 80 per cent rejected rule by one man and 78 per cent rejected one-party rule. Opposition to military rule was weaker, at 66 per cent, and had fallen 11 points over a decade in the 30 countries tracked; 53 per cent said they would accept a military takeover if elected leaders "abuse power for their own ends." [cite:de36_afrobarometer] Latinobarómetro's 2026 report, presented on 2 October 2026 and covering 18 Latin American countries, found that 52 per cent supported democracy and 17 per cent said an authoritarian government could be preferable, up from 16 per cent in 2024; support for democracy was highest in Argentina at 73 per cent and lowest in Guatemala at 35 per cent and Brazil at 41 per cent. [cite:de36_latinobarometro]

In the United States, a longer series uses a sharper wording: "a strong leader who does not have to bother with Congress and elections." A 2018 report for the Democracy Fund Voter Study Group by Lee Drutman, Larry Diamond and Joe Goldman found that 24 per cent of Americans called that good in the World Values Survey of 1995, that the figure peaked at 34 per cent (the report dates the peak to 2011 in one place and 2014 in another), and that it was 24 per cent again in 2017. Eighteen per cent called army rule good in 2017. [cite:de36_vsg] This is the only widely used item that names elections as the thing a leader would not have to bother with, and so the closest to the texts' missing election. About a quarter of Americans said yes; about three quarters did not.

What the numbers do and do not show

Three readings follow from the figures, and each has limits. First, in most democracies a minority, around a quarter, is open to a leader unchecked by parliament or courts, and majorities reject it; the texts' premise that democratic publics are ready to give up voice is not supported by these surveys. [cite:de36_pew_systems] Second, the appeal of competent management is real but takes a different form: rule by experts, at a 58 per cent median, is more than twice as popular as rule by a strong leader. The texts' "national CEO" sits somewhere between the two items, and no survey separates them. [cite:de36_pew_systems] [cite:de6_pein_2014] Third, openness to unchecked rule is not evenly spread. It is higher where people are poorer, on the right in most countries, among the young in the United States, and, in Africa, where elected leaders are seen to abuse power. [cite:de36_pew_short] [cite:de36_afrobarometer] That pattern fits the diagnosis the texts make, that people lose faith in democracy when it fails to deliver, which [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|Part Seven] examines on its merits; it does not show that those respondents want the cure the texts prescribe, which would remove the vote from them as well.

The surveys also cut against the texts' own models in one place. Support for strong-leader rule is high in India and Indonesia, both of which hold national elections, and low in the countries with the oldest parliamentary traditions. Neither pattern resembles the small, voiceless, customer-served city-states the texts admire. The question the texts actually pose, whether people would accept being shareholders' customers with no vote, has not been asked, and nothing in these data shows that a majority anywhere would say yes.
