Part Sixteen established that belief in the Dream has fallen but not collapsed. About a third of Americans told pollsters in 2025 and 2026 that the Dream still holds, against 53 per cent in 2012 on a different wording, and satisfaction with the chance to get ahead by working hard fell from 77 per cent in 2002 to 52 per cent in 2026, with a record partisan gap; yet two-thirds still say they have achieved the Dream or are on their way. [cite:ad1_wsjnorc25] [cite:ad16_colpol_apnorc] [cite:ad16_gallup_opp24] [cite:ad16_brookings_opp26] [cite:ad16_pew_top24] Americans overestimate how often a child from the bottom fifth reaches the top, 11.7 in 100 against 7.8, though one study found underestimation; meritocratic explanations grow with inequality, while the claim that the disadvantaged defend the system most did not survive a large-scale test. [cite:ad16_ast_aer] [cite:ad16_jr_chambers] [cite:ad16_mijs] [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt] Both parties have sold the same Dream in different phrases, and each has enacted laws that narrowed some of its mechanisms and widened others: cash aid reached 21 of every 100 poor families in 2023 against 68 in 1996, the uninsured rate halved after 2010, and the 2025 law lowers resources at the bottom and raises them at the top.
