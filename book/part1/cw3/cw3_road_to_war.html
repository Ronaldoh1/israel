From Nationalization to War, July–October 1956

Stage Two ended on the evening of 26 July 1956, with the Suez Canal Company in Egyptian hands, a story told in [scene:cw2_aswan|the end of the dam offer]. The hinge can be stated in a sentence. Nasser nationalized the Company after the United States and Britain refused to provide economic aid to Egypt; Eden's private judgment, as the historian Peter Hahn quotes it, was that "the Egyptian has his thumb on our windpipe." [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The Company was a joint British and French enterprise that had run the Canal since its construction in 1869, and Nasser offered "full economic compensation for the Company." [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] Three months later Egypt was at war with Israel, Britain and France at once. This section follows the road between those two points as the diplomatic record lays it out, and sets out the case Israel's government made for war in its own words, beside the case its critics make.

Diplomacy, and hints of force. The first response of the maritime powers was conference diplomacy. The State Department's own documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States, groups the weeks of August and September 1956 under the London conferences and the scheme that grew from them, the Suez Canal Users' Association. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3] On 9 September 1956 Dulles proposed that association of "18 of the world's leading maritime nations." The State Department's Office of the Historian records that the plan failed to win full support from any of the contending powers, and that through August, September and October Britain "repeatedly hinted that it might resort to force." [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] Conference diplomacy produced a plan; it did not produce agreement, and the British hints of force continued alongside it.

The parallel crises. The Canal was not the only crisis in the region that autumn, and the documentary record shows how the others ran alongside it. The same volume of Foreign Relations organizes the weeks from 19 September to 4 October under the heading "Increasing Violence Along the Israel–Jordan Border," together with the Macmillan visit; and the weeks from 5 to 29 October under four headings at once: the Security Council debate on the Canal, "the Iraqi Proposal to Move Troops Into Jordan," Anglo-American differences over the users' association, and "Israeli Mobilization." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3] The headings are the editors' arrangement of the documents, not a narrative, and the sources used here do not give the contents of those files. What they show is the shape of the problem as Washington saw it in October: a Canal dispute among the maritime powers, a violent Israeli–Jordanian frontier, a proposal to move Iraqi troops into Jordan, and an Israeli mobilization, all in the same weeks.

Ben-Gurion's statement of 15 October. Two weeks before the war, at the 169th session of the Third Knesset, Ben-Gurion gave the government's account of Israel's position. He told the house that the Hebrew year 5716 had been "a turning point in our security situation." He said that Egypt "still sits in the Gaza Strip," and that between 1954 and 1955 Egypt had caused Israel 242 casualties. He presented the government's posture as defensive preparation, not a first strike. [cite:rs_cw3_knesset_15oct56] The statement matters in two ways. It is the clearest public record of how Israel's government framed the threat on the eve of war; and, read beside the conference that opened at Sèvres a week later, it shows how far the public posture and the private planning had already diverged. That conference is the subject of [scene:cw3_sevres|the next section].

Israel's case, in its own terms. The fullest statement of why Israel went to war came afterwards, in Ben-Gurion's speech to the Knesset of 7 November 1956. He named four reasons. The first was the renewal of the fedayeen raids. The second was "the maritime blockade of Israel and the organization of an economic blockade against Israel throughout the world." The third was the Czechoslovak arms, "enormous quantities of heavy arms." The fourth was a "tripartite military alliance" of Egypt, Jordan and Syria, with their forces "placed under Egyptian command." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov] The Jewish Agency's historical account supplies the numbers on which that case rests. It states that between 1948 and 1956 "a total of 1,300 Israeli citizens were killed or wounded" in cross-border attacks; it records Egypt's announcement in September 1955 that the Straits of Tiran were closed to all vessels; and it records the Egypt–Syria military treaty of October 1955, which Jordan later joined. [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen] These are the Agency's figures, not an independent tally, and the 1,300 counts killed and wounded together across eight years; but they are the figures Israel's argument used.

The maritime blockade Ben-Gurion named had a history at the United Nations. On 1 September 1951 the Security Council, in Resolution 95, had called on Egypt to end its restrictions on "the passage of international commercial shipping and goods" through the Canal, by eight votes to none, a ruling Egypt contested and the story of which is told in [scene:cw1_suez_blockade|the Canal closed to Israel]. [cite:rs_cw1_frus1951_res95] [cite:rs_cw1_spv558] Five years later the restriction still stood, and the Tiran closure of September 1955 had added a second one at the Gulf of Aqaba. [cite:rs_cw3_jafi_fedayeen]

Stated at full strength, the argument ran like this. For two years raids had been organized from Gaza, which Egypt administered. Egypt barred Israeli-bound shipping from the Canal in defiance of a Security Council ruling, and from September 1955 closed the Straits of Tiran to all vessels. Egypt was receiving from Czechoslovakia what Ben-Gurion called "enormous quantities of heavy arms," and in October 1955 it had bound Syria, and later Jordan, into a military treaty under its command. A government that waited for those forces to be ready would be choosing the time of its enemy's attack. Ben-Gurion described the campaign afterwards as "an action in self-defence in accordance with Article 51 of the Charter of the United Nations." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov]

The critical case. Against this stands a second reading, and it rests on documents too. The first is the Sèvres protocol itself: the timing and form of Israel's attack were fixed in a secret agreement with Britain and France whose purpose, on their side, was a pretext to seize the Canal. Avi Shlaim, who reconstructed the conference, reads Ben-Gurion's aims there as reaching beyond defence to territory, and calls the protocol "a monument to French opportunism, Eden's duplicity, and Ben-Gurion's paranoia," a verdict that is his interpretation. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] The second concerns Gaza. According to the Gush Katif Heritage Center's history of the Strip, which cites Hagai Huberman, Ben-Gurion, then defence minister, proposed on 29 March 1955 that Israel reconquer the Gaza Strip, and the government rejected the proposal; and at the cabinet meeting of October 1956 that approved the operation, he listed among the war's aims "[control of] the Gaza Strip." [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] If that account is right, the Strip that Ben-Gurion described to the Knesset on 15 October as the place where Egypt "still sits" was also, in the cabinet room, an objective.

The two cases are not as opposed as they first appear, and neither disposes of the other. The raids, the blockade, the arms and the joint command were real grievances, documented in Israel's own records and, for the Canal, in a Security Council resolution. That Israel had reasons to fear Egypt does not settle why it attacked when it did and in the form it did; the Sèvres record answers that second question, and it answers it with the interests of London and Paris as much as with Israel's. Both can be true together: a government with genuine security grievances chose to act through a secret alliance that served other purposes, and its own aims, by the critical account, included ground it had already sought to take.

What the record leaves open. The sources used here establish what Ben-Gurion told the Knesset and what Shlaim and the Gush Katif Heritage Center report of the cabinet, but they do not include the cabinet minutes themselves, so the October 1956 war-aims list is known here only through a single attributed account. The Jewish Agency's 1,300 and Ben-Gurion's 242 count different periods and are not broken down into dead and wounded in these sources; no independent tally was found to check them against. The contents of the American files on the Israel–Jordan border and on Iraq's proposal to move troops into Jordan are named in the record's headings but were not consulted. And how much weight each of Israel's four stated reasons carried in the decision, against the opportunity France offered, is a question the documents in this section do not settle. They do show that by the middle of October 1956, Israel's government was speaking publicly of defence while preparing, in private, a war whose terms were about to be written down at Sèvres.
