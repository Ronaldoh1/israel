Who Built This, and Why It Never Changes

The American textbook system is criticized from every direction and has barely changed in eighty years. That is not an accident of neglect. Two careful studies by people from inside the world of American education explain how it was built and why it holds.

The campaign that taught publishers fear

In the 1930s the most widely used social-studies books in American schools were the series by Harold Rugg, a professor at Teachers College, Columbia. Rugg's books presented American society as having weaknesses as well as strengths, including in its economy. In July 1939 the Advertising Federation of America, meeting in Manhattan, resolved to act against them. Its research director, Alfred T. Falk, attacked one Rugg book for its "quaint economic theories" and said it was used in 4,200 school systems. The federation urged its sixty affiliated groups to campaign against textbooks containing material critical of advertising. [cite:pen_time_rugg1939] The American Legion joined the attack, and critics called the books pro-socialist. [cite:pen_wiki_rugg]

By September 1940 the books had been banned in about half a dozen school systems. In Bradner, Ohio, the vice-president of the school board seized the school's copies and burned them. In Englewood, New Jersey, the financial journalist B. C. Forbes, a member of the school board, had spent a year calling them "subversive." In Binghamton, New York, two board members proposed a public burning, and the superintendent ordered the books off the library shelves. The series had sold about two million copies and was used in some 4,000 schools. [cite:pen_time_rugg1940] Within a few years it had fallen out of use. [cite:pen_wiki_rugg]

The lesson for publishers was plain. An organized campaign could destroy a successful book, and the safest book was one that gave no organized group a reason to attack it.

The two studies

Frances FitzGerald's America Revised (1979) traced how American history textbooks changed across the twentieth century. She showed that their treatment of immigrants, Black Americans, Native Americans and American foreign policy shifted with the politics of each era, not with new historical discovery; after the urban uprisings of the late 1960s, for instance, pupils suddenly learned that America was multiracial. She called the adoption process "all-controlling." [cite:pen_kirkus_fitzgerald]

Diane Ravitch, a historian of education who served as an assistant secretary in the US Department of Education, [cite:pen_wiki_ravitch] carried the story forward in The Language Police (2003). She documented the largely unseen bias and sensitivity guidelines that publishers and test-makers use to screen their material, and showed pressure arriving from both directions: from the left for representation and against Eurocentric history, from the right against evolution and for church and family values. Publishers, she found, avoided controversy before anyone complained, a habit she called "preemptive capitulation." [cite:pen_mj_ravitch]

Why it holds

The two accounts together describe a loop. Statewide adoption, and the cost of writing books that satisfy every large state's rules, keep the market in the hands of a few big publishers. Those publishers, having spent heavily to win the largest adoptions, become cautious. The caution makes them easy to influence for any group organized enough to show up at a hearing or submit a review. Every party with the power to change the system gains something from it: the publishers face little competition, the large states keep their leverage, and organized advocates of every kind find one narrow gate easier to work than an open market of ideas.

This is the frame for reading the Virginia record earlier in this part. A request to replace "occupation" with "control of" does not need to win an argument in public. It only needs to reach a cautious publisher, in a system built since 1939 to make caution the safest business choice. The same door is open to every organized interest, on every subject. On this subject, the record documents one network using it systematically.
