The Arab World: Curricula That Follow Diplomacy

In the Arab states, what schools teach about Israel has moved with diplomacy. Peace treaties and normalization agreements have been followed, sometimes within a few years, by changes in the textbooks. The changes are real, but almost everything known about them in detail comes from two Israeli sources: IMPACT-se, a monitoring group whose reports Israeli and European officials cite, and the Institute for National Security Studies, an Israeli research centre. Their findings are reported here as theirs. Few comparable independent studies were located for this book, and that gap is itself part of the record.

The states that made peace early

Egypt signed its peace treaty with Israel in 1979. A study of the textbook issued for the 2015–16 school year, by the INSS researcher Ofir Winter, found a full chapter on the treaty that presented peace as a condition for Egypt's economic progress, described Israel as a partner in "friendly" relations, showed Prime Minister Menachem Begin beside President Anwar Sadat, and mentioned the wars and the Palestinian question less often than books from the Mubarak years. [cite:nat_inss_egypt2016]

Jordan made peace in 1994, but its schoolbooks have moved less. IMPACT-se's review of 125 Jordanian textbooks for 2025–26, published in May 2026, reported that a new ninth-grade history book labels the whole land between the river and the sea "Palestine"; that one book presents the treaty as a reluctant step to recover stolen water and land; that a ninth-grade religious-education book presents treachery and the breaking of agreements as traits of the Jews, drawing on an episode from early Islamic history; and that the books describe the 7 October 2023 attack only after an account of Israeli oppression. The report published no response from Jordan's education ministry. [cite:nat_alg_jordan2026]

The states that normalized after 2020

The United Arab Emirates normalized relations with Israel under the Abraham Accords of 2020. IMPACT-se's January 2022 review of 220 Emirati government textbooks from 2016 to 2021 found no incitement and a strong emphasis on tolerance, including respect for Jews, and found that the Accords were taught from grade six, with the endorsement of the country's Islamic authorities. It also found that Israel still did not appear by name on the books' maps, with one exception; that Zionism was presented negatively; and that the books did not teach the Holocaust or the history of Jews in the region. [cite:nat_toi_uae2022] In January 2023 the UAE embassy confirmed that Holocaust education would be added to the curriculum. [cite:nat_alg_uae2023]

Saudi Arabia has not normalized relations, and has said it will not do so before a Palestinian state is established, but its books have changed during the years of US-mediated talks. IMPACT-se reported in May 2024 that the 2023–24 books had dropped the phrase "the Zionist enemy," no longer described Zionism as a "racist European movement," removed passages warning of Israeli plans to expel Palestinians, and left the area of Palestine unnamed on maps where it had been named before; it counted 21 such removals. [cite:nat_newarab_saudi2024] In August 2026 the same group reported that, across 136 books for 2025–26, the curriculum was now "entirely free" of content hostile to Jews, and that the chapter once titled "The Attempt to Create the Zionist Entity" had been renamed "British Mandate in Palestine." [cite:nat_alg_saudi2026]

What the Arab case shows

Two patterns run through these reports. The first is that the curriculum follows the state's foreign policy. Egypt's books warmed under a government invested in the treaty, the UAE's within two years of the Accords, Saudi Arabia's during its negotiations, and Jordan's least, despite a treaty more than thirty years old. In each case a ministry holds the pen, and the ministry answers to the government's diplomacy.

The second is that the changes run mostly one way: toward removing hostility to Israel, and in Saudi Arabia's case toward removing Palestine from the map. Whether that is the end of a hateful curriculum or the erasure of a people from their neighbours' schoolbooks depends on where the reader stands. The same monitor that praises the change is the main source for it, and the people whose name disappears from the map were not consulted. Both facts belong on the record together.
