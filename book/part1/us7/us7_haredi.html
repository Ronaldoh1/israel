Israel: The Haredi Exemption and Its Funding

The second pillar of the religious status quo, after personal status law, is the exemption from military service for men in full-time Torah study. It began in 1948–49 with about 400 yeshiva students. The cap rose to 800 a year after 1967 and was lifted entirely in 1977 under a coalition agreement between Likud and Agudat Yisrael. [cite:us7_globes_draft2024] [cite:us7_wiki_haredidraft] By the end of June 2023 the state counted about 63,000 exempt students. [cite:us7_globes_draft2024] Each attempt to regulate the exemption by law has fallen in court. The Tal Law of July 2002 let students defer service; in 2005 only 31 of the 41,450 students it covered later enlisted. The Supreme Court struck it down on 21 February 2012; a 2014 enlistment law was amended in 2015, and the court struck down those amendments on 12 September 2017. [cite:us7_wiki_haredidraft] On 25 June 2024 a nine-justice panel ruled unanimously that the government had no legal basis for a blanket exemption, that it must draft yeshiva students, and that it could not keep funding yeshivas and kollels (seminaries for married men) for students without a lawful exemption or deferral. Acting President Uzi Vogelman wrote that the government had "severely harmed the rule of law and the principle that all individuals are equal before the law". [cite:us7_globes_draft2024]

Enforcement since then has been limited. Of 3,000 draft notices sent in July 2024, fewer than one in ten recipients reported; by November 2024 the army had approved 7,000 more orders and 1,126 arrest warrants. [cite:us7_wiki_haredidraft] The Israel Democracy Institute (IDI) counts 3,060 Haredi enlistments in the army or civilian service in 2025 against 3,022 in 2015, a fall of about a quarter relative to population. [cite:us7_idi_haredi2025] The coalition answered the courts with legislation. On 13 July 2026 the Knesset passed Basic Law: Torah Study, 63 to 52, declaring Torah study a "foundational value"; the Movement for Quality Government petitioned against it the same day. [cite:us2_toi_torahlaw] A law passed the same month to bar arrests of Haredi draft evaders for about seven months was frozen by the High Court within a day; on 28 July 2026 a nine-justice panel extended the freeze, with the attorney general and the Knesset's own legal adviser urging annulment and the government boycotting the hearing. About 72,000 Haredi men aged 18 to 24 had ignored conscription orders. [cite:us7_toi_arrestlaw2026] [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026] No final ruling on either law had been reported in the sources this book found as of October 2026. Part Nineteen sets the exemption beside the US volunteer force. [scene:us19_service|Part Nineteen: who serves]

The money

The state funds the institutions. The 2026 budget, approved by the cabinet on 10 March 2026, carried more than NIS 5 billion ($1.6 billion) in discretionary coalition funds; by a Berl Katznelson Center count, NIS 2.2 billion of it, 53 per cent, went to Haredi purposes, including NIS 1.56 billion for Torah institutions such as yeshivas and kollels and NIS 107 million for Haredi schools outside the state system ("recognised but unofficial" and "exempt" schools, this book's sum). The Finance Ministry reportedly opposed the allocation, and opposition politicians called it a subsidy for draft evasion in wartime. [cite:us7_toi_coalfunds2026] The population these policies govern is growing. The IDI counts about 750,000 Haredi Israelis in 2009 (10 per cent of the population), 948,000 in 2015 (11 per cent) and 1.45 million in 2025 (14.3 per cent). Yeshiva and kollel students numbered 169,366 in 2023, up 83 per cent in a decade, while employment among Haredi men rose from about a third around 2002 to 52 per cent in 2015 and about 54 per cent now, stalled since 2016. [cite:us7_idi_haredi2025] A third of Haredi families were below the poverty line, against 14 per cent of other Jewish families. [cite:us7_idi_haredi2025news] The schooling side of this record is in Part Seventeen. [scene:us17_schools|Part Seventeen: the school streams]

Who the arrangement serves and who it leaves out follows from its design. It gives each recognised religious establishment authority over its members' family life and gives the Haredi parties, as coalition partners from the 1977 agreement to the 2026 budget, a lever over the draft and over money. It leaves out citizens who belong to no recognised community, couples of mixed faith or the same sex, converts outside Orthodoxy and wives refused a divorce, and it places the burden of military service on those who are not exempt. Israel votes on 27 October 2026, and the draft law, the Torah Study law and the coalition funds are all live questions in that election. [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026] [cite:us2_toi_torahlaw]
