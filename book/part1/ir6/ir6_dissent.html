Israel's Own Dissenters

The strongest challenge to the warnings did not come from Iran, which had every reason to deny them, or from critics abroad. It came from men who had run Israel's intelligence services and army. Some disputed how close Iran was to a bomb; others accepted the danger but argued that an attack would make it worse. None said Iran's programme was harmless. Each is quoted with date and venue, beside the government's response where one was given.

Meir Dagan: "We Know How It Starts"

Meir Dagan led the Mossad from 2002 to the end of 2010 and oversaw the covert campaign against Iran's programme. In closed conversations in his last week in office, reported by Yedioth Ahronoth and the Associated Press on 7 January 2011, he assessed that Iran would not have a nuclear weapon before 2015, and credited unspecified "measures" with the delay. [cite:ir6_arabnews_dagan2011] [cite:ir5_ap_dagan2011] Four months later he went public. In a lecture at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem on 6 May 2011 he called a strike on Iran's nuclear facilities, in one English rendering, "a dumb idea that has no benefits." Barak said: "I am not sure, however, that his remark, as reported in the media, is correct." [cite:ir6_jta_dagan2011] By June 2011, as The Christian Science Monitor reported, Dagan had called an Israeli strike "the stupidest thing I have ever heard," said it would breach international law and predicted "war with Iran. It is the kind of thing where we know how it starts, but not how it will end"; a strike, he said, "will give the Iranians the best excuse to pursue the nuclear race." [cite:ir6_csm_dagan2011] On CBS's 60 Minutes in March 2012 he said "the regime in Iran is a very rational one" and that "an attack on Iran before you are exploring all other approaches is not the right way." He did not rule out force; he added that if someone had to act, "I always prefer that Americans will do it." [cite:ir6_cbs_dagan2012] [cite:ir6a_cbs_dagan]

The Order of 2010 and the Order of 2011

Dagan also objected to how the decision was being made. On 5 November 2012 the Channel 2 programme Uvda reported that at a meeting in 2010 Netanyahu and Barak ordered the chief of staff, Gabi Ashkenazi, and Dagan to put Israel's forces on "P Plus", a pre-attack status for a strike on Iran. Ashkenazi, by associates' account, warned that Israel's enemies would notice and that the step could itself start a war; Dagan, also through associates, said that without the security cabinet's approval the two leaders were making an illegal decision and trying to "steal" a decision to go to war. Barak told the programme that Ashkenazi had said the army lacked the capability, that the alert was part of preparation and that no final decision was made; Ashkenazi denied Barak's account, saying through associates that the army was ready but the mission would be a strategic mistake. Netanyahu said only that he was ready to act "if necessary." [cite:ir6_cbs_uvda2012] The account rests on competing versions; no record of the meeting has been published.

Dagan's successor told the same programme six years later of a second order. Tamir Pardo, Mossad director from 2011 to 2016, said in an Uvda interview broadcast on 31 May 2018 that in 2011 Netanyahu instructed him to start the countdown to a strike, not "for the sake of a drill": "When he tells you to start the countdown process, you know that he isn't playing games with you." Pardo said he checked with legal advisers so that he would not find himself having "carried out an illegal operation"; the report does not say that he refused the order. Netanyahu's office did not comment. [cite:ir6_bg_pardo2018] [cite:ir6a_bg_pardo] Pardo had already set out his view of the threat while in office. Speaking to Israeli ambassadors on 27 December 2011, as Haaretz reported, he said: "Does Iran pose a threat to Israel? Absolutely," but "the term 'existential threat' is used too freely." [cite:ir6_ahram_pardo2011]

Diskin, Ayalon, Halevy and Yadlin

Yuval Diskin, who headed the Shin Bet internal security service until 2011, made the sharpest personal attack. At a public meeting on 27 April 2012 he said: "I don't have faith in the current leadership of Israel to lead us to an event of this magnitude, of war with Iran," accused Netanyahu and Barak of making decisions "based on Messianic feelings," and warned that a strike could bring "a dramatic acceleration of the Iran program." Spokesmen for both men declined to comment. [cite:ir6_fox_diskin2012] When the 2015 nuclear deal was signed, two more former chiefs backed it against their own government's position: Ami Ayalon, former head of the Shin Bet, said "when it comes to Iran's nuclear capability, this is the best option," and Efraim Halevy, a former Mossad director, wrote that "in the Middle East, a decade is eternity," arguing for a real debate on whether no agreement was better than one. [cite:ir6_lobelog_2015] [cite:ir6_db_alter2015] After the United States left the deal, Ya'alon, Pardo and the former military chief Gadi Eisenkot called the withdrawal a mistake (Part Seven), and Amos Yadlin, a former head of military intelligence, said in November 2021 that "the Iranian issue was privatized to one person." Netanyahu's former national security adviser, Yaakov Amidror, answered on the same panel that the withdrawal was "correct, smart, and appropriate." [cite:ir6_toi_yadlin2021] [scene:ir7_withdrawal|The withdrawal]

The Mossad Paper of 2012

The most cited piece of dissent is not a speech but a document. Among intelligence files leaked to Al Jazeera and published with The Guardian on 23 February 2015, the "Spy Cables", was a Mossad assessment dated October 2012 and shared with South Africa's intelligence service. It said Iran "is not performing the activity necessary to produce weapons" and "does not appear to be ready to enrich" to higher levels. Netanyahu had told the UN a few weeks earlier that Iran could reach his red line by the following summer. [cite:hz11_rferl_spycables] [cite:hz11_wiki_spycables] The document has two readings. In one, the Mossad's analysts contradicted the prime minister. In the other, argued by The Times of Israel, the two were "hardly contradictory": the full passage described an Iran closing gaps toward a weapon, and Netanyahu's line concerned enriched material, not a finished bomb. [cite:hz11_toi_spycables] No public evidence shows whether Netanyahu read the paper before speaking. The Hezbollah volume sets the same documents beside the 2012 speech. [scene:hz11_pattern|The bomb that was about a year away]

The American Intelligence Record

US intelligence assessments ran on their own track throughout. The National Intelligence Estimate of December 2007 judged "with high confidence" that Iran halted its covert weapons programme in the autumn of 2003, and with moderate confidence that it had not restarted by mid-2007; the White House said the risk "remains a very serious problem." [cite:hz11_wh_nie2007] [cite:hz11_npr_nie2007] An update, reportedly finished in February 2011, did not overturn that judgment, and the Director of National Intelligence, James Clapper, told the Senate on 31 January 2012: "We continue to assess Iran is keeping open the option to develop nuclear weapons," but "we do not know, however, if Iran will eventually decide to build nuclear weapons." [cite:ir6_cfr_nie2011] [cite:ir6_aca_clapper2012] On 26 March 2025 Tulsi Gabbard, as Director of National Intelligence, testified that the intelligence community "continues to assess that Iran is not building a nuclear weapon" and that Khamenei "has not authorized the nuclear weapons program he suspended in 2003," while noting a stockpile "unprecedented for a state without nuclear weapons." [cite:hz11_bg_gabbard] [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] When President Trump said on 20 June 2025, "She's wrong," Gabbard wrote that her testimony had been taken out of context and that Iran could produce a weapon "within weeks to months, if they decide to finalize the assembly." [cite:ir6_ap_gabbard2025] At the annual threats hearing on 18 March 2026, three weeks into the second war, she declined to say whether the agencies had assessed an imminent nuclear threat: "the only person who can determine what is and is not an imminent threat is the president." The day before, Joe Kent, the administration's top counterterrorism official, had resigned, saying Iran posed no imminent threat. [cite:ir6_abc_gabbard2026] Gabbard herself, having defended the decision to go to war, resigned in May 2026, citing her husband's illness rather than the war (Part Fourteen). [cite:ir14_aje_gabbard] [scene:ir14_who|Who pushed]

Americans Who Resisted a Strike

In 2011 and 2012 the Obama administration argued against an Israeli attack in public. Panetta said on 2 December 2011 that a strike "at best... might postpone it maybe one, possibly two years," and that "of greater concern to me are the unintended consequences," including Iranian retaliation against American ships and bases. [cite:ir6_usip_panetta2011] President Obama told AIPAC on 4 March 2012 that he would use force if necessary but that "already, there is too much loose talk of war." [cite:ir6_wbur_obama2012] General Martin Dempsey, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, said in London on 30 August 2012 that an Israeli strike would "clearly delay but probably not destroy Iran's nuclear program" and that "I don't want to be complicit if they choose to do it"; Ya'alon replied that such remarks led Iran to believe it faced no real risk. [cite:ir6_csm_dempsey2012] Robert Gates, defence secretary until 2011, said on 4 October 2012 that a strike could "prove catastrophic, haunting us for generations" and "would make a nuclear-armed Iran inevitable." [cite:ir6_ynet_gates2012]

The dissent does not by itself prove the warnings false. Most dissenters agreed Iran was building the capacity to make a weapon, and the agencies that said it was not building one recorded its growing stockpile. It does show that the short timelines were contested inside Israel's own security establishment, often by those closest to the intelligence, and that Israeli and American officials alike argued against a strike years before the wars of 2025 and 2026 tested the question.
