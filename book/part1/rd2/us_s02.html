Congress Endorses Balfour: The Lodge–Fish Resolution

On 21 September 1922, President Warren Harding signed a resolution of Congress endorsing a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine. Official American support for a Jewish homeland is usually dated to President Truman's recognition of the new state in 1948. The first formal endorsement by the United States government came twenty-six years earlier. It was requested by American Zionist leaders, opposed by the State Department, and passed without dissent in both houses of Congress.

The initiative came from American Zionist leaders. They approached two Republicans, Senator Henry Cabot Lodge of Massachusetts and Representative Hamilton Fish III of New York, to introduce a resolution endorsing the Balfour Declaration of 1917. The Jewish Virtual Library credits a significant lobbying effort by the American Zionist community, and in particular the work of Rabbi Simon Glazer. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] The effort followed closely on the Keren Hayesod tour of 1921 and 1922 and on Nahum Sokolow's meeting with the President, described in [scene:us_s01|the account of the first American campaigns].

The resolution met objection. Both the State Department and the New York Times opposed it. It passed regardless: the House approved it on 30 June 1922, and the Senate followed by unanimous consent on 13 July. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] [cite:wjc_lodge_fish] The objection of the foreign-policy bureaucracy did not carry the day against a Congress that had been lobbied directly.

On 21 September Harding signed the measure as Public Resolution 67-73. Its text favored "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." Like the Balfour Declaration it echoed, it attached a condition: that nothing should be done to prejudice "the civil and religious rights of Christian and all other non-Jewish communities in Palestine." [cite:wiki_lodge_fish] [cite:jvl_lodge_fish] The condition is as much a part of the record as the endorsement. Congress, like the British government before it, endorsed a national home for one people while stating that the rights of the majority already living there were not to be prejudiced.

The resolution also mattered inside American Jewish life. The World Jewish Congress notes that it helped legitimize Zionism within American Jewry by giving it a "stamp of approval" from the non-Jewish world. [cite:wjc_lodge_fish] That carries weight in light of the previous year, when much of established American Jewry still kept its distance from the cause.

The hearings before the vote had also heard the other side. As noted in the discussion of legal authority, anti-Zionist Jewish witnesses and Palestinian Arab witnesses testified before Congress in 1922. Congress endorsed the declaration unanimously all the same. [cite:jps_1922_congress]

The Lodge–Fish Resolution thus fits the pattern of these years. A policy first stated in a British letter, then written into the League of Nations Mandate, now received the formal endorsement of the United States. It was won through organized advocacy, adopted over the objection of the country's own diplomats, and accompanied by a condition protecting the civil and religious rights of the non-Jewish communities.
