Alamut and the Brethren of Purity

In 1090 Hasan-i Sabbah captured the castle of Alamut, in the mountains of northern Persia, from the Sunni Seljuks; it served as the stronghold of the Nizari Ismailis, a branch of Shiʿi Islam, for nearly two centuries. [cite:sf_ng_alamut] [cite:sf_iis_legends] He took other fortresses by infiltration, bribery and violence, and he used guerrilla warfare, spying and targeted killings carried out by his devotees, the fedayeen. [cite:sf_ng_alamut] The killings were real. In 1092 a Nizari, disguised as a Sufi mystic, stabbed the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk, the same minister who had appointed al-Ghazali to his chair in Baghdad. [cite:sf_ng_alamut] [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] Al-Ghazali in turn wrote a polemic against the Nizaris. [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] The Nizari state collapsed in 1256, when the Mongol commander Hülegü had Alamut destroyed. [cite:sf_iis_legends] [cite:sf_ng_alamut]

The legends are another matter. The name "Assassins" was "originally derived under obscure circumstances from variants of the word hashish", and it later became the common noun for a murderer. [cite:sf_iis_legends] The tales of young men drugged with hashish and shown a false paradise garden to make them kill were spread by the Crusaders and their chroniclers and "reached their height in the popular version attributed to Marco Polo". [cite:sf_iis_legends] The historian Farhad Daftary concludes that these legends "were actually fabricated and put into circulation by Europeans", and calls them imaginative constructions of uninformed Western observers. [cite:sf_iis_legends] The Nizaris survived as Shiʿi minority communities; today they follow the Aga Khan as their imam. [cite:sf_iis_legends]

The Brethren of Purity, the Ikhwan al-Safa, left an encyclopedia and kept their names to themselves. Later writers named a judge of Basra and three friends as the authors, a claim now questioned; the work was probably compiled over several decades between the ninth and early tenth centuries. [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan] It consists of fifty-two epistles in four sections, covering mathematics, natural science, psychology and metaphysics, and it combines a numerology built on the Pythagorean idea that "existing beings correspond to the nature of number" with a Neoplatonic picture of the world emanating from God through Intellect and Soul, and with references to Hermetic writings. [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan] Its stated aim was salvation: "separating the soul from the ties of matter and purifying it". [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan] Some scholars see Ismaili teaching in it; others doubt it. [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan]

The epistles also had a political edge. One criticises a ruler who encouraged religious controversies; another describes the rise and fall of dynasties and predicts the decline of an evil one, read by one scholar as a forecast of the fall of the Abbasid caliphs; a third describes an ideal city. [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan] In 1150 the Abbasid caliph al-Mustanjid "judged the work heretical and sent the existing versions to be burnt". The encyclopedia survived anyway and was translated into Persian and Turkish; the theologian Ibn Taymiyya later condemned it. [cite:sf_sep_ikhwan]

The two stories show two ways in which esoteric knowledge was handled by its opponents. The Nizaris were defeated by the Mongols and then defined by their enemies' legends, so that their name became a word for murder. The Brethren's encyclopedia was condemned and burned by a caliph, and survived in translation. In both cases the record that has come down was shaped by those who held power over it, and the historian's work has been to separate the documented from the told.
