The Rumours, and Which of Them Hold

Scofield's life has been retold for a century by two camps with opposite needs. His admirers, starting with Trumbull in 1920, smoothed it into a story of a gallant soldier and lawyer redeemed by grace. [cite:ts4_trumbull] His critics, starting with Joseph M. Canfield's hostile The Incredible Scofield and His Book (1988), set out to show that the man behind the notes could not be trusted, and some later writers went further and made him the instrument of hidden financiers. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] [cite:ts4_sfiles] Both camps produced claims that the record does not support. This section takes the most repeated of them one by one and gives each a verdict: holds, partly holds, or fails. The test is the same in each case: what a document of the time shows, as against what a later writer asserted.

"A convicted forger who served a prison sentence"

This is the most common version online, and until this volume it was printed in this library's own account of the Scofield Bible ([scene:s05|Road to 1948]). It fails as stated. Scofield was the subject of two forgery complaints in 1877–78, was arrested in Wisconsin in October 1878 and held in the St. Louis jail for much of the following year, and on 6 November 1879 the prosecution entered a nolle prosequi, dropping the charges for lack of evidence. [cite:ts4_rushing] The Department of Justice history of the Kansas office confirms the jailing; it does not mention a conviction. [cite:ts4_doj_ks] There was no trial, no verdict and no sentence. The documented version is damaging enough: much of a year in jail on forgery charges, and a newspaper report that his sister paid off the notes. [cite:ts4_rushing] Even the research site Scofield-Files, which defends him against the funding stories, concludes that he "likely was guilty". [cite:ts4_sfiles] What fails is the word "convicted" and the prison term. Some reference works went the other way: Wikipedia's article still says there is "no extant evidence in the public records" of any jailing, which Rushing's newspaper and docket citations, and the Justice Department's own page, overtake. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_doj_ks] Verdict: jailed awaiting trial holds; convicted and imprisoned fails.

"He served time in a Canadian prison"

Canfield floated the possibility that Scofield had served a prison term in Canada. Scofield-Files reports that Canadian authorities have no record of it, and no document produced by Canfield or anyone since places Scofield in a Canadian prison. [cite:ts4_sfiles] Verdict: fails for want of any record.

"Twice divorced"

This library's earlier account, and the simulation scene it drew on, called him "twice divorced". The record shows one divorce: Leontine Cerré Scofield divorced him for desertion in 1883. His second marriage, to Hettie Hall von Wartz, lasted until his death. [cite:ts4_wiki_kellogg] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Verdict: fails. The underlying charge, that he abandoned a wife and two daughters and later wrote them out of his authorised life and his will, holds. [cite:ts4_wiki_kellogg] [cite:ts4_trumbull]

The war hero, and the deserter

Trumbull's claim that Scofield fought through the war in eighteen battles and won the Cross of Honor for bravery at Antietam fails against the Confederate records: he was discharged on 26 September 1862 after petitioning as an alien, and the Cross was awarded in 1903 by a Dallas veterans' society, not on the battlefield. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] The critics' counter-label, "deserter", partly holds: after his discharge he was ordered back to a Confederate camp, went instead to Union lines and took the federal oath. [cite:ts4_rushing] The same record places him there as a Michigan-born teenager who had asked to go home. Earlier statements in this library that he served "from 1861 to 1865" fail. Verdict: the heroic version fails; the desertion holds, with its context.

"Dr. Scofield"

From the early 1890s Scofield signed himself "Rev. C. I. Scofield, D.D.", and Trumbull calls him "Dr. Scofield" throughout while also stating that he had no formal academic education. No record shows any college granting him a doctorate, honorary or earned, and Wikipedia's article, citing the biographer John D. Hannah on his secrecy about his past, states that he almost certainly gave deliberately inaccurate information about himself to Who's Who and to Trumbull. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] [cite:ts4_trumbull] Verdict: the critics' point holds. It says something about his self-presentation; it says nothing about whether his notes read the texts correctly.

Kansas: bribes, embezzlement and forged notes

The Justice Department's own history states that he was "forced to resign ... due to questionable financial transactions", and the Kansas City Journal reported in 1899 that he left owing thousands to Atchison Republicans, including money collected for Ingalls. [cite:ts4_doj_ks] [cite:ts4_rushing] More specific charges, railway bribes and forged signatures on bank notes, appear in later biographies and remain allegations: none was prosecuted. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Verdict: a forced resignation over money holds; the specific crimes are unproven.

The Lotos Club and Samuel Untermyer

The most consequential story about Scofield is not about his past but about his patrons. It holds that Samuel Untermyer, a Jewish New York lawyer who became a prominent Zionist, sponsored Scofield's admission to New York's Lotos Club and lay behind the Reference Bible. Part of this is documented. Scofield did join New York's Lotos Club: its membership book enters him on 3 March 1900, proposed by Horatio Fraser and seconded by John Elderkin. Untermyer had been a member since 1894. [cite:ts4_sfiles] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Nothing else in the story is documented. Untermyer's name is not among Scofield's proposers. Under the club's rules a candidate was elected unless two negative ballots were cast, so a single existing member had no power to admit anyone. No letter, diary or record shows that the two men ever met. [cite:ts4_sfiles]

The link begins with Canfield. According to Scofield-Files, which cites a scan of the pages, Canfield asked on pp. 173–74 why "a godly man" would "join such a godless club", wrote "we assume" and "There must have been anticipation", and then named Untermyer as the likely sponsor. [cite:ts4_sfiles] That wording has not been checked against a copy of the book for this volume, but no one has produced a document behind the inference. Untermyer's own Zionist prominence came later: his best-known Zionist office, the presidency of the Keren Hayesod fund, began in 1921, the year Scofield died, and the standard reference entry on his life does not mention Scofield at all. [cite:ts4_sfiles] [cite:ts4_wiki_untermyer] From Canfield's assumption the story grew through internet lists (1999), websites (2001–02), and Michael Collins Piper's The High Priests of War (2004), the first to say the Rothschilds paid for the Bible, and on into books and viral posts. [cite:ts4_sfiles] A fact-check by Check Your Fact in March 2025 rated the Rothschild claim false, and the writer Mike Rothschild, who studies conspiracy theories, found no primary source for it at all. [cite:ts4_cyf] [cite:ts4_mrothschild] The documented backers of the Bible were Brethren-linked businessmen and an oil executive, named in [scene:ts5_funding|Part Five]. [cite:ts4_goeman] [cite:ts4_cyf] [cite:ts4_sfiles]

The story fails for a second reason as well. Its shape, a Jewish financier secretly steering what Christians believe, is the shape of one of the oldest antisemitic accusations, and its Rothschild form leans on publications that the writers who traced it describe as antisemitic. [cite:ts4_mrothschild] [cite:ts4_goeman] That is not why it is rejected here: it is rejected because there is no evidence for it. It is named because a reader meets it constantly, and because its persistence is part of the history this volume records. Verdict: shared club membership holds; Untermyer's sponsorship, contact and money fail.

What the man's record can and cannot prove

Critics have used the record to argue that Scofield's Bible was a confidence trick, and admirers have answered that his conversion wiped the slate clean. Scofield's own stated account, through Trumbull, was that grace changed him in 1879, and the documented record after that year contains no further charges. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_rushing] Neither argument reaches the question that matters for this volume. Whether a pre-tribulation rapture or a future national restoration of Israel is in the text of the Bible is a question for the text and its readers; it was argued before Scofield, by Darby and the restorationists ([scene:ts3_darby|Part Three], [scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two]), and it is argued on its merits today. The man's record explains how he presented himself, which is relevant to how the Reference Bible was sold as the work of a learned "Dr." It does not decide whether the notes are right, and it does not make him the author of a movement that was already in motion. The volume's own ledger of these and the wider myths is in [scene:ts14_myths|Part Fourteen].
