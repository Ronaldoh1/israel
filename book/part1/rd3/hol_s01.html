How a Democracy Fell: Weimar's Collapse

In October 1929 the Wall Street Crash struck, and its effects soon reached Germany. By 1933 the Weimar Republic, as Germany's democracy was known, had come apart. Its fall is the background to much of what follows in this part of the book. It is often told as a story of inevitability, in which Hitler rode a wave of popular fanaticism that no one could have stopped. The record tells a different story: a dated sequence of economic collapse, elite miscalculation and a legislature that voted away its own authority. Several of its steps were decisions made by people who believed they were in control.

The first blow came from outside. Germany's recovery in the mid-1920s had rested heavily on a steady flow of American loans. The crash triggered the recall of those loans and plunged the country back into economic catastrophe and mass unemployment [cite:history_weimar_republic]. The timing matters. According to the Holocaust Encyclopedia, genuine popular support for Hitler and the Nazi Party began to build from late 1929 onward, not earlier, while the economy still functioned [cite:ushmm_nazi_rise_to_power]. A recovery built on borrowed money was only as stable as the lenders' willingness to keep lending. Yet hardship alone does not explain what followed. Millions of Germans faced the same conditions and did not become Nazis, and the decisive steps that came next were political choices.

The second point is often left out. The Nazi Party's electoral high point came in July 1932. By November 1932, three months before Hitler became Chancellor, its vote share had fallen to roughly 33 percent [cite:historycollection_weimar_collapse]. Hitler did not take power at the crest of unstoppable momentum. Historians consider it a real possibility that, given more time, the Republic might have weathered this particular storm. That cannot be proven. But the decline is documented, and it undercuts any story of irresistible popular will.

What actually installed Hitler was a bargain among a few powerful men. Franz von Papen, the aristocratic former chancellor, brokered the arrangement that made Hitler Chancellor. He reportedly assured associates that he had Hitler's measure and could control him from within the cabinet, using him as what historians call a "battering ram" against the political left [cite:historycollection_weimar_collapse] [cite:britannica_third_reich]. This was not a popular revolution. It was a calculation by conservative politicians who thought they were the clever ones. The record shows a catastrophic misjudgment about whether Hitler could be controlled; it does not show that Papen or his circle intended or foresaw what came after.

Hitler was appointed Chancellor on January 30, 1933. In February 1933 a fire at the Reichstag, the German parliament, gave the new regime its emergency. The Reichstag Fire Decree that followed suspended civil liberties and opened the way to mass arrests of political opponents [cite:history_weimar_republic]. Historians still debate who set the fire. Its political use is not in doubt: it cleared the path for the next legal step, weeks later.

That step was the Enabling Act of March 1933, which allowed Hitler to pass laws without the approval of Parliament or the President. To secure its passage, Hitler forcibly prevented Communist members of Parliament from voting [cite:history_weimar_republic] [cite:britannica_third_reich]. With its enactment, the Weimar Republic's democratic experiment ended. No coup in the streets ended it. A legislature, under coercion and in the climate of intimidation that followed the fire decree, voted away its own constitutional authority. Not every member who voted for it can be assumed to have done so freely.

Put plainly, the record does not show a nation swept away by fanaticism. It shows a specific sequence: a real economic catastrophe; a genuine but already declining wave of popular support; a small group of powerful men who chose to empower Hitler, believing they could control him; then a fire, an emergency decree, and a parliament that voted itself out of existence. On that foundation the new regime built a legal architecture of exclusion, the subject of [scene:hol_s02|the Nuremberg Laws].
