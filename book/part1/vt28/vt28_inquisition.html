Building the Inquisition, 1184–1330

The Albigensian Crusade conquered the south but did not end the Good Men; they survived in households, villages and mountain refuges for almost a century more. What ended them was a legal procedure, assembled step by step by popes, councils and kings between 1184 and 1252, and run by named friars with notaries and registers. It is usually called "the Inquisition", but in this period it was not one office. It was a set of commissions given by the pope to individual judges, mostly Dominican and Franciscan friars, working with bishops and relying on lay rulers to carry out the gravest sentences. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] It is a different institution from the Spanish Inquisition of 1478 and from the Roman Inquisition founded in 1542, which passed through later names to today's Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith.

It was not the first time a Christian ruler had acted against a dissenting group by law. Constantine's edict against the Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites and others, preserved by Eusebius, had banned their meetings, given their buildings to the catholic church and ordered their books sought out, nine centuries earlier. [cite:vt22_vc3_64] [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Part Twenty-One] What was new in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries was a standing procedure that searched for dissent rather than waiting for it to be reported.

From the bishop's visit to the papal judge

The first step was Ad abolendam, issued by Pope Lucius III at Verona on 4 November 1184, after a meeting with the emperor Frederick I. It condemned by name the Cathars, the Humiliati, the Poor of Lyon (Waldensians), the Arnoldists and others. It told every bishop to visit his diocese once or twice a year, put residents on oath to say what they knew of heretics, and treat anyone who broke that oath as a heretic. Those who could not clear themselves, would not recant or relapsed were to be handed to the lay power for "due punishment" (animadversio debita); their protectors lost public office, the right to go to law and the right to make a will. [cite:vt28_wiki_adabolendam] In 1199 Innocent III's Vergentis in senium classed heresy with treason, a crime against majesty punishable by death. [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda]

The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 made the arrangement general. Its third canon ordered that condemned heretics be "handed over to the secular rulers" for punishment, that the property of lay heretics be confiscated, and that rulers "publicly take an oath" to exterminate the heretics in their lands. [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] In 1229, as the war ended, a council at Toulouse ordered a search of every parish for heretics, the destruction of houses where they were found, and a ban on lay people owning the books of the Old and New Testaments; only a psalter, breviary or Book of Hours was allowed, and "we most strictly forbid their having any translation of these books". [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] The Good Men had read the Gospels aloud in Occitan; the council took vernacular scripture out of lay hands in the region where they had done so. [cite:vt28_gui_fordham]

Gregory IX's inquisitors

Gregory IX turned the bishops' duty into a papal commission. His constitution Excommunicamus of 1231 set the penalties: death by burning for heretics who persisted, prison or canonical penances such as fasting and pilgrimage for those who repented, and excommunication for Catholics who helped heretics. [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] The same year he began naming papal "inquisitors of heretical depravity", mostly Dominican and Franciscan friars, for France, Italy and parts of Germany, and on 20 April 1233 he set up the tribunal for the kingdom of France. Its judges opened cases on their own initiative and sought out suspects themselves, without waiting for an accuser. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] The Church did not itself execute anyone; it handed the condemned to the secular arm, which was obliged to burn them. [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] Historians still weigh the design differently: Thomas Madden reads it as an attempt to bring due process and investigation to a field ruled by mobs and lords, while Walter Ullmann called the procedure "the denial of justice or a hideous caricature of it". [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9]

The new machinery reached beyond Christian dissenters. In 1239 Gregory ordered all copies of the Talmud confiscated, and in Paris in June 1242 a mass burning of Jewish manuscripts followed. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] That was an act of the papacy and the French crown against Jewish books, not a "Jewish" anything; it belongs to the same years and the same logic of seizing texts, and Part Thirty returns to the Church's control of books. [scene:vt30_forbidden_arts|The forbidden arts]

Torture by papal authority

On 6 April 1252 Cathar sympathisers murdered Peter of Verona, papal inquisitor in Lombardy. On 15 May 1252 Innocent IV issued Ad extirpanda to the rulers and cities of Lombardy, Romagna and the March of Treviso. It authorised the use of torture to extract confessions, provided it did not cause loss of life or limb, and required the city authorities to carry out the inquisitors' sentences on pain of excommunication and interdict: the convicted were to be delivered in chains within five days. Heretics' houses were to be razed and their confiscated property split in three, a third to the city, a third to the officials who handled the cases, and a third to the bishop and inquisitors to "promote the faith and extirpate heretics". [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda] Judicial torture was already in use in secular courts; the bull's significance is that a pope authorised it for heresy trials and tied the inquisitors' finances to the property of the condemned. [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda]

The registers

The inquisitors wrote everything down, and their registers are now the main source for the people they prosecuted. The mass burnings came early: on 13 May 1239 some 183 people were burned in Champagne. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] The later work was slower and more bureaucratic. Bernard Gui (1261–1331), inquisitor at Toulouse and author of the best known inquisitors' manual, left a book of sentences that the historian Julien Théry has analysed: 916 decisions concerning 636 people or communities. About 45 people, roughly 6 per cent of the decisions and mostly relapsed heretics, were burned; nearly 30 per cent ended in release or dismissal; more than half imposed prison or penance, often the yellow crosses that marked a repentant heretic. [cite:vt28_mednet_gui] The record is grim, and it is also not the film version of The Name of the Rose: Gui burned far fewer people than his reputation in fiction suggests, and far more than none.

The last Good Men of Languedoc were tracked through the mountains. A revival led by the brothers Pierre and Jacques Autier ended with Pierre Autier's execution at Toulouse in April 1310. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Jacques Fournier, bishop of Pamiers from 1317, ran his own inquisition from 1318 to 1325; by Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's count he conducted 578 interrogations over 370 working days, and five people were burned. His register, taken to Rome when he became Pope Benedict XII in 1334, is in the Vatican Library, and it is the source of Le Roy Ladurie's Montaillou (1975), the portrait of a Pyrenean village drawn from its inhabitants' own words under questioning. [cite:vt28_wiki_fournier] Guillaume Bélibaste, a pupil of the Autiers, was betrayed by Arnaud Sicre, a spy working for the inquisition, and burned at Villerouge-Termenès in 1321. He is remembered as the last Cathar perfect of Languedoc, though that is tradition rather than a demonstrable fact. [cite:vt28_wiki_belibaste]

What the Holy See says now

The Holy See has not repudiated the individual bulls, but it has repented of their method in general terms. At the Day of Pardon in St Peter's on 12 March 2000, Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, prefect of the congregation that descends from the Roman Inquisition, confessed that "even men of the church, in the name of faith and morals, have sometimes used methods not in keeping with the Gospel". [cite:vt28_america_2000] In a letter of 15 June 2004 presenting the proceedings of a 1998 Vatican symposium on the Inquisition, John Paul II repeated the Church's repentance for "intolerance and even the use of violence" in the service of truth, and insisted that such judgements must follow "exact knowledge of the facts" established by historians. [cite:vt28_va_jp2_2004] Neither text names the Cathars, Béziers or Montségur.
