The Jewish National Fund: Land That Would Not Be Resold

The idea of a national land fund was older than the institution that carried it out. Hermann Schapira proposed one at the First Zionist Congress in 1897. He died in 1898 without seeing it established, and over the following Congresses the proposal was debated and deferred again and again. At the Fifth Zionist Congress in Basel, Herzl pressed the delegates to act: "After striving for so many years to set up the fund, we do not want to disperse again without having done anything." The motion passed, and on December 29, 1901, the Jewish National Fund was founded.[cite:jnf_wiki] The delay appears to have reflected ordinary institutional deferral and competing priorities rather than organized opposition to the fund's purpose. Within a month Johann (Yona) Kremenetzky, based in Vienna, was appointed its first director.

The founding resolution was absolute in its terms:

"The JNF shall be the eternal possession of the Jewish people. Its funds shall not be used except for the purchase of lands in Palestine and Syria."

From this followed the Fund's defining rule. Its land was not to be resold. It was leased to settlers on long terms instead, which removed it permanently from the ordinary market.[cite:jnf_wiki] Most land changes hands repeatedly over time; this rule ensured that once a parcel entered the Fund, no later transaction could take it out. It was a deliberate structural choice, not an incidental practice, and it explains why the Fund's holdings only ever grew. Permanent, inalienable land trusts exist in other settings, so the device was not unique in kind. What matters here is that it was written into the institution from the start.

The money came from small donors, and the instrument that collected it began as one person's suggestion. Haim Kleinman, a bank clerk in Nadvorna, Galicia, placed a blue box labeled "Keren Le'umit" in his own office and wrote to the movement's newspaper, Die Welt, proposing the idea more widely. Kremenetzky adopted it, and the first mass-produced boxes were distributed around 1904. By the interwar period roughly one million blue-and-white collection boxes sat in Jewish homes around the world.[cite:wiki_blue_box_jnf] The box gave physical form to the small-donor model the Jewish Colonial Trust had pioneered. The scale of its adoption is documented; what each donor understood or intended is not something the record can show. Kleinman himself visited Mandate Palestine in the 1930s and planned to settle there, but he was killed in the Holocaust before he could.

A familiar account holds that Zionist land purchases before 1948 were sporadic and small. The Fund's own growth over forty-seven years tells another story. Its first parcel, 200 dunams east of Hadera in 1903, was a gift from Isaac Leib Goldberg, a Russian Zionist leader from Vilnius, and later became an olive grove.[cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg] From there the totals climbed steadily:

by 1920, 22,363 dunams; by 1935, roughly 89,500 acres, or 362 square kilometers, across 108 communities; by 1940, 516,000 dunams; and by 1947–48, approximately 936,000 dunams.[cite:jnf_wiki]

That final figure was more than half of all Jewish-held land at the moment of statehood. Total Jewish holdings then came to roughly 2 million dunams, about 7 percent of Mandate Palestine's roughly 26 million dunams.[cite:jnf_wiki] Both readings of these numbers have to be held together. Measured against the whole country, Jewish ownership remained a small share. Measured as an institution, the Fund was not sporadic at all. It operated continuously for nearly half a century, and because of the leasing rule, every acquisition was permanent.

Not every dunam in the larger total came through the same channel, and the land question involved more than purchase alone: who sold, who farmed the land, and what happened to tenants. Those questions are examined in [scene:s04j|the land purchase mechanism]. What the founding of the Fund established in 1901 was the engine itself: a single-purpose institution, funded by a mass of small contributions, holding land in perpetuity on behalf of a people rather than any individual owner.
