The Word as a Shield

The starting point should be stated plainly. Hatred of Jewish people is antisemitism, and nothing in this book says otherwise. A question about what a government does is a question about a government, whether the government is Canada's, Mexico's or Israel's. The difficulty is that a widely repeated claim, that questioning Israel is itself antisemitic, merges these two things. The meaning of the word has not changed: antisemitism means hatred of Jews. What has changed, over roughly the past decade, is where the charge is aimed, and what it now costs the person it is aimed at. This section sets out the definitions in competition, the definition's own drafter's warning, the path by which a guideline became law, and what is also true on the other side: antisemitism is real, and in the United States it stands at a record level.

The most widely cited definition is the working definition of the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance (IHRA), adopted in 2016. Beyond its core sentence, it attaches eleven examples, and seven of them concern Israel. [cite:reformjudaism_five_definitions] [cite:peacenow_ihra_faq] Among them are "denying the Jewish people their right to self-determination, e.g., by claiming that the existence of a State of Israel is a racist endeavor," applying double standards to Israel, and comparing Israeli policy to that of the Nazis. The first of these is listed as a potential example of antisemitism. [cite:reformjudaism_five_definitions] The definition was adopted by the United States government and, as of August 2024, by 35 states and the District of Columbia. [cite:peacenow_ihra_faq]

The definition also contains a safeguard that is rarely quoted. Its own text says that criticism of Israel similar to that leveled against any other country cannot be regarded as antisemitic. [cite:ihra_text_safeguard] That is the standard the definition sets for itself: the questions a person may ask of Canada or Mexico may be asked of Israel. What is disputed is the territory the attached examples cover, the point at which criticism of Israel crosses into hostility to Jews. The history of how the word came to be pointed at that territory is told in [scene:s00b|the section on "Semitic"].

The strongest case for IHRA, in its supporters' terms, runs as follows. Some hostility to Jews does express itself through talk about Israel: holding Jews collectively responsible for a state, singling Israel out by standards applied to no other country, or comparing Jews to their Nazi persecutors. A definition that ignored those forms would miss antisemitism where it actually appears. The examples are framed as potential cases, judged against a text that explicitly protects ordinary criticism. [cite:ihra_text_safeguard] The definition was written, according to its lead drafter, to help governments collect data on antisemitism, which requires a common yardstick. [cite:stern_guardian_2019] And on this view the main alternative goes too far the other way: Britain's Community Security Trust argues that the Jerusalem Declaration legitimizes calls for Israel's elimination. [cite:cst_jda_critique]

That alternative, the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism, was presented in March 2021 by scholars working with the Van Leer Jerusalem Institute and signed by more than 200 scholars of Jewish history and the Holocaust. [cite:jwa_jda] Its signatories and drafters are specialists in antisemitism, in Holocaust and genocide studies, and in Jewish history. By its own framing they are not activists opposed to fighting antisemitism, but scholars who judged that IHRA's examples conflate two distinct things: hatred of Jews, and disagreement with a state's policies or the ideology behind it. [cite:jda_text] Its definition of antisemitism, discrimination, prejudice, hostility or violence against Jews as Jews, or against Jewish institutions as Jewish, is close in spirit to IHRA's core sentence. [cite:jda_text] The divergence lies entirely in the guidelines that follow. Guideline 5 states that opposing Zionism as a form of nationalism, or arguing for constitutional arrangements other than a Jewish-majority state for everyone between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean (a binational state, a unitary democratic state, a federal state or a confederation), is not, on the face of it, antisemitic. [cite:jda_text] The Declaration treats Zionism as one variety of national ideology, and holds that criticizing or opposing it does not on its own constitute antisemitism. But it also lists examples of speech about Israel that are antisemitic. [cite:jwa_jda]

The Declaration has its own critics, who argue that it understates the antisemitism present in some anti-Zionist discourse. Further named critics are set out in the section on "Semitic." Between the two documents sits a third, the Nexus Document, produced by a task force linked to the University of Southern California and Bard College, which takes an intermediate position. [cite:thirdnarrative_three_defs] The question "is anti-Zionism antisemitism?" therefore has no single answer, because the three definitions give three different answers. Each is held by credentialed people, in print.

The sharpest criticism of how IHRA is used comes from the man who led its drafting. Kenneth Stern was the American Jewish Committee's expert on antisemitism and the definition's lead drafter. He has testified that it "was not drafted, and was never intended, as a tool to target or chill speech on a college campus"; it was written to help governments collect data. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] In December 2019 he published an op-ed in the Guardian under the headline "I drafted the definition of antisemitism. Rightwing Jews are weaponizing it," calling the executive order that applied the definition to campuses an attack on academic freedom that would harm pro-Palestinian advocates and Jewish students alike. [cite:stern_guardian_2019] In 2024 he told NPR that weaponizing the definition makes students less safe. [cite:npr_stern_2024] Americans for Peace Now makes a parallel argument: that IHRA chills speech by treating anti-Zionist views as antisemitic. [cite:peacenow_ihra_faq]

The executive order Stern objected to marked the definition's move from guideline into law. In December 2019 it directed federal agencies, including the Education Department's civil rights office, to consider the definition when enforcing Title VI of the Civil Rights Act. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] By August 2024, 35 states and the District of Columbia had adopted the definition in some form. On 1 May 2024 the House of Representatives passed the Antisemitism Awareness Act, which would write the definition into federal civil rights enforcement, by 320 votes to 91, with members of both parties voting no. The American Civil Liberties Union urged a no vote, warning that the bill would likely chill student speech by wrongly equating criticism of the Israeli government with antisemitism. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] Status as of October 2026: the bill is not law, and its Senate version, S.558, stalled in committee in 2025. [cite:legiscan_s558]

The same logic reached private platforms. In July 2024, after an advocacy campaign, Meta began treating the word "Zionist" as a stand-in for Jews in narrowly defined hate-speech cases. The policy does not cite IHRA by name. The argument against it is that the mechanism it applies, treating the name of a political ideology as a proxy for a protected ethnic or religious identity, is exactly the conflation the Jerusalem Declaration had been written three years earlier to name and reject. The stated objective of Cotler-Wunsh, whose part in the campaign is set out with the platforms, was protected-characteristic status for Zionism: the precise claim the Declaration's Guideline 5 runs against. [cite:ihra_text] [cite:jda_text] Whatever one concludes about the policy, a platform that adopted one side of a live dispute among credentialed scholars was not simply following expert consensus, because no consensus existed. The full record of the Meta decision, including who pressed for it and who objected, is in [scene:s00a2|the section on the platforms].

A label works by raising the price of a question, and the price has been documented. In 2025 Columbia University lost $400 million in federal funding within four days of a notice from a federal task force, and about 60 more universities received warning letters. Five days after that notice Mahmoud Khalil, a recent Columbia graduate who had negotiated on behalf of the campus protesters, was detained for deportation. Each case has its own record, and that record includes real failures at Columbia: federal investigators found that the university had no reporting system until the summer of 2024, and that swastika vandalism had gone uninvestigated. Taken together, the cases show why many people choose not to ask.

This is the chilling effect that the ACLU and the definition's own drafter warned about. [cite:politifact_aaa_2024] [cite:stern_guardian_2019] Being called antisemitic is a serious charge because antisemitism is a serious hatred, and that seriousness is exactly what gives the label its force when it is aimed at a question about a government. The person asking risks a job, a place at a university, a visa or an online account. Many stop asking, and the version of events they learned in school goes unexamined. The effect is documented as an institutional one, working through rules and penalties. The record does not measure what happens in any individual's mind, and this book makes no claim about that.

What is also true is that antisemitism is real and rising. The Anti-Defamation League counted 9,354 antisemitic incidents in the United States in 2024, the most since it began counting in 1979, with incidents on campuses up 84 percent. [cite:algemeiner_adl_2024] Some speech that calls itself anti-Zionist does cross into hatred of Jews, and even the Jerusalem Declaration, written to defend criticism of Israel, lists examples of it. [cite:jwa_jda] The long history of that hatred, older than the word itself, is told in [scene:ch01_hatred|the section on the hatred]. At the same time, many of the strongest critics of Israeli policy are themselves Jewish. Misusing the charge and ignoring real antisemitism both do damage, to Jews and to everyone asking honest questions.

A common test case is the slogan "Free Palestine," which is often described in public comment as simply code for antisemitism. That claim is an opinion, not a fact about the phrase, and the record of who uses the slogan contradicts it. Jewish organizations such as Jewish Voice for Peace campaign for Palestinian rights; [cite:w_jvp] Orthodox anti-Zionist traditions predate the state; and Israeli human-rights groups such as B'Tselem document the occupation. Some people who use the slogan do express antisemitism, and that is documented too; many do not. The record supports judging speech by its content, not by its slogan.

That leads to the test. Two questions sort almost every case. The first: is the speech aimed at Jewish people, or at what a government does? The second is the definition's own standard: would the criticism be fair if it were aimed at any other country? [cite:ihra_text_safeguard] If it concerns a government's actions, and the same would be said of Canada or Mexico, it is a political question, and asking it is not hatred. If it concerns Jews as a people, holds them responsible for a state, or recycles old tropes, it is antisemitism, whatever it calls itself.

Several things remain unsettled. The record does not decide which of the three definitions is correct; that is a live dispute among credentialed scholars, and the answer to "is anti-Zionism antisemitic?" depends on which one is adopted. It does not measure how many people have stayed silent, or why. Whether the Antisemitism Awareness Act, or a successor, will become law is open as of October 2026. And the sources used here do not record how Mahmoud Khalil's case was resolved.
