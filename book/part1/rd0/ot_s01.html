The Land Code of 1858: How the Deeds Became Sellable

A familiar account of the early Zionist land purchases holds that Jewish buyers acquired empty or unused land in Palestine from willing local owners. That account begins at the moment of sale. The legal history begins earlier, in a set of Ottoman laws of 1856 to 1867 that decided who could hold a deed, whose name went on it, and who could buy it. Those laws did not concern Zionism, which did not yet exist. They concerned revenue, conscription and the control of foreign purchasers. But they produced the documents through which land in Palestine would later change hands, and they explain how a sale could be perfectly lawful between owner and buyer and still pass over the heads of the people who farmed the land.

The starting point is a prohibition. According to a reference summary drawing on work by Ruth Kark published in 1984, foreigners were officially barred from buying land in Palestine from 1856, and the ban held in practice until 1867. [cite:rs_rd0_landpurchase_wiki] For a decade, then, the question of who could own land was settled against outsiders, whatever the paper said about who owned it inside the country.

Inside the country the paper itself was about to change. In 1858, as part of the reforms known as the Tanzimat, the Ottoman state issued a Land Code, the Arazi Kanunnamesi. It classified land into categories and required titles to be registered individually. The purpose was fiscal and military: registration would secure tax revenue and the conscription rolls. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] The same year brought the Tabu law, the law of title deeds. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] The two can be read as giving practical form to promises made in the Edict of Gülhane of 1839, which had pledged security of property, a regular tax system and regular conscription, as the [scene:rd0_tanzimat|previous section] describes. A state that wished to tax and conscript its subjects needed a register of what they held.

In Palestine the register opened a gap between who owned land on paper and who worked it. Many cultivators did not register the land they farmed. A reference summary, citing Nasser Abufarha, gives the reason: registration brought taxes and conscription, and cultivators resisted it for that reason. [cite:rs_rd0_landpurchase_wiki] Title was frequently recorded instead in other names: village heads, urban merchants or notables, and absentee families living in Beirut and Damascus. The fellahin, the peasant cultivators, went on working the same fields, in practice as tenants. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] Nothing on the ground had to change for this to happen. The village continued to plough and harvest. What changed was the document, and the document now named someone else.

Historians have described the result in strong terms. The same reference summary quotes Yehoshua Porath, writing in 1977, that the 1858 code "brought about the appropriation by the influential and rich families of Beirut, Damascus" and other cities of vast tracts. [cite:rs_rd0_landpurchase_wiki] Kark, in a study published in 2017, finds that land came to be concentrated in "families of urban effendis (absentee landlords)" who lived in cities in Lebanon, Syria, Egypt and Palestine. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] The two readings agree on the shape of what happened: title flowed to urban families, often living outside the district, while cultivation stayed in the villages.

The third law opened the door to outside buyers. In 1867 the Ottoman state issued a law allowing foreigners to acquire real property. Its Article II, as quoted by the United States minister at Constantinople in a dispatch of 5 January 1898, placed foreign landowners "upon terms of equality with Ottoman subjects in all things that concern their landed property." The United States accepted the law by protocol in 1874. [cite:rs_rd0_frus1898_law1867] The barrier of 1856 was gone, and a foreign buyer stood, on paper, where an Ottoman subject stood.

Kark treats the three measures, the 1858 Land Code, the 1858 Tabu law and the 1867 law on foreigners, as one process of privatization, the process that began land survey, registration and mapping in nineteenth-century Palestine. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] In her account, effendi ownership made it easier for Jewish immigrants to buy part of this land. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] She also counts what the purchases built on. According to Kark, more than 52 estate buildings became the first core of Jewish agricultural settlements, and at least 15 became the core of German Christian settlements or religious institutions. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] Those numbers matter for the claim at the head of this section. The estates were not empty ground. They were properties with buildings on them, held by owners who could sell, and they were bought by more than one kind of newcomer: German Christians as well as Jews.

That finding also adjusts the timing in the familiar account. The deeds did not wait for the British Mandate to become saleable. By Kark's count, estates bought under Ottoman rule were already the cores of the first Jewish agricultural settlements. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] The settlements of the First Aliyah, described in [scene:rd0_aliyah|the section on the First Aliyah], were built on land that this legal sequence had made purchasable.

Historians characterize the 1867 law differently, and the difference is real. A thesis written at Marmara University in Turkey on land ownership by foreigners and Jews in late Ottoman Palestine calls the 1867 law, the "Safer Kanunu," an attempt to bring foreigners' purchases under legal control. On that reading the law was meant to regulate, and Jews used gaps in it to buy fertile, strategic land on which the first settlements were later built. [cite:rs_rd0_marmara_thesis] The strongest form of this case is that the Ottoman state did try to keep control of land sales to outsiders, and that the purchases succeeded where the law fell short. Kark's reading is structural. The 1858–1867 laws together concentrated land in effendi hands, and that concentration made both Jewish and German Christian purchases easier. [cite:rs_rd0_kark_2017] The strongest form of her case is that the sales followed from who held the deeds, a structure the laws themselves produced. The first reading places the weight on the buyers' use of the law; the second on the structure of ownership the law produced. The sources do not settle which is right, and the two are not entirely exclusive.

The consequence reached its largest scale after this chapter's period. Under the British Mandate the registered titles were the instruments that could be sold. The largest single transfer to Zionist purchasing companies was the sale of the Beirut-based Sursock family's holdings in the Jezreel Valley in the early 1920s. The land was populated and cultivated, and after the sale its tenant cultivators were evicted, with compensation on terms set by the purchaser. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] Who bought, from whom and at what cost is told in [scene:s04j|the account of the Mandate-era land purchases].

The finding should be stated at its proper size. The 1858 code did not cause the Zionist project. It created a documentary form: a registered deed held in the name of someone who did not farm the land. Through that form, populated land could be lawfully transferred over the heads of the people living on it. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] That is why the phrase "willing local owners" is both accurate and incomplete. The owner on the register might be willing. The family in the field was often not on the register at all, having stayed off it to avoid taxation and conscription.

The record leaves important questions open. The sources here give no figure for how much land in Palestine went unregistered or was recorded in absentee names, and none for how many cultivators stood in the gap between title and tillage. Kark's counts of more than 52 and at least 15 estate buildings are not broken down here by date or place. The Marmara thesis is known only through its abstract, and the 1856 ban and the Porath and Abufarha passages only through a reference summary. Nor do these sources say how often, before 1896, a sale under these laws displaced the people who worked the land; for that period the documented consequence is the settlements themselves, and the evictions on record belong to the 1920s.
