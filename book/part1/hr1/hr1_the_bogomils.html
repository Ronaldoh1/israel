The Bogomils

The Bogomils arose in Bulgaria around the middle of the tenth century and took their name from a priest called Bogomil. Britannica describes the movement as combining dualist doctrines "imported especially from the Paulicians", an older dualist sect, with a local Slavonic reform effort. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] They held that the visible, material world was made by the devil. They rejected the incarnation, baptism, the Eucharist and the organisation of the Orthodox Church, and their moral code condemned marriage, eating meat and drinking wine. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils]

The teaching struck at the Church on two fronts. If the material world belonged to the devil, then the Church's sacraments, which used water, bread and wine, were worthless, and its hierarchy, which ruled through them, had no claim on the soul. Bulgarian authorities held church councils to condemn the Bogomils. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] The movement spread nonetheless through many Byzantine provinces in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. Around 1100 prominent Bogomils in Constantinople were tried and imprisoned, and their leader, Basil, was publicly burned. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils]

In the second half of the twelfth century the movement spread west. By the early thirteenth century, Britannica records, the dualist communities of the east, Paulicians and Bogomils, and of the west, the Cathars, formed a network stretching from the Black Sea to the Atlantic. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] Bogomilism remained strong in Bulgaria until the late fourteenth century and faded after the Ottoman conquest of southeastern Europe in the fifteenth, surviving only in traces of South Slavic folklore. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils]

The Bogomils set the pattern that the rest of this book follows. A movement that rejected the Church's sacraments rejected, by the same act, the Church's power to dispense salvation, and the Church responded as an institution defends its monopoly: councils to define the error, trials to identify its holders, and fire for those who would not recant. The theology was dualist; the conflict was about who held the keys.
