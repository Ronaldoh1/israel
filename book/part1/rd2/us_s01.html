The First American Campaigns: Weizmann, Einstein and the Fight Over the Money

The Mandate's text was settled in London and Geneva, but much of the money and some of the political backing for the national home were being sought in the United States. American support for a Jewish homeland is usually dated to the years after the Second World War, as a response to the Holocaust. The organized American campaign began a generation earlier. By the spring of 1921 the Zionist movement was running national fundraising tours across the United States, and by January 1922 one of its leaders was sitting with the President. It was also, in those same months, fighting an internal battle over who would control the money it raised. American Jewish opinion was not yet its own: much of the established community still kept its distance.

The United States had figured in Zionist and British calculations from the start. Among the motives the British War Cabinet weighed in 1917, when it approved the Balfour Declaration, was the hope of winning sympathy in Russian and American Jewish communities, to help keep Russia in the war and bring America into it. [cite:cohen_palestine_great_powers] What changed after the war was that the movement now needed money on a new scale. At a London conference on 24 December 1920 the Zionist Organization founded the Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund, as its central and permanent fundraising arm, open to non-Zionist donors as well, with an original five-year target of £25,000,000. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] The fund's structure and what it went on to finance are told in [scene:s04i|the account of the Keren Hayesod]. America was where a large part of that money would have to come from.

In March 1921 Chaim Weizmann invited Albert Einstein to accompany him to the United States. The two toured the country for two months that spring, raising money for the new Keren Hayesod and for the Hebrew University. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro] [cite:hnn_isaacson_einstein] The movement was sending the world's most famous scientist on a national fundraising tour. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252]

The tour also brought into the open a struggle over control. The Einstein Papers document it. Weizmann wanted the Keren Hayesod centralized under the Zionist Executive. The American faction led by Supreme Court Justice Louis Brandeis and Judge Julian Mack wanted American control of the money and businesslike management of how it was spent. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc168] [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro] At stake was who would decide how American donations were spent: the world movement's executive, or the American organization that raised them. The sources record each side's position; they do not set out the full arguments each made for it.

The dispute came to a head at the 1921 convention of the Zionist Organization of America in Cleveland. Weizmann's side won, and the Brandeis group withdrew from the leadership of American Zionism. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc168] [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro] Weizmann's centralized model for the fund had prevailed.

The campaign did not stop with the split. In November 1921 a second delegation arrived for a Keren Hayesod tour: Nahum Sokolow, who with Weizmann had drafted the Balfour Declaration, Otto Warburg and Jabotinsky. [cite:balfour_letter] Their tour raised nearly $5 million. On 13 January 1922 Sokolow met President Warren Harding for nearly an hour. [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] Within months, Congress would take up a resolution endorsing the Balfour Declaration, the subject of [scene:us_s02|the next section].

None of this meant that American Jewry as a whole had embraced Zionism. Much of the established community remained non-Zionist. Paul Warburg avoided the cause, and when Einstein invited about fifty non-Zionist notables to take part, few responded. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252] The campaign of 1921 and 1922 was the work of an organized movement within American Jewish life, not the voice of that community entire, and the distance kept by many of its leading figures is as much a part of the record as the money raised.

So the common account needs correcting on two counts. American support for a Jewish national home did not begin after 1945: by 1921, twenty-seven years before statehood, the movement had a national fundraising operation in the United States, a public figure of Einstein's standing on tour, a fight over control of the proceeds, and an audience with the President. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252] But that support was not yet broad, even among American Jews.

The record here leaves several things open. It does not say what Sokolow and Harding discussed, or whether the meeting bore on the congressional resolution that followed. It does not break down the nearly $5 million by source or show how it was spent, nor does it give the sums raised by the spring tour itself. And it records the outcome of the Cleveland convention, not the vote or the arguments made on its floor.
