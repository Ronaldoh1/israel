Roosevelt's Hidden Decline and the Choice of Truman, 1944

On 28 March 1944, seven months before the presidential election, a Navy cardiologist examined Franklin Roosevelt and found serious heart disease. The familiar account of the 1944 campaign treats the president's health as a private matter and the doubts raised about it as partisan exaggeration by his opponents. The medical record shows otherwise. The diagnosis was kept secret. Roosevelt's official physician told the public the opposite of what the cardiologist had found. A second doctor's written judgment that the president would probably not survive another term was locked in a vault for more than sixty years. And the man chosen that summer as Roosevelt's running mate, who would inherit the presidency and with it the American decisions on Palestine, was kept as uninformed as the voters.

The story belongs in a history of Palestine for one reason. The American commitments of these years, Roosevelt's private assurances to Arab leaders and the public promises of the 1944 platforms, would be carried out or broken by his successor. Who that successor would be was settled in the summer of 1944, by a party convention working without the medical facts.

The diagnosis. The examination took place at Bethesda. The cardiologist, Dr. Howard Bruenn, found acute congestive heart failure, an enlarged heart, hypertension and hypertensive heart disease. Bruenn was sworn to secrecy. He did not volunteer a prognosis even to Roosevelt himself, who, by the available account, asked no questions [cite:wiki_dying_president_ferrell]. One historian later wrote that the findings were guarded "on a level with the Manhattan Project." The comparison is striking, but it rests on a documented examination with specific findings, kept from public knowledge by design. What the record does not show is Roosevelt's own state of mind: that he asked no questions is documented; what he privately understood or suspected is not.

The public statement. Roosevelt's official physician since 1933 was Ross McIntire, an ear, nose and throat specialist with no training in cardiology. McIntire assured the press and the nation that the president was "in excellent condition for a man of his age" and that his health was "excellent in all respects" [cite:boston_globe_lahey_memo]. These were not vague reassurances or a difference of medical interpretation. They were specific, quoted public statements, and they contradicted the cardiologist's findings of the same period. The record documents the contradiction. It does not establish McIntire's motive, or exactly how much of Bruenn's findings he knew at the moment he spoke.

The Lahey memorandum. A second physician was consulted separately. Frank Lahey, a Boston surgeon, set down his conclusion in a written memo dated 10 July 1944: Roosevelt was unlikely to survive a full fourth term. The memo was placed in a Boston law firm's vault. It was not found and made public until decades after Roosevelt's death, more than sixty years after it was written [cite:boston_globe_lahey_memo]. The record establishes the memo's content, its date and its eventual disclosure. It does not establish who knew of it in the intervening decades.

Why the vice-presidency mattered. That same July the Democratic convention replaced the sitting vice-president, Henry Wallace, with Senator Harry Truman of Missouri [cite:wiki_1944_dnc] [cite:wiki_choosing_truman]. The reasons for Wallace's removal, and the question of whether Palestine had anything to do with it, are examined in [scene:s07c4|Henry Wallace and Zionism]. Set beside what Roosevelt's doctors knew, the choice of a running mate was in practice, though not in public understanding, the choice of the next president, and the voters who ratified it in November did not know that. The record draws a line here that should be kept. It does not show that every party leader involved in replacing Wallace knew the specifics of Bruenn's or Lahey's findings. Historical accounts document a general awareness among them that the president's health was declining and that this had political consequences, not knowledge of the diagnosis itself.

Why Truman. The documented reasons for choosing Truman were political. As chairman of the wartime Truman Committee, which investigated profiteering by defense contractors, he had earned real credibility and was ranked by 1944 among the most respected officials in Washington. He was acceptable to the labor unions and had supported the New Deal. But he had also opposed Roosevelt's earlier bid for a third term, which made him tolerable to the conservative Southern Democrats who distrusted Wallace's leftward tilt. Contemporary observers mocked the choice as "the Missouri Compromise," because Truman was no faction's first choice, only a candidate enough factions could accept [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] [cite:wiki_1944_dem_vp_selection] [cite:legalclarity_truman_1944_vp].

One complication in his background was real, and it was political. Truman's career had been launched by Tom Pendergast, the corrupt political boss of Kansas City, and party leaders had to work to establish that Truman was not "a Pendergast lackey" [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] [cite:legalclarity_truman_1944_vp]. The connection was discussed and had to be answered. A different theory is sometimes offered: that Truman was chosen because his personal finances or tax affairs left him open to pressure. No documented evidence connects his selection to his finances or to any tax matter [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] [cite:wiki_1944_dem_vp_selection]. On the record, Truman won the nomination by being broadly tolerable, not because anyone held something over him.

Truman kept in the dark. Several historical accounts describe the new vice-president as kept "entirely in the dark" about how ill Roosevelt was [cite:globalsecurity_fdr_veiled_decline]. The man about to inherit the presidency, and with it the American side of every decision on Palestine that this library follows through 1948, had no more medical information than the public. That claim has a limit. Public speculation about Roosevelt's appearance and vigor was itself widespread and documented, and the accounts do not say Truman had no sense at all that the president was failing. What they say is that he lacked the specific findings Bruenn and Lahey had made.

Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, two months after his meeting with King Ibn Saud aboard the USS Quincy and a week after his written assurance to the king [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud]. That meeting and the letter that followed it are told in [scene:s07ca|the Great Bitter Lake meeting]. Their fate passed to a president who had not been told how soon he might have to keep, or break, them.

What the record does not settle. The documents establish the diagnosis, the public statements that contradicted it, the hidden memo and Truman's exclusion from the medical facts. They do not establish what Roosevelt himself believed about his prognosis, what McIntire knew when he spoke, or which party leaders, if any, knew the specific findings when they chose Truman. Nor can they say how the election, or the vice-presidential choice, would have gone had the voters known. Whether the course of American policy on Palestine would have differed under a healthier Roosevelt is a question this record cannot answer, and this book does not speculate on it.
