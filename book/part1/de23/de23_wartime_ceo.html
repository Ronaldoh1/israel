The Wartime CEO: the Return, the Board Fight and the Owners' Vote

When Yarvin left in January 2019, he made more than one promise. "A Founder's Farewell" announced that he had resigned as chief technology officer, board member and voting shareholder of Tlon, that he had handed his galaxies to urbit.org, and that he would no longer contribute code to Urbit or write or speak about it in public. It also set out what the network had to be once he was gone: "The soil in which these flowers grow must be level and neutral." [cite:de23_farewell_2019] Two years later the project was split in two. Tlon, the company, kept building products, chiefly a chat app now called Tlon Messenger, and in 2021 it created the Urbit Foundation, a non-profit, to look after the open-source core of the system. [cite:de23_tlon_faq] [cite:de23_coindesk_2023] Josh Lehman became the Foundation's executive director the same year. [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] This section follows what happened when the money ran short, the founder came back, and the owners of the address space described in [scene:de23_address_space|the previous section] were asked to settle who was in charge.

The return, August 2024

By the summer of 2024 the Foundation was, in CoinDesk's words, "running out of money, with a runway measurable in months." In the week before 21 August its board dismissed Lehman and named Christopher Colby, the founder of a prediction-market start-up, as interim executive director; the Foundation's chief technology officer, Ted Blackman, and a senior engineer, Liam Fitzgerald, left. The board scrapped Lehman's plan to raise money by building a new blockchain layer on top of Ethereum, and the new strategy under discussion was a "utility token," possibly on Base, the network run by the exchange Coinbase. A board member said half the staff would be kept. [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] Another account says the Foundation "admitted that it had run out of capital" and that developers were being paid in address space. [cite:de23_compact_2025]

Yarvin explained his part in his own words. "Josh is an excellent peacetime CEO," he told CoinDesk, but the moment needed someone else: "For that, you need a wartime CEO." He held "no formal position at the foundation" but would lead its strategy, and he summed up the task as "We're here to fix this." [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] Noah Kumin, writing in the Mars Review of Books, reported that the return was announced at an impromptu public meeting and would be "in a big way." [cite:de5_kumin_mars] ARTnews and The New Yorker reported that several senior employees resigned in protest. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de23_kofman] Yarvin later described the state he found the project in as "controlled flight into terrain," a pilot's term for a working aircraft flown into the ground. [cite:de23_compact_2025]

The phrase he chose has a source. In April 2011 Ben Horowitz, co-founder of Andreessen Horowitz, one of Tlon's original investors, published a much-read essay distinguishing two kinds of chief executive. "Peacetime CEO knows that proper protocol leads to winning. Wartime CEO violates protocol in order to win," he wrote; the wartime chief "neither indulges consensus-building nor tolerates disagreements." [cite:de23_horowitz_2011] [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] In start-up writing the idea is a management doctrine, not a political one. It is also close to the argument Yarvin has made for countries since 2008, that a state should be run by a single chief executive who need not win consent, which [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six] sets out from his texts. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] No document shows him linking the two, and the parallel is noted here as a parallel.

Two organisations and a senate

The return took place at the Foundation, not at Tlon, and the difference matters for what followed. Tlon is a company with shareholders. The Foundation is a non-profit whose board, under the network's rules, answers to the Galactic Senate, the 256 galaxies, which the Foundation later described as having a "duty to select the board of directors." [cite:de23_epoch_2025] One account notes that the relationship between the two bodies "was unclear to the casual user." [cite:de23_compact_2025] A founder without a title, a board of three and a senate of owners made an unusual chain of command, and 2025 tested every link.

The fight, 2025

Money remained the first problem. In February 2025 Yarvin sought new funding from Andreessen Horowitz, The New Yorker reported. [cite:de23_kofman] A newsletter writer reported in July that he had persuaded the firm to invest $4 million "in exchange for more address space," adding that he did not know the terms; no filing or named outlet has confirmed the amount. [cite:de23_rettig] The New Yorker also reported, from an unpublished draft of his, that his newest plan was to promote Urbit as an elite private club whose members would be the stars of a new public sphere. [cite:de23_kofman]

The leadership fight is documented only in community newsletters, and both writers declared an interest. On 6 July 2025 a writer publishing as Cordelia, who said she had known the Foundation's executive director Ryan Kneer for more than a decade and urged a vote in his favour, reported that the Foundation had had four executive directors in a year. Kneer, in charge for eight months, had "joined forces" with Yarvin the previous November. In late June or early July, she wrote, Kneer removed Yarvin from the board while Yarvin was lobbying the board's other two members to dismiss Kneer, and Kneer then called a vote of no confidence and asked the galaxy holders to replace the board; at the time of writing 19 galaxies had voted, 18 for and one against. [cite:de23_marbl] On 27 July a second writer, Lane Rettig, who said he owns some Urbit address space, described Yarvin as "Executive Director-by-fiat," quoted him calling the period "real Roman times," and called the Foundation "default dead," start-up slang for an organisation that will run out of money without new funding. [cite:de23_rettig] Yarvin's own account of the dispute has not been published, and neither newsletter quotes him on it.

The owners decide, October 2025

The outcome came from the Foundation itself. On 9 October 2025 it announced that "the Galactic Senate has elected to replace the board of the Urbit Foundation," after "multiple votes," with a three-seat board identified, as is the custom, only by Urbit names. The new board appointed a new executive director, ~sicdev-pilnup, in succession to ~sorreg-namtyv, Yarvin's Urbit identity, "who is leaving to found a new application development company" to build on the Urbit stack. [cite:de23_epoch_2025] [cite:de23_farewell_2019] The announcement admitted that the Foundation "struggled during periods of uncertainty around both funding and leadership," and said its outside capital had come from venture capitalists and Bitcoin philanthropists. [cite:de23_epoch_2025] It did not give the vote counts, say whether Kneer's July proposal was the one that passed, or name Yarvin's new company or its backers, and no named outlet has reported them.

Status as of October 2026: the Foundation has operated under that board and director for a year, publishing technical work through at least May 2026. [cite:de23_bloglist] Its accounts are not public, and the a16z investment reported in 2025 is the last reported financing. Tlon continues to run its messenger and hosting service. [cite:de23_tlon_faq] No named outlet has reported Yarvin's return to a role at the Foundation, or further changes to its governance, since the October 2025 announcement.

What the episode shows

[scene:de6_claims_vs_record|Part Six] graded the move from "A Founder's Farewell" to "wartime CEO" YELLOW, a reversal shown with his own explanation, and the fuller record leaves that grade where it was. He had promised to stay silent about Urbit and to leave it level and neutral; he came back to run its strategy without a title, explaining that the crisis needed a different kind of leader. The record also adds the end of the story: the owners' senate, not the founder, chose who would run the Foundation, and the founder left to start something else. [cite:de23_farewell_2019] [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] [cite:de23_epoch_2025] No account documents coercion, misuse of money or any wrongdoing by anyone in the dispute.

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis is the Sophia platform's, not a finding. For Book Four it reads exit in its worst case: not an escape open to anyone who wants to leave a state, but an enclave for the few, in which owners rule without a veto from the people who live there, a rehearsal for the sovereign executive the theorists describe. Both kinds of exit in this book, the physical zones of [scene:de22_seas_and_zones|Part Twenty-Two] and the digital one in this part, can be tested against it.

Documented: the design of Urbit rule by ownership, in its author's own words. He called its naming scheme "digital feudalism," its government "proof of stake" and, by one account, "a transparent plutocracy"; only galaxies vote; and the top tier was concentrated in the hands of the company, its staff and its foundation, 185 of 256 galaxies in 2019. [cite:de23_moronlab_2010] [cite:de23_objections_2016] [cite:de23_compact_2025] Documented, too, is the founder returning in a crisis to lead the Foundation's strategy without a title, under a phrase whose source text says the wartime leader does not tolerate disagreement; one newsletter writer called him executive director "by fiat." [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] [cite:de23_horowitz_2011] [cite:de23_rettig] Among the physical zones, Próspera's company-run charter on a Honduran island, set out in [scene:de22_prospera_v_honduras|Part Twenty-Two], led to an arbitration claim against Honduras first put at up to $10.775 billion; in their memorial of 15 October 2025 the claimants sought restitution or, in the alternative, $1.63 billion in damages, and the case was pending on the merits as of October 2026. [cite:de22_po6] [cite:de23_riotimes_icsid]

Probable: that Urbit's design carried its author's politics. The vocabulary is his and documented, from "digital feudalism" in 2010 to "accountable monarchies" in a 2024 podcast, but he also insisted the network be neutral, and no document states that Urbit was built to demonstrate his political program. [cite:de23_moronlab_2010] [cite:de23_blockworks] [cite:de23_farewell_2019] Probable, too, that exit was costly at the top and cheap at the bottom: a galaxy was reported at over a million dollars in 2022, while an ordinary user's identity is issued free by the main app. [cite:de23_astra_2022] [cite:de23_tlon_faq] The enclave, on this record, is the governing tier, not the network.

Not supported: that any exit project has worked as a sovereign without a veto. The record runs against it at every level. In Urbit the founder was checked: the galaxy owners used their vote, replaced the board, and a new director succeeded him as he left. [cite:de23_epoch_2025] The check came from the same narrow tier the design empowers, not from the users; holders of planets still have no vote, and their remedy remains to leave, which they can do at no cost. [cite:de23_voting_docs] [cite:de23_tlon_faq] The network is small, and no published report links it to crime or to the machinery of the state. [cite:de23_point_2022] In Honduras, the host state's Congress repealed the law on private zones in April 2022 and its Supreme Court ruled the zones unconstitutional on 20 September 2024. [cite:de23_riotimes_icsid] In the United States, the 2023 pledge of ten "Freedom Cities" on federal land had produced no bill, executive order or land action by 2026, and was rated stalled ([scene:de22_argentina_and_freedom_cities|Part Twenty-Two]). [cite:de23_politifact_fc] What exit has needed, in each case, is a host's law and an investor's money, and the hosts' legislatures, courts and owners have used the vetoes the worst case says would vanish. Who enforces a charter against the person holding power, the question [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight] asks of Yarvin's state, was answered in his own network by a vote of the owners; [scene:de33_two_tests|Part Thirty-Five] sets that answer beside the federal government's experience, and these grades are carried into [scene:de40_worst_case_scorecard|Part Forty-Three].
