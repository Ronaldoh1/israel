Objections at the Time

In 1948, as the partition debate played out, a rabbi's organization, a CIA officer and a former college dean all went on record against the campaign for a Jewish state. A familiar assumption holds that no one in the United States questioned lobbying on behalf of a foreign state until much later, in the era of AIPAC. The familiar version misses these voices. Objections were raised as events unfolded, by Jewish organizations, by a CIA officer and by a Christian committee, and a foreign parliament protested as well. The objections were made and published. They were also outweighed.

One organized opposition came from the American Council for Judaism. Founded in 1942, it was led by Lessing Rosenwald with Rabbi Elmer Berger. The Council argued that Judaism was a religion, not a nationality, and it opposed partition. In 1948 it objected to United Jewish Appeal funds being used "for political and foreign governmental purposes." [cite:djn_1948_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman] Its weight should not be overstated. The Council's own historian documents it as a small minority that mainstream Jewish organizations opposed, and Zionist sources describe it as a fringe group. [cite:kolsky_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman]

A more mainstream body also stopped short of endorsing a Jewish state. At the 1944 congressional hearings, the American Jewish Committee proposed an international trusteeship for Palestine instead of a Jewish commonwealth. [cite:djn_1944_hearings] Together, the two bodies show that American Jewish opinion in these years was not one position. Jewish institutions argued openly for alternatives to statehood.

Criticism also came from inside the national-security world. In 1948 Kermit Roosevelt Jr. published a 24-page pamphlet, "The Partition of Palestine: A Lesson in Pressure Politics." [cite:ptsem_kr_pamphlet] Roosevelt would later serve as the CIA's field commander in the 1953 coup in Iran. [cite:w_kermit_jr] That same year, with Virginia Gildersleeve, former dean of Barnard College, he formed the Committee for Justice and Peace in the Holy Land. The committee warned that "extreme Zionist pressure" risked disrupting national unity. [cite:mosaic_cia_zionism] [cite:w_kermit_jr] Its argument concerned the political process in Washington, the same concern Reston had raised in the New York Times in 1946.

Objections came from abroad too. Iraq's parliament protested the congressional resolutions of 1944. [cite:djn_1944_marshall]

None of these objections changed the outcome. The pattern matches the one traced in the preceding chapters on [scene:s07j|the warnings] and the CIA estimate. Concerns were stated clearly, on the record and in time, and then set aside. Formal action on foreign agents came only much later, with a 1962 order under the Foreign Agents Registration Act and the Fulbright hearings of 1963.

These critics matter for two reasons. First, they refute the idea that lobbying for a foreign state was an unthinkable subject before recent decades; the question was posed in print in 1948. Second, the critics included Jewish organizations as well as Christian and governmental voices, so the debate cannot be reduced to one community against another. It was an argument among Americans about how their government was making a decision. In the years before 1948, one side of that argument consistently won.
