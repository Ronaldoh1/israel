The Scorecard

This section grades the charges Thiel, Yarvin and Land bring against democracy, one by one, against the evidence gathered in this book and its companion volume. A grade is a judgment, so the method is stated first, and a reader who weighs the evidence differently can apply the same method and reach a different score.

The method

The charges are taken from the authors' own words, cited, not from summaries of their views by others. Each charge is split into two claims that are graded separately. The first is the symptom: whether the thing they describe is real. The second is the cause: whether the record shows that democracy itself produces it, as opposed to the features of the American system that depart from majority rule. Both are graded in the book's three registers. Documented means a primary record or independent reporting establishes it. Probable means the record makes it likely without proving it. Not supported means the record does not show it, or runs against it. A third column records something different: whether the reforms in Parts Six and Seven would answer the charge, on the evidence about those reforms where they have run, marked yes, partly or no. The grades are not added into a single score, because the charges are not of equal weight and the book does not claim to know how a reader should weigh them.

The charges, graded

The first charge is Land's: "winning elections is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying." [cite:de7_audit_land] The symptom is documented in part. Outside spending reached a record $4.5 billion in 2024, with large sums on both sides from Future Forward, MAGA Inc. and the party leadership super PACs, and a handful of donors gave nine-figure sums. [cite:dt4_os_2024record] [cite:de7_os_donors2024] Access is sold: lobbyists' revenue falls by a quarter when the senator they worked for leaves office. [cite:dt7_aer_revolving] But "overwhelmingly" is not supported. The top fundraiser wins about 79 per cent of contested primaries, a correlation that does not separate money from the strength that attracts it; a structural model of House races found super PACs' net effect "muted"; Michael Bloomberg's $1 billion bought 61 delegates; Elon Musk spent more than $20 million backing a Wisconsin Supreme Court candidate who lost by ten points; and AIPAC's super PAC, the United Democracy Project, spent more than $30.6 million in a single 2026 Senate primary in Michigan, the largest sum it had ever spent on one race, and its candidate lost. [cite:dt4_bonica_2016] [cite:dt4_cox_2024] [cite:dt4_wiki_bloomberg2020] [cite:dt4_npr_wisc2025] [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026] The money's scale follows rulings, not majority vote. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:dt4_volokh_speechnow] The reforms answer this charge partly, within the ceiling the Court has set.

The second is that voting is "very weak" and that, as Thiel put it, there is "little hope that voting will make things better." [cite:de1_nyt_interview] [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] The symptom is probable in a narrow form: when the affluent and the middle disagree, the affluent have an edge, though its size is disputed. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] [cite:ad15_enns] [cite:de7_branham_ifs] What is documented is that the system weighs votes unequally by design: 87 House districts fell within five points of even on the 2025 index, 94 per cent of 2024 presidential campaign events were held in seven states, and about one eligible voter in five took part in the 2022 primaries. [cite:dt3_cook_pvi2025] [cite:dt2_fv_events2024] [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] That votes are weak because the system is democratic is not supported. The reforms answer the charge partly: the compact would make every presidential vote count equally, and commissions are associated with more competitive districts. [cite:dt7_nelson_ps]

The third is Yarvin's charge about voters themselves, that voting provides "this almost pornographic stimulus" of status rather than judgment. [cite:de35b_nyt] The symptom is partly documented: 70 per cent of adults can name the three branches of government and 13 per cent can name none. [cite:dt8_annenberg2025] Whether that makes democracy fail is not supported by anything in this book. The reforms do not answer it; ranked-choice ballots, if anything, ask more of voters. [cite:dt6_na_review]

The fourth is that a chief executive would govern better than an elected government; Yarvin suggested that one could "just pick one at random" from Fortune 500 chief executives and put that person in charge. [cite:de35b_nyt] The symptom, a government that often fails to deliver, is documented by its own auditors, from the launch of HealthCare.gov to $2.8 trillion in estimated improper payments since 2003. [cite:de7_gao_hcgov] [cite:de7_gao_highrisk2025] That democracy causes it is not supported: the federal workforce has not grown with the population since the 1960s and much of the work has passed to contractors. [cite:de7_kamarck_size] Nor does the alternative have a record in its favour, since comparative research finds the growth penalty among autocracies concentrated in regimes run by one person. [cite:de8_nber_w34093] The reforms do not answer this charge; they change elections, not administration, and ranked-choice counts take longer.

The fifth is the Cathedral, Moldbug's name for the universities, press and civil service, which in his account rule together without being elected. [cite:de1_land_gwern] The diffusion of responsibility is documented: the federal rulebook grew from 22,877 pages in 1960 to 188,346 in 2021, and the "problem of many hands" was named by a political theorist in 1980. [cite:de7_cei_cfr] [cite:de7_thompson_hands] A coordinated, unelected ruling body is not supported; the claim that the diffusion is the purpose of the design, rather than a cost of it, is Moldbug's addition, and the companion volume found no record of it in [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|What the Diagnosis Gets Right]. [cite:de7_thompson_hands] The reforms do not answer this charge.

The sixth is that democracy is "indistinguishable from the expansion of the state." [cite:de7_audit_land] The record is split: the volume of rules grew, while the federal civilian workforce fell from 1.1 to 0.6 per cent of the population between 1967 and 2018. [cite:de7_cei_cfr] [cite:de7_kamarck_size] That democracy drives the growth of rules is not shown. The reforms do not answer it.

The seventh is Thiel's, that freedom and democracy are not compatible. [cite:de3_cato_thiel] On the one measure of freedom the companion volume applied, it is not supported: Freedom House scores the United States 81 out of 100, against 48 for Singapore and 18 for the United Arab Emirates, the places the texts point to as models. [cite:dt1_pew_indices2026] [cite:de8_fh_sg2026] [cite:de8_fh_uae2026] Thiel's freedom is chiefly economic freedom, and how to weigh it against political freedom is a question of values that no record settles. The reforms do not address it.

The eighth is the offer itself: "No voice, free exit." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Hirschman, whose distinction the texts borrow, found that easy exit tends to "atrophy the development of the art of voice," and exit from a country is the response least available to most people. [cite:de8_hirschman] As a substitute for the vote it is not supported. But it is here that Yarvin's offer remains stronger than anything in this book. Ranked-choice voting, the compact and proportional rules all remain forms of majority or plurality rule, and a person in a lasting minority who wants out of the arrangement altogether is offered nothing by them. For that person, and for the wealthy and mobile, exit is a real offer on paper, though nowhere yet tested as the basis of a state.

The test

The verdict on Thiel's and Yarvin's argument, in three registers. Documented: the symptoms they describe are mostly real, from collapsing trust to concentrated money and a government that struggles to deliver; the American system departs from majority rule in the ways Parts Two to Five mapped; and their texts, as far as this library has examined them, do not engage the reforms that would make it more democratic. [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] [cite:dt2_brit_splits] [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] [cite:ad15_lii_cu] Probable: that the unequal weighting of votes and the sale of access explain part of the gap in responsiveness, and that matching funds and independent commissions would narrow it somewhat. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] [cite:dt7_aer_revolving] [cite:dt7_nelson_ps] Not supported: that democracy as such has failed. On none of the eight charges does the record show democracy itself as the cause, and the alternative they propose has no record at all. [cite:de8_land_de]

The scorecard cuts against this book as hard as against its subjects. The reforms answer two of the eight charges, and only partly; voter knowledge, the delivery of government, the diffusion of responsibility and the wish of a lasting minority to leave are all beyond them. Some publics in more proportional democracies, Israel's and Spain's among them, are nearly as dissatisfied as Americans, while Sweden's, Germany's and the Netherlands' are far less so, and satisfaction everywhere moves with events and with who holds power. [cite:dt8_pew_intl2026] The defensible conclusion is narrower than either side's slogan. Democracy has not failed on the record, and finishing it would answer the critics' charges about money and the weight of a vote; it would not, by itself, answer the rest. What it would take to finish it, and what is due to be decided, is the subject of [scene:dt9_the_routes|Part Nine].
