Who They Were: Teachers of Gnosis in the Second and Third Centuries

The Sophia platform's Reignite pillar calls them the "Evangelical Gnostics": Christians of the second and third centuries who read the gospels as a call to gnosis, a direct, inner knowing of God, rather than to belief in a set of propositions handed down by bishops. The label is the platform's own. No ancient group used it, and it should not be confused with modern evangelical Protestantism. This Part uses it once, here, and from now on uses the names the record supports: Valentinians, Sethians, Basilideans, Carpocratians, Simonians and the readers of the Thomas literature. The distinction matters because these were not one church. They were teachers, schools and writings that shared a few convictions and disagreed about much else, and almost every claim about "the Gnostics" as a single body turns out, on inspection, to be true of one group and false of another.

A word coined in the seventeenth century

Gnosis is ordinary Greek for knowledge. Some second-century Christians did use the word for themselves: Irenaeus of Lyon, writing about 180, says of the followers of Carpocrates that "they style themselves Gnostics". [cite:vt24_ah1_25] But "Gnosticism", the noun that bundles all these groups into one movement, was first coined in the seventeenth century by the Cambridge Platonist Henry More. [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] Modern scholars dispute whether the category is useful at all. Michael Williams, who writes the Encyclopaedia Britannica's entry, argued in 1996 for "dismantling a problematic category", and Karen King made a related case in 2003. [cite:vt24_brit_gnosticism] [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] Most specialists now treat the movement as having arisen inside Christianity: David Brakke and Simone Pétrement describe it as an intra-Christian response to Jesus's death and reported resurrection, and James Robinson, editor of the Nag Hammadi library in English, wrote that pre-Christian Gnosticism "is hardly attested" in a way that settles the question. [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] A minority holds that the Sethian myth began as a Jewish or pagan speculation that was later Christianised. [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism]

Simon Magus: a founder built by his opponents

The heresy hunters needed a first heretic, and they found him in the Acts of the Apostles: Simon, a Samaritan magician who offered the apostles money for the power to give the Holy Spirit. Acts says nothing about any doctrine of his. Irenaeus, writing around 180, made him the source "from whom all sorts of heresies derive their origin", and attached to him a full myth: Simon, he wrote, took a woman named Helena from a brothel in Tyre, called her the first conception of his mind, the mother of all, fallen and trapped in body after body, and presented himself as the highest power come to free her. Irenaeus adds that Simon's "mystic priests" practised magic and that his followers worshipped images of Simon as Jupiter and Helena as Minerva. [cite:vt24_ah1_23] Whether any of this goes back to the first-century Simon, or describes a later sect that took his name, cannot be settled; the account comes only from an opponent building a family tree of error back to a disreputable founder. The fallen-Wisdom pattern in it is real and recurs in later texts, which is why Simon appears at the head of the story at all.

Basilides and Carpocrates

Basilides taught in Alexandria in the first half of the second century. Irenaeus credits him with a heaven of 365 levels whose ruler is Abraxas, the letters of whose name add up to 365; with the teaching that "salvation belongs to the soul alone"; and with the claim that Simon of Cyrene was crucified in Jesus's place while Jesus, wearing Simon's form, stood by and laughed. [cite:vt24_ah1_24] Carpocrates, also Alexandrian, is the teacher whose followers called themselves Gnostics. Irenaeus says a woman of the school, Marcellina, came to Rome when Anicetus was its bishop and drew many followers, and that the group crowned images of Christ and set them beside images of Pythagoras, Plato and Aristotle. [cite:vt24_ah1_25] Both portraits come from hostile pens; for Basilides, almost nothing survives in his own words.

Valentinus and his students

Valentinus is the best documented of these teachers and the one closest to the centre of the church. Probably educated in Alexandria, he taught in Rome in the middle decades of the second century. [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] Tertullian of Carthage, writing about 206, says Valentinus "had expected to become a bishop" and broke with the church when "another obtained the dignity" through the claim that confessing the faith under persecution had given him. [cite:vt24_tert_adval] The passage does not name the city, and it is a hostile story told sixty years later, so the popular version, that Valentinus was nearly elected bishop of Rome, is a reading of Tertullian rather than a documented event. In another work Tertullian places Valentinus and Marcion as believers in the Roman church who were expelled, and says Marcion's gift of money to that church was handed back to him. [cite:vt24_tert_praescr]

Valentinus's students carried the school across the empire. Ptolemy and Heracleon led its western branch; Theodotus taught in the East, and parts of his teaching survive only because Clement of Alexandria copied them into his notebooks. Ptolemy's Letter to Flora, addressed to a Christian woman asking about the Law of Moses, survives complete because Epiphanius of Salamis quoted it in order to refute it, in book 33 of his Panarion; before 1945 it was one of the few authentic Gnostic writings scholars could read. [cite:vt24_wiki_flora] [cite:vt24_ect_flora] The Valentinians did not think of themselves as a separate religion. They called themselves Christians, read the same gospels, and, as the next sections show, many of them stayed inside the ordinary congregations.

Sethians and the Thomas literature

"Sethian" is a modern label, introduced by the German scholar Hans-Martin Schenke and others, for a family of texts that trace true knowledge through Seth, the third son of Adam and Eve. The family includes the Apocryphon of John, Zostrianos, Allogenes, the Three Steles of Seth, Marsanes, the Trimorphic Protennoia and the Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit. Its myth runs from an unknowable God through a first emanation, Barbelo, to the fall of Sophia, whose unauthorised act produces the creator Yaldabaoth and the rulers of the visible world. [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism] No ancient writer describes a "Sethian church", and whether the texts reflect one community or a reading tradition shared among several is open. The same caution applies to the Gospel of Thomas and related writings: a "Thomas community" is a scholarly hypothesis, and it has nothing to do with the living Saint Thomas Christians of Kerala in India. The texts themselves are treated in [scene:vt25_sayings_gospels|Part Twenty-Five] and [scene:vt25_secret_cosmos|its section on the Sethian cosmos].

Who is not in this Part, and who is still here

Three groups often bundled with the Gnostics are kept apart in this book. Marcion of Sinope taught two gods but had no Sophia myth, no aeons and no secret knowledge, and his followers founded churches across the empire with their own scripture, a shortened Luke and ten letters of Paul; Adolf von Harnack and many scholars since have declined to call him a Gnostic, though some, like G. R. S. Mead, did. [cite:vt24_audit_wiki_marcionism] The Manichaeans, followers of the prophet Mani (216 to about 274–277), formed a separate world religion. [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] The medieval Cathars, who also taught that an evil power ruled the visible world, are separated from the ancient groups by some eight centuries with no documented chain of transmission, and appear in [scene:vt28_cathars|Part Twenty-Eight].

One tradition of this family is not history. The Mandaeans, whose central figure is John the Baptist and whose scripture is the Ginza Rabba, practise repeated baptism in flowing rivers and keep their own priesthood. Encyclopedic sources describe them as the last surviving Gnostic religion from antiquity, though their origins are disputed. They number an estimated 60,000 to 100,000 worldwide. In Iraq their community fell to about 5,000 by 2007 and perhaps 3,000 by 2019; about 90 per cent of Iraqi Mandaeans were killed or fled after the 2003 invasion, and the largest diaspora community is now in Sweden. [cite:vt24_wiki_mandaeans] Modern Gnostic churches are a separate, recent strand: the Église Gnostique founded in Paris in 1890 by the archivist Jules Doinel, after visions he dated to 1888–89, is the best-known starting point, and its founder's later career, which included a return to Catholicism and work with the anti-Masonic hoaxer Léo Taxil, shows how quickly such revivals mixed with other currents. [cite:vt24_wiki_doinel] These churches describe themselves as revivals; none can document an unbroken line from the second century.
