What an Ordinary City Is: Chicago, Atlanta and Madrid as the Measure

To call a city an anomaly is to compare it with something. The comparison is usually left unstated, which is one reason the claim that London, the Vatican and Washington are "not ordinary cities" is so easily exaggerated or dismissed. This section states it. It takes three cities that no one calls anomalous, Chicago, Atlanta and Madrid, describes in a few lines where their powers come from and who can override them, and then sets the volume's three cities against that measure, point by point.

The American rule: cities belong to states. In the United States the basic rule was stated by the Supreme Court in 1907, in Hunter v. City of Pittsburgh: municipal corporations are "political subdivisions of the state, created by it," and they are "wholly under its legislative control." [cite:dv1_hunter_1907] Chicago shows how much freedom a state can grant inside that rule. Under Illinois's 1970 constitution, any municipality of more than 25,000 people is a "home rule unit" that "may exercise any power and perform any function pertaining to its government and affairs." But the same article lets the state legislature deny or limit those powers by a three-fifths vote of each house, or reserve a power to the state altogether; it bars home rule units from defining and punishing felonies; and it gives them only such power to impose income taxes or long prison terms as the legislature allows. [cite:dv1_il_const_art7] Chicago's residents, meanwhile, vote for that legislature, and for their state's two senators and their own members of Congress. Serious crimes in Atlanta are prosecuted by the Fulton County District Attorney, an officer the county's voters elect for four-year terms. [cite:dv1_wiki_fulton_da] The ordinary American city, in short, is subordinate to a government its residents help to elect, and its criminal justice is in the hands of local or state officials answerable to the same voters.

A capital that is still a city: Madrid. Madrid is a useful European measure because it is a capital with a special legal regime and is still an ordinary municipality. Spain's capital law of 2006 gives the "Villa de Madrid" a special status as the seat of the state's general institutions. Its government is a city council, the Pleno, made up of the mayor and the councillors, to which the mayor answers and which can remove him by a motion of censure; the mayor appoints a governing board. [cite:dv1_boe_ley22_2006] Its capital functions are handled by an interadministrative commission of state, region and city, in equal numbers, chaired by the state and acting only by unanimity, on matters such as security for events in the capital, official state ceremonies and protocol. Its municipal police exercise judicial police powers under state law, and the city's security board includes the commanders of the national security forces. [cite:dv1_boe_ley22_2006] Madrid is special, but its specialness is written into an ordinary statute, negotiated among three levels of elected government, and it confers no sovereignty and removes no one's vote.

The three anomalies, measured. Set against that baseline, each of the volume's cities departs from the ordinary in a different direction, and the direction matters for everything that follows.

The City of London has more self-government than an ordinary city of its size, not less. It keeps its own police force, for which its Common Council is the police authority under the Police Act 1996, and an electorate in which businesses as well as residents vote. [cite:dv1_police_act_1996] [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] But its privileges are held under Parliament, which its residents elect and which could remove them. Its anomaly is one of privilege inside a state, not of sovereignty.

Vatican City departs in both directions at once. It is a sovereign state: no outside legislature can override its laws, its ruler holds every power, and its government, the Holy See, has relations with 184 states. [cite:dv3_vn_fundlaw_2023] [cite:dv1_dfat_holysee] Yet it is far less self-sufficient than any ordinary city, leaning by treaty on Italy for its water, its prisons, the policing of its main square and the minting of its coins. [cite:dv3_lateran_text] [cite:dv3_wiki_vatican_city]

Washington departs in the opposite direction from London. Its residents have less control over their own city than the residents of Chicago, Atlanta or Madrid: the legislature that can overrule them is one they cannot vote for, their judges and chief prosecutor are chosen by the President, and in 2025 and 2026 the President took control of their police for a month and has kept National Guard troops in their streets for more than a year. [cite:dv1_conan_dc] [cite:dv4_crs_dc_courts] [cite:dv4_wiki_eo14333] [cite:dv4_wbal_guard_2026]

What the measure settles. The claim that these three are "not ordinary cities" is true, and the measure shows exactly how. It also shows what the claim does not establish. Only one of the three is sovereign, and it is the smallest and the most dependent on its neighbour. The other two are anomalies of status inside states, one with too much self-government and one with too little. None of the three holds, in its own right as a city, the powers the popular story attributes to it. Those powers, as the following parts show, belong to the institutions the cities house: the London market and the British state, the Holy See, and the federal government of the United States.
