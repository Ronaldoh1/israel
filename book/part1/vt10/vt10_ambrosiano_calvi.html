God's Bankers: Sindona, Marcinkus and the Fall of Banco Ambrosiano

The Holy See's bank, the Institute for the Works of Religion (IOR), is described in [scene:vt9_ior|Part Nine] as it runs today: a small institution with published accounts and a Church clientele. Its reputation was made in the 1970s and 1980s, when its president was an American archbishop, Paul Marcinkus, and two Italian bankers who did business with it, Michele Sindona and Roberto Calvi, ended their lives in a prison cell and under a London bridge. This section sets out what the court records and contemporary reporting establish about that period, what they leave unresolved, and what the Holy See said about it at the time. It names individuals because the record names them; none of them stands for the Church, and the Holy See's legal position, that it bore no liability, is given beside each finding.

Marcinkus and Sindona

Paul Marcinkus, a priest of the Archdiocese of Chicago, was made secretary of the IOR by Paul VI in 1968 and was its president from 1971 to 1989. [cite:vt10_wiki_marcinkus] [cite:vt10_wiki_ior] The Holy See had begun to use the Sicilian financier Michele Sindona as an adviser after it lost its Italian tax exemption on investment income in 1968, and his association with the IOR dates from 1969. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior] [cite:vt10_wiki_sindona] Sindona's banks failed in 1974. In the United States, Franklin National Bank, which his holding company had controlled since 1972, was declared insolvent on 8 October 1974; in Italy his Banca Privata Italiana went into liquidation, and its liquidator, the lawyer Giorgio Ambrosoli, was shot dead in Milan on 11 July 1979. [cite:vt10_wiki_sindona] A federal court in Manhattan convicted Sindona in 1980 on 65 counts, including fraud and perjury, and sentenced him to 25 years. Extradited to Italy on 25 September 1984, he was sentenced to 12 years for the fraudulent bankruptcy of Banca Privata Italiana on 16 March 1985 and to life imprisonment on 18 March 1986 as the instigator of Ambrosoli's murder. Two days later he drank coffee laced with cyanide in Voghera prison; he died on 22 March 1986. [cite:vt10_wiki_sindona] One estimate put the IOR's loss from the Sindona collapse at 35 billion lire. [cite:vt10_wiki_ior]

Calvi, the IOR and the Ambrosiano

Banco Ambrosiano was a Milan bank founded in 1896 by a Catholic lawyer, Giuseppe Tovini. Roberto Calvi became its general manager in 1971 and its chairman in 1975, and built a web of foreign subsidiaries in Luxembourg, the Bahamas and South America; Marcinkus sat on the board of one of them, Ambrosiano Overseas in Nassau. [cite:vt10_wiki_ambrosiano] [cite:vt10_wiki_marcinkus] The IOR was a major shareholder in the bank. [cite:vt10_wiki_ambrosiano] [cite:vt10_wiki_ior] Calvi was a member of Licio Gelli's masonic lodge Propaganda Due (P2), and in 1981 he was given a four-year suspended sentence and a fine of $19.8 million for moving $27 million out of Italy in breach of currency law; he kept his post while he appealed. [cite:vt10_wiki_calvi]

Before the collapse the IOR gave Calvi letters of patronage, also called letters of comfort, concerning companies in his network; their creditors could read them as a sign that the Vatican bank stood behind those companies' debts. [cite:vt10_sage_ambrosiano] [cite:vt10_lat_marcinkus] In 1982 the Ambrosiano could not account for about $1.29 billion, most of it lent to Panamanian companies that were nominally owned by the IOR. [cite:vt10_wiki_ambrosiano] [cite:vt10_csm_1984] The Bank of Italy took control in June, and in August 1982 a new bank, Nuovo Banco Ambrosiano, under Giovanni Bazoli, took over the old one's business. [cite:vt10_wiki_ambrosiano] [cite:vt10_sage_ambrosiano] Marcinkus's own account was that the letters had been issued to limit further lending to the affiliated companies, that Calvi used them for other purposes, and that Calvi had released the IOR in writing from any liability under them. [cite:vt10_avv_anfossi] [cite:vt10_lat_marcinkus] In 1990 he said: "I have never done anything wrong." [cite:vt10_lat_marcinkus]

Blackfriars Bridge

Calvi left his Rome flat on 10 June 1982 on a false passport in the name "Gian Roberto Calvini." At 7.30 on the morning of Friday 18 June 1982 a postal clerk found his body hanging from scaffolding beneath Blackfriars Bridge in London, with five bricks and about $14,000 in three currencies in his pockets. [cite:vt10_wiki_calvi] A first London inquest, in July 1982, returned a verdict of suicide; a second, in July 1983, returned an open verdict, finding that the cause could not be determined. [cite:vt10_wiki_calvi] [cite:vt10_sage_ambrosiano] Calvi's family pressed the case for years. His body was exhumed in December 1998, a forensic report commissioned by an Italian court confirmed in October 2002 the findings of an earlier private report obtained by the family, and the City of London Police reopened the case as a murder inquiry in September 2003. [cite:vt10_wiki_calvi]

In Rome, five people went on trial for the murder on 5 October 2005: Giuseppe Calò, Flavio Carboni, Manuela Kleinszig, Ernesto Diotallevi and Silvano Vittor. On 6 June 2007 the court acquitted all five for insufficient evidence, while holding that Calvi had been murdered. The appeal court confirmed the acquittals of Calò, Carboni and Diotallevi on 7 May 2010, and the Court of Cassation confirmed them on 18 November 2011. [cite:vt10_wiki_calvi] No one has been convicted of killing Calvi. The record therefore supports two statements and no more: Italian courts treated the death as murder, and no court found who committed it. No document in that record ties the death to the Holy See or to the IOR, and the theories that do so remain theories.

The Geneva settlement

The Italian Treasury minister, Beniamino Andreatta, called the IOR a de facto partner of the Ambrosiano and demanded that the Vatican pay 1,159 million dollars. The Secretary of State, Cardinal Agostino Casaroli, set up a joint Italian–Vatican commission; it split in the autumn of 1983, two of the three Italian experts finding the IOR responsible and the three Vatican members taking the opposite view. [cite:vt10_avv_anfossi] On 17 August 1983 the Vatican members advised a negotiated settlement, and a meeting at Castel Gandolfo that month, attended by John Paul II, approved one, with Marcinkus the only dissenter, according to documents published in 2023 by Francesco Anfossi. [cite:vt10_avv_anfossi]

The agreement was signed in Geneva on 25 May 1984. The IOR undertook to pay the creditors of the old Ambrosiano about $240–250 million, described as a voluntary contribution, and admitted no liability. [cite:vt10_avv_anfossi] [cite:vt10_time_1984] TIME reported that the payment was made "solely because of moral considerations" and that the Holy See "has always insisted that it has no legal or financial responsibility." [cite:vt10_time_1984] Vatican officials said they had been the victims of a "hidden project" by Calvi. [cite:vt10_csm_1984] The sum is reported differently in different sources, as the figure below shows; none of the sources found gives the text of the agreement, so the exact figure should be read as a range.

The arrest warrant and the Lateran Treaty

The settlement did not end the criminal case. On 20 February 1987 the examining magistrate in Milan issued arrest warrants against Marcinkus and two IOR lay officials, Luigi Mennini and Pellegrino De Strobel, for complicity in the fraudulent bankruptcy of Banco Ambrosiano. [cite:vt10_cc_609_1988] Marcinkus stayed inside Vatican City. On 17 July 1987 the Court of Cassation annulled the order confirming the warrant and held that the Italian courts had no jurisdiction, because Article 11 of the Lateran Treaty exempts the "central entities" of the Catholic Church from interference by the Italian state; the Constitutional Court declared further challenges to Article 11 inadmissible on 6 June 1988. [cite:vt10_cc_609_1988] [scene:vt3_what_each_got|Part Three: what each side got in 1929] Others in the Ambrosiano affair were convicted in Milan: Gelli's 12-year sentence for the crash was confirmed by the Court of Cassation in April 1998. [cite:vt10_wiki_ambrosiano] Marcinkus resigned from Vatican service on 30 October 1990, served as a parish priest in Sun City, Arizona, and died there on 20 February 2006, aged 84, without having been tried. [cite:vt10_wiki_marcinkus] [cite:vt10_lat_marcinkus]

What the record settles

The documents establish that the IOR under Marcinkus did business with two bankers who were later convicted, that it issued letters which creditors could read as support for Calvi's offshore companies, that it paid roughly a quarter of a billion dollars to those creditors while denying liability, and that the Lateran Treaty, as read by Italy's highest court, kept its officials out of an Italian dock. [cite:vt10_cc_609_1988] [cite:vt10_time_1984] They do not establish who killed Calvi, and no source found for this volume supports earlier claims that the Ambrosiano had ties to a "Rothschild banking network" or that Gelli organised postwar escape routes through the Vatican; both are dropped here for want of any document. The later cases, from 2010 onward, are taken up in [scene:vt10_reform_aml|the reform section] of this part.
