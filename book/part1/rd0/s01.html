The Board Before the Game, 1789–1815

Many accounts of Israel's origins begin with the Holocaust, as though nothing earlier mattered. Yet the dynasties, states and religious institutions that would later fund, permit or shape the Zionist project were all in place by 1789, a century before the political movement was founded. Before Zionism existed, power in Europe and the Near East ran through crowns, the Church and banking families.

The Napoleonic wars redrew the map of Europe, and they made the fortune of one such family. Mayer Amschel Rothschild (1744–1812) was born in the Frankfurt Judengasse, the walled ghetto where Jews were legally required to live. He trained in the 1760s as a dealer in rare coins for aristocratic collectors, an entry point into the German court economy, and in 1769 he was appointed court factor to Landgrave Wilhelm IX of Hesse-Kassel, one of the few financial roles open to Jews at the time. In 1806, when the Landgrave fled the French, Mayer Amschel was entrusted with his fortune. At his death in 1812 he was rising rather than risen, and his will instructed his sons to work only among themselves and to share information across borders.

The sons were placed in five capitals: Amschel in Frankfurt, Salomon in Vienna, Nathan in London, Carl in Naples and James in Paris. [cite:schama_two_rothschilds] The dates given for the London and Paris houses vary across the sources used for this book, from 1809 to 1811 for London and from 1812 to 1817 for Paris. The structure itself is not in doubt. Five branches allowed the family to move capital and information across borders faster than any state, to spread political risk, and to lend to governments at war with one another. The family's coat of arms, five arrows in a fist, commemorates it. The model was distinctive in scale but not unique; the Barings, the Warburgs, the Bleichröders and later the Morgans worked in similar ways. By 1815 the dynasty had become the most reliable creditor to regimes that kept collapsing. In London, Nathan helped finance British subsidies to allies in the final campaign against Napoleon, and Wellington's army was partly paid through Rothschild couriers moving gold to Spain. The famous Waterloo story is treated in [scene:bc_s02|the section on the London house]. The sources behind this book disagree about it: one treatment holds that Nathan's information advantage was real but the market story embellished, while the Rothschild Archive states that nothing in the record suggests he was first to know of the victory.

From the outset, the record distinguishes between branches, and so must any honest history. Much later, the Paris branch under Edmond de Rothschild would fund early Jewish settlements in Palestine while personally opposing Herzl, and the London branch would produce Lord Walter Rothschild, the addressee of the Balfour Declaration in 1917, who did not draft it. Two branches took two positions. Antisemitic conspiracy writing collapses the distinction; the historical record preserves it. [cite:schama_two_rothschilds] The dynasty's later decline is also documented: the Frankfurt mother house closed in 1901, and the Austrian branch's property was expropriated by the Nazis in 1938.

Around the family stood the powers. Britain, financed at scale by Nathan's house, would a century later issue the Balfour Declaration, a move that served three British interests at once: securing the approaches to Suez, keeping Russia in the First World War, and drawing America into it. [cite:cohen_palestine_great_powers] France, in 1791, became the first European state to grant Jews full legal citizenship. The same France would produce the Dreyfus Affair in 1894, the false conviction of a Jewish army captain that persuaded Theodor Herzl that legal emancipation had not made Jews safe. [cite:herzl_diaries]

The Papal States, and after 1870 the Vatican, formed a third pole. The Vatican would not recognize the State of Israel from its founding in 1948 until 30 December 1993, when the Vatican-Israel Fundamental Agreement was signed. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition]

To the east lay Imperial Russia, home to the largest Jewish population in Europe. The Pale of Settlement, created under Catherine the Great in 1791, confined most Russian Jews to the western provinces. After the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in March 1881, waves of pogroms swept Ukraine and southern Russia. About two million Jews left the empire between 1881 and 1914, and the great majority went to America, not Palestine. [cite:cohen_palestine_great_powers]

Palestine itself was Ottoman, as it had been since 1516 and would remain until 1917. Under the millet system, Christian, Muslim and Jewish communities had substantial internal self-government, and after the parliamentary reforms of 1876 Palestinian delegates sat in the Ottoman parliament. [cite:wiki_ottoman_palestine] That fact alone sits uneasily with claims that Palestine had no political existence before the twentieth century. This was the board on which the later game would be played.
