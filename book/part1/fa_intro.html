This book is about the arts that claimed to read what is hidden: the stars, the cards and the lots, the dark mirror and the oracle, sacred number, the plants that open the mind, and the dead. Every one of them was condemned by some authority at some time. The Council of Trent banned books of divination and magic in 1564, and the Catholic Catechism still says that "all forms of divination are to be rejected". [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116]

The reasons given were not always the same, and this book keeps them apart. Some arts were forbidden as fraud, some as a danger to a ruler, some as a wrong owed to God alone, and some, in the Americas, as idolatry. In 1620 the Inquisition of New Spain posted an edict against peyote on church doors from Mexico to the Philippines. [cite:vt30_wiki_spondent] [cite:vt30_torch_popes] [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] [cite:fa_harvard_peyote] Some bans were later reversed: Rome allowed Chinese Christians to honour their ancestors in 1939, and the United States made ceremonial peyote lawful in 1994. [cite:fa_brit_rites] [cite:og_mtsu_airfa]

The record also cuts the other way, and this book says so. A medium who founded a movement confessed to fraud on a New York stage. The United States government paid for psychic spying for more than twenty years and ended it when a review found it had never produced usable intelligence. [cite:fa_wiki_fox] [cite:fa_wiki_stargate] The book does not claim the forbidden arts work. It asks who forbade them, on what grounds, and what the people who practised them were looking for. The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads them as disciplines of attention.
