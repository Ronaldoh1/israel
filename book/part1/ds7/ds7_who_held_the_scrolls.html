Who Held the Dead Sea Scrolls

Every book on this shelf has asked the same question: who held the texts, and how did that holding shape what others could know? The Dead Sea Scrolls answer it in less than a century, in documents that can be checked.

The record shows three differences from the older stories on this shelf. The first is that the scrolls were not chosen or rejected by any council. They were a library before the lists were drawn, holding the books later called scripture beside those later set aside. [cite:ds_vq_nature] The second is that the gate held by the international team was not a church's gate, whatever the suppression theory claimed. It was the gate of a profession: of scholars who treated unpublished texts as their own, a gate opened in the end by other scholars, a computer and a library. [cite:ds_jta_pirated] [cite:ds_vq_pubcontro] The third is that the gate is now fully open. In 2011 the Israel Museum and Google put five of the larger scrolls online, including the Isaiah Scroll, and the Israel Antiquities Authority's Leon Levy digital library has made thousands of fragments, imaged with advanced techniques, accessible to the public for the first time. [cite:ds_brit_dss] [cite:ds_iaa_discovery]

The record also shows the continuity. A small group held texts that others wanted to read, and held them for decades; a state, like an empire before it, claimed them as heritage; and a market, like the auction at Maqdala described in The Ethiopian Bible, turned sacred objects into goods. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] [cite:ds_abc_jordan] [cite:et_cup_maqdala] What changed is the end of the story. The canon was closed by an office; the scrolls were opened by outsiders. Anyone with a screen can now read the oldest copy of Isaiah. The pattern of knowledge held by an office is not a law of history. It is a habit of institutions, and the scrolls show that it can be broken.
