East Africa Rejected: Palestine Confirmed by Choice

The Seventh Zionist Congress met in Basel from July 27 to August 2, 1905. It formally and finally rejected the British offer of territory in East Africa, and Britain withdrew it.[cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress] The vote closed the question the Sixth Congress had opened two years earlier, and with it the only serious territorial alternative that the movement's central body formally entertained after 1897.

The same Congress also settled the question of leadership. Herzl had died the previous year, and the delegates elected David Wolffsohn, co-founder of the Jewish Colonial Trust and designer of the movement's flag, as president of the World Zionist Organization. The two decisions coincided in time, but nothing in the record suggests one caused the other. The succession followed from Herzl's death regardless of how the East Africa debate ended.

The rejection had a price, and its clearest measure is Israel Zangwill. A British-Jewish writer and Zionist leader, Zangwill had led the pro-East Africa "Territorialist Faction" at the Sixth Congress in 1903. His argument then was not that East Africa should replace Palestine but that the offer could be used as leverage in future negotiations over Palestine with the Ottomans. When the Seventh Congress rejected the offer outright, Zangwill led 28 delegates out of the hall in protest and founded the Jewish Territorial Organization, known as the ITO, dedicated to finding a territory for Jewish settlement anywhere, no longer tied to Palestine. He was not a lone dissenter. At the time he had the support of the Rothschild brothers Leopold and Baron Nathan Mayer.[cite:jewishaction_uganda_truth]

The ITO's search was wide. Its best-remembered effort was the Galveston Movement, which directed Jewish immigrants to the United States through the Texas port rather than New York. It also sent exploratory expeditions to Canada, Australia, Iraq (then Mesopotamia), Libya (Cyrenaica) and Angola. Zangwill went to court in England in an attempt to restrain the Jewish Colonial Trust legally from colonizing anywhere outside Palestine, Syria, Asiatic Turkey, Sinai and Cyprus, a fight over the movement's own territorial boundaries.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] Years later Zangwill himself admitted, "There was not a land on earth that we did not think about." The breadth of the search and the legal battle both suggest a serious, sustained effort, not a symbolic one.

It achieved little lasting settlement anywhere. Zangwill rejoined the mainstream Zionist movement before his death in 1921, and the ITO was officially disbanded around 1925.[cite:encyclopedia_territorialism] The reconciliation matters as much as the split. Zangwill's departure was a disagreement about territory; it did not mean he abandoned Jewish causes.

The familiar account treats East Africa as a footnote and holds that there was never a real alternative to Palestine. The record of 1903 to 1905 does not support that view. The question was decided by formal Congress votes on two occasions, and its resolution cost the movement a walkout in 1903 and a permanent organizational split in 1905. Afterwards an organized, funded and in some quarters prominently backed faction went looking for another territory and did not find one that worked.

Palestine's status as the movement's goal was therefore confirmed by elimination, not by default. It survived two organized internal challenges: the 1903 vote to investigate East Africa, and the ITO's departure to search elsewhere. The Congress chose Palestine over a real, available, British-backed offer, and the faction that sought an alternative failed wherever it looked, though not for lack of trying.[cite:wiki_jewish_territorial_organization] That is a different claim from saying no alternative ever existed. The record shows the outcome and the process; it does not reveal a single rationale behind every delegate's vote.

This episode is also one of the five dates on which a larger argument rests, that the territorial question was settled decades before the catastrophe of the 1930s and 1940s, a theme drawn together in [scene:s07a|five dates before 1933].
