Part Nine followed the money abroad. Israel's own spending on public diplomacy grew from about $150 million in 2025 to a budgeted $730 million for 2026, and the Quincy Institute estimates more than $1 billion since October 2023. [cite:pm_jta_730m] [cite:pm_quincy_eighth] In Britain, about 180 MPs in the 2019–24 Parliament received pro-Israel funding worth more than £1 million; in Canada, CIJA lobbied or hosted 58 percent of the MPs running in 2025 before the country barred lobby-sponsored travel; in Europe, ELNET has drawn at least $11 million from American funders since 2022; and the Israel Allies caucus network reached 50 countries. [cite:pm_declassified_quarter] [cite:pm_maple_cija] [cite:pm_intercept_elnet] [cite:pm_ih_iaf50]

What is settled is the scale where disclosure exists, the fact that most of the private money, at home and abroad, is American, and the rise of the Israeli government's own budget above any single American organization's. What is contested is how much any of it achieves: ELNET credits itself with a German arms purchase, Israel's own diplomats doubt that messaging can outweigh policy, and the British debate turned on whether to examine the influence at all. What the record leaves open is almost everything in countries without disclosure rules. The hypothesis that the effort serves arms and aid fits the United States and Germany; the hypothesis that it protects Israel's place at the United Nations fits only when restated as a contest for votes and observer seats. Part Ten draws the whole architecture together.
