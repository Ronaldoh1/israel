How the West Lost Enoch, and What Rome Did in Ethiopia

A common version of this story says that the Vatican suppressed the Book of Enoch and that Ethiopia, beyond Rome's reach, kept the "uncensored" Bible. The record supports half of that. Ethiopia did keep Enoch while the Latin and Greek churches let it go, and Rome did try, once, to bring the Ethiopian church under its authority. But Enoch dropped out of Western use through the judgments of individual scholars and bishops in the fourth and fifth centuries, long before any papal act concerned it, and the Roman mission to Ethiopia in the seventeenth century fought over rite, Sabbath and doctrine, not over Enoch. No document found for this part shows a Roman order against the book. [cite:vt27_hessayon] [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] This section sets out both stories in order.

Read, then doubted: c.100–420

Enoch's early Christian readership was wide. The Letter of Jude quotes it as prophecy, the Epistle of Barnabas cites it as scripture, and a long list of second- and third-century writers, from Justin Martyr and Irenaeus to Clement of Alexandria, Origen and Cyprian, knew it. [cite:vt27_biblia_jude] [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The doubts were present from the start, and they came from the Jewish canon. Around 200 the North African writer Tertullian answered people who rejected the book because it "is not admitted into the Jewish canon either". His defence was that Noah, Enoch's great-grandson, could have preserved it through the Flood or renewed it by the Spirit, and that it "possesses a testimony in the Apostle Jude". [cite:vt27_na_tert_cultu]

The decisive judgments in the Latin West came from two of its most influential scholars. Jerome, the translator of the Latin Bible, wrote in 392 that the Letter of Jude was itself doubted, "because in it he quotes from the apocryphal Book of Enoch it is rejected by many". [cite:vt27_na_jerome_vir] Enoch was a liability that nearly cost Jude its own place. Augustine, in Book Fifteen of The City of God, written around 420, took a middle position that became the Western one. He accepted that "Enoch, the seventh from Adam, left some divine writings", because Jude says so, and that "there is some truth in these apocryphal writings". But "their antiquity brought them under suspicion", and they were not in the canon "preserved in the temple of the Hebrew people by the diligence of successive priests"; they contained so much that was false that they have "no canonical authority". [cite:vt27_logos_augustine] Augustine's test was the Jewish canon kept by the Temple priesthood, the same test Tertullian had argued against two centuries earlier.

Two points matter for Book Two's question. First, the objection was scholarly and textual: the book was not in the Hebrew Bible, it claimed to come from before the Flood, and parts of it seemed fabulous. Second, it came from individual writers in Bethlehem and Hippo, not from Rome, and it was an opinion about canon, not an order to destroy anything. In the Greek East the same doubt spread; rabbis of the second century and Church Fathers such as Athanasius likewise rejected the Enochic books, after which they "largely vanished from view". [cite:vt27_hessayon] None of the canon lists examined in Part Twenty-Three includes Enoch, and none needed to name it to leave it out. [scene:vt23_left_out|What was left out]

Fragments and rumours: c.800–1700

In the Greek world Enoch survived as quotation. Around 800 the Byzantine chronicler George Syncellus copied long excerpts from the Book of the Watchers into his world chronicle, and when the scholar Joseph Scaliger printed them in 1606 they started a European search for the whole book. [cite:vt27_hessayon] Reports that Ethiopia had a complete Enoch circulated. In the seventeenth century the French collector Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc obtained an Ethiopic manuscript said to be Enoch, but the German Ethiopic scholar Hiob Ludolf examined it and dismissed it as a different, worthless work, which for a time made Europeans doubt that an Ethiopic Enoch existed at all. [cite:vt27_sc_enoch2]

Bruce, Laurence and the return of the text: 1773–1912

The Scottish traveller James Bruce spent years in Ethiopia in search of the source of the Nile and returned in 1773 with three copies of the Ge'ez Enoch. He gave one to the Royal Library in Paris, one to the Bodleian Library in Oxford, and kept one at his family house, Kinnaird. [cite:vt27_sc_enoch2] [cite:vt27_hessayon] For nearly thirty years little was published. In 1800 the French orientalist Silvestre de Sacy translated the first chapters of the Paris copy into Latin. In 1821 Richard Laurence published the first English translation, from the Bodleian copy, and argued that it was the same work the Church Fathers had known in Greek. [cite:vt27_sc_enoch2] [cite:vt27_hessayon] August Dillmann published the first edition of the Ge'ez text in 1851, and R. H. Charles a critical edition in 1906 and his English translation in 1912. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The Aramaic fragments from Qumran, published in 1976, then showed that the book Ethiopia had kept was a faithful descendant of texts read in Judea before the Christian era. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

What Rome did in Ethiopia

Rome's direct dealings with the Ethiopian church are documented, and they were not gentle. In 1439, during the Council of Florence, Pope Eugene IV sent a message proposing union to the Ethiopian emperor Zara Yaqob; nothing came of it. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] In the early seventeenth century Portuguese Jesuits worked at the Ethiopian royal court. From 1622 the emperor Susenyos moved toward Rome, and the pope named a Portuguese Jesuit, Afonso Mendes, Latin Patriarch of Ethiopia. Mendes reached the country in June 1625, and on 11 February 1626 Susenyos and Mendes publicly acknowledged the primacy of the Roman see and made Catholicism the state religion. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic]

What Mendes then did is the core of the record. He condemned the Ethiopian observance of the Saturday Sabbath, which the church had officially accepted alongside Sunday in the fifteenth century, and the church's frequent fasts. He ordained Catholic priests, had Latin texts including the Mass translated into the local language, and told Ethiopian women that their children were in hell because they had been baptised in the Orthodox church. Many people were forced to convert by threats, imprisonment and battle. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] Rebellion followed. After a battle on 7 June 1632 in which the emperor's soldiers killed large numbers of his own people, Susenyos rescinded his edict on 14 June 1632, declaring that Catholics might keep their faith but no one would be forced; he abdicated the same year. His son Fasilides restored the Orthodox church and in 1634 exiled Mendes and most of the missionaries; other accounts date the Jesuits' expulsion to 1633, the year Mendes began his journey out, and in 1665 Jesuit books were ordered burned. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

The Latinisation campaign targeted the church's rite, its Sabbath, its fasts, its baptisms and its christology, and the attempt was coercive. The available record of it, however, says nothing about the canon, Enoch or Jubilees. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes] The documented book-burning of the episode was done by the Ethiopian side, against Jesuit books, after the mission failed. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

Rome and Ethiopia since

Catholic missions returned in the nineteenth century, when the Italian Lazarist Justin de Jacobis became the first prefect apostolic of Abyssinia in 1839. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] In 1919 Pope Benedict XV founded the Pontifical Ethiopian College in the Vatican. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] The Ethiopian Catholic Church today is a self-governing Eastern Catholic church in full communion with Rome, using the Ge'ez rite, with Addis Ababa as its metropolitan see since 20 February 1961; it counted about 70,800 members in 2017, against tens of millions in the Orthodox Tewahedo Church. Its Eritrean dioceses became a separate church in January 2015. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

The Ethiopian Bible matters for Book Two's argument not because Rome failed to cut it, but because it shows what a Christian canon looks like when it grows in a different place under different authorities: larger, with porous edges, holding the books the West doubted alongside its own laws and stories. The excluded texts of the earlier parts, from Thomas to Pistis Sophia, have no such home church; Enoch and Jubilees do. [cite:vt27_cowley1974] [cite:vt27_eotc_books]
