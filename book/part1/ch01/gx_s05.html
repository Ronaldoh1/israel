One Verse, Two Readings: Christians and the Promise to Abraham

Two opposite claims are often made about the Bible and the modern State of Israel. One holds that scripture requires Christians to support the state. The other holds that scripture has nothing to do with the question. Neither describes the actual situation, which is a long-running argument among Christians themselves over what one set of verses means and to whom they now apply. The verse at the center of it is Genesis 12:3, the text Christian Zionists cite most. It was spoken to Abram, later Abraham: "I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse; and all peoples on earth will be blessed through you." The promise of land follows in the same narrative, at Genesis 12:7, 15:18 and 17:8.

The words themselves are not in dispute. The question is who the "you" of the promise is today. One answer identifies it with the Jewish people and, since 1948, with the State of Israel, a secular state. Another holds that the promise was fulfilled in Christ and the church and does not pass to any modern government. The two answers lead to opposite political conclusions from the same text.

The Christian Zionist reading treats the verse as a standing instruction. Groups such as the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem describe it as the biblical mandate for standing with Israel [cite:icej_cz] [cite:rmc_cz]. On this reading the blessing and the curse attach to how nations and individuals treat the Jewish people and their state now, and the land promise of Genesis 12:7, 15:18 and 17:8 remains in force. Many believers in dispensationalism, a system of reading biblical prophecy, go further and regard Israel's restoration as a precondition of Christ's return. That framework was popularized by the notes of the Scofield Reference Bible, whose history is told in [scene:s05|the Scofield Bible]. The older British and American Protestant campaign for a Jewish restoration to Palestine is told in [scene:bc_s05|the Protestant restoration lobby]. The strongest form of this reading rests on the text's plain wording: the blessing and the curse as spoken, and the land promised again at 12:7, 15:18 and 17:8.

The other reading belongs to covenant theology and to what theologians call "amillennial" theology. It holds that the promise to Abraham is fulfilled in Christ and in the church, citing Paul's letter to the Galatians (3:16 and 3:29), and that it does not transfer to a modern government [cite:studylight_gen12] [cite:pctii_gen12]. Some scholars add a point about emphasis: the passage stresses blessing to all the nations of the earth more than it stresses land [cite:pctii_gen12]. The strongest form of this reading is that it takes its key from within the Christian scriptures themselves, in Paul's own reading of the promise, and gives full weight to the verse's last clause: "all peoples on earth will be blessed through you."

The argument is old, but in June 2025 it became public in a way that reached far beyond seminaries. Senator Ted Cruz cited Genesis 12:3 in an exchange with Tucker Carlson, and Carlson asked whether the political state led by Benjamin Netanyahu is the same "Israel" as the one in scripture [cite:think_gen12]. The question he put is the hinge of the whole dispute. Cruz's position rests on the identification of the biblical Israel with the modern state. Carlson's question rests on the distinction between them. Neither man settled it, and no verse can settle it on its own, because the disagreement is about how to read the verse, not about what it says.

The first reading is the theology behind the largest pro-Israel constituency in the United States. A frequent question about the dispensationalist reading is why the Roman Catholic Church never campaigned against it. The premise is half right. Rome never ran a public campaign. But it did rule on the theology. In the 1940s the Holy Office, the Vatican's doctrinal authority and the predecessor of today's Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, judged that premillennialism, the prophetic framework at the core of Scofield's system, "cannot safely be taught" [cite:catholic_answers_rapture_tract]. The rejection is still in force. Paragraph 676 of the Catechism of the Catholic Church rejects millenarianism as "the intrinsic perversity" of a falsely secularized understanding of the Kingdom of God [cite:catholicculture_five_myths_rapture]. The position is public and available to anyone. It is simply not widely known.

It stayed quiet for structural reasons. Catholic doctrinal statements are addressed to Catholics, through the Church's own teaching, not issued as public rebuttals of particular Protestant authors or their reference Bibles. Rulings against specific Protestant theological systems routinely remain inside Church documents and catechetical teaching rather than becoming public campaigns, and the Church generally does not campaign against the doctrines of Protestant denominations with which it is not in active dialogue. This was a general pattern, not a special case invented for dispensationalism [cite:worldfinance_vatican_bank_history]. Rome also had no authority over Protestant publishing in the first place. Its doctrinal jurisdiction runs over Catholic teaching, and the Reformation had ended any mechanism by which it could approve or block a Protestant publisher's Bible centuries before Scofield. What the ruling records is the Church's own position on the theology, not an attempt or an ability to control what Protestant publishers printed. Nor does the silence show indifference: the ruling, and its place in the current Catechism, record a sustained position, only not a public one. Whether individual Catholics or parishes ever hear of paragraph 676 is a different matter, and the record here does not measure it.

Rome's diplomatic record shows what a church that did not read the promise in dispensationalist terms actually did when Zionism came to it. In January 1904 Theodor Herzl sought an audience with Pope Pius X to ask for Vatican support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. The Pope refused. His documented answer was that the Church could not support Jewish restoration to a land central to its own theology while denying Christ [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition]. That refusal held as Vatican policy for 89 years. In 1949, a year after Israel's founding, the Vatican worked behind the scenes at the United Nations to make Israel's admission conditional on two commitments: allowing the return of Palestinian refugees, and formal guarantees for Christian and Muslim holy sites, particularly in Jerusalem. Ben-Gurion said that year: "There is a major religion in the world, which has to settle with us a historical reckoning." The campaign failed. Israel was admitted to the United Nations in May 1949 without the guarantees the Holy See had sought.

What changed the Church's course was a change in its doctrine. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate of 1965 revised Catholic teaching on Judaism, stating that God "holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers." That shift was the precondition for what followed. On 30 December 1993 the Holy See and Israel signed the Fundamental Agreement in Jerusalem, establishing diplomatic relations, 45 years after Israel's founding, and ambassadors were exchanged in 1994. Even then, property rights, tax exemptions and the status of Jerusalem were left for continuing negotiation under the agreement's own terms [cite:wiki_fundamental_agreement]. The documented reasons for the long delay were three: the Church's theology of the Jewish people's role in salvation history, the unresolved status of Jerusalem's holy sites, and concern for the Palestinian Christian and Muslim communities under the new state [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition]. After the UN General Assembly's vote of November 2012, the Vatican formally recognized the State of Palestine, and in 2015 it signed an accord governing the Church's activity in Palestinian-controlled territory, which drew a formal Israeli protest [cite:gulfnews_vatican_palestine_accord]. The full story is told in [scene:vat_s01|the Vatican's 45 years].

Two further points complicate any picture of a simple religious alignment. Within Judaism, too, views of the state's religious meaning differ: early Zionism was largely secular, and some Haredi Jews oppose a Jewish state before the coming of the Messiah. And Palestinian Christians, about 1% of the population today, are part of this story too, as a community with its own history in the land and not only as a subject of other Christians' theology.

The record settles what the verse says, which traditions read it which way, what Rome ruled and when, and how long it took the Holy See to recognize Israel. It does not settle the theological question, which is not a historical one: whether a promise made to Abraham applies to a state founded in 1948 depends on a reader's prior commitments about how scripture is to be read. Nor does the material here say how many Christians hold each reading. What it does show is that "the Bible requires it" and "the Bible is irrelevant" are both wrong as descriptions. The verse matters a great deal to many Christians, and they draw opposite conclusions from it.
