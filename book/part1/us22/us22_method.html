How the First Scorecard Graded the Two Systems, and How This One Does

Part Twenty-One set every measure in one ledger and added no total. The question it left open is the one readers put first: taken together, which system serves the people under it better, and by how much? An earlier version of this comparison, the simulation's chapter on the two systems, answered with a number. This part takes that number seriously, reproduces it, shows where it held and where it did not, and then grades both systems again across everything this volume measured, publishing every score and every weight so that a reader who disagrees with a choice can change it and recompute. [scene:us21_ledger|Part Twenty-One: the ledger] [scene:usil_s11|The simulation's composite verdict]

The first scorecard

The simulation scored 21 systems for each country from 0 to 5, on six criteria it named for every system: access, cost, outcome, efficiency, resilience and equity. Where no comparable figure could be found for both countries, it said, a system was discussed but left out of the total. [scene:usil_s01|The simulation's method note] It then reported two totals. Averaged over all 21 systems, including finance, energy, central banking, taxation, telecommunications, the environment and immigration, Israel scored 3.23 and the United States 3.07, a gap of 0.16. Averaged over the eleven systems it judged to be about what a government delivers directly to its residents, Israel scored 3.71 and the United States 2.49, a gap of 1.21, and that was the figure it put first. [scene:usil_s11|The simulation's composite verdict]

The arithmetic reproduces. The eleven published scores average 2.49 for the United States and 3.70 for Israel; the published 3.71 is a rounding difference of one hundredth. The direction it found also survives everything that follows: on the services a household feels most directly, health coverage, life expectancy, debt, paid leave, family support and safety from violence, Israel's system scores higher, and on the newer and wider measure below it still does. What did not survive is the scorecard's method, for five reasons a hostile reader would find in an afternoon.

What the first scorecard could not show

First, it named six criteria for every system but published none of the 126 criterion scores per country, and it did not say how the six were combined into a system's score. A reader could see that healthcare scored 1.6 and 5.0, but not what access, cost or equity contributed, so no single score could be checked or recomputed. Second, several systems were scored on one or two figures with no stated rule for turning them into a score: emergency services, 3.0 and 5.0, beside ambulance response times of about seven minutes against under three, and culture and happiness, 3.0 and 5.0, beside the two countries' happiness ranks. Third, some scores ran against the figures shown next to them. Housing scored the United States higher, 3.0 to 2.0, beside a homelessness count of about 219 per 100,000 Americans against about 4 for Israel; the score presumably weighed Israel's house prices, but the scorecard did not say so. Fourth, the one-sided debt score, 1.5 to 4.5, leaned in part, alongside national and household debt figures, on a contested estimate that two-thirds of American bankruptcies are medically driven; the next section sets that estimate beside the competing one. [scene:us22_scores|The scores, area by area] Fifth, and most important, the scorecard had no system for rights and coercion at all, and like most national statistics it described Israel's citizens and residents only. It could therefore not answer the question a reader most often asks after the services: which state is the more oppressive? Nor did it count the 5.56 million Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza, roughly a third of the people under Israel's control. [cite:us9_pcbs_2025] [scene:us9_us|Part Nine: who is governed without a vote]

There was a smaller inconsistency too. The public-safety score set a gun-homicide rate for Israel, about 2 per 100,000, against an American gun-death rate of 12.8 that counts suicides and accidents as well; the two figures measure different things. The rules of the new scorecard

The new scorecard keeps the simulation's best idea, separate totals for separate questions, and replaces its method with one that can be checked line by line. It follows six rules.

  • Every score is a measure. Each of the 43 rows in the next section is one named figure from one named source, taken from the part of this volume that audited it, or from the sources added for this part. No row is a judgment. Where only one country has a figure, the row is shown and left out of every total.
  • Every measure is scored against printed goalposts. Each measure has a floor worth 0 and a goal worth 10, round numbers chosen around the range of rich democracies; a country's score is its position between the two, capped at 0 and 10. This is the method the United Nations uses for its Human Development Index. The goalposts are a choice, so they are printed beside every row, so that a reader can see, for example, that the homelessness row runs from 300 per 100,000 (0) to none (10), and the final section tests how much they matter.
  • Results are scored, spending is reported. A system is graded on what its money delivers, so spending on health, schools and research is reported beside the results but not scored. The one exception is public family benefits, because there the spending is itself the benefit households receive.
  • Areas, not rows, are weighted equally. The rows fall into twelve areas: eight citizen services the simulation scored, a ninth on the family budget that applies the Sophia platform's American Dream module to both countries, then liberty and coercion, the economy and public finances, and equity inside each system. Each area's score is the plain average of its rows, so an area with many rows does not outweigh an area with one.
  • Three totals answer three questions. A household score averages the nine household areas; a liberty score is the liberty-and-coercion area; an economy score is the economy area. The overall score averages all twelve areas. No total is offered as more than what it is: this platform's disclosed synthesis of published measures, which a reader who weighs the areas differently will weigh differently.
  • Israel is scored twice where it matters. Its citizens view uses its national figures, which cover its citizens and residents. Its everyone-governed view adds the Palestinians of the West Bank and Gaza wherever a measure concerns how the state treats the people under its control: the Freedom House score weighted by population, detention without charge, and the share of people governed who cannot vote for the government that rules them. The service areas are scored for citizens in both views, because under the Oslo accords the Palestinian Authority, not Israel, runs health, schools and welfare for most of the West Bank; a reader who holds Israel responsible for those services as the occupying power would score its services lower still.
  • Every total is tested. The final section recomputes the result under other choices: every row weighted equally, liberty counted twice, three broad pillars only, every goalpost range widened by a quarter, no goalposts at all, and each area or row left out in turn. A conclusion that holds under all of them is reported as a finding; one that flips is reported as depending on the choice that flips it.

Some systems the simulation scored are discussed in this volume but not scored here, because the volume found no figure measured the same way in both countries: emergency services, transport, construction, energy and water, where Part Fifteen compares how each is organised rather than how well it performs; the price of a home against income, where the two countries' affordability measures cannot be set against each other; police killings, for which Israel publishes no series; and student debt, medical debt and the top one per cent's share of income, which Israel does not compile in a comparable form.
