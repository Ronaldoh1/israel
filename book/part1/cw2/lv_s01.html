Operation Susannah and the Lavon Affair, 1954–1955

In the summer of 1954 Egypt and Britain were negotiating the evacuation of British forces from the Suez Canal area, a negotiation whose terms are followed in [scene:cw2_free_officers|the section on Egypt's revolution]. In the same months, Israel's military intelligence service, Aman, set in motion a covert operation inside Egypt meant to damage Egypt's relations with the West while that negotiation went on. Israel's own Defense Ministry archive, which calls the episode "the 'Unfortunate' or the Lavon affair," describes it as "a failed Israeli covert operation." [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] The operation came to be known as Operation Susannah. Its exposure cost two of its operatives their lives on a Cairo gallows, sent others to prison for years, forced a defence minister from office, and opened a quarrel over a single question, who gave the order, that Israeli politics did not put to rest for nearly a decade.

A common assumption holds that Israel has never acted against the interests of its own Western friends. The record of 1954 does not support it. The targets of Operation Susannah included American information libraries in Cairo and Alexandria and British-owned cinemas, and the plan was for the blame to fall on Egyptians. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] What the record shows, and what this section follows, is how the operation was built, how it failed, who was punished for it, and why the question of who authorized it was never formally answered.

The cell and its purpose. Aman built the instrument from the local Jewish community. Unit 131 was a cell of Egyptian Jews, recruited and trained for sabotage, and in July 1954 it was activated to plant incendiary devices in Egypt. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] According to the Defense Ministry archive, the unit was commanded by Major Motke Ben-Zur. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] The archive is explicit about the aim. The bombings were meant to sabotage Egypt's relations with the West while Egypt negotiated with Britain over evacuating the Canal area, and they were to be blamed on the Muslim Brotherhood, on Egyptian Communists, or on "local nationalists." The targets were Egyptian, American and British-owned cinemas, libraries and American educational centres, and the bombs were timed to go off hours after closing. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] An older summary of the affair adds the strategic object: the attacks were meant to persuade Britain to keep its forces in the Suez Canal Zone. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

The intended logic, as the sources describe it, was a chain of attributions: American and British property in Egypt would burn, Egyptians would be blamed, Egypt's relations with the West would suffer, and Britain might keep its troops at Suez. Each link depended on the one before it, and the whole depended on no one discovering who had actually lit the fires.

Reduced to its mechanism, the operation was a redirected message: an act designed so that it would appear to come from a different sender. That is a description of how it was meant to work, not a judgment of its place in any longer history. The operation is sometimes called the earliest documented Israeli false-flag operation; the sources used for this section do not establish that it was the first of its kind, and this book does not rest anything on the claim.

July 1954. The most detailed dated sequence comes from the Arab outlet Raseef22, which reconstructed it from Egyptian sources, and it should be read as that outlet's account. According to Raseef22, coded signals went out on Israeli radio on 16 June 1954. On 2 July devices were placed in mailboxes in Alexandria. On 14 July the American libraries in Cairo and Alexandria were set alight. For 23 July the cell planned devices at a Cairo station, at the Rivoli theatre, and at Alexandria's Metro and Rio cinemas. [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] An Israeli account broadly agrees on the first two dates. Writing in The Jewish Press in 2022, Saul Jay Singer dates the bombings to 2 July, at the Alexandria post office, and 14 July, at the USIA libraries in Alexandria and Cairo and a British-owned theatre. [cite:rs_cw2_jewishpress_susannah] The two accounts differ on detail: Singer places a British-owned theatre among the targets of 14 July, while Raseef22 places the cinemas among the devices planned for 23 July.

The night of 23 July ended the operation. Raseef22 reports that on that evening an Alexandria police officer put out the burning trousers of Philip Natanson outside the Rio cinema, and that his arrest led to the others. [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] Point of No Return, a blog on the Jewish refugees from Arab countries, describes a phosphorus device igniting in his pocket. [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] The older summary gives the same essential fact: a device ignited prematurely in an operative's pocket in Alexandria, and the cell was arrested. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

Did anyone die? On this the sources conflict, and the conflict cannot be settled from the material available. Ynet, reporting in 2005, says the attacks caused no casualties. [cite:rs_cw2_ynet_2005] Singer, in 2022, also writes that the July operations "caused no injuries or deaths." [cite:rs_cw2_jewishpress_susannah] Raseef22's Egyptian-sourced account says that people died in the Alexandria fire of 2 July. [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] No primary record of casualties was found in the research for this book. On one side stand two Israeli outlets; on the other, one account drawn from Egyptian sources. Neither side's version can be confirmed or ruled out on this evidence, and the question remains open. [cite:rs_cw2_jewishpress_susannah] [cite:rs_cw2_ynet_2005] [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon]

The arrests and the trial. Eleven Egyptian Jews were arrested. Their trial ran from 11 December 1954 to 27 January 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] Two defendants, Moshe Marzouk and Shmuel Azar, were sentenced to hang. Six received long prison terms: Victor Levi, Philip Natanson, Robert Dassa, Marcel Ninio, Meir Meyuhas and Meir Za'afran. Two were acquitted. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged]

Appeals for mercy went to Egypt's leader, Gamal Abdel Nasser, who rejected them, and Marzouk and Azar were executed in Cairo on 31 January 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] Two of those arrested died in custody. One of them was Max Bennett, an Israeli officer, also recorded as Meir Max Bineth. [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] According to Raseef22, he took his own life in detention before the trial session; the older summary says he took his own life in prison. [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] [cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

The cell's handler was not among the condemned. Avri Elad, the cell's handler, is identified in the sources as a double agent. He was later tried in secret in Israel and jailed for ten years. [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] [cite:rs_cw2_ynet_2005] The sources used here say no more about what Elad passed to whom, or when.

The exposure had consequences inside Egypt's own state as well. Raseef22 writes that the affair accelerated the founding of Egypt's General Intelligence Directorate in 1955, under Zakaria Mohieddin. [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] That is the outlet's assessment and is reported here as such.

Who gave the order. In Israel the failure turned at once into a struggle over responsibility, and the struggle took its name from the minister at its centre. The question was disputed from the start, and three men gave three answers. Defence Minister Pinhas Lavon denied any knowledge of the operation. The head of military intelligence, Colonel Binyamin Gibli, said he had received a verbal instruction from Lavon. The Chief of Staff, Moshe Dayan, blamed the minister. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] The Prime Minister, Moshe Sharett, according to the Defense Ministry archive, "was kept out of the loop." [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] Sharett appointed a two-man commission to establish the facts; in the archive's words, it "did not expose the truth." [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon]

The structure helps explain why the question was not answered. The order, on Gibli's account, was verbal. The Prime Minister had not been told, and the inquiry he set up did not resolve it. A chain of command with no written order in the record left each man able to deny the decision.

Sharett's private diary records what the affair did to the government he led. On 14 January 1955, less than two weeks before the verdicts in Cairo, he wrote that he "would never have imagined that we could reach such a horrible state of poisoned relations." [cite:rs_cw2_rokach_lavon] Elsewhere he gave his own verdict on the minister, one that neither convicted nor acquitted him: even if Lavon "did not give the order, his role in preparing the ground for the Affair is undeniable." [cite:rs_cw2_rokach_lavon] These passages are quoted in the translation published by Livia Rokach in her 1980 study of the diaries. They are Sharett's contemporaneous words, and they show the Prime Minister himself unable to say who had issued the order.

The two cases can each be put at full strength. The case against Lavon rests on Gibli's statement that he had a verbal instruction, on the Chief of Staff's support for that account, and on Sharett's judgment that Lavon had, at the least, prepared the ground. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_rokach_lavon] The case for Lavon rests on his own consistent denial, on the absence of any written order in the record used here, and on the fact that the only formal inquiry of the time, in the archive's words, "did not expose the truth." [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] Each side's evidence was testimony from an interested party, which helps explain why the affair outlived the operation.

The outcome in 1955. Lavon resigned in February 1955. Gibli and Ben-Zur were dismissed. On 21 February 1955 David Ben-Gurion returned to the government and replaced Lavon as defence minister. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_israeled_lavon] The political consequences of Ben-Gurion's return, and the widening split between him and Sharett over the use of force on the borders, are told in [scene:cw2_sharett_ben_gurion|the section on Sharett and Ben-Gurion].

Who paid. The cost of the operation fell almost entirely on the people Aman had recruited to carry it out. The planners lost their offices; the operatives lost their lives or their liberty. Marzouk and Azar were hanged. Bennett died in custody. Six others served long sentences in Egyptian prisons. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] For decades the state that had sent them did not acknowledge them. Israel long denied involvement, including after the peace treaty with Egypt in 1979. [cite:rs_cw2_ynet_2005] [cite:wiki_lavon_affair]

The first official recognition came half a century later. On 30 March 2005 President Moshe Katsav presented certificates to the nine agents Aman had recruited; three survivors attended. Ynet reported the words of David Kimche, a former deputy chief of the Mossad, on the lesson the services drew: "We knew never to go down that road again." [cite:rs_cw2_ynet_2005] The ceremony was the state's first official recognition of the agents. It did not settle the question of which official had given the order.

The operatives came from Egypt's Jewish community, and the operation belongs to that community's history as well as to Israel's. The larger history, of the departures of Jews from Arab and Muslim countries between 1948 and 1972, is told in [scene:s00e2|the history of the Jews of the Arab world], where this affair is treated as one documented case among several causes, specific to Egypt.

What the record does not settle. Four questions remain open on the evidence used here. The first is the order: the testimony points in opposite directions, the order was verbal, and the inquiry of 1955 did not resolve it. The second is casualties: two Israeli accounts say no one was hurt, one Egyptian-sourced account says people died in Alexandria on 2 July, and no primary record has been found to decide between them. The third is the tally of the accused: the sources give eleven arrests and account for ten by verdict, two hanged, six imprisoned and two acquitted, while also saying that two of the arrested died in custody, and they do not name the second or fully reconcile the count; nor do they say whether Bennett, described as an Israeli officer, is counted among the eleven Egyptian Jews. The fourth is the operation's institutional home: the Defense Ministry archive places it in military intelligence, while Raseef22's headline calls it the Mossad's first operation in Egypt. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_raseef_lavon] [cite:rs_cw2_jr_hanged] The double agent Elad's role is identified but not described.

The affair did not end in 1955. In 1960 it was reopened amid evidence that documents had been forged; the renewed dispute split the ruling Mapai party and contributed to Ben-Gurion's resignation in 1963, and the question of the order's origin remained formally unresolved. [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] That second round belongs to the years of Stage Four, where it is told in full.
