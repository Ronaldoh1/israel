Interpretive Lens: IS-RA-EL

This section is an interpretive lens, not a finding. It sets out a symbolic reading of the name Israel that this platform holds and has offered since its first volume, and it is headed and phrased as a reading throughout. It does not claim that the name was built from the names of Egyptian gods, it does not replace the Hebrew philology set out in [scene:ts13_name|the first section of this part], and it generates no documentary claim anywhere in this volume. It is placed here, after the record and after the documented inner readings, so that a reader can see exactly where the record ends and the lens begins. The shorter version in the library's opening chapter is at [scene:s00g|IS-RA-EL: a symbolic reading].

The reading, stated in full

Read through this lens, the name divides into three syllables, and each is taken as a principle. IS is read as Isis, the great goddess of Egypt: in this reading, the divine feminine, the receptive, gathering, lunar principle. RA is read as the sun god of Egypt: the divine masculine, the radiant, generative, outward-moving principle. EL is the Most High, the Semitic word for God that stands above both. On this reading the name describes an architecture of consciousness in three tiers: two polarities, feminine and masculine, receptive and radiant, brought into union under the divine that contains them. "Israel", read this way, is not first a place or a lineage. It is a state a human being can arrive at, the condition of a person in whom the two sides have been integrated and turned toward the highest.

The lens then reads Genesis 32 as a picture of how that state is reached. Jacob is alone, at night, at a river crossing. He contends with a figure who is at once a man and divine, which in this reading is the meeting of the human self with the larger self it has avoided. He is wounded and does not let go. He asks for a blessing, not a victory. At dawn the sun rises on him and he receives a new name, and he names the place for what he saw: the face of God. In this reading the old name, the one who grasps at the heel of another, gives way to a name for the integrated person, who has stopped trying to be someone else, as Jonathan Sacks put it in his own terms, and stands upright. [cite:ts13_esv_gen32] [cite:ts13_sacks_be] The lens treats the story less as a record reserved for one line of descent than as a template open to anyone who undertakes the struggle.

Where the lens touches the documented traditions

The lens is the platform's own, but parts of it rest on documented teaching, and it is worth saying which. The idea that Israel names an inner condition is Philo's: for him Israel was "the man who sees God", and a kind of soul as well as a people. [cite:ts13_philo_mut] [cite:ts13_alesso_philo] The idea that Jacob is the one who holds opposed forces in balance is a teaching of Kabbalah, in which Jacob is Tiferet, the sefira that reconciles Chesed and Gevurah and whose planetary sign is the sun. [cite:ts13_chabad_luria] [cite:ts13_wiki_tiferet] The idea that the night was a struggle to become oneself is current in mainstream rabbinic commentary. [cite:ts13_sacks_be] What the lens adds is specific: the Egyptian names, the reading of the two poles as feminine and masculine, and the claim that this pattern is written into the syllables of the word. That addition is not found in Jewish or Christian commentary, and it is the part that philology does not support.

What philology says about the syllables

The objections are precise and should be stated in full. The name is Hebrew, Yisra'el, and its syllables do not divide as IS-RA-EL in the original script: the first element is a verb form built on a root such as sarah or sarar, and only the last element, El, is a divine name. [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname] The Hebrew scholar Michael Heiser, reviewing the reading in July 2008, set out three further problems. "Isis" is the Greek form of the goddess's name; the Egyptian was closer to "Awset". The middle syllable of the Hebrew name is written with the letters resh and aleph, while the Egyptian sun god's name, as it appears in Hebrew in the name of the pharaoh Hophra in Jeremiah 44:30, is written with ayin. And Hebrew and Egyptian belong to different branches of their language family, so their syllables do not line up. He conceded the third element: El is spelled with aleph and lamed, as the reading requires. [cite:ts13_heiser_pdf] [cite:ts13_heiser_maxwell] On the philology, then, IS and RA are a reading of the English spelling, and EL is the one element the reading shares with the etymology.

Who has proposed it

The origin of the IS-RA-EL reading could not be traced to a dated first source. The earliest proponent this volume could document is Jordan Maxwell, a lecturer who described himself as an "independent scholar in the field of occult / religious philosophy", dated his interest to 1959, was religion editor of Truth Seeker magazine for three and a half years and appeared on more than 600 radio programmes. [cite:ts13_sw_maxwell] Heiser's critique of July 2008 attributes the claim to him in these words: "Israel" derives from "three deity names: Isis-Ra-El". Heiser gives no title, lecture or year for Maxwell's statement, only his website, so the date of Maxwell's first use is not established. [cite:ts13_heiser_maxwell] [cite:ts13_heiser_pdf] A second current reads the syllables in a related but distinct way: the glossary of Glorian, a publisher of modern Gnostic teaching, reads IS as recalling "Isis and the Isiac Mysteries", RA as the solar Logos, and EL as "the inner, profound God within each one of us", and treats the people of Israel as the awakened parts of a person's inner being, with Egypt standing for enslavement to the ego. The entry is undated and unsigned. [cite:ts13_glorian] Beyond these, the reading circulates widely online in unsigned forms. No dated source earlier than Heiser's 2008 critique was found, and no ancient, rabbinic, patristic or medieval kabbalistic text that proposes it. The honest statement is that the reading is modern, that its first author is unknown, and that this platform has taken it up and labelled it.

Why the platform keeps it, and how

The platform's position is that a reader kept from the reading is not protected but left to meet it elsewhere without its limits. So it is given here with the philology beside it. What the lens offers is not an etymology but a symbolic key: a way of gathering into one memorable image the documented themes of the inner traditions, that the self must hold receptive and active together, and that the name of Israel, in the readings of Philo, the Kabbalah and modern rabbinic teaching, has long been understood to describe a transformation as well as a people. Read through this lens, the name holds together what many institutions separated. Read through philology, it is a Hebrew name about God's striving or ruling, explained in Genesis as Jacob's striving. A reader may hold the first as a meditation and the second as the record.

Two limits apply wherever the lens appears in this library. It never supplies evidence about any historical actor's intent, and nothing in this volume's account of Darby, Scofield, CUFI or any government depends on it. And it is never used to say that anyone has taken a name that belongs to someone else. The contrast the lens draws, examined in [scene:ts13_deed|the last section of this part], runs between an inward reading and a literal one, and both are found inside Judaism and inside Christianity.
