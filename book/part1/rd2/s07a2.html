Who Ran Palestine: The High Commissioner and the Seven Who Held the Office

The council's failure left one office at the center of Palestine's government. The Mandate is often written about as though "the British government" ran Palestine as a single, faceless machine. The documents show something more particular. From 1920 to 1948 the country was governed day to day by a sequence of seven appointed High Commissioners, with acting commissioners filling the gaps between them, and each of them made decisions that can be dated and traced. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine] Herbert Samuel opened the office in 1920. His successors, Plumer, Chancellor, Wauchope, MacMichael, Gort and Cunningham, presided in turn over the riots of 1929, the Arab Revolt, the years of the 1939 White Paper, the Jewish armed campaign against British rule, and the withdrawal of 1948. What follows describes the office itself, the first man to hold it, whose decisions fall inside these years, and, more briefly, the six who came after.

The office drew its power from the Mandate itself. Article 1 gave the Mandatory "full powers of legislation and of administration." [cite:rs_md_un_origins] Britain exercised those powers through a High Commissioner based in Jerusalem, at the head of a civil government that ran everything from passports and currency to police, courts and railways, and that employed Arabs and Jews alike. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] Above him stood London and, more distantly, the League of Nations. The Colonial Office conducted the negotiations over Palestine's constitution in 1922, and the Mandatory reported to the League on its administration. [cite:rs_lc_cmd1700] [cite:rs_lc_report1923] Beside the administration, Article 4 of the Mandate recognized the Zionist Organization as the "appropriate Jewish agency," a public body to advise it. [cite:rs_md_avalon]

What the office lacked tells as much as what it held. The constitution of 1922 placed the High Commissioner at the head of a Legislative Council of twenty-two members, ten of them officials and twelve elected. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] That council never sat. After the boycott of the 1923 elections, Britain governed with an advisory council of British officials only, and the High Commissioner ruled without an elected legislature for the rest of the Mandate. [cite:rs_lc_albright] [cite:rs_lc_cojs_boycott] How that happened is told in [scene:rd2_council|the account of the Legislative Council]. Its effect on the office was lasting: for the rest of the Mandate, the "full powers of legislation and of administration" that Article 1 gave the Mandatory were exercised in Palestine through the High Commissioner without any elected body beside him.

The first man to hold this office had a direct link to the Balfour Declaration. Herbert Samuel was a British Cabinet minister and an advocate of Zionism, and a memorandum he wrote in 1915 influenced the Balfour Declaration itself. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel] He was appointed in 1920 by the same British Cabinet that had issued the declaration, and by many accounts he was the first Jewish governor of the land since Roman times. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel_hc] [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] The appointment was itself a point of contemporary debate. It sharpened Arab suspicion that the Mandate was built to deliver the Balfour promise, and it placed inside a single administration the double charge of fostering a Jewish national home while safeguarding the rights of the Arab majority. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] He served until 1925, and the administrative and legal foundations he laid framed the work of every administration after him. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel] [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

Samuel's record in office does not fit either of the simple portraits drawn of him. According to J. B. Schechtman's account in the Encyclopaedia Judaica, one of his first acts, in July 1920, was an amnesty for those imprisoned over the Jerusalem riots of that April, Jews and Arabs alike. [cite:rs_hg_ej_jabotinsky] He also pardoned Amin al-Husseini, whom a British military tribunal had convicted in his absence after the same violence. [cite:rs_mu_ushmm] [cite:rs_mu_jvl] What became of Jabotinsky's sentence under the amnesty is disputed, as told with [scene:ri_s01|the riots of 1920 and 1921]. Samuel lent his name, as Honorary President, to the Pro-Jerusalem Society of Ronald Storrs, Jerusalem's military governor until 1920 and its civil governor from that year. [cite:rs_al_ashbee1921] [cite:rs_al_openjerusalem] In 1921 he made Amin al-Husseini Mufti of Jerusalem, after recording Amin's pledge to keep the city calm and after Storrs, at his request, secured a rival candidate's withdrawal. [cite:rs_mu_cojs_samuel_note] [cite:rs_mu_cojs_klein] On 20 December 1921 the Supreme Moslem Sharia Council Order created the council over which Amin would preside. [cite:rs_mu_smc_order] Whether the Mufti's appointment was a manipulation of an election or a judgment about public order is set out in [scene:rd2_mufti|the account of the Mufti's appointment].

The violence of 1921 tested the office early. After the Jaffa riots, on 15 May 1921, Samuel temporarily prohibited Jewish immigration, and on 3 June he tied future immigration to the country's economic capacity, the principle the 1922 White Paper would write down. [cite:rs_ta_cojs_whitepaper] He created a mixed Arab-Jewish militia, which the last 400 Palestinian Legionnaires of the Jewish Legion joined. [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] On 1 September 1922 he proclaimed the Palestine Order in Council in force, the constitution under which the Mandate was to be governed. [cite:rs_lc_order1922] When the Legislative Council it created failed in 1923, the office he handed on governed with officials alone. [cite:rs_lc_albright]

Taken together, these acts resist a simple portrait. A Zionist by conviction, chosen by the Cabinet that issued the Balfour Declaration, Samuel suspended Jewish immigration after Jaffa and backed as Mufti a man he had pardoned. The documents support neither the portrait of a governor who simply served the national home nor its opposite.

The six who followed him inherited the office he had shaped and held it through crises that belong to later chapters of this book. They are told here only as the record gives them, to show how the office ran in practice.

Field Marshal Herbert Plumer, who came to the post from command of the British Expeditionary Force's Second Army, served from 1925 to 1928. He was named without consultation with the World Zionist Organization, yet he was regarded as committed to fairness toward Zionist aspirations. When he died in 1932, a Jewish Telegraphic Agency obituary described his tenure as "the only period when complete tranquility existed in Palestine." [cite:jta_plumer_obituary] [cite:toi_diaries_1929_officials] That is a contemporary judgment of comparative calm, set against what came before and after, not a claim that those three years were free of tension.

Sir John Chancellor served from December 1928 to September 1931, a period of what has been described as "tremendous tension." It began with a dispute at the Western Wall on Yom Kippur 1928 and culminated in the Hebron Massacre of 24 August 1929. Chancellor was not in the country when the massacre happened. He was on home leave in England through the critical period of July and August 1929. Harry Luke served as acting High Commissioner throughout, and Chancellor returned to Jerusalem only on 29 August 1929, five days after the killings. [cite:toi_diaries_1929_officials] [cite:encyclopedia_hc_palestine] Two facts stand side by side here, and both are real: his absence during the acute crisis, and his responsibility as head of the administration whose wider policies formed its setting. The episode also shows how the office worked when its holder was away. Authority did not lapse; it passed to an acting commissioner, who carried it through the massacre itself.

Arthur Wauchope held the office from 1931 to 1938, through years of rising Jewish immigration and the outbreak of the Arab Revolt of 1936–39. Historians generally regard him as more sympathetic to Zionist settlement than his successors, and his approach has been called relatively conciliatory. He resigned as the revolt intensified. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine] The record used here gives his general reputation and the crisis that ended his term, not an itemised account of his decisions.

Harold MacMichael, from 1938 to 1944, administered Palestine as the 1939 White Paper was put into effect and through the early years of Jewish paramilitary resistance. In 1944 Lehi, also known as the Stern Gang, attempted to assassinate him in an ambush on his motorcade. He survived, wounded; his wife was seriously injured. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

The shortest tenure, and the one most often left out of popular accounts, belonged to Field Marshal John Vereker, 6th Viscount Gort. He served from November 1944 to November 1945 and retired because of ill health after barely a year. He died the following year, 1946. [cite:wiki_john_vereker_gort] His brevity is itself the notable fact. The record does not attribute major policy shifts to him, and this book does not claim any.

The last was Alan Cunningham, from 1945 to 1948. He presided over the most violent period of Jewish paramilitary resistance, including the bombing of the King David Hotel, and over the chaotic British withdrawal from Palestine in May 1948. [cite:wiki_hc_list_palestine]

Naming all seven matters because twenty-eight years of rule did not run on a single setting. Each administration made its own choices within the broader policy framework: how firmly to enforce limits on immigration, how to respond to violence coming from more than one direction, and even whether to be present or on leave when a crisis broke. [cite:encyclopedia_hc_palestine] Treating the period as one undifferentiated British policy erases a calm three years under one man, an absence during a massacre under another, and an assassination survived under a third. The opposite error is just as real. No one High Commissioner's character explains the Mandate's outcome. Each worked inside a much larger structure: a Mandate held by Britain under the League, directed from London, and from 1923 governed without an elected chamber in Palestine.

Some of what a reader would want to know is not in the record used here. It does not name every acting commissioner who filled the gaps between tenures, beyond Harry Luke in 1929. It does not show, for any of the seven, how much of what he did was his own decision and how much followed instructions from the Colonial Office. It does not give the content of Samuel's 1915 memorandum, only that it influenced the Balfour Declaration. And for Wauchope, MacMichael, Gort and Cunningham it gives a reputation and a few dated events rather than a full account of their administrations; those belong to the later chapters in which their crises fall.
