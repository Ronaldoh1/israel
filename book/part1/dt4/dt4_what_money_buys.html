What Money Buys

The previous section showed how much is spent and by whom. Whether that spending buys anything is a separate question, and the evidence on it is mixed. It comes in three forms: studies of whether government policy follows the wishes of the affluent more than the wishes of everyone else; studies of whether the candidate with more money wins; and individual races in which the heaviest spender won or lost. This section takes each in turn, then sets out the money trail as far as the record supports it.

Whose preferences become policy

The most cited study is by the political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page, published in 2014. They examined 1,779 policy questions from 1981 to 2002 and found that average citizens had little or no independent influence once the preferences of economic elites and organised interest groups were taken into account. A proposed change with low support among the affluent was adopted about 18 per cent of the time; one with high support, about 45 per cent. Business groups carried about twice the weight of mass-membership groups. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] Answering critics in 2016, the authors held that none of the objections undermined their central claim that "Americans who are less well off have essentially no influence over what their government does". [cite:de7_gp_reply]

The critics did not deny the pattern; they disputed its size and meaning. Peter Enns found that the preferences of middle-income and affluent Americans were correlated at 0.94 across 1,836 survey questions, so that the middle usually gets what it wants because it wants the same thing as the affluent; he called this "coincidental representation". [cite:ad15_enns] Omar Bashir found that when median-income Americans favoured a change by 80 per cent or more, it happened 47 per cent of the time, against 52 per cent for the affluent. [cite:ad15_bashir] Alexander Branham, Stuart Soroka and Christopher Wlezien found that the rich and the middle class agree about 90 per cent of the time, and that when they disagree the rich win 53 per cent of the time, "only a little more often than the middle". [cite:de7_branham_ifs] Both readings rest on the same data. On the first, the affluent decide and the rest are carried along. On the second, the affluent have an edge that is real but modest, and most of the apparent capture is agreement.

Whether the bigger spender wins

The candidate who raises the most usually wins, but that does not show that money is the reason. Adam Bonica of Stanford studied all 5,005 congressional candidates from 2010 to 2014 and found that simply predicting the top fundraiser would win picked the winner in 79 per cent of contested primaries, against 58 per cent for a model built on candidates' backgrounds. His paper treats this as prediction, not cause: money flows to candidates who were already likely to win, and early money flows through professional networks, which helps explain why lawyers were about 99 times more likely than the average citizen to be elected to Congress. [cite:dt4_bonica_2016]

Studies that try to isolate the effect of outside money find it smaller than the totals suggest. Christian Cox of the University of Arizona modelled House elections from 2002 to 2018 and estimated what would have happened without super PACs. In his model, Republicans' share of the general-election vote would have fallen by about 2.2 per cent, roughly six or seven seats a cycle, though the margin of error crosses zero; super PACs had mostly backed challengers; and Cox described their overall effect as "muted" by competition, because each side's spending provokes the other's. [cite:dt4_cox_2024] It is an unreviewed working paper covering only the House.

When the biggest spender lost

Individual races show the limits as plainly as the studies. Michael Bloomberg spent more than $1 billion of his own money seeking the 2020 Democratic nomination and won only American Samoa before withdrawing on 4 March 2020. [cite:dt4_wiki_bloomberg2020] In Wisconsin's Supreme Court election on 1 April 2025, Elon Musk spent more than $20 million backing Brad Schimel, about one dollar in five in the most expensive court race in American history, and Schimel lost by ten points. [cite:dt4_npr_wisc2025] In 2026, AIPAC's United Democracy Project spent more than $30.6 million in Michigan's Democratic Senate primary for Haley Stevens and against Abdul El-Sayed, and El-Sayed won; in the Illinois primaries of 17 March 2026, AIPAC-linked spending of more than $21 million backed candidates who won two of four House races. [cite:pm_itt_elsayed] [cite:pm_prospect_illinois] Peter Thiel's $15 million each for J. D. Vance and Blake Masters carried both through their 2022 Senate primaries; Vance won the general election and Masters lost his. [cite:de15_cnbc_results] [cite:de15_wiki_az2022]

Other spenders have better records. Fairshake had backed the winner in 33 of the 35 House and Senate primaries it entered by June 2024, including more than $10 million against Katie Porter in California's Senate primary. [cite:dt4_wiki_fairshake] A group that chooses where to spend can pick races it expects to win, so a high win rate shows selection at least as much as influence, and neither a single defeat nor a single victory measures what money does across all races.

The money trail

Set out as a chain, the record supports some links firmly and others only as one of two readings. What is documented is the input, the legal mechanisms, the concentration of donors, and the advantage of well-funded candidates in primaries. What is contested is how much policy follows the money once a candidate takes office, and therefore who, if anyone, bears a measurable cost. [cite:dt4_os_2024record] [cite:dt4_bonica_2016] [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] [cite:de7_branham_ifs]

Public opinion is less divided than the research. In a Pew survey of 8,480 adults in July 2023, 72 per cent favoured limits on what individuals and organisations can spend on campaigns and 11 per cent opposed them; 80 per cent said donors have too much influence on Congress; Pew reported large majorities of Republicans and Democrats alike holding that view, and support for limits outnumbering opposition by at least three to one in every group it measured. [cite:dt4_pew_money2023] In a 2018 University of Maryland survey, about three-quarters backed a constitutional amendment to overturn Citizens United, including 66 per cent of Republicans and 85 per cent of Democrats. [cite:dt4_time_umd] The rules on money, in other words, were set by courts against the stated preference of most voters in both parties.

The test

Nick Land's 2012 text holds that "winning elections is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying", and proposes to convert the whole landscape of lobbying into shares in a state run as a company. [cite:de7_land_de] Measured against this part's facts, the claim splits. Documented: political money is large, rising and concentrated, more than $1 billion of it in 2024 came from sources voters cannot see, and both parties' congressional leaders depend on it. [cite:dt4_os_2024record] [cite:dt4_issueone_2026] Probable but disputed: that policy follows the affluent more than everyone else, since the Gilens and Page finding and its critics read the same data differently. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] [cite:de7_branham_ifs] Not supported: that elections are "overwhelmingly" bought. The best estimates of super PAC effects are modest, and the heaviest spenders of 2020, 2025 and 2026 lost races they paid heavily to win. [cite:dt4_cox_2024] [cite:dt4_wiki_bloomberg2020] [cite:dt4_npr_wisc2025] [cite:pm_itt_elsayed]

The question of whose failure this is turns on a design fact. The present system was not chosen by voters; it was built by the courts from 1976 to 2026, and majorities in both parties say they would limit it. [cite:dt4_wiki_buckley] [cite:dt4_wiki_cu] [cite:dt7_skadden_nrsc] [cite:dt4_pew_money2023] [cite:dt4_time_umd] That supports the book's reading that this is a failure of an unfinished democracy rather than of democracy as such. The record also cuts against the book. If money's measured effect on who wins is modest, and if most voters already agree with the affluent most of the time, then taking money out would change less than its advocates promise. [cite:dt4_cox_2024] [cite:ad15_enns] Land's own answer, converting influence into tradable shares, would not remove the money; it would formalise it, a point argued in [scene:de7_the_other_diagnosis|The Dark Enlightenment Architecture].
