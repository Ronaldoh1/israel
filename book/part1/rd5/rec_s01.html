Who Recognizes a Nation?

On May 14, 1948, eleven minutes after Israel declared its independence, one man in Washington decided that the United States would recognize it. He did so by press release. Some remember American recognition as an act of Congress. It was not. The decision belonged to a single office.

The constitutional basis is a short clause. Article II, Section 3 provides that the President "shall receive Ambassadors and other public Ministers." [cite:const_art2s3] In Zivotofsky v. Kerry (2015), the Supreme Court held that this clause gives the President the exclusive power to recognize foreign sovereigns. [cite:scotus_zivotofsky] Read through that ruling, the power Truman used in 1948 was the President's alone.

Truman used it on the day of the declaration. Eleven minutes after Israel's proclamation, he announced de facto recognition, acceptance of the new government as a working reality, in a short press release. [cite:truman_recognition] Full legal, or de jure, recognition followed on January 31, 1949. In the interval, as an earlier chapter noted, the Soviet Union became the first state to extend full legal recognition, on 17 May 1948 ([scene:so_s01|Moscow's recognition]).

Congress had no vote on the question. Its role was indirect: resolutions of support, appropriations, and the Senate's confirmation of ambassadors, including the first American ambassador to the new state. These were real powers. None of them decided whether the United States would recognize Israel. That choice was made in the White House.

It was also made against the written advice of the government's own senior officials. The Secretary of State, the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary and the CIA had all advised against it ([scene:ore_s01|the CIA's warning]). Their warnings, from the State Department, the Pentagon and the newly created intelligence agency, are traced earlier in this part of the book. Truman overruled them.

The constitutional point gives the story of 1948 its shape. Because recognition rested with the President alone, months of argument over partition, over trusteeship, and over oil and strategy came down in the end to one decision-maker. Advisers could warn, Congress could pass resolutions and advocates could press their case. Only the President could decide.
