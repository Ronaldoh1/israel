16 June 1963: Ben-Gurion Resigns

In the middle of June 1963 a letter from Kennedy to Ben-Gurion was waiting to be delivered. Sent as telegram 938 and dated 15 June, it set out the schedule of visits to Dimona that the State Department had drafted four days earlier: one in mid-summer 1963, another in June 1964, and visits every six months after that. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] It was never handed over. Ben-Gurion announced his resignation, and the letter had no addressee. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That undelivered letter is the only point at which the documents connect the battle of the letters, told in [scene:s11b|the previous section], to the end of Ben-Gurion's premiership. Whether there was any connection beyond the timing is the question this section has to answer, and the honest answer is that the record does not show one.

The date. Even the day is given differently by different sources. The document reproduced in the National Security Archive's collection gives 16 June 1963 as the day Ben-Gurion stepped down. The editors' own introduction to the same collection gives 15 June. Wikipedia's infobox gives 26 June 1963 as the end of his term. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] The three dates need not all describe the same act. The first two refer to the resignation itself, and this book follows the document's 16 June; the third plausibly marks the day the successor government took office, which would end his term in the formal sense. But the date on which Levi Eshkol's government was sworn in has not been confirmed in the research for this stage, and 26 June is therefore given here as Wikipedia gives it, not as an established fact. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion]

What Ben-Gurion said. He gave "personal reasons," and he never explained them publicly. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] The resignation was unexpected. According to Wikipedia's account, the cabinet asked him to reconsider, but, unlike in 1953, few serious efforts were made to dissuade him. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] He chose his own successor, Levi Eshkol. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion]

Because Ben-Gurion said so little, every other explanation is someone else's. There are three, and they rest on evidence of very different kinds.

The first explanation: exhaustion and isolation. The historian Yechiam Weitz, as summarized in Wikipedia's account, attributes the resignation to Ben-Gurion's political isolation, his suspicion of colleagues and rivals, an inability to engage with the full range of reality, and a belief that his life's work was disintegrating. Weitz suggests that the decision may reflect Ben-Gurion's state of mind at the time. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] This is a historian's interpretation, not a document; it is reached here through a secondary summary, not Weitz's own text. But it is consistent with the one thing Ben-Gurion said, "personal reasons," and with the cabinet's muted response.

The second explanation: Germany. The CIA believed at the time that Ben-Gurion's conciliatory policy toward West Germany was a cause of his resignation. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] This is a contemporary American intelligence judgment, recorded in the declassified documents; it shows what the agency believed, not what moved Ben-Gurion. Its context was real. In the first months of 1963 Israel's government had been divided over the German scientists working in Egypt. Israel State Archives documents released in 2013, as reported by Israel Hayom, show that the "fear campaign" against those scientists was exposed in March 1963 and that Isser Harel, who headed the Mossad, "had to resign" after clashing with Ben-Gurion and Shimon Peres, who believed the campaign would rupture relations with West Germany. [cite:rs_cw5_israelhayom_2013] In the Knesset debate reported on 12 April 1963, Menachem Begin said Ben-Gurion had "forced" the security chief's resignation. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630412] Harel, who had headed the Shin Bet from 1948 and the Mossad from 1952, was "forced to resign in 1963." [cite:rs_cw5_enc_harel] That crisis, told in [scene:cw5_damocles|the German scientists], set Ben-Gurion's German policy against his own intelligence chief and drew an attack from the opposition. It is background to the resignation. No source collected here states that it caused it.

The third explanation: Kennedy. Some Israeli leaders thought Kennedy's pressure over Dimona may have contributed. Pinhas Sapir and Israel Galili are named among them. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The historian Avner Cohen goes furthest, suggesting that the resignation "may have been triggered, in part, by Kennedy's pressure." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_findings] The wording is careful and should be kept as it is: "may have," "in part." Cohen offers it as a possibility, not a finding. The editors of the letters, of whom Cohen is one, do not claim that the pressure caused the resignation. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

What the record shows. No document collected for this stage shows that the letters caused the resignation. The strongest evidence for a connection is circumstantial. Kennedy's letter of 17 May had warned that American support "could be seriously jeopardized" without reliable information on Dimona; Ben-Gurion's reply of 27 May had asked for delay; a sharper letter with a fixed schedule was ready by 15 June; and Ben-Gurion resigned the next day. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Two of his senior colleagues and a historian who co-edited the letters have thought the pressure may have played a part. The strongest evidence against treating it as the cause is that Ben-Gurion never said so, that he did not receive the 15 June letter, that the CIA at the time looked to Germany instead, and that the one historian of his state of mind cited here looks to his isolation. The sequence of dates is real. A sequence is not a cause, and none of these sources presents documentary proof that one followed from the other.

The question a reader is most likely to ask, whether Kennedy's pressure made Ben-Gurion resign, therefore has a plain answer on the present record: it may have contributed, in the view of some who were close to the events, and no document shows that it did.

What followed for the letters is clear. Kennedy did not wait long for the new government. On 4 July 1963 he sent Eshkol the same text and schedule that Ben-Gurion had never received, and the argument resumed with a new prime minister, as told in [scene:cw5_eshkol_agreement|Eshkol's answer]. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

After the resignation. Ben-Gurion did not leave politics. A year later he insisted that the Lavon affair be investigated and Eshkol refused; in 1965 he failed to unseat Eshkol, broke with Mapai in June 1965 to found Rafi, and lost to the Alignment in the election of November 1965. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_bengurion] That struggle belongs to Stage Six.

The record leaves the central question open. Ben-Gurion's "personal reasons" were never explained, and the sources reached here do not include any statement of his own that ties the resignation to Washington, to Germany or to anything else. The exact date on which his successor's government took office remains to be confirmed. What the documents establish is narrower: a letter was waiting, and the man it was addressed to resigned before it arrived.
