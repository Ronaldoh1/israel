The Mediator: Count Bernadotte's Two Plans, May–September 1948

In the first weeks of the war of 1948 the United Nations named a mediator. For four months, from late May to mid-September 1948, one man carried the organization's attempt to stop the fighting and to sketch a settlement. He produced two sets of proposals, very different from each other. Neither was accepted. He was killed the day after the second was dated. Yet his last report outlived him, and two of its proposals reappeared in the General Assembly's Resolution 194 three months later.

The man and the appointment. Folke Bernadotte came to the post with a reputation made in the last months of the Second World War. As head of the Swedish Red Cross he is credited with saving some 20,000 inmates of German concentration camps. [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte] He was appointed United Nations mediator in Palestine on 20 May 1948. [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte] [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio]

The sources differ, in a small way, on who appointed him. The UN's own press release of 17 September 1948 records that he was chosen by the five permanent members of the Security Council, sitting as a committee of the General Assembly under a General Assembly resolution of 14 May 1948. [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio] Encyclopaedia Britannica says more simply that the Security Council named him. [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte] The difference is not trivial for a reader trying to understand where his authority came from. On the UN's account his mandate flowed from a General Assembly resolution, with the five permanent members acting as its committee. On the shorter account it came from the Council. The UN's own contemporary record is the more detailed of the two, but this book records both.

The two truces. His first task was to stop the shooting. The first truce he negotiated took effect on 11 June 1948 and lasted until 9 July. [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio] On 15 July the Security Council ordered a second truce, which was to last "until a peaceful adjustment of the future situation of Palestine is reached." [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio] The difference in wording mattered. The first truce was a pause with an end date, negotiated by the mediator. The second was ordered by the Council and had no end date at all: it was meant to hold until there was a settlement. It was inside that open-ended truce that Bernadotte wrote his final report.

The Rhodes suggestions of 28 June 1948. During the first truce, on 28 June 1948, Bernadotte presented the parties with a set of written suggestions at Rhodes. The American government's published diplomatic record preserves their text. [cite:rs_rd6_frus_bernadotte_june] They did not start from the partition plan. They proposed that Palestine, including Transjordan, might form a union of "one Arab and one Jewish" member. Within that union, the Negev, in whole or in part, would go to Arab territory, and Western Galilee, in whole or in part, to Jewish territory. The City of Jerusalem would be included in Arab territory, with municipal autonomy for its Jewish community. Each member would control immigration within its own borders. [cite:rs_rd6_frus_bernadotte_june]

Two shared facilities would serve the whole union: a free port at Haifa, including the refineries and terminals, and a free airport at Lydda. And the suggestions proposed recognition of the right of people displaced by the conflict "to return to their homes without restriction and to regain possession of their property." [cite:rs_rd6_frus_bernadotte_june] That phrase is the first appearance in this chapter of the language of return. It was unconditional. It set no date, required no prior peace, and offered no alternative of compensation.

According to the mediator's own later report, both parties "flatly rejected" the June suggestions. [cite:rs_rd6_palquest_progress] The sources gathered here record the rejection but not the parties' own statements of their reasons. What the text itself shows is what each side was being asked to accept. The Jewish side was asked to accept a union rather than an independent state with its own frontiers, and to see Jerusalem, with its Jewish community, placed in Arab territory with only municipal autonomy. The Arab side was asked to accept a Jewish member of a joint union, with its own control of immigration. That is a reading of the text, not a record of either side's stated objections. Whatever reasons each gave, the mediator's second plan started from different premises.

The Progress Report of 16 September 1948. The report Bernadotte completed in September, dated 16 September 1948, began from a premise the June suggestions had avoided: "A Jewish State called Israel exists in Palestine." [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_progress_report] [cite:rs_rd6_palquest_progress] The union with Transjordan was gone. In its place the report drew a territorial settlement between a recognized Jewish state and the Arab territory around it.

On territory it reversed part of the June scheme and kept part. The Negev should be Arab territory and Galilee should be Jewish territory. Ramleh and Lydda should be in Arab territory. The precise frontiers would be set by a UN technical boundaries commission. Jerusalem would no longer be placed in Arab territory; it would be under "effective United Nations control." Haifa would be a free port and Lydda a free airport, as in June. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_progress_report] [cite:rs_rd6_palquest_progress]

On the refugees, the report urged that Arab refugees be allowed to return to Jewish-controlled areas "at the earliest possible date," with adequate compensation for those choosing not to return. It proposed a Palestine conciliation commission to carry the settlement forward. And on the Arab territory, it saw "compelling reasons for merging the Arab territory of Palestine with the territory of Transjordan." [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_progress_report] [cite:rs_rd6_palquest_progress] The idea of joining Arab Palestine to Transjordan survived from June, but now as a merger of the Arab part alone, not a union that took in the Jewish state as well.

Set side by side, the two plans show how far the mediator moved in eleven weeks. The June suggestions treated Palestine and Transjordan as one framework to be divided into two members. The September report accepted Israel as a state, gave it Galilee, assigned the Negev, Ramleh and Lydda to Arab territory, removed Jerusalem from either side's control, and turned the right of return "without restriction" into return "at the earliest possible date," paired for the first time with compensation as the alternative.

The assassination. Bernadotte was killed in Jerusalem on 17 September 1948, the day after the report was dated, by members of the Stern Gang (Lehi). A French air force colonel serving as a UN observer, André-Pierre Sérot, was killed with him. [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte] [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio] The UN noted that the completed report reached Paris almost simultaneously with the news of his death. [cite:rs_rd6_un_bernadotte_bio] The government's response to the killing, including the dissolution of Lehi and the amnesty that followed, belongs to [scene:mil_s01|the absorption of the undergrounds into one army].

What the report left behind. A plan that both parties rejected in June and that its author did not live to defend in September might have died with him. It did not, for two reasons. The first was Washington. Four days after the killing, on 21 September 1948, Secretary of State George Marshall endorsed the plan, including its award of the Negev to the Arab side, and the endorsement became an issue in an American presidential campaign; how President Truman then backed away from it is told in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's decisions after the election money]. The second was the General Assembly. Two of the report's central proposals, a conciliation commission and the return of refugees with compensation for those who chose not to return, reappeared in the Assembly's Resolution 194 of December 1948, whose full text and competing readings are set out in [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194]. Britannica also credits Bernadotte's work with laying the groundwork for the UN Truce Supervision Organization and for UNRWA, the relief agency for Palestinian refugees. [cite:rs_rd6_britannica_bernadotte]

What the record does not settle. The documents here establish what Bernadotte proposed, when, and that both sides rejected his first plan. They do not record, in the parties' own words, why each rejected it, nor how either side responded formally to the September report before events overtook it. Nor do they settle the narrower question of which UN body appointed him; the UN's contemporary account and Britannica's summary differ, and both are recorded above. What is beyond doubt is the direction of travel between June and September. A mediator who had begun by trying to fold the new state into a union with Transjordan ended by taking its existence as the starting point, and by proposing the refugees' return, with compensation as its alternative, in terms the General Assembly took up in Resolution 194.
