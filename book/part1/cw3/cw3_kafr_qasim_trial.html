The Kafr Qasim Trial and the Black Flag, 1957–1960

The killing of villagers at Kafr Qasim (47, 48 or 49, by different counts) on 29 October 1956, told in [scene:mg_s01|the previous section], was kept from the Israeli public for weeks. When it could no longer be kept, the state put the men who had done the shooting on trial before its own military court. The trial produced two results that have been argued over ever since. It convicted eight members of the Border Police of killing Israeli citizens, and its presiding judge wrote a test for unlawful orders that the Israeli army still teaches. Yet within about a year of the verdict every one of the men was out of prison, and the brigade commander who had set the curfew was tried separately, for procedural offences only, and fined a sum so small that it became a byword. Both results are documented, and neither cancels the other.

The court and the charge. The case was heard by a military court presided over by Judge Benjamin Halevy, as case MD/57/3, the Military Advocate against Major Shmuel Malinki and 10 others. [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The Jewish Virtual Library gives eleven Border Police officers and men as charged; Ynet, writing in 2014, says thirteen were tried. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] [cite:rs_cw3_ynet_rivlin] The case title, Malinki "and 10 others," matches the figure of eleven. [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] The historian Danny Orbach, who worked from the court's protocol, footnotes sessions running from March 1957 to January 1958. [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] The trial therefore ran for the better part of a year.

The verdict, 16 October 1958. The court gave its judgment on 16 October 1958, almost two years after the killing. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported it from Tel Aviv on 24 October. Eight of the accused were convicted. Major Shmuel Malinki, who had relayed the order to his men, was sentenced to 17 years. Lieutenant Gabriel Dahan, whose platoon had fired at the village entrance, and Lance Corporal Shalom Offer received 15 years each. Corporals Gavriel Ulliel and Edmond Nachmani and Privates Makhluf Karoush, Eliyahu Avraham and Albert Pahims received seven years each. The officers were stripped of their rank. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_verdict] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq]

The judgment set out why. As the JTA dispatch quoted it, the court held that an officer "should display courage not only in the face of the enemy." It stated its "clear and unreserved belief" that it is forbidden for a soldier in any army to "kill without due process of law." And it found that Malinki had extended an illegal order "by including women and children." [cite:rs_cw3_djn_verdict] The last finding matters for the question of where responsibility lay. On the court's reading the order Malinki received was already illegal, and he made it worse in passing it down.

The black flag. The passage for which the trial is remembered is Halevy's test of when a soldier must refuse an order. As Leora Bilsky quotes it, "Illegality that pierces the eye and revolts the heart" overrides the duty to obey. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] The image the ruling gave the doctrine is a flag: a "manifestly illegal" order is one over which "a black flag flies," and soldiers must refuse it. That standard is still taught in the Israeli army. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] The doctrine answered, in law, the defence that every defendant at Kafr Qasim could make: that he had done what he was told. The court accepted that the men had received orders and held that these were orders no soldier could lawfully carry out.

Orbach's reading of how the doctrine was made is worth setting beside the usual account, which credits it to the bench. In his study of the trial he argues that the black-flag doctrine emerged from the clash between the trial's attorneys, its defendants and its judges, not from the judges alone, and that the trial as a whole reshaped relations between Israel's army and its civilian state, and the civic standing of the country's Arab minority. [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] On his account the doctrine was the product of an adversarial process.

The reactions. The JTA dispatch of October 1958 recorded how different parts of Israeli society, and its neighbours, took the verdict. The defendants' families campaigned for pardons. The Chief of Staff, Chaim Laskov, told the chief military prosecutor to study whether further trials were needed, including of Shadmi. The opposition party Herut said the verdict had "left confusion in the minds of soldiers." In Kafr Qasim, the village elders called it "courageous" and "just." Across the line, the Jordanian newspaper Falastin praised the court's "firmness," but alleged that Shadmi had been smuggled out of the country. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_verdict] The elders' judgment deserves notice. The people with the most reason to doubt the court said, in October 1958, that it had done justice; what followed in the next year would test that view.

Shadmi's trial. Issachar Shadmi, the brigade commander who had changed the curfew hours, and whom Malinki had testified told him he wanted no sentimentality and no arrests, was not tried with the men below him. He was tried separately, by a special court-martial. Orbach notes that the prosecutor, Brigadier General Moshe David, did not hold Shadmi responsible for the killings and charged him only with procedural offences. [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] In February 1959, as Wikipedia's account has it, Shadmi was acquitted of murder and convicted of extending the curfew without authority. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The two accounts sit uneasily together, since one says no killing charge was brought and the other records an acquittal of murder; the sources used here do not resolve the point.

"Shadmi's penny." The penalty was a fine, and the sources describe it in at least five ways. Wikipedia, Yuval Ben-Ami in +972 Magazine and Tom Segev (as quoted on the site Hyehudi) all give 10 prutot. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_972_benami] [cite:rs_cw3_hyehudi_leibowitz] The pruta was the thousandth part of the Israeli lira, so 10 prutot was one hundredth of a lira, which is how this library's summary of the period states it. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The Jewish Virtual Library also gives 10 prutot but describes the sum as "1/1000 of an old Israeli pound," which contradicts its own figure, since 10 prutot was a hundredth. PalQuest gives "a single pruta (a thousandth of the Israeli pound)." Jonathan Cook calls it "a penny." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] The figure most sources share is ten prutot, one hundredth of a lira. Whatever the exact coin, the point on which every source agrees is that it was a token. Palestinian citizens of Israel remember it as "Shadmi's penny." [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre]

The reductions. The long sentences of October 1958 did not last. On 3 April 1959 the appeal court cut Malinki's sentence to 14 years and Dahan's to 10. The Chief of Staff then reduced them further, to 10 and 8. President Ben-Zvi pardoned or reduced the sentences of the others. With a one-third remission, according to Wikipedia, all of them were out of prison by about November 1959. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The Jewish Virtual Library states that "no one served more than three and a half years"; PalQuest, that "by 1960, all soldiers had been released." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] The three accounts are compatible in substance, and differ in emphasis: within about a year of a judgment that set seventeen years as the heaviest sentence, no one convicted of the killings remained in prison.

Afterwards. The two officers went on to official posts. According to Wikipedia's account, Malinki was put in charge of security at the Negev Nuclear Research Center, and Dahan was placed in charge of "Arab affairs" in Ramla in 1960. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] PalQuest describes the same pattern as the government having "compensated" the killers with official posts, including posts over Arab citizens in Lydda. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] The officer who had led the platoon that fired on the returning workers of Kafr Qasim was, four years later, responsible for Arab affairs in an Israeli town.

A protest from within. Not every Israeli voice in the debate that followed came from the defendants' side. Yeshayahu Leibowitz responded in Haaretz, in a passage Tom Segev quotes in The Seventh Million, with a sarcastic call for "a mass petition" to revise the Nuremberg judgments, since the men convicted there had also acted on orders. [cite:rs_cw3_hyehudi_leibowitz] One reading of the comparison is that it was meant to show where the argument for leniency led: if obedience excused the men of Kafr Qasim, it excused everyone who had ever pleaded it.

Accountability, or its absence? The trial supports two readings, and each rests on documents. The first holds it a landmark. An Israeli military court convicted Israeli soldiers of killing Arab citizens of Israel, in a judgment that said plainly no soldier in any army may "kill without due process of law," and in doing so it created the doctrine of the manifestly unlawful order, which has outlived every sentence it imposed. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_verdict] [cite:rs_cw3_orbach_2013] The elders of Kafr Qasim called the verdict just in 1958, and two later Israeli presidents, Rivlin and Herzog, are counted on this side of the argument. Orbach's argument that the trial reshaped civil–military relations gives this reading its strongest scholarly form.

The second reading looks at what happened to the people rather than to the doctrine. The commander who set the curfew and, on his subordinate's testimony, framed the order was charged only with procedural offences and fined a token sum. Every sentence was cut until no one convicted served more than three and a half years. And the two convicted officers were given posts in security and in Arab affairs. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] PalQuest calls the outcome "a mockery of justice." [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] On this view the doctrine was real, and so was the state's unwillingness to let it cost anyone very much.

The two readings are not, in the end, opposed claims about the facts; they agree on the facts and differ on which of them defines the trial. The record leaves some things unsettled. The number tried is given as eleven or thirteen. Whether Shadmi faced a murder charge at all is stated differently by Orbach and by Wikipedia's account. The exact coin of his fine varies by source. Who decided to try Shadmi apart from his subordinates, and on narrower charges, is not documented in the sources used here beyond the prosecutor's name, and Laskov's instruction to consider further trials is not followed, in these sources, by any record of a further trial. What the villagers were paid, the reconciliation ceremony the state organized in 1957, and the long argument over whether the killing was linked to a plan for the Triangle's population are told in [scene:cw3_kafr_qasim_memory|the sulha, Plan Mole and the memory of Kafr Qasim].
