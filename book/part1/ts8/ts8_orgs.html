The Embassy, the Bridges and the Fellowship

While the Moral Majority treated Israel as one cause among several, a set of smaller organisations made support for Israel their whole purpose. Three of them, founded or rooted in the 1960s to the 1980s, show the forms that work took: a Christian "embassy" in Jerusalem, a Jerusalem-based charity, and a Chicago fundraising body run by an Orthodox rabbi that became the largest channel of Christian money to Israel. Each is described here first in its own words, then by the documents and reporting about it.

The International Christian Embassy Jerusalem, 1980

The International Christian Embassy Jerusalem (ICEJ) dates itself to September 1980. In its own account, the Knesset had declared reunited Jerusalem the eternal and indivisible capital of the Jewish state, Arab oil states threatened an embargo against countries with embassies in the city, and the last 13 foreign embassies moved to Tel Aviv. A group of evangelicals then founded a "Christian Embassy" as an act of solidarity with what it called the Jewish people's "deep spiritual longing and attachment to this city." [cite:ts8_icej_about] [cite:ts8a_icej_about] The name and the location were chosen to make a point: unlike other international bodies, its members regarded Jerusalem as Israel's capital. [cite:ts8_wiki_icej]

The ICEJ describes its calling as a "ministry of comfort" to Israel and the Jewish people, based on Isaiah 40:1–2, and says it aspires "to develop pro-active expressions of Christian support for Israel in every nation on earth." [cite:ts8_icej_about] Its best-known event is an annual Christian celebration of the Feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem, which draws several thousand participants from almost 100 countries; its Israel and German branches are led by Jürgen Bühler. [cite:ts8_wiki_icej] The ICEJ was thus set up precisely because embassies left Jerusalem. Its stated position on the city is its own, not that of the governments whose citizens make up its branches. The embassy moves to Jerusalem from 2018 onward are taken up in [scene:ts12_embassy|Part Twelve].

Bridges for Peace

Bridges for Peace is a Jerusalem-based Christian charity that traces itself to Douglas Young, an American; a report of February 2015 described the organisation as marking its 50th year, and recalled that Young turned over his ministry's van for use as an ambulance as Israel prepared for war in May 1967. It describes its stance as "unconditional friendship to Israel and the Jewish community," and lists its work as serving Israel's needy, countering antisemitism and teaching Christians the history of Christian antisemitism. Through a US arm it has donated ambulances to Magen David Adom, Israel's emergency service; its international president in 2015 was Rebecca Brimmer. [cite:ts8_hfl_bfp] Other accounts give 1976 for the organisation's formal founding; no document establishing either date was found for this volume, so the year is left open. No audited figures for its scale were found.

The International Fellowship of Christians and Jews, 1983

The third model reversed the direction of the others. The International Fellowship of Christians and Jews (IFCJ) was founded in 1983 by Rabbi Yechiel Eckstein, an American Orthodox rabbi, to raise money from Christians for Jewish causes. By the time of his death at his home in Jerusalem in February 2019, aged 67, he had raised "hundreds of millions of dollars," mostly from Christians. [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein] Eckstein's own explanation, in a 2002 article quoted in the Jewish Telegraphic Agency's obituary, was that most evangelicals were pro-Israel "because it is part of their theology," and he said he could not accept "conditional love" from donors who expected a payback. [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein] [cite:ts8a_jta_eckstein]

The fellowship's sharpest critics were Israeli rabbis. In 2001 Chief Ashkenazi Rabbi Avraham Shapira published a letter condemning the use of Christian money to "expand Christian missionary propaganda," and Rabbi Yosef Shalom Elyashiv signed a letter forbidding cooperation with Eckstein, calling it "close to idolatry." The fellowship's funding of the Jewish Agency's immigration work stopped in 2014 amid a dispute over the agency's recognition of the fellowship, and Eckstein had long doubted the agency's efficiency; the IFCJ began its own immigration operation, offering each new immigrant a $1,000 grant on top of the Jewish Agency's benefits. [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein] [cite:ts8a_jta_eckstein] His daughter Yael Eckstein had become the fellowship's global executive vice-president before his death and is named as president and chief executive in its filings from 2020. [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein] [cite:ts8_pp_ifcj]

Because the IFCJ files a public Form 990 with the US tax authorities, its scale can be stated from its own returns rather than from claims. Its revenue was about $107 million in 2011 and stayed between $110 million and $133 million a year through 2019. It then rose steeply: $158 million in 2020, $220 million in 2021, $271 million in 2023 and $318 million in 2024, of which $307 million was contributions. In 2024 it reported expenses of $272 million and assets of $233 million. [cite:ts8_pp_ifcj] [cite:ts8a_pp_ifcj] The returns show how much was raised; they do not by themselves show what share came from evangelical donors, though the founder's obituary describes the donor base as mostly Christian. [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein]

Three models compared

Taken together, the three organisations show that "Christian support for Israel" was not one machine. The ICEJ made a political statement about Jerusalem its founding act; Bridges for Peace presented itself as a charity; the IFCJ was a Jewish-led fundraising body whose most determined opponents were senior rabbis in Israel, not critics of Israel. [cite:ts8_icej_about] [cite:ts8_hfl_bfp] [cite:ts8_jta_eckstein] None of them was set up as a lobby of the US Congress. That role, on a national scale, was taken in 2006 by a new organisation in San Antonio. [scene:ts8_cufi|Christians United for Israel]
