The modern conflict did not begin on an empty map. This chapter sets out what the record shows about the land before either national movement took shape. It comes here because two of the most repeated claims about this history, that one people was absent for two thousand years and that the other appeared only in the twentieth century, are claims about this period, and the documents answer them directly.

It begins with three thousand years in brief: the Merneptah stele of about 1207 BCE, which names Israel as a people; the Tel Dan and Mesha stelae of about 840 BCE; Rome's conquest, the two failed revolts and the renaming of the province as Syria Palaestina; and the history of the name "Palestine" from Herodotus to Jund Filastin.[cite:rs_ch01_cojs_merneptah] [cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] The next section shows Ottoman Palestine as an administered place. No province bore its name, but from 1872 the district of Jerusalem answered directly to Constantinople, and its three districts held roughly 600,000 people by the late nineteenth century.[cite:wiki_mutasarrifate_jerusalem] [cite:ips_ottoman_palestine] A section on the Old Yishuv follows the Jewish community that lived in the four holy cities before Zionism, about 7,000 people in 1800, and the long argument over whether Jews formed the majority of Jerusalem.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv] [cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] The chapter closes by separating three questions that are often run together: who lived on the land (the Ottoman census of 1871–72 counted 381,954 people, excluding the Bedouin), whose connection to it runs back unbroken, and when each modern national identity took shape.[cite:cjpme_historical_demography]

The disputes are real and are given in full: over Hadrian's motive in renaming Judaea, over how far Palestinian continuity runs, over which count of Jerusalem should decide a claim. What the chapter establishes beneath them is that both histories are documented, and that neither erases the other.
