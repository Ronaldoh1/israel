The Great Work

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The alchemists called their aim the Great Work, and they summed up its method in a phrase: solve et coagula, "dissolve and bind", the "continual cycling between fixed and volatile forms of matter". [cite:gn_pu_solve] From the beginning some of them understood the work as inner as well as outer. The oldest known books on alchemy, written around 300 by Zosimos of Panopolis, framed the transformation of metals as a mirror of inner purification. [cite:vt24_wiki_zosimos] The historian Kyle Fraser argues that Zosimos presented the alchemical dyeing of metals as a baptism that brings gnosis. [cite:vt24_fraser2007]

The inner reading follows Zosimos. The base metal is the ordinary self, heavy and dull; the furnace is the discipline that breaks it down; dissolution is the loosening of everything fixed in it; binding is its recombination in a new form; and the gold is not a metal but a self that does not tarnish. In this reading the alchemist's real laboratory is his own life, and the crucible is attention.

Part Four supplies the reading's sharpest image. For three centuries English kings treated the making of gold as a matter for the treasury: forbidden when it threatened the coin, licensed when the crown needed money. [cite:ht_loc_multipliers] Newton, who wrote more than a million words on the work, ended his life guarding the coin and hanging a counterfeiter. [cite:ht_neh_newton] The outer gold was always a matter of power, and whoever held power meant to control it. The inner gold of Zosimos's reading cannot be minted, licensed, counterfeited or seized. That is the difference between the two works, and it is why, on this shelf, the inner one is the one worth reading for.

The record limits the reading. Most alchemists worked with real metals and furnaces, and their writings are also the early history of chemistry; Newton's papers belong to that history as much as to any spiritual one. [cite:ht_neh_newton] The inner reading follows one strand of the tradition, the one that runs from Zosimos, and does not speak for the whole.
