The King David Hotel Bombing

On July 22, 1946, explosives tore through the southern wing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem. The bombing is sometimes remembered, where it is remembered at all, as a minor incident in a British office building. The record does not allow that description. The attack killed 91 people. It was carried out by an organization whose commander later became Prime Minister of Israel. It hardened British opinion toward leaving Palestine. And on its own documented merits, it stands among the most consequential single events of the Mandate, the period of British rule.

The attack came three weeks after the Black Sabbath raid. The Irgun, an underground militia commanded by Menachem Begin, detonated explosives in the basement of the southern wing, the part of the building that housed the British Mandate Secretariat and the military command. The explosives were reportedly hidden in milk churns. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The 91 dead included British, Arab and Jewish civilians and administrative staff. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] It was the deadliest single attack on the British during the entire Mandate period. The sources give the total toll and its spread across communities. They do not provide a full list of the victims.

As the previous chapter showed, the target was chosen for specific reasons. The Secretariat wing was the administrative and military center of British rule. Irgun intelligence believed that documents seized from the Jewish Agency, the body representing the Jewish community of Palestine, during Operation Agatha were being processed there. The bombing was meant both to destroy that evidence and to strike the administration that had carried out the raid.

Whether the people in the building were adequately warned is a question the record has not resolved. By the Irgun's own account, warning calls urging evacuation were placed about 25 minutes before the detonation, to the hotel switchboard, to the French consulate and to the Palestine Post newspaper. British investigators and later historians have disputed whether any warning reached the hotel's management in time to act, or whether it was dismissed as a hoax. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] Both accounts are documented. Neither has displaced the other, and this book does not choose between them.

The man who commanded the Irgun at the time had a long political future. Menachem Begin later served as Israel's Prime Minister, from 1977 to 1983. [cite:wiki_menachem_begin] The line from commanding this operation to holding the country's highest office is a matter of record. Begin's own later justifications and public statements about the bombing are not covered in the sources used here, and nothing should be inferred about them from this account.

Within the Jewish political leadership, the reaction was public. The Jewish Agency, whose seized documents had helped bring about the attack, publicly condemned the bombing. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The condemnation laid bare the tension between the mainstream political leadership and the armed groups. The sources document the public statement, not the private views of individual officials or the internal debate behind its wording.

In London the effect was significant. The bombing is documented as having markedly hardened British public and parliamentary opinion toward leaving Palestine altogether. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] In that way it contributed to Britain's decision in 1947 to hand the whole question to the United Nations. It was not the only cause; Britain faced several pressures at once. But it was one significant factor among them.

The future of Palestine was moving from a British problem to an international one. When the United Nations took up the question in 1947, the Jewish Agency was ready with a prepared case, and the other party chose not to appear. That story is told in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair].
