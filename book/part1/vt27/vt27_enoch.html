1 Enoch: The Book the New Testament Quotes

In Genesis, Enoch is a single paragraph: the seventh man in the line from Adam, who "walked with God" and then "was not, for God took him". Out of that gap grew one of the largest bodies of Jewish writing from the centuries before Yeshua, and its best-preserved form is the book Ethiopian Christians call Mashafa Henok and scholars call 1 Enoch. It is scripture for the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches and for the Beta Israel, the Jews of Ethiopia, and for no other living church or Jewish community. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] This section sets out what the book is, how it survived, what each of its five parts teaches and where scholars disagree.

What it is and how it survived

1 Enoch is not one book but five, written by different authors over about three centuries and later joined together. The oldest parts, the Book of the Watchers and the Astronomical Book, date to the third century BCE; the youngest, the Parables, is usually placed between the late first century BCE and the first century CE. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] It was written in Aramaic. Fragments of eleven Aramaic manuscripts were found in Cave 4 at Qumran from 1948 onward and published by Józef Milik in 1976; they cover every part of the book except the Parables. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] A Greek translation existed in antiquity, and a sixth-century Greek manuscript of chapters 1–32, the Codex Panopolitanus from Akhmim (ancient Panopolis) in Egypt, was published in 1892. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

The only complete text is the Ge'ez translation, made from the Greek in Aksumite times. Scholars sort its manuscripts into two families, one of six manuscripts from the fifteenth to eighteenth centuries and one of eleven mostly from the eighteenth; R. H. Charles used 23 Ge'ez manuscripts for his edition of 1906. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] Without Ethiopia, most of 1 Enoch would be known only from fragments. The English quotations below are from Charles's translation, published in 1912–1913 and now in the public domain. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1]

Book one: the Watchers

The book opens with a blessing and a vision of God coming to judge the earth, and its ninth verse is the one the New Testament quotes: "And behold! He cometh with ten thousands of His holy ones", to execute judgment and convict the ungodly. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] Then comes the story that gives the book its name, an expansion of the six obscure verses of Genesis 6 about "the sons of God" and "the daughters of men". The Watchers, angels of heaven, see the "beautiful and comely daughters" of humankind and desire them. Led by Shemihazah, "they were in all two hundred; who descended in the days of Jared on the summit of Mount Hermon", and bound themselves by an oath. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

The Watchers take wives and father giants, who consume the work of human hands and then humans themselves. They also teach. "Azazel taught men to make swords", and the other angels teach metalwork, adornment, spells, root-cutting and the reading of the stars: in this book, knowledge that should not have been given comes down from fallen heaven. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The cry of the earth reaches God; the archangels are sent; Azazel is bound in the desert, and God declares of the corruption his teaching caused, "to him ascribe all sin". [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] The giants destroy one another and the Flood cleanses the earth.

Enoch's role is that of a scribe between worlds. The fallen Watchers ask him to carry their petition for forgiveness to heaven; in a vision he is taken up through walls of crystal and fire into a house where "the Great Glory sat thereon", and returns with the answer that they will have no peace. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] Angels then guide him through the cosmos: to the places where the stars are kept, the prison of the fallen angels, the mountain of the dead where souls wait for judgment, and the tree of life. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] This is among the oldest known Jewish accounts of a human being taken up to see the throne of God, and it shaped later Jewish and Christian writing on heavenly ascent.

Book two: the Parables and the Son of Man

The Parables return to judgment, now in three long "similitudes". Enoch sees "One who had a head of days", and with him "another being whose countenance had the appearance of a man"; the angel explains: "This is the son of Man who hath righteousness". [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch2] This figure was "named before the Lord of Spirits" before the sun and the stars were made, "For from the beginning the Son of Man was hidden", and he will sit on the throne of glory to judge the kings and the mighty who oppressed the righteous. [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch2] In the closing chapter the title is applied to Enoch himself: "This is the Son of Man who is born unto righteousness". [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch2]

Because the gospels use "Son of Man" as Jesus's way of speaking of himself, the Parables are at the centre of a long scholarly argument. Two points are settled. The phrase's main biblical source is Daniel 7, where "one like a son of man" comes with the clouds before the Ancient of Days, and the Parables are themselves an expansion of that vision. And no fragment of the Parables was found at Qumran, while every other part of 1 Enoch was. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] What is not settled is their date. Milik proposed the third century CE, which would make them later than the gospels; most scholars reject that and place them around the turn of the era, which makes them a near contemporary of the gospel tradition. Whether the gospel writers knew them, or both drew on a shared Jewish expectation, is open. The claim that the Christian title "comes from Enoch" goes beyond the evidence; the claim that it has nothing to do with Enoch does too. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

Book three: the Astronomical Book

The third book, possibly the oldest of all, is a manual of heaven's order given by the angel Uriel. Sun, moon and stars move through gates in the sky by fixed laws, and the year has 364 days, four seasons of 91 days, so that each feast falls on the same weekday every year. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The calendar was not academic. A 364-day year set its users against the lunar calendar of the Temple authorities, and the same calendar runs through Jubilees and the Dead Sea Scrolls. [cite:vt27_wiki_jubilees] The book's teaching is that the cosmos keeps God's law exactly and that sinners are those who fail to keep time with it.

Book four: the Dream Visions

Two dreams make up the fourth book. In the first Enoch foresees the Flood. The second, called the Animal Apocalypse, retells history from Adam to the Maccabean revolt in which people appear as animals and angels as men: the patriarchs are bulls, Israel are sheep, the nations wild beasts and birds of prey, and the sheep's careless shepherds are angels who let too many be destroyed. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The vision ends with a ram with a great horn leading a revolt, the judgment of the shepherds and the fallen stars, and a new house in which all the animals become white bulls again. It was written during or just after the revolt of 167–160 BCE, and its scholars date it by the point at which its "prophecy" stops matching events, around 163 to 142 BCE. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

Book five: the Epistle of Enoch

The last book is Enoch's teaching to his children. It contains the Apocalypse of Weeks, which divides all history into ten "weeks" ending in the judgment and a new heaven. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] Its moral voice is sharp and social. Series of woes condemn those who build houses with others' toil, deal in deceit and oppress the poor: "Woe to you, you rich, for you have trusted in your riches", a line often set beside the Letter of James's "Come now, you rich!". [cite:vt27_itb_enoch94] The book ends with an account of the miraculous birth of Noah, whose shining appearance makes his father Lamech fear he is the child of a Watcher. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch]

Enoch in the New Testament and the early church

The Letter of Jude, one of the 27 New Testament books, cites Enoch by name: "Enoch, in the seventh generation from Adam, prophesied", and what follows is the verse quoted above, 1 Enoch 1:9, the Lord coming with his holy ones "to execute judgment upon all". [cite:vt27_biblia_jude] [cite:vt27_ccel_enoch1] The letter treats it as genuine prophecy. Other New Testament passages, such as the references in 1 and 2 Peter to imprisoned spirits and to angels held in chains until the judgment, are widely read as echoes of the Watchers story. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] The Epistle of Barnabas, an early second-century Christian writing, quotes Enoch as scripture, and Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Athenagoras, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Origen and Cyprian all knew it, some approvingly and some not. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] For the first three Christian centuries, in other words, Enoch was read across the church, not on its margins. How it fell out of use everywhere except Ethiopia is told in the last section of this part. [scene:vt27_how_the_west_lost_enoch|How the West lost Enoch]

What scholars dispute

Beyond the date of the Parables, three questions remain open. First, the book's relation to Genesis: some scholars read the Watchers story as an expansion of Genesis 6, others argue that an older Enochic tradition lies behind the terse Genesis text. Second, whether there was an "Enochic Judaism", in Gabriele Boccaccini's phrase, a distinct priestly or scribal movement for which these books were central and which fed the community at Qumran; the idea has strong advocates and sceptics. Third, how far the Ethiopic text, translated from Greek, preserves the Aramaic original; where the Qumran fragments survive, they show both agreement and real differences. [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] None of these disputes concerns whether the book was read and valued by early Jews and Christians; that is documented. They concern who wrote it, for whom, and how the parts came together.
