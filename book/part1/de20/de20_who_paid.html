Who Paid: Lives Abroad, Services at Home, and the Ledger

The cost of the cuts fell on three groups: people abroad who had relied on American aid, people at home who use the agencies that shrank, and the workers and contractors who lost their jobs. The evidence is of very different kinds for each. Deaths abroad are, so far, the output of models, not counts. Service at home is measured by agency statistics that the agencies themselves and their critics read differently. Lost jobs are counted, though on more than one definition, as [scene:de20_the_cuts|the previous section] explained. This section keeps each kind of evidence in its own register and does not infer harm from a cut alone.

The USAID estimates: what each study did

The most quoted figure comes from a study in The Lancet, published on 1 July 2025 by Daniella Cavalcanti, Davide Rasella, James Macinko and colleagues from Brazil, Spain, the United States and Mozambique. They compared mortality in 133 low- and middle-income countries from 2001 to 2021 with the amount of USAID support each received, controlling for income, education, population and health systems, and found the aid associated with about 91 million deaths averted, a 15 per cent lower all-cause death rate and a 32 per cent lower death rate among children under five. Then they ran a simulation to 2030: if the 83 per cent cut held, they projected more than 14 million additional deaths, including more than 4.5 million children under five. [cite:de20_ucla_lancet] Those are projected deaths from a statistical model, not deaths observed or counted.

Three other estimates use different methods. The Center for Global Development's Charles Kenny and Justin Sandefur multiplied average costs per life saved, about $3,457 for health aid and $17,837 for humanitarian and food aid, by the dollars actually cut, and arrived at roughly 500,000 to 1 million lives lost on 2025 spending, or 670,000 to 1.6 million on commitments; they wrote that without direct monitoring data "any estimates of lives lost remain guesswork," and that their figures might be pessimistic because some lifesaving aid was protected. [cite:de20_cgd_update] A second Lancet paper, published on 3 February 2026 by a team including Rasella, modelled cuts by all donors, not only the United States, and projected 9.4 million additional deaths by 2030 in its baseline and 22.6 million in a severe scenario. [cite:de20_st_lancet26] A live model run at Boston University by Brooke Nichols showed 781,343 projected deaths for one year as of 30 July 2026. [cite:de20_reason_brown]

The studies are disputed on method. Aaron Brown, a statistician at New York University, called the 14 million figure "patently absurd," arguing that a design comparing trends over time would credit almost anything that grew while death rates fell. [cite:de20_fix_brown] Writing in Reason in August 2026, he set the first study's 91 million averted deaths against his own estimate of about 79 million for the entire global decline in deaths over the period, noted that the two Lancet papers cannot both be right about the size of USAID's effect, and cited one early check against observed data: in Egypt in May 2025 the United Nations recorded 46,365 deaths, against about 58,000 implied by the model, a single data point that he said proved little either way. [cite:de20_reason_brown] Nichols, defending the first paper, called its look backward "quite robust" while agreeing that its projections "carry greater uncertainty." [cite:de20_fix_brown] The State Department's response was through an unnamed official, who said the studies rested on "incorrect assumptions," and later through a statement that "some recent 'studies' are rooted in outdated thinking." [cite:de20_ap_usaidjuly] [cite:de20_st_lancet26] Full mortality data for the poorest countries will take years to arrive. [cite:de20_reason_brown]

Services at home

Social Security is the clearest case of a service measured two ways. In July 2025 the Washington Post reported that monthly calls to the agency had risen from about 6.6 million to 8.6 million, that average hold times had grown from 75 to 93 minutes, that the agency had stopped publishing its wait-time figures, and that it was moving about 1,000 field-office staff to the phones; some forty of its roughly 1,200 field offices had lost at least a quarter of their staff, and the agency was cutting 7,000 jobs to reach 50,000. [cite:de20_wapo_ssa] In June 2026 Commissioner Frank Bisignano told Congress that phone hold times had fallen 89 per cent, from 42 minutes to under five, and field-office waits by 30 per cent, with more than 2,000 staff reassigned to the phones. [cite:de20_fnn_ssa] The agency's inspector general had found that in fiscal 2025 callers who took a call-back waited more than 100 minutes on average to reach an agent, and about 15 minutes excluding call-backs; the methods behind the commissioner's figures and the inspector general's are not the same and cannot be compared directly. [cite:de20_fnn_ssa] Staff and union witnesses said the phone gains came at the expense of claims work and training. [cite:de20_fnn_ssa]

At the IRS the Treasury's tax inspector general counted 25,386 separations and 294 layoff notices by May 2025, about a quarter of the workforce, including about 26 per cent of revenue agents, the staff who audit returns; the National Taxpayer Advocate warned that a filing season needs "a sufficient number of trained employees." [cite:de20_tigta_irs] The National Weather Service, down more than 550 staff, was operating with fewer weather-balloon launches, and vacancies at Texas forecast offices drew scrutiny after the July 2025 floods; it was then allowed to rehire 450. [cite:de20_cnn_nws] Scrutiny is not a finding, and no official review cited here attributes any forecast failure to the vacancies. At Veterans Affairs the planned 80,000 layoffs were dropped. [cite:de20_bl_va] At the consumer bureau, an injunction has kept most staff in place while its budget was cut nearly in half. [cite:de20_ab_cfpb]

Workers and contractors

The workers who paid are counted in the previous section: about 25,000 probationary employees whose dismissal a judge later found unlawful but did not reverse, and a net federal workforce about 256,000 smaller. [cite:de20_reuters_alsup] [cite:de19_gao_workforce] Contractors paid too, though the record is thinner. Booz Allen Hamilton, one of the largest government consultancies, announced on 23 May 2025 that it would cut about 2,500 jobs, 7 per cent of its staff, mostly in its civilian business, citing the push to cut civilian contract spending and slower procurement. [cite:de20_ng_booz] At the same time, many claimed cancellations were not what they seemed. GAO found that only 43.2 per cent of DOGE's claimed contract savings were tied to contracts actually terminated in whole or part, that 2,503 contracts listed as cut, worth $27.4 billion in claimed savings, showed no termination at all, and that the method behind about 96 per cent of claimed grant savings could not be verified. [cite:de20_gao_receipts] One early entry, a $1 billion Social Security contract with the firm Leidos, turned out to be a single $560,000 task order to redesign a form. [cite:de20_ge_contracting]

What the cuts bought

Asked under oath whether DOGE had reduced the deficit, Nate Cavanaugh answered, "No, we didn't." [cite:de20_abc_depos] Chris Edwards of the Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank that favours smaller government, estimated that the workforce reductions save about $41 billion a year, just over 2 per cent of the deficit, and said, "They reduced the workforce, but that really hardly affected the deficit." [cite:de20_cs_256k] DOGE's own website claimed about $110 billion in contract, grant and lease savings by July 2026, a figure GAO could not verify for most of its parts; the office did not respond to the auditors. [cite:de20_gao_receipts] The full claims ledger is in [scene:de21_the_ending|Part Twenty-One].

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis this book tests treats government cuts as the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination. On this part's material, the following is documented. An agency created and funded by Congress was dismantled by executive action, and a judge found it likely that this usurped Congress's authority, though the finding was stayed. [cite:de20_ewn_rubio83] [cite:de19_upi_chuang] Firings a court later held unlawful were allowed to stand, and layoffs went ahead under Supreme Court stays that expressly left their legality undecided, in what one justice called the power "to repeal statutes by firing all those necessary to carry them out." [cite:de20_reuters_alsup] [cite:de20_npr_trumpvafge] [cite:de20_rollcall_mcmahon] The cuts were uneven, with Education nearly halved and Homeland Security nearly untouched, and, on a DOGE staffer's own sworn answer and a libertarian economist's estimate, their stated fiscal purpose of cutting the deficit was not met. [cite:de19_gao_workforce] [cite:de20_abc_depos] [cite:de20_cs_256k]

Probable is that the cuts moved capacity away from regulatory, aid and service agencies and left enforcement intact; that is a pattern in the numbers, not a stated plan. The intent behind it is contested: Musk's own words are on record for USAID ("Time for it to die") and for the need to "delete entire agencies," while the administration's stated aim was efficiency. [cite:de11_cbs_wgs] [cite:de19_pbs_usaid] Not supported: that the deaths projected abroad are counted deaths, or that any study's single figure is settled; and that the cuts were aimed at a sovereign executive as the Dark Enlightenment texts describe one, for which no document exists. The record also runs against the reading. Several cuts were reversed, at Veterans Affairs, the Weather Service and Health and Human Services, often after public pressure; the consumer bureau's layoffs remained blocked by the courts as of October 2026; and the auditors, inspectors general and judges who documented all this kept working. [cite:de20_bl_va] [cite:de20_cnn_nws] [cite:de20_biospace_hhs] [cite:de20_ab_cfpb] The resemblance to Yarvin's RAGE, "Retire All Government Employees," described in [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six], is rhyme, not provenance. [cite:de6_tnr_duran] [scene:de21_the_dividend|Part Twenty-One] turns to the promises made for the cuts, the conflicts around them, and how the office ended.
