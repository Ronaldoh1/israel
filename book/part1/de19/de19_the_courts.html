The Courts: What Was Decided, and What Was Not

DOGE was sued from its first weeks, by unions, retirees' groups, states, former USAID staff and a government-ethics group. There is no authoritative count of the suits. An early tally by NewsNation, on 20 March 2025, put "nearly a fifth" of the 132 cases then filed against the administration as related to Musk and DOGE. [cite:de19_nn_count] The Congressional Research Service, the research arm of Congress, counted ten pending suits under the Privacy Act alone on 17 September 2025, covering the Treasury, Labor, the consumer bureau, Education, OPM, the IRS and Social Security. [cite:de19_crs_lsb11370] Those ten are the only count from an official source; suits over appointments, records, layoffs and grants come on top. This section follows the cases that decided the most, and is exact about what each court decided, because the most common error in public accounts of these cases is to read an emergency order as a verdict.

The data suits and the gate of "irreparable harm"

To stop an action before trial, a plaintiff must show a likely win on the merits and also "irreparable harm," an injury that money or a later order could not repair. Most of the DOGE data suits turned on that second test. In Alliance for Retired Americans v. Bessent (the Treasury payment systems), AFL-CIO v. Department of Labor and EPIC v. OPM, judges refused preliminary injunctions because irreparable harm was not shown; in AFGE v. OPM the Manhattan court granted one; in a suit by teachers over Education and Treasury data, the appeals court for the Fourth Circuit vacated an injunction on 12 August 2025. [cite:de19_crs_lsb11370] A public account sometimes calls the Treasury case "AFL-CIO v. Treasury"; the labour federation's case was against the Labor Department, and the Treasury case was brought by a retirees' group.

The fullest arc is AFSCME v. SSA, brought on 21 February 2025 by two unions and the Alliance for Retired Americans. Judge Ellen Lipton Hollander in Maryland barred DOGE's access to Social Security systems holding personal data on 20 March and on 17 April issued a preliminary injunction ordering DOGE to delete non-anonymised personal data it held. [cite:de19_ch_afscme] On 6 June 2025 the Supreme Court stayed that injunction while the appeal went on. The stay decided nothing about whether the access was lawful; Justice Kagan noted her disagreement, and Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justice Sonia Sotomayor, dissented on the ground that the government had shown no irreparable harm to itself. [cite:de19_ch_afscme] [cite:de19_crs_lsb11370]

On 10 April 2026 the full Fourth Circuit, writing through Judge Toby Heytens, held by nine votes that the plaintiffs were likely to have standing, because handing their personal data to DOGE resembled the old wrong of intrusion upon seclusion, and then, in a part joined by only two other judges, vacated the injunction because irreparable harm had not been shown on the record the district judge had. [cite:de19_ca4_afscme] That part described "the even more recent—and even more alarming—allegations" the plaintiffs had raised in March, while ruling that they were not before the appeals court. [cite:de19_ca4_afscme] Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson, concurring, added that "the whistleblower report has not yet been substantiated." [cite:de19_ca4_afscme] Six judges, led by Judge Robert King, dissented, arguing that what the government's own corrections had disclosed was much worse than what the district court had seen. [cite:de19_ca4_afscme] Back in Maryland, on 26 June 2026 Judge Hollander allowed discovery into DOGE's handling of the data, citing the government's misstatements and its noncompliance with earlier orders; the case was in discovery as of the latest docket summary. [cite:de19_ch_afscme]

The one merits ruling on unlawful access came on 20 October 2026, when Judge Denise Cote held that OPM violated the Privacy Act by giving its DOGE team personnel data. [cite:de19_bgov_afge] The other direction is also on the record. In New York v. Trump, brought by nineteen states over the Treasury payment systems, Judge Jeannette Vargas had limited DOGE's access in February 2025 and later allowed vetted staff in; on 23 September 2026 she dismissed the access claims as moot because Treasury's DOGE team no longer existed, and dismissed the claims over an automated payment-review system because the states had not shown that any payment owed to them was frozen. [cite:de19_or_nyvtrump] [cite:de19_fox_vargas]

Records: what the Supreme Court did, and did not, decide

A widely repeated line says the Supreme Court exempted DOGE from the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), the law that opens agency records to the public. It did not. Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW) asked for DOGE's records days after the January order and sued when refused; Judge Christopher Cooper found the U.S. DOGE Service "likely an agency subject to FOIA" and allowed CREW limited discovery to test that. [cite:de19_ncc_cert] On 6 June 2025 the Supreme Court stayed the discovery orders and sent the case back, holding that the parts requiring disclosure of the content of DOGE's recommendations inside the executive "are not appropriately tailored," and that "separation of powers concerns counsel judicial deference and restraint." [cite:de19_scotus_crew] On whether FOIA applies, the order said only that the test "cannot turn on the entity's ability to persuade"; Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson would have refused the government's application. [cite:de19_scotus_crew] In October 2026 the Court agreed to hear the case in full (No. 25-1103), on whether the office "wields independent authority or is merely a board that exists to advise the president," with argument expected early in 2027. [cite:de19_ncc_cert] The question is open as of October 2026.

Appointments: who was in charge

The Constitution's Appointments Clause requires officers who wield significant federal authority to be appointed by the president with the Senate's consent or under a law Congress passes. In Does 1–26 v. Musk, former USAID staff argued that Musk exercised such authority without either. The White House had told another court that Musk was a senior adviser with no authority of his own. [cite:de11_audit_fisher] On 18 March 2025 Judge Theodore Chuang wrote that if an adviser were "actually exercising significant authority on governmental matters," treating the clause as satisfied would reduce it to "a technical formality," and that the decision to shut USAID had "usurped the authority of the public's elected representatives in Congress." [cite:de19_upi_chuang] He ordered systems access restored. The Fourth Circuit stayed his order on 28 March 2025; the case went on, a class was certified in August 2025, and on 4 March 2026 the appeals court blocked the depositions of senior officials, Musk among them. The case was in discovery as of September 2026. [cite:de19_ch_does]

Where the data went next: tax addresses and citizenship checks

Two later fights concern uses of the data rather than DOGE's access to it. The IRS's disclosure of taxpayer addresses to ICE was stayed in November 2025 and the stay upheld on appeal on 8 September 2026. [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:ad13_audit_irs_dcir] The Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements (SAVE) programme, which Homeland Security, its immigration service and DOGE announced they were overhauling on 22 April 2025, was linked for the first time to Social Security records and opened to bulk checks of whole voter rolls. [cite:de19_dhs_save] [cite:de19_npr_save] The government says it checked more than 65 million voters in 26 states and flagged 28,635; in Texas about a fifth of those flagged were later found to be citizens. [cite:de19_npr_save] In June 2026 Judge Sparkle Sooknanan held that the system broke the Social Security Act and the Privacy Act; on 25 September 2026 the Supreme Court stayed her order, relying on a 1996 immigration statute, over a dissent by the three liberal justices. [cite:de19_npr_save]

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis this book tests holds that government cuts were the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto the destination. On this part's material, the following is documented. DOGE teams were given a standing order of access to unclassified systems across the government; at Treasury, OPM, Social Security and six other agencies auditors and courts found controls bypassed, vetting unverifiable or access unlawful; Social Security data left the agency's approved systems for a commercial service, with the contents still unknown; and data held for benefits and taxes was put to immigration and voter-roll checks. [cite:de19_eo14158] [cite:de19_gao_treasury] [cite:de19_gao_sixagency] [cite:de19_abc_hatch] [cite:de19_bgov_afge] [cite:de19_npr_save] Documented, too, is the government's correction of its own sworn statements in court. [cite:de19_ch_afscme]

Probable, on this record, is that the speed of the access outran the safeguards the law requires, which is what the auditors' language says in plainer words. Not supported: that data was taken out to foreign hands or to Musk's companies, a claim that rests on allegations the agencies deny and inquiries still open; that a single "master database" was built; and that anyone used the voter-roll agreement to move data. [cite:de19_krebs_nlrb] [cite:de19_palantir_record] [cite:de19_abc_hatch] The record also runs against the reading in two ways. The courts did not give the executive a free hand: judges found access unlawful, ordered discovery after misstatements, and the Supreme Court took the records question for full review rather than closing it.
