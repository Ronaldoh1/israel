Does Scripture Forbid a Jewish State? Jeremiah and the Three Oaths

Debates about Israel are often framed as if Judaism spoke with one voice on the question of a Jewish state: either the tradition commands one, or it forbids one. The tradition itself holds both positions, and the argument between them is far older than political Zionism. This book presents that argument; it does not decide it.

One starting point is the prophet Jeremiah's instruction to the Jews carried into exile in Babylon in the sixth century BCE. It told them to build their lives where they had been carried:

seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captives, and pray unto the LORD for it

The verse is Jeremiah 29:7. [cite:sefaria_jer29]

A second starting point is a passage in the Babylonian Talmud, Ketubot 110b–111a, which derives from the Song of Songs three oaths. The first is that Israel should not go up to the land "as a wall," meaning en masse and by force. The second is that it should not rebel against the nations. The third, binding the nations in turn, is that they should not oppress Israel excessively. [cite:w_three_oaths]

Many Orthodox authorities read the oaths as barring a sovereign Jewish state before the coming of the Messiah. Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum of Satmar built his 1959 work Vayoel Moshe on them, and the Neturei Karta group holds the same position today. [cite:w_vayoel] Agudat Israel, founded in 1912, organized Orthodox opposition to secular Zionism.

On the other side stood the religious Zionists. They cited Nachmanides, who counted settling the land among the commandments, and Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook, the first Ashkenazi chief rabbi of Mandate Palestine, who saw even secular Zionism as part of a process of redemption. [cite:w_kook] [cite:w_religious_zionism] Some in this camp argued that the oaths had lapsed after centuries of persecution. Others argued that the Balfour Declaration and the League of Nations had supplied the nations' consent.

Opposition did not come only from the Orthodox. Reform Judaism's platform of 1885 rejected a return to the land altogether, a position the movement later reversed. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] Early Zionism itself was mostly secular, which is one reason so many rabbis opposed it at first: the movement was proposing to achieve by politics what many of them believed only the Messiah could bring.

The balance of opinion has since shifted. Today most Orthodox Jews in Israel accept or support the state, while anti-Zionist Haredi groups such as Neturei Karta remain a minority. But the shift came later. In the period covered by this part of the book, the question of whether Jewish law permitted a return to the land by political means was open, and leading authorities answered it in opposite ways.

The point matters for everything that follows. When Herzl and his successors built a political movement, they did so in the face of serious religious objection from within Judaism, as well as with support from parts of it. Neither the objection nor the support can be dismissed as marginal or inauthentic. Both are readings of the same texts, and both have a long lineage inside the tradition.
