The Nuremberg Laws: Persecution Given Legal Form

On September 15, 1935, the Nazi-controlled Reichstag, Germany's parliament, met in special session at Nuremberg, where the party was holding its rally. It passed two laws [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_laws]. The vote was unanimous. One law defined who counted as German by blood; the other policed the boundary that definition drew. Between them they stripped German Jews of citizenship. It is often assumed that Nazi persecution began with violence and that the legal apparatus came later, almost as an afterthought. The sequence was the reverse. Before the mass violence of Kristallnacht, and before the camps, the regime built a legal architecture of exclusion. Law came before the pogrom.

The first measure, the Reich Citizenship Law, defined a citizen as a person "of German or related blood" who proves through conduct a willingness to serve the German people and Reich [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. That single definition turned German Jews from citizens into "subjects" without full political rights. No violence was needed. A whole population lost its legal standing through a statute.

The second, the Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honor, reached into private life. It banned marriage between Jews and "subjects of the state of German or related blood," and declared such marriages invalid even if they had been concluded abroad to evade the law. It made "extramarital intercourse" between the two groups a crime. It barred Jews from employing non-Jewish German women under 45 in their households. Violations were punishable by imprisonment [cite:yadvashem_nuremberg_blood_law]. The regime's own name for the new crime was Rassenschande, "race defilement," a word that shows the ideology behind the law [cite:wiki_rassenschande]. The text of the statute is documented. Its enforcement varied across regions and years, and the text alone does not record every prosecution brought under it.

The laws did not define a Jew by belief. Under the Nuremberg Race Laws, anyone with three or four Jewish grandparents was Jewish, whatever his or her religious practice [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. Germans who had not practiced Judaism in decades, and even people who had formally converted to Christianity, could be classified as Jews. Conviction, conversion and long non-observance offered no legal escape. Ancestry alone decided.

The bureaucracy then refined the categories. On November 14, 1935, the First Supplementary Decree to the Reich Citizenship Law created the Mischling, or "mixed-blood," classification for people with one or two Jewish grandparents, with its own legal restrictions [cite:ushmm_nuremberg_race_laws]. Different degrees of ancestry carried different legal consequences. Charts were printed to help officials, and ordinary citizens, work out a person's racial status under the law [cite:facinghistory_nuremberg_laws]. A person's legal existence had been reduced to a sum about grandparents. The full administrative detail of the Mischling categories lies beyond this account; what matters here is that the system existed, and how it worked.

The Reichstag that passed these laws was the same body whose surrender of authority in 1933 is described in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse]. Having voted away its power to check the government, it now met to pass, without a dissenting vote, the laws that excluded Jews from citizenship.

The order of events is the point. By September 1935 Germany had a complete legal framework, passed without dissent, that defined Jewish identity by blood, stripped citizenship and criminalized intermarriage. It came years before the mass violence of Kristallnacht, which escalated what the statutes had already established. Exclusion was legislated first, then enforced through mounting violence. And only weeks before the Nuremberg session, in August 1935, the Zionist Congress at Lucerne had voted to continue its transfer agreement with the same state, as described in [scene:hv_s01|the Haavara Agreement].
