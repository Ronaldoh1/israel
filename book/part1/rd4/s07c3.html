Roosevelt's Hidden Decline, 1944

On 28 March 1944, seven months before the election, a Navy cardiologist examined Franklin Roosevelt and found serious heart disease. The familiar version of the 1944 campaign treats the president's health as a private matter and the questions raised about it as partisan exaggeration by his opponents. The medical record tells another story. His official physician told the public the opposite of what the cardiologist had found, and a second doctor's written judgment that Roosevelt would probably not survive another term was locked away for decades. The voters who returned Roosevelt to office in November 1944 did so without knowing what his own doctors knew. So, by multiple accounts, did the man they elected as his vice president.

The cardiologist was Dr. Howard Bruenn, and the examination took place at Bethesda. He diagnosed acute congestive heart failure, an enlarged heart, hypertension and hypertensive heart disease. Bruenn was sworn to secrecy. He did not volunteer a prognosis even to Roosevelt himself, who reportedly asked no questions [cite:wiki_dying_president_ferrell]. One historian later described the secrecy around these findings as guarded "on a level with the Manhattan Project." The comparison is striking, but it rests on a real examination with specific and serious results. What Roosevelt privately understood or suspected about his condition is not documented; the record says only that he did not ask.

The public heard something very different. Ross McIntire, Roosevelt's official physician since 1933, was an ear, nose and throat specialist, not a cardiologist. He assured the press and the nation that the president was "in excellent condition for a man of his age" and that his health was "excellent in all respects" [cite:boston_globe_lahey_memo]. These were specific, quoted statements, and they flatly contradicted Bruenn's diagnosis made at the same time. The record documents the contradiction. It does not establish McIntire's motive, or how much of Bruenn's findings he had absorbed when he spoke.

A third physician reached a darker conclusion. Frank Lahey, a Boston surgeon consulted separately on Roosevelt's condition, wrote a memo dated 10 July 1944 concluding that Roosevelt was unlikely to survive a full fourth term. The memo was placed in the vault of a Boston law firm. It was not discovered and made public until decades after Roosevelt's death, more than sixty years after it was written [cite:boston_globe_lahey_memo]. Who knew of its existence in the intervening years is not documented.

These facts change the meaning of a decision taken that same summer. At the 1944 Democratic convention the party replaced Vice President Henry Wallace with Harry Truman on the ticket [cite:wiki_1944_dnc] [cite:wiki_choosing_truman]. A vice-presidential choice is usually a secondary matter. Given what Roosevelt's doctors knew, this one was in practice the selection of the next president, though the voters did not understand it that way. That inference has limits. Historical accounts document a general awareness among party leaders of Roosevelt's declining health and its political implications, not necessarily knowledge of what Bruenn or Lahey had specifically found.

Why Truman? A natural suspicion is that a compromise candidate is chosen because someone holds leverage over him. No documented evidence connects Truman's selection to his personal finances or to any tax matter. The documented reasons are political. Truman had earned real standing as chairman of the wartime Truman Committee, which investigated profiteering by defense contractors, and by 1944 he ranked among the most respected officials in Washington. He was acceptable to the labor unions and had supported the New Deal. But he had also opposed Roosevelt's earlier bid for a third term, which made him tolerable to conservative Southern Democrats wary of Wallace's leftward tilt. Contemporaries mocked the choice as "the Missouri Compromise," precisely because Truman was no faction's first choice, only the man enough factions could accept [cite:trumanlibrary_missouri_compromise] [cite:wiki_1944_dem_vp_selection] [cite:legalclarity_truman_1944_vp]. The one real complication in his background was political, not financial. His career had been launched by Tom Pendergast, the corrupt political boss of Kansas City, and party leaders had to work to establish that Truman was not "a Pendergast lackey" [cite:legalclarity_truman_1944_vp].

Truman himself, according to multiple historical accounts, was kept "entirely in the dark" about how serious Roosevelt's condition was [cite:globalsecurity_fdr_veiled_decline]. That does not mean he had no sense at all that the president was failing; public speculation about Roosevelt's appearance and vigor was widespread. It means he lacked the specific medical knowledge Bruenn and Lahey possessed. The man who would inherit the presidency in April 1945, and with it the whole of American policy in the Middle East, entered office knowing little more about his predecessor's health than the voters who had elected them both.
