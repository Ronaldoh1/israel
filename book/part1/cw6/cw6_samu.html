Samu, November 1966: The Raid, the Report and Resolution 228

Nine months after the Skyhawk decision, the arms relationship met its first test on the ground. On the morning of 13 November 1966 Israeli forces crossed the armistice line into the Jordanian-ruled West Bank and destroyed much of the village of As Samu. The raid struck a government that, by Washington's reckoning, had kept up a good system of tacit cooperation with Israel on the border. In the days that followed it produced an urgent American review of how to help Jordan, a warning from the American president to Israel, riots against King Hussein's government in the West Bank, and, twelve days after the raid, a Security Council censure for which the United States voted. The best record of what happened on the ground is the report of the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization, and this section begins there, noting at each point which details the observers saw for themselves and which rest on the word of Jordanian witnesses.

The mine, 11 or 12 November. The raid answered a killing. The archives of the Israel Defense Forces describe the operation, which they call Operation Magressa, as a response to a mine attack near the West Bank border two days earlier, in which three Israeli soldiers on patrol were killed, and they attribute the mine to Fatah. [cite:rs_cw6_idf_archive_samu] An American document of the time gives a different attribution and a slightly different date: the CIA memorandum of 2 December 1966 says Israel attributed the mining of the road on 12 November to the Palestine Liberation Front. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_samu] Walt Rostow's memorandum to Johnson of 15 November also counts three Israelis killed. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] The sources therefore agree that three Israeli soldiers died, and the Israeli archive places the attack near the West Bank border; they differ on whether the group was Fatah or the Palestine Liberation Front, and on whether the mine was laid on the 11th or the 12th. The campaign of raids of which this mine was part, and the 61 sabotage incidents Israel attributed to Fatah by December 1966, are told in [scene:cw6_fatah|Fatah's campaign]. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966]

The morning of 13 November. The report of UNTSO's Chief of Staff, Lieutenant-General Odd Bull, to the Secretary-General, circulated as document S/7593, reconstructs the morning by the clock. According to Jordanian witnesses quoted in the report, Israeli tanks crossed the line at about 05:45. UNTSO asked for a ceasefire by 08:55 and received no Israeli reply. The chairman of the Mixed Armistice Commission then called a ceasefire for 09:45, which both sides accepted. United Nations observers reached the scene at 10:35. [cite:rs_cw6_untso_s7593] The sequence matters for what follows: the observers arrived nearly five hours after the crossing, when the fighting was over, and what they could verify was the damage, not the conduct of the operation.

The report's description of the force is accordingly second-hand. Jordanian witnesses described about 80 M-48 Patton tanks, two columns of more than 40 half-tracks, 155 mm and 105 mm guns, and Mirage aircraft; UNTSO recorded these details as their testimony and did not independently verify them. [cite:rs_cw6_untso_s7593] In Washington Rostow described a "3000-man raid with tanks and planes." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] The two descriptions agree on armour and aircraft; neither is a count by a neutral observer.

What the observers found. The physical findings are UNTSO's own. As Samu was a village of about 5,000 people in 1,200 houses. The observers found 125 houses completely demolished, along with the village clinic, a six-classroom school and a workshop; one mosque and 28 houses were damaged. At Khirbet Jimba 15 stone huts had been destroyed, and the police post at Rujm el Madfa'a was almost totally destroyed. [cite:rs_cw6_untso_s7593] The CIA's figure five days later, about 150 buildings destroyed, is of the same order. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d338]

The dead. The number killed is less certain, and UNTSO's own report does not settle it. Its totals give 3 civilians and 15 soldiers killed, and 97 civilians and 37 soldiers wounded; yet the same report notes death certificates for 14 military personnel and 5 civilians. [cite:rs_cw6_untso_s7593] The two counts within one document differ by one soldier and two civilians, and the sources consulted do not explain the discrepancy. Israel declined to produce its own casualties as witnesses before the observers. [cite:rs_cw6_untso_s7593] The CIA, writing on 18 November, gave a much higher figure: about 50 killed, half of them soldiers. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d338] That estimate is more than twice either of UNTSO's, and the CIA document as consulted does not give its basis. UNTSO's counts put soldiers at most of the dead; the CIA puts them at about half.

Israel's case. The Israeli rationale, in its strongest form, ran as follows. Israeli soldiers had been killed by a mine near the West Bank border, and the West Bank was territory Jordan ruled. [cite:rs_cw6_idf_archive_samu] By December 1966 Israel attributed 61 sabotage incidents to Fatah since its emergence in January 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] On this reasoning, a government whose patrols were being killed from across the line was entitled to strike the territory from which the attackers came. The IDF archive presents the operation as exactly that: a response to the mine attack launched from the West Bank. [cite:rs_cw6_idf_archive_samu]

Washington's objection. The American government did not accept the case, and its reasons are on paper. On 15 November Rostow told Johnson that the raid "was out of all proportion to the provocation and was aimed at the wrong target." It had, he wrote, "wrecked a good system of tacit cooperation between Hussein and the Israelis," and he suggested slowing military deliveries to Israel "inconspicuously." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] The phrase "the wrong target" carried the whole objection. The government that sponsored Fatah, in the CIA's account, was Syria's; the government that had kept its border with Israel quiet was Jordan's, and it was Jordan that had been struck. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966] The same day Assistant Secretary of State Sisco told Israel's ambassador, Harman, that Washington was "dismayed by Israeli attack," feared that "this is not the end but the beginning," and expected a "strong resolution" at the United Nations. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d335] Syria's sponsorship of Fatah is the subject of [scene:cw6_syria|Syria's radical turn].

The danger to Hussein. The CIA spelled out what the raid meant for Jordan. Its special memorandum of 18 November judged that the raid had "shattered the modus vivendi" on the border, that Jordan's army was "clearly humiliated," and that a coup or assassination attempt against Hussein "cannot be ruled out." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d338] The Jordanian objection, put in its strongest form and in the terms of these American documents, was that the king had kept a system of tacit cooperation with Israel on the border, that the raiders were not his, and that the blow had fallen on his army and his throne rather than on their sponsors. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d333] Unrest followed. According to GlobalSecurity's account, rioting against the Jordanian government followed in the West Bank on a scale beyond any earlier unrest, and Hussein used the army to restore order. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_samu]

Johnson's letters. On 23 November Johnson wrote to Hussein. "Words of sympathy are small comfort when lives have been needlessly destroyed," the letter began, and the president ordered an urgent review of how the United States could help. [cite:rs_cw6_lbj_hussein] To Israel he sent a warning. The FRUS editors record that Johnson told Israel that another such raid would lead to the suspension of American military shipments. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_samu] Coming nine months after the Skyhawk sale told in [scene:s11d|the two tracks of 1966], the warning put the new supply relationship itself on the table.

Resolution 228, 25 November 1966. The Security Council voted twelve days after the raid. Resolution 228 censured Israel for "a large-scale and carefully planned military action" in breach of the United Nations Charter and of the Israel–Jordan armistice, and warned that "actions of military reprisal cannot be tolerated." It was adopted by 14 votes to none, with New Zealand abstaining; the United States voted in favour. [cite:rs_cw6_res228] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d349] Read against Israel's case, the resolution's two key phrases can be taken as an answer to it: "carefully planned" rejected any reading of the raid as a spontaneous or local response; "military reprisal" named the policy and declared it unacceptable whatever the provocation.

The money that followed. The raid's last consequence was a bill, and Jordan presented it. Jordan's army commander, General Khammash, asked Washington for what Rostow called "a stupendous $200 million." Rostow planned to "whittle" it down, and the FRUS editors record that a military aid package for Jordan was agreed in December 1966. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d355] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_samu] The sum of that package is not given in the sources consulted. The chain, as these American documents present it, runs: an Israeli raid humiliated Jordan's army; the United States, fearing for Hussein's throne, paid to rearm it; and the cost of the raid to Washington was counted partly in aid to the country that had been struck.

What the record does not settle. Several questions the sources do not answer. Who laid the mine, Fatah or the Palestine Liberation Front, is given differently by the Israeli archive and the CIA, and its date by a day. The size and composition of the raiding force rest on Jordanian testimony and on Rostow's round figure, not on a neutral count. The number of Jordanian dead differs within UNTSO's own report and the CIA's estimate is more than twice either figure, and none of the sources consulted reconciles them. The size of December's aid package to Jordan, and the date and scale of the West Bank riots beyond GlobalSecurity's account, are not in the record here. Nor do these documents show why Israel's government chose Jordan rather than Syria as the target; the American objection that it was "the wrong target" is a judgment by its critics, not an account of its reasoning.
