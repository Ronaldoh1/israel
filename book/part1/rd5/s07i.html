UNSCOP and the Empty Chair

In 1947, eleven members from as many countries set out to recommend what should become of Palestine, and one of the two parties declined to speak to them. The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine, known as UNSCOP, is often described as an impartial international body that investigated the problem and reached its recommendation independently. How its hearings actually worked complicates that picture. The committee did not weigh two prepared cases against each other. It heard one, at length, while the other side stayed away.

The General Assembly established UNSCOP in May 1947, at Britain's own request, after London had handed the question to the United Nations. Its eleven members came from Australia, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Guatemala, India, Iran, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden, Uruguay and Yugoslavia. [cite:unscop_ecf] On its face it was a genuinely international body spanning several continents, not a proxy for the United States or Britain.

Its work, however, was boycotted entirely by Palestinian Arab representatives. As a result, the committee heard testimony almost exclusively from Zionist representatives. [cite:unscop_ecf] The Arab Higher Committee for Palestine, the leading Palestinian Arab political body, refused to take part because it regarded the whole framework as illegitimate. In its view, any United Nations body that set out to decide Palestine's future without first affirming the self-determination rights of the existing majority was biased before it began. The Jewish Agency, by contrast, engaged fully. It supplied detailed logistical help, including its own proposed itinerary for the committee's site visits in Palestine.

David Ben-Gurion presented the Agency's case, testifying as its head. He told the committee plainly that an independent Jewish state was the only acceptable political outcome. [cite:unscop_testimony] The map the Zionist side presented was substantially the 1937 Peel Commission map, with the Galilee, the Negev and West Jerusalem added. [cite:unscop_testimony] It was less a new proposal than an extension of an earlier one, and it came after more than a year in which partition had been the Agency's avowed policy, as described in [scene:s07h|the previous chapter on the Agency's campaign]. One historical account says the Agency's presentation overwhelmed the committee "with data, logic, and cogent analyses of every central and tangential issue," while the boycotting side offered nothing to weigh against it. [cite:unscop_testimony] The imbalance did not arise because the Arab position was weak. It arose because that position was never formally put before the committee for comparison.

The limits of this point matter. The record does not show that Arab perspectives were absent from every channel open to the committee. UNSCOP traveled through the region and could observe conditions directly, and it would be wrong to claim its members heard nothing of Arab objections. What the record does establish is more specific: no formal, prepared Arab case was presented to UNSCOP to set against the Jewish Agency's. That is a narrower claim than saying the process was rigged, and it is the claim the evidence supports.

UNSCOP issued its report on September 3, 1947. It was not unanimous. A seven-member majority recommended partition into a Jewish state and an Arab state joined by an economic union. A three-member minority proposed a federal state instead. [cite:unscop_wiki] The report, produced after hearings shaped by the boycott, became the direct basis for the revisions made by the United Nations Ad Hoc Committee and for the General Assembly vote of November 29, 1947. [cite:unscop_wiki]

The empty chair was a choice, made by the Arab Higher Committee on grounds of principle. Its consequence was that the record on which partition was built held one side's data, maps and arguments. Between the report and the vote, the decisive pressure would shift to Washington, where the government's own experts were warning against the course the President was taking.
