Faces, Drones and Satellites: Seeing at a Distance

The tools in the parts before this one work through records; the tools here work through sight. A camera on a phone, a tower on the border, a drone over a protest and a satellite in low orbit all collect images, and software turns images into identities and tracks. Face recognition is the technology that does the turning, so it comes first. A face-recognition system converts a photograph into a "template," a list of numbers that describes the geometry of the face, and compares templates. There are two kinds of comparison. One-to-one "verification" asks whether a face matches one stored photograph, as at a passport gate. One-to-many "identification" asks who, in a whole collection of stored photographs called the gallery, this face most resembles, and it always returns a ranked list of candidates; a threshold set by the operator decides which candidate counts as a "match." [cite:de26_nist_frvt] [cite:de26_duane_clearview]

Clearview and the scraped gallery

Clearview AI is distinguished less by its software than by its gallery. The company describes itself as a search engine for faces: it collects photographs of people from public websites and builds a facial vector for each, so a single photograph returns other pictures of the same person and links to where they appeared. [cite:de26_duane_clearview] Its early investors included Peter Thiel, who with Naval Ravikant put in more than $200,000 that later converted into equity, a tie set out in [scene:de10_philosophy_vs_practice|Part Ten]. [cite:de10_wiki_clearview] On 5 September 2025 Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) finalised a sole-source contract with Clearview with a total value of $9.225 million over a base year and three option years, and $3.75 million obligated at signing, citing the company's "unique proprietary capabilities"; its stated purposes were child-exploitation cases and investigations of assaults on officers. [cite:de26_bu_clearviewice] [cite:de28_audit_clearview_piid] Private suits over the gallery have not ended. A class settlement under Illinois's biometric privacy law, which would have paid class members a 23 per cent equity stake in the company, was vacated by the Seventh Circuit on 13 July 2026, because the nationwide class had no representative of its own when the stake was divided; the case went back to the district court, and as of October 2026 there is no completed settlement. [cite:de26_duane_clearview] An earlier settlement with the American Civil Liberties Union, which does bind the company, is in [scene:de26_counterweights|the last section of this part].

Mobile Fortify: the face check in the street

ICE's own face-recognition tool is a phone app. Mobile Fortify, first reported by 404 Media on 17 July 2025, lets an officer point a phone at a person's face and compare it against about 200 million images drawn from Homeland Security, State Department, Customs and Border Protection (CBP), FBI and state records; it returns a name, date of birth, nationality, an "alien number" and whether an immigration judge has ordered the person removed. [cite:de26_404_fortify] A Homeland Security privacy assessment dated February 2025, obtained by 404 Media under the Freedom of Information Act and reported in November, said plainly that "ICE does not provide the opportunity for individuals to decline or consent" to the scan. Photographs taken are kept for 15 years, including those of United States citizens, since an officer cannot know a person's status before running the check. Representative Bennie Thompson said ICE officials had indicated they would treat a Mobile Fortify hit as outweighing documentary proof of citizenship such as a birth certificate, and called the practice "frightening, repugnant, and unconstitutional." [cite:de26_reason_fortify]

The border as a camera

At ports of entry the face check has become a rule rather than an option for non-citizens. A CBP final rule published in November 2025 and effective on 26 December 2025 lets the agency photograph all non-citizens on entry and exit, by air, land and sea, removing earlier exemptions for diplomats and most Canadian visitors. Under the governing records notice, non-citizens' photographs may be kept for up to 75 years; citizens may opt out by asking for manual inspection, and their verification photographs are deleted within 12 hours. CBP's operations chief called the rule "a major milestone" toward the biometric entry-exit system Congress first required after 2001. [cite:de26_bu_entryexit]

The three systems share one pipeline, from a camera to a template to a gallery to a ranked list to a person who decides. They differ in whose photographs fill the gallery and in how long a scan is kept.

What goes wrong

The documented failures are of two kinds. The first is in the algorithms. In December 2019 the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal standards laboratory, tested 189 algorithms from 99 developers and found that "the majority" showed demographic differentials: in one-to-one matching, false positives for Asian and African American faces were often 10 to 100 times higher than for white faces, depending on the algorithm, though the most equitable algorithms were also among the most accurate. [cite:de26_nist_frvt] The second is in the people using them. A Washington Post investigation in January 2025 found at least eight Americans wrongfully arrested after face-recognition searches, seven of them Black, across 15 police departments in 12 states; in six of the eight cases police did not check the person's alibi. [cite:de26_fortune_wapo] No comparable public count exists for Mobile Fortify.

Drones and towers

Overhead, the border agency's aircraft have been lent to the interior. Flight logs obtained by 404 Media show that CBP flew drones at least 50 times in support of ICE between March 2024 and March 2025, a period spanning two administrations, possibly including its MQ-9 Predators; in June 2025 CBP flew Predators over protests against ICE in Los Angeles, which it described as "officer safety surveillance." [cite:de26_404_drones] On the ground, the border is being lined with sensor towers. In June 2026 Anduril, the defence company whose federal contracts and investor ties are listed in [scene:de14_network_map|Part Fourteen], announced a $363 million CBP award for more than 200 Extended Range Sentry Towers, which detect and track objects more than five miles away and feed Anduril's Lattice software, the program that fuses sensor feeds into one picture for an operator. [cite:de26_eb_anduril]

Satellites and one provider

In March 2024 Reuters reported, from five sources, that SpaceX's Starshield unit was building a network of hundreds of imaging and relay satellites for the National Reconnaissance Office under a $1.8 billion contract signed in 2021, designed to give near-continuous imagery of most of the Earth; the network is separate from Starlink, SpaceX's commercial internet constellation, and the agency declined to describe SpaceX's role. [cite:de26_toi_starshield] SpaceX and Anduril were also among 12 companies given prototype agreements, with a combined ceiling of $3.2 billion, for space-based interceptors in the Golden Dome missile-defence plan; Palantir was not among them. [cite:de26_st_sbi] The dependence this creates is not hypothetical. In Ukraine, a single company's decisions on where Starlink would work shaped a war, as [scene:de11_tesla_spacex_and_the_state|Part Eleven] sets out; the Pentagon confirmed in June 2023 that it was paying for the service. [cite:de11_dn_pentagon] [cite:de11_cnbc_isaacson] At home, the Federal Aviation Administration tested Starlink terminals in 2025 while the company's founder held a government role ([scene:de21_conflicts_and_contracts|Part Twenty-One]). [cite:de21_ap_starlink] Even the Pentagon's AI chat platform now reaches Grok through "Starshield AI." [cite:de26_ds_genaimil]

Capability is not intent

Each layer here is documented and in federal hands. Persistent overhead imaging combined with plate readers and face recognition could reconstruct a person's movements without a phone. That combination is a capability, stated as such. What the record does not show is the layers joined into one domestic tracking system, or any of them pointed at a named critic for speech; that would need its own document, and none has surfaced. The fusion of data the layers produce is the subject of [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Nine]; the stack ICE buys is in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty]. If it were connected

The seeing tools already touch one other layer each. Documented: Mobile Fortify joins a face seen in the street to the government's own records, matching it against DHS, State Department, CBP and FBI images and returning a name and immigration history; and CBP's drones have flown for ICE. [cite:de26_404_fortify] [cite:de26_404_drones] One case shows a face match reaching the travel layer: a Minnesota woman was addressed by name by a Border Patrol agent who said he had used face recognition, and her Global Entry was revoked three days later; the link between the two is alleged in a pending suit, set out in [scene:de25b_who_can_stop_you|Part Twenty-Seven]. [cite:de25b_bu_cleland] Possible, with no document: Mobile Fortify's matches, Clearview's results or drone video feeding ICE's Palantir systems, where a name can be resolved to an address with a "confidence score," and HART, DHS's planned biometric system designed to be shared across agencies but not operational when last assessed; the ecosystem map in [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|Part Thirty] grades each of these links possible, because the tools share a customer but no record shows a feed. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite] [cite:de28_hart_pia] Such a join would need an interface and a work order inside the agency, not a new law; what stands in the way is agency policy, the privacy assessments DHS's inspector general found skipped in 2023, and the courts. [cite:de28_oig_23_61] Not supported: a standing link from the NRO's satellite imagery to domestic enforcement; no contract, interface or authority in the record points to one.
