Unit 8200, Gaza and the Limits of American Sight

The question at the centre of this volume can be put precisely for signals intelligence. Americans' communications flow to Israel under the 2009 memorandum. Does any comparable flow, or any American oversight, reach the other way, into Israel's surveillance of Palestinians in Gaza and the West Bank? This section tests the claim against what the documents show: what Unit 8200 does in the occupied territories, on whose infrastructure, what the United States says it receives about Gaza, and what American oversight bodies can see.

What Unit 8200 does at home. The NSA's 2013 description of the partnership lists Israel's priorities, among them what it calls "Palestinian terrorism." [cite:ow_int_cash2014] The clearest account of the unit's work on Palestinians came from its own reservists. In September 2014, 43 veterans and reservists of Unit 8200, ten of them officers, wrote to Prime Minister Netanyahu, the chief of staff and the head of military intelligence that "the intelligence gathered harms innocents and is used for political persecution," and that "our conscience no longer permits us to serve this system." [cite:ow4_jta_letter] One signatory said in a radio interview that the unit's task was "not defense but ruling another people." [cite:ow4_jta_letter] The signatories were 43 people from a unit the NSA counted in 2007 at about 5,500 conscripts and 1,200 career officers, and their account is their own; one signatory declined to give examples, citing field security. [cite:ow4_jta_letter] [cite:ow4_int2019]

On American infrastructure. In August 2025 a joint investigation by The Guardian, +972 Magazine and Local Call reported that Unit 8200 had stored millions of intercepted Palestinian phone calls from Gaza and the West Bank on Microsoft's Azure cloud, in data centres in the Netherlands and Ireland, under a system insiders described as handling "a million calls an hour"; at least 11,500 terabytes had been uploaded by July 2025. [cite:ow4_newarab_azure] Sources told the reporters the archive was used to identify airstrike targets in Gaza and to justify arrests in the West Bank. [cite:ow4_newarab_azure] The Israeli military said its cooperation with Microsoft rested on "legally supervised agreements" and complied with international law. [cite:ow4_newarab_azure]

Microsoft's response was the most concrete act of review in the record, and it came from a company, not a government. On 25 September 2025 its president, Brad Smith, said an internal review had found "evidence that supports elements of The Guardian's reporting," including the Israel Ministry of Defense's use of Azure storage in the Netherlands and of AI services, and that Microsoft would "cease and disable specified IMOD subscriptions and their services." [cite:ow4_eweek_msft] "We do not provide technology to facilitate mass surveillance of civilians," he wrote. [cite:ow4_tc_msft] Microsoft reviewed its own business records and did not access the customer's data. [cite:ow4_eweek_msft] An Israeli security official told the Associated Press the move would cause "no damage to the operational capabilities" of the military, and Israel Hayom reported a shift to another provider, reportedly Amazon Web Services. [cite:ow4_cbc_msft] [cite:ow4_ih_msft] The public record does not show any review of the matter by an American government body.

The people. Unit 8200's alumni hold documented roles in American technology. Check Point's founders Gil Shwed and Shlomo Kramer served in the unit; Nir Zuk, another alumnus, founded Palo Alto Networks; CyberArk's chief executive Udi Mokady is described as an alumnus. [cite:ow4_sw_alumni] Assaf Rappaport, a co-founder of Wiz, served in Unit 8200; he and his co-founders led Microsoft's Cloud Security Group after Microsoft bought their earlier company in 2015, and Google completed its $32 billion purchase of Wiz on 11 March 2026. [cite:ow4_sequoia_wiz] [cite:ow4_globes_wiz] These are records of military service and commercial careers. No document cited in this book shows any of these companies passing data to Israeli intelligence, and none is implied.

What the United States receives about Gaza. Before October 2023, according to the Wall Street Journal, the United States relied largely on Israel for intelligence on Gaza; after the Hamas attack it expanded its own collection. [cite:ow3_wsj_sharing] On 3 November 2023 the Pentagon announced: "In support of hostage recovery efforts, the U.S. is conducting unarmed [unmanned aerial vehicle] flights over Gaza." [cite:ow4_d1_drones] That is the documented American eye over Gaza, and its stated purpose is the hostages. On the conduct of the war, the government's own report to Congress of 10 May 2024, required by National Security Memorandum 20, said that "Israel has not shared complete information to verify whether U.S. defense articles were specifically used" in incidents of concern, and that the lack of American personnel on the ground made it "difficult to assess or reach conclusive findings on individual incidents." [cite:ow4_axios_nsm20] The same report found it "reasonable to assess" that American weapons had been used in instances inconsistent with Israel's obligations under international humanitarian law. [cite:ow4_axios_nsm20] It also recorded that Israel had shared some information on targeting decisions and battle-damage assessments. [cite:ow4_axios_nsm20]

What American oversight bodies can see. The memorandum creates no enforceable rights and its approval by the surveillance court has not been disclosed. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] The terms of American intelligence-sharing agreements with foreign governments are not published at all: the most recent public text of the core Five Eyes agreement dates from 1955, and freedom-of-information requests for current terms had not produced records. [cite:ow4_js_ukusa] Members of Congress asking about the post-7 October sharing were writing letters to the Director of National Intelligence for details. [cite:ow3_wsj_sharing] And the use of shared intelligence inside Israeli operations rests, as Part Three showed, on Israeli assurances. [cite:ow3_wsj_sharing]

The test, stated. On the documented record the claim holds in a precise form. Raw American intercepts, including Americans' communications, go to Israel by written arrangement; the published accounts describe no reciprocal arrangement; and the American government's own statements about Gaza describe incomplete information and reliance on Israeli assurances rather than independent sight. [cite:ow4_dawn_guardian] [cite:ow4_axios_nsm20] [cite:ow3_wsj_sharing] The claim needs three qualifications. The United States watches Israel too, including its prime minister. [cite:ow4_aj_wsj2015] Since November 2023 it has flown its own drones over Gaza, for a stated and limited purpose. [cite:ow4_d1_drones] And the NSA treated some Israeli requests, such as targeting data for killings in 2006, as "particularly problematic"; the documents do not record the outcome. [cite:ow4_int2019] What is not documented is any American access to Unit 8200's own surveillance of Gaza and the West Bank comparable to Israel's access to American raw collection. The public record does not show that such access exists; it also does not prove that it does not.
