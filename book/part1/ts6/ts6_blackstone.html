The Blackstone Memorials, 1891 and 1916

The first time the restorationist idea was put in writing before an American president, it came from a Chicago property developer. William E. Blackstone was born on 6 October 1841 in Adams, New York, was converted at a Methodist revival at the age of eleven, and settled in Oak Park, Illinois, in 1870, where he did well in building and property investment before giving most of his time to religious work. He died on 7 November 1935. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] Earlier parts of this volume traced the British line of restorationist thought and the Bible-conference movement through which John Nelson Darby's system reached American Protestants. [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|Shaftesbury and the British restorationists] [scene:ts3_niagara|Niagara and the prophecy conferences] Blackstone belongs to that second current, and his case shows how a prophetic belief was turned into a political request.

The evangelist and his book

In 1878 Blackstone published Jesus Is Coming under his initials, W.E.B. It was translated into more than forty languages. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] Jonathan Moorhead, writing in the journal of the Evangelical Theological Society in 2010, calls it "the most important early book on premillennialism" and its author "an ardent supporter of dispensational premillennialism." [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] The book predates the Scofield Reference Bible by thirty-one years, one of several dates that tell against the claim that Scofield invented Christian Zionism. [scene:ts14_myths|Myths, contested claims and what is settled]

Blackstone's interest in the Jewish people had a second side that later accounts often leave out. After a meeting in Chicago on 4 November 1887 he became the first superintendent of a committee for evangelistic work among Jews; renamed the Chicago Hebrew Mission two years later, it still operates as Life in Messiah. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] On 24 and 25 November 1890 he organised a Conference on the Past, Present, and Future of Israel at the First Methodist Episcopal Church in Chicago, attended by Orthodox Jewish and Protestant leaders but not by leaders of Reform Judaism, which called on the great powers, in the Wikipedia entry's words, to "give Palestine to the Zionists" and passed resolutions of sympathy for the Jews of the Russian Empire. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] He first saw a Jewish return as a step toward the conversion of Jews to Christianity, which he hoped would hasten Christ's return; after the pogroms in Eastern Europe he came to argue that a homeland was needed because Europe and the United States would not admit enough Jewish refugees. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] Both reasons stayed with him. As late as 27 January 1918, speaking to a Zionist meeting at Clune's Auditorium in Los Angeles, he told his audience that God's plan for the Jews was repentance and conversion to Christianity. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets]

The memorial of March 1891

On 5 March 1891 Secretary of State James G. Blaine introduced Blackstone to President Benjamin Harrison, and Blackstone handed over the petition that became known as the Blackstone Memorial. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] Its opening was a question about refugees: "What shall be done for the Russian Jews?" It went on: "Where shall 2,000,000 of such poor people go?", noting that "Europe is crowded and has no room for more peasant population." Its answer was a further question: "Why not give Palestine back to them again?" It described Palestine as the Jews' "inalienable possession from which they were expelled by force," and drew a parallel with the Treaty of Berlin of 1878, under which the powers had given "Bulgaria to the Bulgarians" and "Servia to the Servians." [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] [cite:ts6_wiki_cz] The Wheaton College archive, which holds the Blackstone papers, quotes another line from it: "Let us restore them to the land of which they were so cruelly despoiled by our Roman ancestors." [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro]

The request itself was specific. The memorial asked the President and the Secretary of State to press the governments of Europe to hold "an International Conference to consider the condition of the Israelites, and their claims to Palestine." [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] It did not ask the United States to take Palestine or to send settlers; it asked for diplomacy among the powers that then decided the fate of Ottoman provinces.

Its signatories were the memorial's main political asset, and their number is not settled. Moorhead counts 413; the Wikipedia entry, drawing on Paul Merkley's history, gives 431; earlier chapters of this library said "more than four hundred." [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] [scene:bc_s05|Restoration before Zionism] The named signers are not in dispute: John D. Rockefeller, J. Pierpont Morgan, the Speaker of the House Thomas Reed, Cyrus McCormick, the evangelist D. L. Moody, Chief Justice Melville Fuller and the future President William McKinley were among them. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] The Wheaton archive describes the signers as religious leaders, Christian and Jewish, together with civic, business and political figures. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] The petition was therefore not a document of one denomination or one theology; it gathered men who signed for reasons of their own.

What the memorial achieved is modest on the record. Moorhead reports that Harrison "received the Memorial kindly and promised to give it his consideration," and that Blaine showed personal interest. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] The Wheaton archive states that there was no other official response, and suggests, without claiming more, that the memorial may have contributed to American protests to Russia, one factor in Russia's withdrawal of its expulsion decrees. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] Harrison's annual message to Congress in December 1891 did not mention Blackstone; one historian cited by Moorhead read it as a reaction to the appeal. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] No international conference was called.

The second memorial, 1916–1918

Blackstone's petition gained a second life a quarter of a century later, through the leader of American Zionism. In May 1916 Nathan Straus wrote to Blackstone that Louis Brandeis "agrees with me that you are the father of Zionism, as your work antedates Herzl." Moorhead dates the letter 8 May; the Wikipedia entry gives 16 May and quotes Straus describing Brandeis as "perfectly infatuated" with Blackstone's work. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] Brandeis himself wrote on 28 May 1916 that the document "is destined to become of historical significance." [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] Brandeis asked Blackstone to prepare a new memorial for President Woodrow Wilson, and Zionist leaders worked with him on it. [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] Brandeis's own channel to Wilson in these months, and his part in the drafting of the Balfour Declaration, are set out in the Road to 1948 volume. [scene:s04h|Hadassah, Brandeis and the ZOA]

The second memorial carried a different kind of weight from the first. Instead of tycoons, it gathered churches. The General Assembly of the Presbyterian Church in the U.S.A. endorsed it, and Blackstone collected eighty-two individual endorsements from Chicago, Los Angeles, New York and Ithaca. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] The Wheaton archive holds addenda from the Los Angeles Methodists, the Los Angeles Baptists and the Chicago Methodists. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] The Wikipedia entry describes the endorsement as coming from most of mainstream American Protestantism, a wider claim than the named bodies support. [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial]

How, and whether, the memorial reached Wilson is disputed in the sources. The Wheaton archive says Blackstone presented it unofficially in May 1916; the Wikipedia entry says it was presented privately in May 1917 and never made public. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] Moorhead, working from the papers, says it was never formally presented, partly because the British disliked its international character, and that Wilson's approval of it reached Rabbi Stephen Wise only on 31 August 1918, after the Balfour Declaration and after British forces had taken Jerusalem. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] On that account the memorial was a document American Zionist leaders valued and used, not an instrument that moved Wilson before November 1917. The other accounts read it differently: the Wheaton archive suggests the 1916 presentation may have influenced Wilson's favourable response to the Declaration, and the Wikipedia entry says the memorial strongly influenced him. [cite:ts6_wheaton_intro] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial]

What the record shows

Three things are documented. A dispensationalist evangelist put the case for a Jewish return to Palestine before an American president in 1891, five years before Herzl's Der Judenstaat; he did it with the signatures of the country's financial and political elite; and in 1916 the leader of American Zionism sought him out to do it again. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] [cite:ts6_wiki_memorial] [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] Blackstone also sent Herzl a Bible with the prophecies of return marked, but Moorhead notes that there is no proof Blackstone influenced Herzl. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] The title "father of Zionism" was a compliment from Straus and Brandeis, and Moorhead declines to argue for it. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets]

Two things are not shown. The record does not show that either memorial changed American policy; the first produced courtesy and the second arrived after the decision it was meant to support. And it does not show a single motive. Blackstone's own words joined humanitarian alarm at the fate of Russian Jews, a prophetic timetable, and an explicit hope for Jewish conversion. [cite:ts6_wiki_blackstone] [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] Brandeis's letters, as quoted, praise the petition's historical significance; nothing in the sources cited here shows that he shared its prophetic premises or its hope of conversion. [cite:ts6_moorhead_jets] The partnership was real, and on the evidence it rested on goals that were not the same.
