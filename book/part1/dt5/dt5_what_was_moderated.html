What Was Moderated, and in Which Direction

Complaints about platform moderation come from opposite directions, and each side tends to cite only its own cases. This section sets the documented cases side by side under one standard: what the company did, what evidence exists about why, what the company itself said, and what happened afterwards. Moderation here means a platform's enforcement of its own rules, by removing posts, suspending accounts, limiting features or reducing how widely content is shown. As the previous section explained, none of it is a First Amendment violation unless government is behind it. The question here is narrower and factual: whose speech was restricted, and how well is that established.

Palestine-related content

The most detailed allegation of one-directional moderation concerns Palestinian and pro-Palestinian content on Meta's platforms, Facebook and Instagram. On 21 December 2023 Human Rights Watch published a report based on 1,050 cases from more than 60 countries, drawn from 1,285 reports it received by 28 November 2023 in response to a public call for evidence. Of the 1,050, it classed 1,049 as peaceful content supporting Palestine and one as content supporting Israel. It identified six recurring patterns, from removal of posts and suspension of accounts to limits on features and what users call "shadow banning", a sharp fall in a post's visibility without notice. The report states its own limit: the cases were self-reported, and it "is not a global comparative analysis of overall censorship." [cite:dt5_hrw_2023] The figure of 1,049 to one therefore describes who answered the call, not the rate at which Meta enforced its rules against each side. Meta replied that "the implication that we deliberately and systemically suppress a particular voice is false," and that the report ignored the difficulty of enforcing policies "during a fast-moving, highly polarized and intense conflict." [cite:dt5_ktnv_meta]

A stronger source comes from Meta itself. The company commissioned the consultancy BSR to examine its moderation during the fighting of 10 to 21 May 2021, and published the result on 22 September 2022. BSR found an "adverse human rights impact" on Palestinian users' freedom of expression, assembly and political participation, through over-enforcement against Palestinian content and under-enforcement against content that did break the rules. It named causes rather than motives: an automated classifier for hostile speech in Arabic with no Hebrew equivalent, too few reviewers who spoke either language, and Meta's reading of US terrorist designations, which BSR said had a "disproportionate focus" on groups that identify as Muslim. Meta said it would implement 10 of BSR's 21 recommendations, partly implement four, assess six and decline one. [cite:dt5_lawfare_bsr] This is a documented finding of disparate impact. It is not a finding of intent.

Meta's Oversight Board, the outside panel that reviews the company's content decisions, then examined two specific practices. In March 2024 it found that Meta's blanket treatment of the Arabic word "shaheed", usually translated as "martyr", "substantially and disproportionately restricts free expression." Meta had said the word probably accounted for more removals "than any other single word or phrase" on its platforms. In July 2024 it lifted the blanket ban and now removes the word only when it appears with "signals of violence", such as images of weapons. [cite:dt5_medianama_shaheed] On 4 September 2024 a majority of the Board ruled on three posts using the slogan "from the river to the sea" and left them up, finding that the phrase "has multiple meanings." The Anti-Defamation League and the World Jewish Congress said they were "disappointed" and called the phrase "inherently genocidal and anti-Semitic." [cite:dt5_national_river]

Moderation also moved in the other direction on the same subject. In July 2024 Meta began removing attacks on "Zionists" where the word is used as a stand-in for Jews or Israelis, for instance comparisons to animals or claims that Zionists control the world or the media. The rule does not apply to content "explicitly about the political movement", and Meta acknowledged "nothing approaching a global consensus" on what the word means. [cite:dt5_dm_zionist] The distinction it draws, between a political movement that can be criticised and a people who cannot be attacked as a group, is the same one this library applies throughout. On X, Elon Musk took the opposite position to the Oversight Board on the same slogan, writing on 18 November 2023 that "'decolonization,' 'from the river to the sea' and similar euphemisms necessarily imply genocide," and that clear calls for extreme violence would lead to suspension. [cite:dt5_wt_musk] When TikTok's US business passed to a joint venture in which Oracle, Silver Lake, MGX and other investors hold 80.1 per cent and ByteDance 19.9 per cent, around 22 January 2026, users raised fears about Palestine-related content. A video resurfaced in which the new chief executive, Adam Presser, said months earlier that TikTok treated "Zionist" used to demean someone as hate speech. No new moderation policy was announced with the deal. [cite:dt5_national_tiktok]

The laptop story, the Trump suspension and the reversals

The best-known complaints from the right concern the 2020 election. On 14 October 2020 Twitter blocked links to a New York Post story about material said to come from Hunter Biden's laptop, under its policy on hacked materials. The next day its chief executive, Jack Dorsey, wrote that "straight blocking of URLs was wrong," and the company changed the policy to allow labels instead of removal. [cite:dt5_wt_dorsey] Meta reduced the story's distribution while fact-checkers reviewed it. In a letter to Representative Jim Jordan, released on 26 August 2024, Mark Zuckerberg wrote that "in retrospect, we shouldn't have demoted the story." [cite:dt5_wrap_zuck] The internal Twitter documents Musk released after buying the company, published from December 2022 as the "Twitter Files", showed takedown requests from both the Trump White House and the Biden campaign. Matt Taibbi, one of the journalists given the files, wrote that he had seen no evidence of government involvement in the laptop decision. [cite:de11_wiki_tfiles]

After the attack on the Capitol on 6 January 2021, Twitter permanently suspended President Trump's account on 8 January, citing "the risk of further incitement of violence"; Facebook suspended him indefinitely. On 5 May 2021 the Oversight Board found Facebook's suspension "justified" but its indefinite length "indeterminate and standardless", and gave the company six months to set a rule. [cite:dt5_engadget_ob_trump] After Musk bought Twitter he restored Trump's account on 19 November 2022, following a poll of users. [cite:de11_globe_trump] On 7 January 2025 Meta ended third-party fact-checking in the United States in favour of user-written Community Notes, said its systems had "gone too far," and Zuckerberg described the recent elections as "a cultural tipping point towards once again prioritizing speech." [cite:dt5_opb_meta2025]

What the record shows

Three things are documented. First, platforms made decisions that restricted speech on both sides of the Israel–Palestine argument and on both sides of American partisan politics, and in several cases the companies themselves later called those decisions wrong. Second, the best evidence of a systematic skew, the BSR study, attributes it to tools, staffing and legal caution, not to a decision to silence one side. Third, the rules changed most sharply when ownership or political conditions changed: Twitter's rules after Musk bought it, Meta's after the 2024 election. [cite:dt5_lawfare_bsr] [cite:de11_globe_trump] [cite:dt5_opb_meta2025] What is not documented is a comparable measure of how often each side's content is removed across platforms. Neither the companies nor the studies cited here publish one, so any claim that one side is censored more overall goes beyond the evidence.

The common thread is not which side lost. It is that a small number of private owners decided, and then re-decided, the terms of public argument for their users, with no appeal outside the company. The one place where users do hold rights against that process is where government stands behind it. [scene:dt5_government_pressure_and_the_way_back|Government pressure and the way back]
