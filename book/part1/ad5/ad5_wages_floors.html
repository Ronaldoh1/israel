The Frozen Floors

The promise that work pays has a legal form in the United States. On 24 May 1937 Franklin Roosevelt asked Congress for "a fair day's pay for a fair day's work," and on 25 June 1938 he signed the Fair Labor Standards Act, which took effect that October with a minimum wage of 25 cents an hour and a maximum workweek of 44 hours before overtime, covering industries that employed about a fifth of the labour force. [cite:ad5_dol_flsa1938] Every floor in this section descends from that act or sits beside it: the federal minimum wage, the lower cash wage for workers who earn tips, the salary line above which an employer need not pay overtime, and a handful of other dollar amounts written into federal law. What they share is that each is a fixed number of dollars. Congress, or the Labor Department acting under the law, has to change it by a deliberate act. When it does not, prices keep rising and the floor sinks without any vote being taken.

Two terms carry the argument. A nominal amount is the number printed in the law: $7.25. A real amount is what that number buys, restated in the prices of a single year so that different years can be compared; this section uses the Economic Policy Institute's series in 2025 dollars, which adjusts for consumer prices. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] A floor that stays the same in nominal terms while prices rise is falling in real terms.

The federal minimum wage

Congress has raised the federal minimum many times, always by passing a new law with new dollar amounts. It went from $1.40 to $1.60 in February 1968, to $3.10 in 1980 and $3.35 in 1981, then waited nine years for $3.80 in 1990 and $4.25 in 1991. Two steps followed in 1996 and 1997, to $5.15. After another ten-year gap, a 2007 law raised it in three annual steps on 24 July of 2007, 2008 and 2009, to $7.25. It has not changed since. [cite:us13_dol_minwage] As of October 2026 the federal minimum has stood at $7.25 for more than seventeen years, the longest stretch without an increase since the law began; the previous longest, from 1997 to 2007, lasted ten. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025]

In 2025 dollars, the minimum was worth $12.11 an hour in 1968, its highest point. It was worth $10.45 in 2009, the year it reached $7.25, and by 2025 the same $7.25 was worth exactly $7.25 in that year's prices. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] The arithmetic: $7.25 divided by $12.11 is 0.60, so the floor buys about 40 per cent less than it did in 1968; $7.25 divided by $10.45 is 0.69, so it buys about 31 per cent less than when it was last raised. An earlier EPI calculation, in mid-2022 prices, found that $7.25 then bought less than the minimum had at any time since February 1956. [cite:ad5_epi_lowest2022] Inflation in 2026 has continued to erode it: consumer prices rose 3.7 per cent in the twelve months to September 2026. [cite:us14_bls_cpi_sep26]

The national number now matters less than it once did, because states have moved. In 2026, 30 states and the District of Columbia set minimums above $7.25 and 20 states use the federal figure; the average state minimum rose from $11.18 in 2025 to $11.51 in 2026, and the District's reached $17.95 in July 2025. [cite:us13_bp_states2026] The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted about 843,000 hourly paid workers at or below $7.25 in 2024, 1.0 per cent of the 80.3 million paid by the hour, against 13.4 per cent in 1979. Only 82,000 were paid exactly $7.25; the other 760,000 earned less, which the law allows for some groups, chiefly tipped workers. About two-thirds worked in leisure and hospitality, mostly restaurants; 43 per cent were under 25 and 56 per cent worked part time. [cite:ad5_bls_mw2024]

The federal floor still marks what full-time work at the bottom is legally worth in the 20 states that use it. A worker paid $7.25 for 40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year, earns $15,080. The federal poverty guideline for 2026 is $15,960 for one person and $21,640 for two, so a full year of full-time work at the federal minimum leaves a single adult $880 below the line and a parent with one child $6,560 below it. [cite:ad5_de_pov2026] [cite:ad5_usac_pov2026]

The tipped cash wage

Since the 1966 amendments, employers of workers who earn tips have been allowed to pay a lower cash wage and count tips toward the minimum. The cash wage was originally half the minimum, and in 1991 that half came to $2.13. In 1996 Congress changed the formula so that the cash wage stayed at $2.13 while the regular minimum went up, and it has stayed there since. [cite:ad5_ogletree_tip] If tips and cash wage together fall short of $7.25, the employer must make up the difference. The $2.13 is now 29 per cent of the federal minimum rather than 50. Measured with the same EPI price ratio used for the minimum, which turns 1991's $4.25 into $9.18 in 2025 dollars, the $2.13 of 1991 was worth about $4.60 in today's prices; it has lost a little over half its value. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] This is a dollar figure that has not changed for thirty-five years, and it governs the industry where most sub-minimum pay is found. [cite:ad5_bls_mw2024]

The overtime line

The 1938 act requires time-and-a-half after the standard week, but it exempts executive, administrative and professional employees, and the Labor Department has always used a salary test to keep the exemption from swallowing low-paid workers. The test was $30 a week in 1938 and was raised every few years to 1975, when it reached $155 for executives and $170 for professionals. [cite:ad5_crs_ot2007] It then stayed put for 29 years, until a 2004 rule set $455 a week. The next attempt, a 2016 rule at $913, was blocked by a Texas federal court before it took effect. A 2019 rule raised the line to $684 a week, $35,568 a year, from 1 January 2020. [cite:ad5_kutak_ot2019] [cite:ad5_ogletree_ot2019] A 2024 rule raised it to $844 on 1 July 2024 and scheduled $1,128 for January 2025, with updates every three years; on 15 November 2024 Judge Sean Jordan of the Eastern District of Texas set the rule aside nationwide, finding that it let salary predominate over job duties, and the line returned to $684. [cite:ad5_hunton_ot2024] In April 2025 the government asked the appeals court to pause its appeals while the Labor Department reconsidered, and in May 2026 a further appeal was dropped and dismissed. [cite:ad5_k12dive_ot] [cite:ad5_law360_ot2026] Status as of October 2026: the line is $684 a week.

Using EPI's price ratios again, $455 in 2004 was worth about $735 in 2025 dollars and the $155 of 1975 about $790, so the current $684 buys less than either. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] A salaried manager paid $36,000 a year can lawfully work fifty or sixty hours a week without overtime if the job meets the duties test; a raise in the line is the only federal mechanism that moves such workers back into overtime coverage.

The record on this floor runs across both parties in the same way. A Republican administration raised the line in 2004 and again in 2019; Democratic administrations wrote the 2016 and 2024 rules; courts struck down the two larger increases on the ground that the Labor Department had exceeded the authority Congress gave it. [cite:ad5_kutak_ot2019] [cite:ad5_hunton_ot2024] The Labor Department itself took the position in 2004 that automatic indexing was beyond its power, and the automatic updates in the 2016 and 2024 rules fell with them in court. [cite:ad5_dwt_ot2024]

Other numbers the law forgot, and one it remembered

The same pattern appears outside wage law. Supplemental Security Income, the federal cash benefit for poor older and disabled people, disqualifies anyone with more than $2,000 in countable savings, or $3,000 for a married couple. Those limits were reached by gradual steps between 1985 and 1989, the only increase since the programme was enacted with limits of $1,500 and $2,250 in 1972. Had they been indexed from 1972 they would have been $9,929 and $14,893 in 2023. The income rules have the same history: benefits fall by $1 for every $2 earned above $65 a month and by a dollar for each dollar of other income above $20. More than two-thirds of recipients have no savings at all. [cite:ad5_cbpp_ssi] A bill to raise the limits to $10,000 and $20,000 has sponsors from both parties in both chambers; as of June 2026 it had not passed. [cite:ad5_ffyf_ssi2026] [scene:ad11_benefits_cliff|Part Eleven] returns to how such limits interact with earnings.

The counterexample is the $600 threshold above which a business must file a Form 1099 reporting payments to a contractor. Set in 1954 and never indexed, its 1954 value equals about $5,900 in 2025 dollars, using EPI's price ratio for that year of about 9.8. [cite:ad5_onpay_1099] [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] The 2025 reconciliation law raised it to $2,000 for payments made after 31 December 2025 and indexed it to inflation from 2027. [cite:ad5_natp_1099] Congress can index a dollar figure when it chooses to; this one, a paperwork threshold for businesses, it did.

Where the floor works as promised

The record also shows the floor doing what it was built to do. The share of hourly workers paid at or below the federal minimum fell from 13.4 per cent in 1979 to 1.0 per cent in 2024, and most of the remainder are teenagers, part-timers and tipped workers whose total pay may exceed the minimum once tips are counted. [cite:ad5_bls_mw2024] The federal system was designed to let states go higher, and they have: 30 states and the District of Columbia now set floors above the federal one. [cite:us13_bp_states2026] The strongest critics of a higher federal floor point to the Congressional Budget Office's 2019 analysis, which found that a $15 minimum would raise pay for at least 17 million people but could cost as many as 3.7 million jobs; the same report's central estimate and its low end of roughly zero are what supporters cite. [cite:ad5_wjct_cbo2019] Both readings rest on the same document. The disagreement is about trade-offs, not about whether the floor has fallen.

Interpretation, not finding: the frozen floor as a tax on time

The Sophia platform reads the frozen floors together as one mechanism: a set of fixed dollar figures that were never indexed and therefore work as a slow tax on the passage of time, levied on the people with the least room to absorb it. In this reading, the absence of an index is itself the design, because inaction requires no vote and leaves no record of who chose it.

The strongest counter-readings are these. First, the dollar form of the floors is old and was written into the 1938 act long before inflation became persistent; legislators of both parties have raised the minimum many times, and the Labor Department's view that it lacks power to index the overtime line was stated in 2004 and later matched by court rulings striking down automatic updates. [cite:ad5_dwt_ot2024] [cite:ad5_hunton_ot2024] Second, the federal floor now binds for very few workers because states have replaced it, which is how the federal design was meant to work. [cite:ad5_bls_mw2024] [cite:us13_bp_states2026] Third, Congress indexed the 1099 threshold in 2025, which shows that indexing is a choice made case by case rather than a rule against it. [cite:ad5_natp_1099] What the record establishes is narrower than either reading: each frozen figure named here lost much of its real value, the losses fell on low-paid workers, disabled people and older people with little savings, and in every case the remedy required an act that was not taken.
