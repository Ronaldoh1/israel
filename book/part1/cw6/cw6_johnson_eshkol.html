Johnson and Eshkol: Two Capitals in the Icebox, November 1963–1966

Stage Five ended in Dallas on [scene:s11c|22 November 1963], when the president who had pressed Israel over its reactor at Dimona was killed and Johnson inherited both the pressure and the policy around it. This section follows what Johnson did with that inheritance in his first eight months: how he answered Israel's first request for American tanks, how he received the first Israeli prime minister to make an official visit to Washington, and how, in the same weeks, he worked to keep Nasser's Egypt from treating Dimona as a reason for war. The documents show a president who did not choose between the two capitals. He tried to hold both.

The policy Johnson inherited. The State Department's historians, summarizing the documents they published for 1964–1968, describe Johnson's approach as a continuation of Kennedy's: good relations with both Nasser's United Arab Republic and Israel at once. Central to it, in their words, was keeping the Arab-Israeli dispute "in the icebox." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] The phrase describes a method rather than a goal. Washington did not try to settle the conflict; it tried to keep each side's demands from forcing a choice that would cost it the other. Kennedy's two pressure tracks on Israel, the inspection of Dimona that he had demanded in [scene:s11b|the letters of 1963] and the quieter questions of arms, passed to a president who wanted both handled without a public break.

Israel's request: tanks first. The first test came from Israel's embassy. In a meeting recorded for the file by Komer, Ambassador Harman, Minister Gazit and the military attaché, Colonel Ram Ron, put Israel's needs to Bundy and to Komer himself. Komer's record refers back to Eshkol's letter of 4 November 1963 and to a meeting of 14 November 1963; the Israelis were pressing a request already in train before Kennedy's death. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7] They asked for three things: surface-to-surface missiles, modern tanks, and some answer to the growing naval strength of the United Arab Republic. Tanks were the immediate priority. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7]

The tank request was specific. Israel wanted to replace about 300 Shermans with 200 M48A3s and 100 M60s, and then to obtain about 200 more M60s over two to three years. To show what the burden already was, the memo recorded that defense took about 40% of Israel's regular budget and 11% of its gross national product. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7] Those two figures were the Israeli argument in numbers: a small state already spending at that rate could not buy its way to parity alone, and it was asking the one power that might supply it at a price it could bear.

Bundy's answer. Bundy's reply, as Komer recorded it, went to each request in turn and gave little. On missiles he was dismissive of the threat Israel cited: "in practical military terms Nasser's missiles were useless." He then turned the question back on Israel. Missiles added to Israel's "existing nuclear potential," he warned, could open a new dimension in the arms race. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7] The phrase is worth noticing. In a memo of record, a senior White House official spoke to Israeli diplomats of their country's nuclear potential as a thing that already existed; the Dimona question was not separate from the arms question, it was the reason the missile request was unwelcome. Bundy also warned that 1964 would be a hard year for another reason: the United States planned to take a pro-Israeli stance on the Jordan waters, and that would cost it with the Arab states. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7] Washington's support on water was, in effect, offered as a reason why it could not also be seen supplying tanks.

Early 1964: no, but through Europe. An interagency review in early 1964 settled the tank question for the time being. The United States declined to sell tanks directly, the editors write, because of its policy of not becoming a major arms supplier to the region. It agreed instead to help Israel obtain tanks from Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] The decision had two halves, and both mattered. The refusal kept the formal policy intact; the help through Europe meant that Israel would get the armour by another route, with Washington's knowledge and assistance. How that route ran through West Germany, and what happened when it became public, is told in [scene:cw6_germany|Bonn's secret tanks].

Dimona and Nasser, March–June 1964. While the tank question was being settled, Johnson pressed Eshkol on a different point, and here Eshkol held out for three months. The episode is documented by William Burr and Avner Cohen in a National Security Archive collection of 2020. Washington wanted to tell Nasser, in effect, that Dimona was not what he feared. Eshkol refused to allow it on 3 March and again on 23 March 1964. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_dimona_nasser] On 19 March Johnson wrote to him that the danger was "a desperate pre-emptive attack": an Egyptian strike on the reactor, launched because Cairo believed Israel was building a bomb. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_dimona_nasser] Eshkol refused again in a reply of 21 April. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_dimona_nasser] Johnson's reasoning is in his letter: a reassured Nasser was less likely to strike. Eshkol's reasons for refusing are not set out in the summary of the record fetched here, beyond his question on 1 June, below.

The visit, 1–2 June 1964. Eshkol arrived in Washington at the start of June. Welcoming him at the White House on 1 June 1964, Johnson called it "the first official visit by a Prime Minister of Israel," and pledged that the United States would continue to help, "particularly in the field of desalinization of water." [cite:rs_cw6_ucsb_welcome] The public emphasis was water, not arms. In private the talks covered both. According to the American memorandum of conversation, Johnson told Eshkol that the United States "could not provide tanks directly" but would help Israel get them elsewhere. Eshkol asked: "please do not push us into the Centurions." Johnson said that the United States was "violently against nuclear proliferation." And on the issue Bundy had flagged months earlier he was unequivocal: "We back Israel fully on the Jordan water." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d65]

Komer's account to Under Secretary Ball, by telephone, gives the President's private line more bluntly. On tanks, Johnson had told Eshkol "we could not give them but we would help all we could." He had raised Dimona. He hoped Israel would "bite the bullet on the IAEA," the International Atomic Energy Agency, the international body whose controls Washington wanted applied to Israel's reactors. And he asked Israel not to "force us to say publicly that we are behind you." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d66] That last request is the icebox policy in a sentence. Washington would support Israel; it did not want to be made to say so in a way that cost it Cairo.

On Dimona and Nasser, the first day of talks brought one more refusal. On 1 June Eshkol asked, "Why tell Nasser?" On 2 June, after a private meeting, he conceded, according to Komer's memo of 3 June as Burr and Cohen give it. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_dimona_nasser] The same day the two governments issued their joint statement. It restated American support for "the territorial integrity and political independence of all countries in the Near East," voiced concern at "the diversion of vitally important resources from development to armaments," and agreed on joint studies of desalting. [cite:rs_cw6_joint_stmt_1964] Read against the private record, each clause carried its own weight: the first was the old American formula, offered to all states in the region and not to Israel alone; the second was a polite reproach to everyone buying weapons, Israel included; the third was the visible reward.

What Komer counted. Komer reported to the President that the visit was "a distinct plus." He listed what Israel had agreed to. It would let the United States reassure Nasser on Dimona. It would accept observers from the IAEA in a joint desalting study. It accepted IAEA controls, in principle, on any large desalting reactor. And it would seek an IAEA agreement on its small reactor within nine months. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d68] Each concession was real and each was limited. None of them put Dimona, the reactor that mattered, under international safeguards; the IAEA was to enter by way of the desalting study, any large desalting reactor, and the small reactor. Komer's four points show what Washington could obtain in exchange for warmth and water: access at the edges of the programme, not at its centre.

The Egyptian track. The other capital was being worked at the same time, and the documents make the balance visible. Johnson wrote to Nasser, and the letter produced a pledge in July 1964 that the United Arab Republic would not develop nuclear weapons. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] Washington then tried to go further. In September 1964 McCloy went to Cairo on missiles, the subject Bundy had told the Israelis did not matter militarily but which Israel cited as a reason for its own requests. The mission failed: Nasser told him that only a solution of the Palestine problem would end the arms race. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] The two governments had each given Johnson something on nuclear weapons in the summer of 1964, and neither would give him anything on the arms race itself.

Relations with Cairo then ran downhill. The State Department editors trace the decline through disputes over Yemen, the Congo and Vietnam. Aid connected with the Congo ended in April 1965; the last funds under P.L. 480, the American food-aid programme, were released in June 1965; and in June 1966 Rusk advised against renewing the P.L. 480 agreement, the United States extending $70 million in food credit instead. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] The shrinking of food aid from a programme to a credit line marks how far the Egyptian half of the icebox policy had worn by 1966, while the Israeli half, as the following sections show, moved from refusing tanks to selling them.

Did the visit change arms policy? The record supports two readings, and they turn on different time frames. On the first, the visit changed nothing on arms. The memorandum of conversation and Komer's account to Ball both record the same answer the interagency review had already reached: the United States "could not provide tanks directly" and would only help Israel obtain them from Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d65] [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d66] On this view, what Eshkol took home was water, desalting and warm words; the policy against becoming a major arms supplier held. On the second reading, the visit opened the road to the change it formally refused. The volume's editors note that the European route, German tanks fitted with American modernization kits, depended on secrecy, and that within a year Washington was selling tanks to Israel directly. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] The first reading is the better account of what was decided in June 1964; the second is the better account of what that decision led to once the secret could not be kept. The documents fetched here do not show that anyone in Washington planned the second outcome in June 1964.

What the record does not settle. Several things remain open. The memoranda record that Eshkol conceded on Nasser after a private meeting on 2 June, but not what was said in it, or what, if anything, he was offered. The editors' summary records that Johnson's letter to Nasser produced the July pledge, but it does not, in the text fetched here, say what the letter told Nasser about Dimona. Komer's four concessions are documented as promises; whether Israel sought the IAEA agreement on its small reactor within the nine months is not in these sources. And the larger question the icebox policy posed, whether holding both capitals was possible at all, was not answered in 1964. It was answered over the next three years, as tanks, aircraft, water and raids each forced the choice that Johnson had hoped to postpone.
