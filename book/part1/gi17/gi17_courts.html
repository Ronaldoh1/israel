The Courts

Three kinds of court have ruled on Israel's conduct since 2024: the International Court of Justice, which settles disputes between states and gives advisory opinions; the International Criminal Court, which tries individuals; and national courts, which decide whether their own governments may keep supplying arms. The record of each is set out below with Israel's answer beside it. The fuller accounts are in Gaza, Documented and The West Bank, Documented, and this section links to them rather than retelling them. South Africa v. Israel

South Africa brought its case under the Genocide Convention at the end of 2023, alleging genocidal acts committed with genocidal intent. Israel answered that it was defending itself, that the statements of officials South Africa cited were not policy, and that it had no genocidal intent. [cite:gz_ch_icj] The Court has ruled three times on provisional measures, which are orders made while a case proceeds and do not decide it. On 26 January 2024 it found "at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa" plausible and ordered Israel to prevent acts within the Convention, to prevent and punish incitement, and to enable humanitarian aid; it did not order a ceasefire. [cite:gz_oj_icj2024] On 28 March 2024, citing "the spread of famine and starvation," it ordered Israel unanimously to ensure the "unhindered provision at scale" of food, water, electricity, fuel, shelter and medical care, including by opening more land crossings, and, by 15 votes to 1, to ensure that its army did not violate Palestinians' rights under the Convention, including by blocking aid. [cite:gi17_icj_order0328] On 24 May 2024, by 13 votes to 2, it ordered Israel to "halt its military offensive and any other action in the Rafah Governorate" that might inflict conditions of life that could bring about the group's physical destruction. [cite:gz_lawfare_rafah] Israel said it "has not and will not" conduct such actions in Rafah and called the genocide charge "false, outrageous and morally repugnant." [cite:gz_toi_rafah]

The case is now in its written stage. Israel filed its counter-memorial, and on 21 May 2026 the Court set 22 November 2027 for South Africa's reply and 22 May 2029 for Israel's rejoinder. As of October 2026 no judgment on whether genocide was committed exists, and none is expected for years. [cite:gz_icj_timelimits]

The advisory opinions

The Court's most direct ruling on the territories this volume concerns came in an advisory opinion requested by the General Assembly. On 19 July 2024 it found Israel's continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory unlawful and said Israel must end it "as rapidly as possible," cease all new settlement activity, evacuate all settlers and make reparation. It found that Israel's policies amounted to "systemic discrimination," and that all states must neither recognise the situation as lawful nor help maintain it. Eleven of the fifteen judges agreed with all of its findings; the vice-president, Julia Sebutinde, dissented. [cite:wb_livelaw_icj2024] [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] Netanyahu replied: "The Jewish people are not occupiers in their own land, including in our eternal capital Jerusalem, nor in Judea and Samaria, our historical homeland." [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] The Assembly turned the opinion into a twelve-month deadline that expired in September 2025, as described in the previous section. [cite:wb_ahram_unga2024] [scene:gi17_un|The United Nations]

A second opinion, of 22 October 2025, concerned aid. The Court said that Israel, as occupying power, must "ensure the basic needs of the local population" and was "under an obligation to agree to and facilitate relief schemes provided by the United Nations and its entities," UNRWA among them, and it found that Israel had not substantiated its allegations against UNRWA's staff. Israel, which did not take part in the proceedings, said it "categorically rejects" the opinion. [cite:gz_ji_ao2025] Advisory opinions state the law as the Court finds it; they are not judgments in a case and carry no enforcement mechanism of their own. [cite:wb_livelaw_icj2024]

The arrest warrants

On 21 November 2024 the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for Netanyahu and his former defence minister, Yoav Gallant, finding reasonable grounds to believe that they bore responsibility for the war crime of starvation as a method of warfare, for intentionally directing attacks against civilians, and for the crimes against humanity of murder, persecution and other inhumane acts. It issued a warrant the same day for Mohammed Deif, commander of Hamas's armed wing, for murder, extermination, torture, rape and hostage-taking. [cite:gz_un_icc] [cite:gz_almon_icc] The court ended proceedings against Deif on 26 February 2025 after prosecutors reported that he had been killed in July 2024. [cite:gz_ji_deif] Netanyahu called the warrants "anti-Semitic"; President Joe Biden called them "outrageous." [cite:gz_almon_icc] Israel, which like the United States is not a party to the court's statute, challenged its jurisdiction; in October 2025 the challenge was still pending and the court had found "no legal basis" to withdraw the warrants in the meantime. [cite:gz_toi_icc2025] [cite:wb_aj_icc_timeline]

The United States answered with sanctions on the court itself. Executive Order 14203 of 6 February 2025 cited what it called "illegitimate and baseless actions targeting America and our close ally Israel," and the Treasury and State Department then listed the prosecutor, judges, both deputy prosecutors, the UN Special Rapporteur Francesca Albanese and three Palestinian human rights organisations. The court said it "stands firmly by its personnel." [cite:ow11_eo14203] [cite:ow11_wiki_eo14203] [cite:ow11_icc_stmt2025] Three of its judges sued in New York in June 2026, and in October 2026 a federal court in Maine refused to dismiss a First Amendment challenge to the sanctions programme. [cite:ow11_jurist_judges2026] [cite:ow11_gs_maine2026] Part Eleven of The One-Way Mirror follows each designation. [scene:ow11_icc|The One-Way Mirror: sanctioning a court] As of October 2026 neither warrant against an Israeli official had been executed.

National courts and the F-35

The most concrete litigation concerned parts for the F-35 fighter, which Israel's air force flies and which is assembled from components made in several countries. In Britain, Foreign Secretary David Lammy suspended about 30 of 350 export licences for Israel in September 2024 after reviewing its compliance with humanitarian law, but the suspension did not cover F-35 parts. The Palestinian group Al-Haq, joined by Oxfam, challenged that exception. On 30 June 2025 the High Court rejected the challenge, saying the "acutely sensitive and political issue is a matter for the executive." The government had argued that suspending the parts would affect the "whole F-35 programme" and have a "profound impact on international peace and security." [cite:gi17_aje_f35uk]

The Dutch case went the other way and then back. In February 2024 an appeals court in The Hague ordered the government to stop exporting F-35 parts to Israel, citing a clear risk of violations of international law, and the government complied while appealing. On 3 October 2025 the Supreme Court held that the appeals court "was not entitled to make its own assessment" of that risk, but ordered the minister to reassess the licence within six weeks, with the halt remaining in force. The government had argued that foreign policy belongs to government and that the United States would supply the parts anyway. [cite:gi17_es_dutch] On 14 November 2025 the cabinet said it would keep the shipments halted pending a final decision within six months; since April 2025 Dutch permits for Israel have been assessed case by case. [cite:gi17_nlt_dutch2025] A later decision was not found in the reporting checked for this part.

International courts have issued orders and opinions of unusual breadth against a state that rejects their authority or their conclusions; the binding orders have no enforcement arm except states, and the national courts, which do have that arm, have deferred to their governments in one case and only partly in the other. Israel's position throughout has been that the courts are biased or lack jurisdiction, that it is fighting a war of self-defence, and that it has no intent to destroy anyone.
