The New CIA's First Major Warning

On 28 November 1947, one day before the United Nations voted on partition, a ten-week-old American intelligence agency put its judgment in writing: partition would mean war. The familiar version of events holds that American intelligence either never assessed partition or supported it. The documents say otherwise. The newest agency in Washington assessed it formally and warned against it on the eve of the vote.

Before 1947, American foreign intelligence ran first through the Office of Strategic Services, or OSS, created in 1942 and led by William J. Donovan, whom Roosevelt appointed. [cite:w_oss] The OSS was dissolved in October 1945. Its analysts and operatives fed the agencies that followed, beginning with the interim Central Intelligence Group in 1946. [cite:w_oss] The National Security Act of 1947 then created the Central Intelligence Agency, effective 18 September 1947, together with the National Security Council and the structure that became the Department of Defense. [cite:w_nsa1947] Truman appointed the first Director of Central Intelligence, Rear Admiral Roscoe Hillenkoetter. [cite:w_hillenkoetter]

The agency was barely ten weeks old when it delivered its estimate on Palestine. Its Office of Reports and Estimates issued ORE-55, "The Consequences of the Partition of Palestine," dated 28 November 1947. [cite:cia_ore55] The summary opened with a prediction: if the General Assembly adopted partition, armed hostilities between Jews and Arabs would break out. The United Nations would then be morally bound to enforce its decision. Jewish forces would initially prevail, because of superior organization and equipment. And by backing partition, the United States had already lost much of its prestige in the Arab world; an imposed partition would put American commercial and strategic interests in serious danger. [cite:cia_ore55] The declassified estimate is held in the CIA's own reading room. [cite:cia_ore55]

The estimate did not stand alone. It echoed warnings already on file from Loy Henderson at the State Department, from the Joint Chiefs of Staff and from Defense Secretary James Forrestal, described in [scene:s07j|the previous chapter]. Within roughly ten weeks, four separate parts of the American government had warned in writing against American sponsorship of partition: the State Department's Near East office, the Joint Chiefs, the Secretary of Defense and the new CIA.

The case on the other side came from the President's domestic political advisers, led by Clark Clifford and David Niles. Their view prevailed, both in the partition vote and later in the recognition of Israel eleven minutes after its declaration of independence. The record shows that the warnings were made and read. It does not show them shaping the outcome.

The estimate is worth a closer look, both for what it predicted and for how it framed the risks. It predicted war. It also predicted that the Jewish side would hold the early advantage, because of organization and equipment. The concerns it set out were American interests: prestige in the Arab world, commerce and strategy. That was the language of the State Department and the Pentagon as well, and it was the language that lost.

A question often asked about this period is whether Washington understood what it was backing. On the evidence of ORE-55, it did. American intelligence assessed partition, in writing, and predicted war and damage to American interests. The decision was taken anyway.
