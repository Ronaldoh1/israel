The Suppression Ledger: Every Documented Act, c.180 to 2023

Book Two has followed one question through eighteen centuries: what the institutional church did to the people and books that taught a direct, unmediated knowledge of God. Parts Twenty to Thirty-One answered it one period at a time. This section gathers their findings into a single ledger, so that the record can be read as a whole before the verdict in [scene:vt32_verdict_two|the next section] is given. The rule for entry is the same throughout: an act goes in only if a surviving document names who acted, when, against whom, and what followed. Acts that are told about but not documented are set out separately, in the second table, beside what the record shows instead.

Four kinds of act, and who did them

The documented acts fall into four kinds, and keeping them apart is the first finding. The first is argument and exclusion: a bishop writes a refutation, names a book or a teacher, and declares them outside the church. Irenaeus of Lyon did this about 180, naming the Valentinians, the "Gospel of Truth" and a "Gospel of Judas"; Tertullian of Carthage, about 200, argued that heretics had no standing even to dispute over scripture. [cite:vt24_ah1_pref] [cite:vt25_iren_311] [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_1_31] [cite:vt24_praescr] The second is the restriction of reading: a list of approved books and a warning against the rest, as in Athanasius's Festal Letter of 367, which calls the apocrypha "an invention of heretics" but orders nothing destroyed. [cite:vt23_na_festal39] The third is the destruction of books by order. The fourth is force against people: confiscation, exile, imprisonment, torture and death. The third and fourth kinds required the power of a state, and for most of the period covered here that power belonged to emperors, kings and princes rather than to bishops. The ledger names the actor that acted, and notes where a church authority asked a ruler to act.

The second finding is about Rome. Before the fifth century the bishop of Rome was one actor among several. The heresy hunters of the second to fourth centuries wrote from Lyon, Carthage, Alexandria and Cyprus; the first ranking of the great sees, Nicaea's canon 6, set Alexandria's authority beside Rome's customary one. [cite:vt24_wiki_panarion] [cite:vt22_canons] The papacy becomes the principal actor in the ledger only from the twelfth century, and the "Vatican", as a state, only in 1929.

What the first table shows

Three points stand out. The first state order in the table to burn religious books was a pagan emperor's, against the Manichaeans, and the second was the same government's against the Christian scriptures in 303. [cite:vt21_harland_mani] [cite:vt21_lact_dmp] The first Christian emperor used the same instrument against a Christian teacher: Constantine's letter ordered any treatise by Arius to be "consigned to the flames", on pain of death for concealment, and later laws did the same to Eunomian, Montanist and Nestorian books. [cite:vt22_socrates1] [cite:vt21_fc_laws] Second, the one imperial act that names a Gnostic group is Constantine's edict preserved by Eusebius. It names the Valentinians among five groups, forbids them to meet "in any private house", hands their houses of prayer to the catholic church and, Eusebius says, directed "that search should be made for their books". [cite:vt21_vc3_64] [cite:vt21_vc3_65] [cite:vt21_vc3_66] Eusebius does not say what was done with the books found, and no surviving order to burn a Gnostic gospel by name has been found in the research for this book. [cite:vt24_vc366] Third, of all the texts laid out in Parts Twenty-Five and Twenty-Six, only the Gospel of Judas is condemned by name in an ancient source, by Irenaeus about 180 and Epiphanius in the 370s; the Gospel of Mary appears in no ancient condemnation, and the Gospel of Thomas enters the lists late, in Cyril of Jerusalem and the sixth-century Decretum, as a Manichaean book. [cite:vt26_na_iren131] [cite:vt26_arys_epiphanius] [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] [cite:vt25_decretum]

What the second table shows

The change between the two tables is the arrival of lethal force organised by the Church itself. From Ad abolendam in 1184, bishops and then papal inquisitors searched out heretics and handed the unrepentant to the secular power; the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 obliged rulers to swear to expel them and gave crusaders against heretics the indulgence of the Holy Land; Ad extirpanda in 1252 authorised torture. [cite:vt28_wiki_adabolendam] [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda] The fires were lit by lay officials, but under canons that required it. The scale is also documented within limits: Bernard Gui's own sentences, the best-counted series, record about 45 burned among 636 cases, and the Albigensian Crusade, often given a toll of a million, has no contemporary source for any total. [cite:vt28_mednet_gui] [cite:vt28_nwe_crusade] The Council of Toulouse in 1229 went further than any ancient authority against books: it forbade lay people to own the scriptures themselves, apart from the Psalter and prayer books. [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] And the same tribunals that hunted Christian dualists confiscated and burned the Talmud in Paris in 1242, an act against Jews and Judaism that belongs in the same ledger. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9]

After the sixteenth century the instruments change again: lists and declarations replace the stake. The Index of Forbidden Books ran from 1559 to 1966, and the ban on Freemasonry, first issued in 1738, still stands in the form of a 2023 declaration that membership is incompatible with the faith. [cite:vt14_brit_index] The later entries are of a different kind again, statements of regret: John Paul II's address on Galileo in 1992 and the Day of Pardon of 12 March 2000, when Cardinal Ratzinger said that "men of the church" had "sometimes used methods not in keeping with the Gospel". [cite:vt29_coyne_galileo] [cite:vt28_america_2000] That is the Holy See's stated position on the record set out above, and it is given here beside it.

Told and recorded

The second table sets the most common popular versions of this history against the record found in Parts Twenty to Thirty. Each "told" claim is one that circulates widely, including in earlier material on this platform; each correction is sourced in the Part named.

The last row is marked differently because there the popular version is broadly right, and the record supports Ron's point: no tithe was documented for any of the Gnostic schools, and the Apocryphon of John curses anyone who would exchange its teaching "for a gift". [cite:vt24_apjohn] What the record adds is that the proto-orthodox churches of the same centuries did not tithe either: Justin Martyr's Rome, about 155, collected what "each thinks fit", and Irenaeus contrasted the Jewish tithe with Christian giving of everything. [cite:vt20_justin_67] [cite:vt20_na_irenaeus_iv18] The compulsory tithe came with the church of the sixth century and the state of the eighth, as [scene:vt31_tithe|Part Thirty-One] sets out.
