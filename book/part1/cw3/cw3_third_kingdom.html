The Third Kingdom and the Retreat, 6–8 November 1956

A common memory of the Sinai campaign holds that Israel won the war and was then forced out of its gains over the following months, in the long diplomatic fight of early 1957. That fight happened, and it is told in [scene:cw3_withdrawal|the withdrawal fight of 1957]. But the decision in principle to withdraw was taken much faster, within about a day and a half of Israel's moment of greatest triumph. Between a victory letter read to the troops at Sharm el-Sheikh and a government statement accepting the UN's resolution, Israel's public position moved from the language of ancient kingdoms to the promise that it had "never planned to annex the Sinai desert." The documents of 6, 7 and 8 November 1956 record that turn hour by hour.

The victory. The IDF Archives state that Israeli forces occupied the Sinai peninsula "within 100 hours," and that it was in Israeli hands by 5 November 1956; Sharm el-Sheikh was taken that day. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_archive] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_1956israel] At Sèvres on 24 October Ben-Gurion had said that "The Straits of Tiran are the State of Israel's Suez Canal." [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres]

At a ceremony at Sharm el-Sheikh the day after the fighting ended, about 6 November, Moshe Dayan, who commanded the campaign, read the troops a letter from Ben-Gurion. According to an account in the Israeli news site Walla, which quotes the Hebrew in 2023, the letter linked the conquest to biblical history and said that Yotvat (Tiran), described in the letter as a former independent Hebrew state, "will return to be part of the Third Kingdom of Israel." [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] The phrase placed Tiran inside the kingdom it named. The letter's archival text and its exact date were not fetched for this book; the quotation rests on Walla's report.

A second witness remembered the same moment from the other side of Israeli politics. Uri Avnery, a journalist at the time and a political opponent of Ben-Gurion, recalled in his book Israel Without Zionists Ben-Gurion's "victory announcement ... proclaiming the 'Third Kingdom of Israel'" and then, within about a day, "his speech announcing, in a broken voice, that he had acceded to President Eisenhower's request and agreed to the retreat." [cite:rs_cw3_avnery] That is a recollection, written later, by a man who opposed the government. It agrees with Walla on the phrase and with the documents below on the speed of the reversal; its description of Ben-Gurion's voice is Avnery's alone.

The Knesset, 7 November. On 7 November Ben-Gurion reviewed the campaign before the Knesset. His reasons for the war, the fedayeen raids, the blockade, the Czechoslovak arms and the joint command of Egypt, Jordan and Syria, are set out in this chapter's opening section. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov] On the future, the speech took positions that left almost no room for retreat. "The armistice agreement with Egypt is dead and buried and cannot be restored to life," he said. The armistice lines had "no more validity." And: "On no account will Israel agree to the stationing of a foreign force, no matter how it is called, in its territory" or in any area Israel held. At the same time he offered Egypt direct peace negotiations without preconditions. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_7nov] [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d534]

The speech was given on a day of mounting pressure elsewhere. The Soviet notes of 5 November had already arrived; his reply of 8 November is told in [scene:cw3_soviet_hungary|Moscow's notes and Budapest]. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bulganin1] The same day Hammarskjöld warned Ambassador Lodge that persistent Israeli defiance could prompt so strong a UN reaction that Israel might be expelled. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d534] And in the evening, in Washington, the President wrote.

Eisenhower's message, 7 November. The message Eisenhower addressed to Ben-Gurion on 7 November was sent at 6:52 p.m. Washington time; a copy had been handed to Reuven Shiloah at 6:15. It urged Ben-Gurion to "comply with the resolutions of the United Nations General Assembly," and it warned that an Israeli refusal to withdraw "could not but bring about the condemnation of Israel as a violator" and could "impair the friendly cooperation between our two countries." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d550] [cite:rs_cw3_app_ike_7nov] As of 2 a.m., the FRUS record notes, it had not yet reached him. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d550] It was released publicly on 8 November. [cite:rs_cw3_app_ike_7nov] One reading of the phrase "friendly cooperation between our two countries" is that the President was telling the Prime Minister that the relationship itself was in question.

The reply and the statement, 8 November. Ben-Gurion's reply of 8 November conceded the central point while keeping a condition. Israel would "willingly withdraw our forces," he wrote, upon the "conclusion of satisfactory arrangements with the United Nations" for the international force. "We have never planned to annex the Sinai desert." And he asked that Egypt abandon "its policy of boycott and blockade" and enter direct peace negotiations. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_ike_bg] [cite:rs_cw3_app_ike_7nov] The day before, he had said Israel would accept no foreign force on ground it held; now the arrangements for that force were the condition of Israel's leaving.

The government's statement of the same day put the decision more plainly: "It was finally agreed to accept the United Nations Resolution and withdraw Israel's forces." It listed the war's three aims: to destroy the forces that threatened Israel, to liberate Israeli territory held by an invader, and to secure free navigation in the Gulf of Eilat and the Suez Canal. It acknowledged that only the first had been fully achieved. And it quoted the Prime Minister: "There is no power in the world which can nullify our victory." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] The statement did not mention Tiran as part of any kingdom. Its second aim, "Israeli territory held by an invader," it did not further define in the text fetched here.

The manner of leaving. In Washington on the morning of 8 November, at 10:45, Eban met Assistant Secretary of State Rountree. He told him that the manner of Israel's withdrawal mattered as much as the act itself. The State Department, for its part, opposed a visit by Ben-Gurion to Washington that would bypass the UN, and approved a meeting only "after compliance." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d557] Rountree also told Eban that Israel's withdrawal mattered for "Western-Soviet relations" and that failure to comply "would endanger world peace." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d557] Israel was asking for a negotiated exit with something to show for it; Washington was offering a meeting only after Israel had done what the UN asked. That gap, between withdrawal on terms and withdrawal first, is what the next four months were fought over.

Begin's position, 16 November. Not everyone in the Knesset accepted the turn. On 16 November Menachem Begin praised the government's refusal to leave Sinai until Egypt made peace, and concluded, in the summary published by the Menachem Begin Heritage Center of a report in the Jewish Herald, that parts of the Land of Israel still needed to be liberated. [cite:rs_cw3_begin_center] The source is a summary, not Begin's words, and it describes the government's position as a refusal at a moment when the government had already agreed in principle to withdraw on conditions. The difference shows how the 8 November decision could be read at home: as a promise to leave, or as a refusal to leave until conditions were met.

What "Third Kingdom" meant. Two readings of the episode are offered, and each has documentary support. The first takes the language at face value: Ben-Gurion's letter tied Tiran and Sinai to the biblical kingdoms, and his Knesset tone the next day was that of a leader claiming lasting gains; on this reading the rhetoric expressed a territorial and messianic claim that was then abandoned under pressure. [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] [cite:rs_cw3_avnery] The record of Sèvres gives this reading weight: an Israeli eighth point sought "permanent annexation" of the area east of a line from El Arish through Abu Ageila and Nakhl to Sharm el-Sheikh, and Britain and France never backed it. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres]

The second reading treats the letter as rhetoric for soldiers on the day of victory, not policy. On 8 November Ben-Gurion wrote to the President that Israel had "never planned to annex the Sinai desert," and the cabinet accepted withdrawal within a day; the war aims it listed were security and navigation, not territory in Sinai. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_ike_bg] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] The strongest form of this case is that a government planning annexation would not have agreed to leave in principle within thirty-six hours of conquest. The strongest reply is that the agreement was made under the pressure of a Soviet threat, an American warning and a UN demand arriving together, and that a letter to the American President is the last place one would expect an annexationist aim to be stated. The documents fetched here do not settle which reading is right; they establish that both the letter and the disavowal were written, a day or two apart, by the same man.

The memory. The episode outlived 1956. The scholar Uri Bar-Joseph, reviewing work by the historian Avi Raz in 2013, summarizes it as "brutal pressures" by the Eisenhower administration that led Ben-Gurion to announce a full withdrawal 24 hours after a Knesset speech implying that Israel was not ready for one. That memory, Bar-Joseph writes, weighed on Israel's cabinet in June 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo] How it did so belongs to [scene:s12|the crisis of May–June 1967].

Several things remain open. The text and date of the "Third Kingdom" letter are known only from Walla's 2023 report and Avnery's recollection; no archival copy was fetched. What passed in Israel's cabinet between the Knesset speech of 7 November and the decision of 8 November is not in these documents, and so the relative weight of Eisenhower's message, the Soviet note and the UN's warning cannot be measured from them; Eisenhower's message, by FRUS's own note, had not yet reached Ben-Gurion in the early hours of 8 November. And the conditions Ben-Gurion attached to withdrawal on 8 November, the "satisfactory arrangements" for the UN force, were not defined in the letter; what they turned out to mean is the story of the withdrawal fight.
