The largest Bible in Christianity was never controlled by Rome. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church counts eighty-one books in its scripture, among them the Book of Enoch and the Book of Jubilees, which the Western churches set aside and Europe forgot, until a Scottish traveller brought Ethiopian copies home in 1773. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] [cite:vt27_hessayon] For readers who come to this shelf from The Canon, Ethiopia is the great counter-example: a Christian kingdom whose canon was formed outside the councils, the lists and the papal offices that fixed the Western Bible.

It is not a counter-example of freedom from power. The Ethiopian Bible was kept by a church bound to a throne for sixteen centuries, by kings who claimed descent from Solomon through a book written to prove it, and by monks who guard an object no one else may see. It was attacked from outside: by Jesuits who converted an emperor, by a Fascist army that massacred monks, by a British expedition that carried away hundreds of manuscripts on elephants, and finally by a revolutionary state that took the church's land and strangled its patriarch. [cite:vt27_wiki_ethcatholic] [cite:et_lrb_holywar] [cite:et_cup_maqdala] [cite:et_wiki_theophilos] This book follows the Ethiopian scriptures through all of those hands.

The companion volume, The Vatican Architecture, sets out the eighty-one books, Enoch, Jubilees and the Ethiopian Maccabees in detail. [scene:vt27_church_and_canon|The Ethiopian Bible, in The Vatican Architecture] This book builds on that survey and asks the question that runs through this shelf: who held the book, what did holding it give them, and who paid? As elsewhere, the documents come first, and readings are marked as readings.
