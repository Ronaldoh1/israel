The Architecture on One Page

The nine parts before this one each followed a single channel. Put together, they describe one architecture with a clear shape. At the top are donors, most of them American individuals and foundations, whose money is pooled by a small number of organizations. In the middle are those organizations, AIPAC's four bodies above all, and the channels the law allows each of them: lobbying, campaign contributions, earmarking, independent spending, charity-paid travel. At the bottom are the places the money reaches: members of Congress and candidates, state legislatures and treasuries, and, through parallel organizations and the Israeli government's own spending, parliaments abroad. The totals. The table gathers the volume's principal figures in one place. Each comes from a different source, measures something different and covers a different period, so the figures cannot be added together into a single total, and this volume does not attempt one. They show the order of magnitude of each channel and how it has changed.

Three changes. Read across the parts, the architecture has changed in three ways in the period this volume covers. The first is scale: campaign money tied to the network rose from about $15 million a cycle in pro-Israel contributions in 2018 to between $95 million and $127 million for AIPAC's two committees alone in 2024, depending on the count. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs2020] [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026] The second is method: a network that for seven decades lobbied and steered donations without spending in its own name now spends directly in primaries, often in advertising that does not mention Israel and, increasingly in 2026, through committees whose names do not show who funds them. [cite:punchbowl_udp_no_israel] [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] The third is reach: the Israeli government, once a background presence, became a direct spender on American opinion and American officials, through registered agents and through trips it paid for itself.
