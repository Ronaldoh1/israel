The Partnership Era, 2009–2026

After 2009 the relationship was described in American law not as aid to a friendly state but as a partnership. Two statutes said so in their titles, a ten-year pledge set a record, the Pentagon moved Israel into the command that runs American forces in the Middle East, and the Department of Homeland Security opened America's borders to Israeli visitors without visas. After 7 October 2023 the scale of transfers changed again. This section follows the statutes and executive actions in order and closes with the negotiations, still open in October 2026, over what replaces the current pledge.

Two partnership acts, 2012 and 2014. The United States–Israel Enhanced Security Cooperation Act of 2012 was introduced in the Senate by Barbara Boxer of California with 71 co-sponsors, passed the Senate by unanimous voice vote on 29 June 2012 and was signed by President Obama on 27 July. It declared it American policy to help preserve Israel's qualitative military edge, extended the authority for the WRSA-I stockpile and for loan guarantees, called for support for Iron Dome and required a QME report within 180 days. [cite:ow_jvl_2012act] Two years later the United States–Israel Strategic Partnership Act of 2014, sponsored by Boxer and Roy Blunt of Missouri, passed the Senate unanimously on 18 September and the House by voice vote on 3 December. It declared Israel a "major strategic partner," again extended the stockpile authority, set out the visa-waiver goal, authorized joint homeland security research on border, maritime and aviation security, and asked for a report on cyber cooperation. [cite:ow_jvl_2014act] Neither bill was recorded on a roll call in the House; both passed without a single recorded vote against.

The record pledge, 2016. On 14 September 2016 the Obama administration concluded the third ten-year memorandum, for FY2019–2028: $38 billion, made up of $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing and $5 billion for missile defence, or $3.3 billion and $500 million a year. The White House called it "the largest single pledge of military assistance in U.S. history." The memorandum also ended Israel's right to spend part of its aid on Israeli-made equipment and on fuel, so that up to $1.2 billion more a year would go to American-produced capabilities. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] Congress later wrote the FMF level into law: Section 1273 of the FY2021 National Defense Authorization Act authorizes not less than $3.3 billion a year through 2028. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

CENTCOM, 2021. On 15 January 2021, days before the Trump administration left office, the Pentagon moved Israel from the area of European Command to that of Central Command, which runs American operations from Egypt to Central Asia. The Wall Street Journal reported that President Trump ordered the change; the Pentagon said "the Abraham Accords paved the way." Israeli liaison officers now sit at CENTCOM's headquarters in Tampa. [cite:ow_inss_centcom] The change placed Israel inside the same American command structure as the Arab states it had long been kept apart from, and Part Seven follows its consequences.

Iron Dome and the visa waiver, 2021–2023. After the May 2021 Gaza war, House Democratic leaders removed $1 billion to replenish Iron Dome from a stopgap spending bill when some members objected, then passed it as a separate bill on 23 September 2021 by 420 votes to 9. [cite:ow1_pbs_irondome2021] On 27 September 2023 the Secretaries of Homeland Security and State designated Israel for the Visa Waiver Program, with Israeli travellers able to use the electronic system from 30 November, after Israel committed on 19 July 2023 to reciprocal treatment of all American citizens, including Palestinian Americans. [cite:ow_dhs_vwp] Part Six examines how that reciprocity commitment has been applied.

After 7 October 2023. The war that began with Hamas's attack on 7 October 2023 brought the largest transfers since 1973. Iron Dome batteries were supplied from US Army stocks. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] On 20 April 2024 the House passed the Israel Security Supplemental Appropriations Act, 366 to 58, with 21 Republicans and 37 Democrats voting against; the Senate passed the combined package 79 to 18 on 23 April, and the President signed it as Public Law 118-50 on 24 April. [cite:ow1_clerk_roll152] [cite:ow1_lobby_hr815] It provided $3.5 billion in FMF, $4 billion for missile defence and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser system. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] In August 2024 the State Department notified more than $20 billion in sales, including F-15IA aircraft. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] By April 2025 Israel had 751 active Foreign Military Sales cases worth $39.2 billion, and Israel's Ministry of Defense reported that 800 aircraft and 140 ships had delivered more than 90,000 tons of materiel by May 2025. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

Emergency authority, 2025. The Arms Export Control Act lets Congress review major arms sales before they proceed, but Section 36(b) allows the executive to waive that review by declaring an emergency. On 28 February 2025 Secretary of State Marco Rubio signed such a declaration covering about $4 billion in sales to Israel. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] The State Department's statement of 1 March said nearly $12 billion in major sales had been approved since the inauguration, and that the declaration reversed what it called the previous administration's "partial arms embargo" and its "baseless and politicized conditions" on aid; Rubio said Israel has "no greater ally in the White House than President Trump." [cite:ow1_state_rubio2025] The critics' case, which Part Eight takes up, is that emergency declarations remove the one point at which Congress can examine a sale; the government's case is that a partner at war needs weapons faster than the review period allows. A December 2023 Senate resolution that would have required a human-rights report on Israel's use of American weapons under Section 502B of the Foreign Assistance Act failed. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

What replaces the pledge, 2026. The 2016 memorandum expires at the end of FY2028. In January 2026 Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was reported to seek an end to American military aid within ten years, and on 1 June 2026 he wrote to Representative Marlin Stutzman that "the time has now arrived for us to move from aid recipient to partner." [cite:ow1_wiki_nextmou] On 5 June 2026 Israel's Ministry of Defense announced that formal talks had opened on a framework to "gradually transition from aid to a completely reciprocal partnership," based on joint research, development and co-production; the Israeli team is led by the ministry's director-general, Amir Baram, with Ambassador Yechiel Leiter, and the American team by State Department Counselor Daniel Holler and Ambassador Mike Huckabee. [cite:ow1_mod_mou2026] Reporting in July 2026 said Israel was pressing to accelerate the talks before the midterm elections. As of October 2026 the public record reached for this volume does not show a concluded agreement. [cite:ow1_wiki_nextmou] Israel's government describes the shift as a move to reciprocity. Critics argue that it would hide the money rather than end it: Hadar Susskind of New Jewish Narrative, writing in The Nation on 22 June 2026, argued that folding support into Pentagon procurement and co-production would leave "no annual appropriation to debate" and "no line item to block." [cite:ow1_nation_susskind] Both are arguments about a framework whose terms are not yet public.

The partnership era thus did two things at once. It enlarged the transfers and embedded Israel more deeply in American command and border systems, and it began, at Israel's own suggestion, the move away from grants.
