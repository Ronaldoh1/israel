Remembering the Dead

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

In 1939 the Holy See allowed Chinese Christians to take part in the rites honouring their ancestors, after two centuries of prohibition. [cite:fa_brit_rites] In the Andes three centuries earlier, the honoured ancestors were burned. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] And on the gold tablets of the Greek mysteries, the dead were told to drink from the Lake of Memory and say: "my race is heavenly". [cite:vt33_getty_goldtablets]

The inner reading takes ancestor veneration as a practice of memory. To set a tablet in the house, to keep the dead close, to speak to them on their days, is to remember where one comes from and to whom one answers. The gold tablets name forgetting as the soul's danger and memory as its rescue; the honouring of ancestors applies the same rule to a family. A person who remembers their dead remembers that their own life is part of a longer one.

The reading notes that the authority which forbade the Chinese rites in 1704 allowed them in 1939, and that the Andean ancestors had no such reprieve. [cite:fa_brit_rites] [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] What changed between the two decisions was not the practice but the judges' understanding of it, which is the lesson of this whole book: an art is often forbidden by those who have not yet understood what it is for.

The record limits the reading. Chinese ancestral rites and Andean mallqui veneration belong to different religious systems with their own beliefs about the dead, and the gold tablets come from Greek initiations; their joining here is the reader's.
