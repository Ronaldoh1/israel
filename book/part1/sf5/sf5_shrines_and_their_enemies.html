The Shrines and Their Enemies

The veneration of saints' graves is central to much popular Sufism, and it has long been opposed by reform movements that regard building over graves as forbidden innovation, bidʿa, and the shrines as idolatrous. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi] The most famous case is the cemetery of al-Baqi in Medina. It was first demolished in 1806 by the Saudi-Wahhabi alliance known as the Emirate of Diriyah, rebuilt in the mid-nineteenth century, and destroyed again in 1925 or 1926, when Ibn Saud's forces granted permission for demolition; the work, carried out by the Ikhwan, a Wahhabi militia, reportedly began on 21 April 1926. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi] Domes, cupolas and mausoleums were razed, reportedly including the tombs of the Prophet's parents, his uncles, his son Ibrahim and four Shiʿi Imams. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi]

The historian Adeel Mohammadi argues that the destruction was also political: a way of asserting Najdi and Saudi authority over the Hijaz, and perhaps of signalling victory over Shiʿi Islam. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi] Both Sunni and Shiʿi Muslims protested; Shiʿi Muslims still mark the anniversary as a day of sorrow, and Saudi authorities have reportedly rejected calls to restore the tombs. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi]

The deadliest attack came in Egypt. On 24 November 2017, during Friday prayers, gunmen attacked the al-Rawda mosque, east of Bir al-Abed in North Sinai, which is associated with the Jaririya Sufi order, one of the largest in the region. [cite:sf_wiki_rawda] The reported toll is 311 dead, including 27 children; early reports gave 305. [cite:sf_wiki_rawda] No group claimed the attack, but reports pointed to the Islamic State's Sinai branch, and prosecutors said the attackers carried its flag. In January 2017 the Islamic State magazine Rumiyah had published an interview in which a Sinai commander condemned Sufi practices and named this district as one of three where Sufis lived that the group intended to "eradicate". [cite:sf_wiki_rawda]

The pattern of this shelf appears here in its harshest form. A movement that claims to restore pure religion treats an inner tradition's practices, its shrines, its music and its veneration of saints, as corruption, and destroys them: first the buildings, then, in the case of al-Rawda, the worshippers. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi] [cite:sf_wiki_rawda] The opponents named here are specific: the Emirate of Diriyah and Ibn Saud's Ikhwan in Medina, and the Islamic State's Sinai branch at al-Rawda. The victims were Muslims, and the protests against the destruction came from Muslims too. [cite:sf_wiki_baqi]
