The Ledger: 33 American Universities by Founding Tradition

A recurring claim in material about Catholic influence holds that the leading universities of the United States, and of the West generally, are Catholic or Jesuit institutions, or are controlled by them. The way to test a claim of that kind is to take a fixed list chosen in advance by someone else, record for each institution who founded it and what its relationship to any church is now, and count. This section does that for 33 American universities, with the University of Virginia, founded in 1819 under a plan that deliberately left out a professor of divinity, as the contrast case.

The selection rule

The list is the top of the best-known American ranking, the U.S. News & World Report "Best National Universities" list in its 2027 edition, reported on 22 and 23 September 2026. Because of ties, exactly 33 institutions occupy ranks 1 to 31; the next institutions are tied at 34. [cite:vt13_pq_usnews2027a] [cite:vt13_pq_usnews2027b] [cite:vt13_boston_usnews] Using a ranking says nothing about whether the ranking is right; it only fixes the list before the founding records are examined, so that the selection cannot be tilted toward or away from church-founded institutions. The University of Virginia ranks 26th, so the contrast case is inside the list. [cite:vt13_pq_usnews2027b]

Each institution is placed in one of four groups by the body that founded it, according to the institution's own history or a standard reference: Catholic (founded by a diocese, a bishop or a religious order), Protestant (founded by a denomination, its ministers or a church body), private nonsectarian (founded by private persons with no church sponsor, or expressly as nonsectarian) and public (founded by a state or territorial legislature). The present-status column records only what the cited source states; where the source records no present church tie, the column says "private" or "public".

What the count shows

Of the 33, three were founded by Catholics: Notre Dame by the Congregation of Holy Cross in 1842, and Georgetown (1789) and Boston College (1863) in the Jesuit tradition. [cite:vt13_wiki_nd] [cite:vt13_gu_history] [cite:vt13_bc_jesuithist] None was founded by a diocese. Thirteen were founded by Protestant denominations or their ministers: Congregationalists (Harvard, Yale, Dartmouth), Presbyterians (Princeton), the Church of England (Columbia), Baptists (Brown, Chicago), Methodists (Duke, with Quakers; Northwestern; Vanderbilt; Emory; USC) and Universalists (Tufts). [cite:vt13_wiki_colonial] [cite:vt13_wiki_duke] [cite:vt13_wiki_nu] [cite:vt13_wiki_uchicago] [cite:vt13_vu_manual] [cite:vt13_emory_methodist] [cite:vt13_wiki_usc] [cite:vt13_wiki_tufts] Ten were private foundations with no church sponsor, several of them expressly nonsectarian, and seven were founded by state or territorial legislatures. [cite:vt13_wiki_cornell] [cite:vt13_washu_hist] [cite:vt13_wiki_nyu] [cite:vt13_wiki_uc]

Pennsylvania is the one hard call. The standard table of colonial colleges records its tradition as Church of England but "officially nonsectarian", and it is counted here as nonsectarian; moving it to the Protestant column would make the count 14 Protestant and 9 nonsectarian and change nothing else. [cite:vt13_wiki_colonial] Penn's founding date is also a choice: 1740 was adopted in 1899, while its charter dates from 1755. [cite:vt13_wiki_colonial]

Founding is not present governance, and the Protestant column shows it most clearly. Vanderbilt's Board of Trust severed the university's ties with the Methodist Episcopal Church, South, in June 1914; the University of Southern California severed its formal church ties in 1952; Northwestern later became non-sectarian; Tufts describes itself as nonsectarian. [cite:vt13_vu_manual] [cite:vt13_wiki_usc] [cite:vt13_wiki_nu] [cite:vt13_wiki_tufts] Only Duke and Emory still record a link to the United Methodist Church, described in Emory's case as a "historical affiliation". [cite:vt13_wiki_duke] [cite:vt13_emory_methodist] The three Catholic institutions keep their religious identity, but each is held by a board that includes lay members, a change made at Notre Dame in 1967 and at Boston College in 1972. [cite:vt13_nd_hesburgh] [cite:vt13_bc_jesuithist] [scene:vt13_jesuit_universities|Jesuit universities: who governs them]

The contrast case: Virginia

The University of Virginia was designed as an argument. On 4 August 1818 the commissioners who had met at Rockfish Gap signed a report, drafted by Thomas Jefferson, that sent the legislature a plan for the university. In it they wrote that, "in conformity with the principles of our constitution, which places all sects of religion on an equal footing," they had "proposed no professor of Divinity". [cite:vt13_fo_rockfish] [cite:vt13_fo_rockfish_draft] The same report did not remove religion from the curriculum: "the proofs of the being of a god" were assigned to "the province of the professor of ethics". [cite:vt13_fo_rockfish] The Virginia General Assembly established the university's charter on 25 January 1819; its Board of Visitors met for the first time that year with Jefferson as Rector, and it opened on 7 March 1825 with five professors present, all of them foreign-born. [cite:vt13_uva_charter] [cite:vt13_monticello_timeline] The colonial colleges on the list were all founded within a church tradition, even where, as at Princeton and Pennsylvania, they were officially nonsectarian; Virginia's founders put in writing why their university would have no professor of divinity. [cite:vt13_wiki_colonial] [cite:vt13_fo_rockfish]

The contrast should not be overdrawn. Virginia was not the only public foundation of its time: the University of North Carolina had been chartered by its legislature on 11 December 1789, and the University of Michigan was established by the territorial legislature on 26 August 1817 as the Catholepistemiad, with a Catholic priest, Father Gabriel Richard, and a Protestant minister, John Monteith, among its founders. [cite:vt13_ncdncr_unc] [cite:vt13_um_founding] Michigan is a useful corrective: a public university, created by a legislature, with a priest among its founders. [cite:vt13_um_founding] By mid-century private founders were also writing nonsectarian rules into their charters, as at Washington University in 1853 and Cornell in 1865. [cite:vt13_washu_hist] [cite:vt13_wiki_cornell]

What the ledger cannot show

The ledger records founders and present affiliation. It does not measure influence, and it cannot support a claim that any church, the Holy See or the Society of Jesus controls any of these universities today: no document found for this volume shows such control beyond the sponsorship and canonical links described in the previous section. [scene:vt13_jesuit_universities|Jesuit universities] On the evidence of the list itself, the claim that America's leading universities are Catholic or Jesuit institutions fails: three of 33 are Catholic, and the founding tradition most represented is Protestant, most of it long since detached from the churches that began it. [cite:vt13_pq_usnews2027b] [cite:vt13_wiki_colonial] [cite:vt13_vu_manual] [cite:vt13_wiki_usc] The ranking itself is a snapshot as of October 2026 and will change; the founding records will not.
