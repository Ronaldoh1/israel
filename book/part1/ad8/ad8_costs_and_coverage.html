What Americans Pay, and What the Coverage Buys

The American Dream as taught includes a promise about health: a steady job comes with insurance, insurance protects a family from ruinous bills, and the country that spends the most on medicine gets the best care. Each part of that promise has a history and a record. This section starts with the history, because the link between a job and health insurance was not a law of nature or a free-market outcome. It was built, step by step, by wartime regulation and tax law. It then sets out what the country spends, what a family pays for coverage at work, who is left uninsured or thinly insured, and what the 2025 budget law changes. The promise as taught is set out in Part One. [scene:ad1_as_taught|How the Dream is taught]

Designed friction: how a job came to carry health insurance

Before the Second World War, few Americans had any health insurance; by 1940 roughly 9 per cent had coverage for hospital costs. During the war the National War Labor Board capped wage increases from 1942 to hold down inflation, but health insurance and other fringe benefits were exempted from the cap. Firms competing for scarce workers could not offer more pay, so they offered benefits. In 1943 an administrative tax ruling first excluded employer-paid premiums from workers' taxable income, in a narrow form, and the 1954 Internal Revenue Code wrote the exclusion into law. By 1950 about half the population had hospital coverage. [cite:ad8_thomasson2017] The economist Melissa Thomasson, studying the 1954 change, found that the tax subsidy increased the growth of group insurance, especially among union members and employed people. [cite:ad8_thomasson_aer]

This is friction by design in the plain sense: Congress chose to make health coverage bought through an employer cheaper, in after-tax terms, than coverage bought any other way. The choice is still large. The Tax Policy Center put the cost of the exclusion at $299 billion in lost income and payroll taxes in 2022, the single largest tax break in the federal code, and it is worth more to people in higher tax brackets: on a $1,000 premium, the saving is $254 for a worker in the 12 per cent bracket and $347 for one in the 22 per cent bracket. [cite:ad8_tpc_esi] The structure has a second consequence that the taught version rarely mentions: coverage is tied to the job, so losing or leaving the job can mean losing the insurance.

Public programmes were later built around the employer system rather than in place of it. President Lyndon Johnson signed Medicare, for people 65 and older, and Medicaid, for people with low incomes, on 30 July 1965. Medicare was extended to many disabled people and to people with kidney failure in 1972; the Children's Health Insurance Program followed in 1997, a prescription drug benefit (Medicare Part D) was enacted in 2003 and began in 2006, and the Affordable Care Act of 2010 created subsidised insurance marketplaces for people without job-based or public coverage. [cite:ad8_cms_history] Laws signed by presidents of both parties built this layered system; none replaced its employer core.

What the country spends, and what it gets

National health spending, as counted by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, reached $5.3 trillion in 2024, or $15,474 per person and 18.0 per cent of the economy. [cite:us16_cms_nhe2024] In 1970 the figure was $353 per person, which is $2,208 in 2024 dollars; spending per person has therefore risen about sevenfold after inflation ($15,474 ÷ $2,208 ≈ 7.0). [cite:us16_pkff_spending] On the World Health Organization's narrower international definition, measured in dollars adjusted for purchasing power so that a dollar buys the same basket in each country (purchasing power parity, or PPP), American spending per person rose from $4,549 in 2000 to $13,473 in 2023. [cite:ad8_wb_chepc]

The Commonwealth Fund's 2026 comparison of rich countries, using 2024 data, found the United States spending nearly twice the OECD average as a share of its economy and about one and a half times as much per person as Switzerland, the next-highest spender. Outcomes did not follow. American life expectancy, at 79 years, was two years below the OECD average; the country had the second-highest rate of avoidable deaths among the countries studied, after Mexico; maternal deaths were nearly 19 per 100,000 live births in 2023, against fewer than 5 in eleven of eighteen countries. The United States and Mexico were the only countries in the comparison without universal coverage. [cite:ad8_cmwf_global26] On the WHO's measure of healthy life expectancy, the years a newborn can expect to live in good health, the American figure for 2021 was 63.2 years. [cite:us22_who_hale21] Spending buys a great deal of care, and American life expectancy reached a record 79.0 years in 2024, but it does not buy outcomes in proportion. [cite:us16_cdc_db548] Why it costs so much is a question of prices as much as of use, and the next section follows the prices. [scene:ad8_insurance_machine|The insurance machine]

What a family pays for coverage at work

Three terms carry most of the cost of insurance. The premium is the price of the policy itself, paid every month whether or not anyone is sick. The deductible is the amount a patient pays for care each year before the insurer starts paying most of the bill. The out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling on what a patient pays in a year, apart from premiums. KFF's annual Employer Health Benefits Survey, released on 22 October 2026, found that the average premium for family coverage through an employer was $28,303 in 2026, up about 5 per cent in a year. Workers paid $7,471 of it directly through payroll deductions, about 27 per cent; the employer paid the remaining $20,832 ($28,303 − $7,471). Among workers in plans with a general deductible, the average for single coverage was $1,955, and 38 per cent of covered workers faced a single deductible of $2,000 or more, up from 34 per cent a year earlier. Workers at firms with many lower-wage employees paid a larger share of the family premium, 31 per cent against 27 per cent. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026]

Over the last five years family premiums rose 28 per cent, against 26.9 per cent for wages and 24.4 per cent for prices generally, so the recent gap is narrow. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] Over longer periods it was wide. From 2001 to 2011 family premiums rose 113 per cent while workers' wages rose 34 per cent and inflation 27 per cent. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2011] From $15,073 in 2011 the family premium reached $28,303 in 2026, an 88 per cent rise. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2011] [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] Because the tax code treats the employer's share as untaxed pay, a dollar of premium growth is, for the worker, a dollar of compensation that arrives as insurance rather than wages. [cite:ad8_tpc_esi] Part Five follows wages themselves. [scene:ad5_wages_floors|Wages and floors]

The uninsured and the underinsured

The share of Americans with no insurance at all has fallen by half since the Affordable Care Act. More than 50 million people, nearly 17 per cent, were uninsured in 2009; the rate reached a then-record low of 8.8 per cent in 2016. [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] In 2025, 26.7 million people, 7.9 per cent, went without coverage all year; 53.5 per cent of people had coverage through employment and 35.8 per cent had public coverage. [cite:us16_census_2025]

Having a card is not the same as being protected. The Commonwealth Fund defines adults as underinsured when, despite being insured all year, their out-of-pocket costs other than premiums reach 10 per cent of household income (5 per cent for people below twice the poverty line), or their deductible reaches 5 per cent of income. In its 2024 survey of working-age adults, 56 per cent were adequately insured all year; 23 per cent were insured all year but underinsured, 12 per cent had a gap in coverage and 9 per cent were uninsured. Two thirds of the underinsured had coverage through an employer, and 44 per cent of the underinsured reported medical or dental debt. [cite:ad8_cmwf_underins24] Cost shapes behaviour: in May 2026, 28 per cent of adults told KFF they had skipped or postponed needed care in the past year because of cost. [cite:us22_kff_costs26]

The 2025 law and the end of enhanced subsidies, status as of October 2026

Two changes are now reshaping coverage. The first is the expiry of the enhanced marketplace subsidies enacted in 2021. They lapsed at the end of 2025; the House passed a three-year extension by 230 to 196 on 8 January 2026, but the Senate did not pass one. [cite:ad8_astho_ptc] KFF estimated that subsidised enrollees who kept the same plan would see their premium payments rise about 114 per cent on average, from $888 to $1,904 a year. [cite:ad8_kff_enroll26] [cite:us16_ib_subsidy] Plan selections for 2026 fell to 23.1 million, 4.9 per cent below the 2025 record of 24.3 million, before counting people who later dropped out for non-payment. [cite:ad8_hfma_enroll26] As of October 2026 Congress had not passed an extension for 2027, so the original, smaller credits apply again in the coming plan year. [cite:ad8_audit_hci2027]

The second is the budget reconciliation law signed on 4 July 2025. The Congressional Budget Office estimated that the enacted law will leave about 10 million more people uninsured in 2034, 7.5 million of them through its Medicaid provisions and 2.1 million through marketplace changes; with the subsidy expiry, the total rises to about 14.2 million. [cite:us16_kff_obbba] The largest single provision is a work requirement. From 1 January 2027, adults aged 19 to 64 covered under the Medicaid expansion must show 80 hours a month of work, community service, a work programme or at least half-time education, or monthly income of at least $580. Parents of children aged 13 or younger, medically frail people, pregnant and postpartum women and several other groups are exempt. The law lets states seek good-faith extensions to the end of 2028, but the federal interim final rule of June 2026 limits the first extension to six months. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] The CBO estimated that the work requirement alone cuts federal Medicaid spending by $326 billion over ten years and adds 5.3 million uninsured in 2034; renewals every six months for expansion adults, starting with renewals due from 31 December 2026, add another 700,000. [cite:ad8_kff_obbba_prov] As of October 2026 the 1 January 2027 start date stood; five governors had asked the Department of Health and Human Services to delay enforcement until states could show they were ready, and no decision had been reported. [cite:ad8_audit_hfn_gov] Because compliance must be shown and renewed, the requirement adds a reporting step to coverage itself; Part Eleven follows how such rules interact with earnings and benefits. Where it works as promised

Parts of the promise hold. Coverage is far wider than it was: the uninsured rate in 2025 was less than half its 2009 level, and of the 79 per cent of working-age adults insured all year in 2024, about seven in ten (56 ÷ 79) had coverage that met the Commonwealth Fund's adequacy test. [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] [cite:us16_census_2025] [cite:ad8_cmwf_underins24] Americans pay a smaller share of the national bill out of their own pockets than they used to: 10.9 per cent of current health spending in 2023, down from 15.1 per cent in 2000. [cite:us16_wb_oop] Medicare, signed in 1965, made coverage close to universal at 65, and life expectancy in 2024 was the highest on record. [cite:ad8_cms_history] [cite:us16_cdc_db548] Over the most recent five years, premiums grew at almost the same pace as wages. [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] The failures are specific: a high price level, a deductible-based design that leaves a quarter of insured working-age adults exposed, and coverage that depends on a job or, from 2027, on monthly proof of hours.
