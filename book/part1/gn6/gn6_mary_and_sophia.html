Outside the Jar: Mary, Pistis Sophia and the Quarrel with Peter

The Nag Hammadi jar was not the only source of Gnostic books. Three other codices had reached Europe long before 1945, and each took decades or centuries to be published. Two of them contain the texts in which the question of women's authority among the disciples is argued most openly, and in both the argument is with Peter, the apostle on whom the bishops of Rome founded their claim.

The oldest to arrive was the Bruce Codex, bought by James Bruce in Upper Egypt around 1769 and held by the Bodleian Library in Oxford since 1848; it contains the Books of Jeu and an untitled treatise, and it is in very poor condition. [cite:vt26_wiki_bruce] The Askew Codex was bought by Anthony Askew from a bookseller in 1772 and sold by his heirs to the British Museum in 1785. Its 178 parchment leaves hold the long work known as the Pistis Sophia; the first edition of the Coptic text appeared only in 1851, after its editor's death. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] The Berlin Codex was bought by Carl Reinhardt in Cairo in January 1896 and presented to scholars that July. Its Gnostic texts were finally edited by Walter Till in 1955, fifty-nine years after the purchase. [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex]

The Berlin Codex begins with the Gospel of Mary, of which less than half survives; two Greek fragments from the third century show that it circulated in Greek, and scholars date its composition to the first half of the second century. [cite:vt26_nasscal_gmary] [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] In it the risen Saviour teaches that "Sin doesn't exist, but you're the ones who make sin", that "the Son of Humanity exists within you", and, as he leaves, gives an instruction that reads like a warning against what the church would become: "Don't lay down any rules beyond what I've given you, nor make a law like the lawgiver, lest you be bound by it". [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] The disciples weep and fear they will be killed. Mary comforts them and, at Peter's request, tells them of a vision in which the soul rises past hostile powers. [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary]

Then Peter objects. Did the Saviour really speak with a woman privately, without the disciples' knowledge, he asks, and are they to listen to her? Andrew says he does not believe the teaching. Levi defends her: if the Saviour made her worthy, who is Peter to reject her? [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] Karen King reads the scene as a second-century argument over two linked questions, private revelation and women's authority to teach, and most scholars identify this Mary as Mary Magdalene. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] The Pistis Sophia stages the same quarrel at greater length. In it the risen Jesus spends eleven years teaching his disciples, Mary Magdalene asks most of the questions, and Peter complains: "we will not endure this woman, for she taketh the opportunity from us". Later Mary says she is afraid of Peter, "because he threatened me". [cite:vt26_mead_ch1] [cite:vt26_mead_ch36] [cite:vt26_mead_ch72] [cite:vt26_bge_pistis]

The quarrel had an institutional outcome. In about 591 Pope Gregory the Great preached a homily in Rome that merged three women of the gospels, the sinful woman of Luke, Mary of Bethany and Mary Magdalene, into one; the identification shaped Western tradition for fourteen centuries, though Gregory did not use the word "prostitute". [cite:vt26_pearse_greg33] On 22 May 1994 John Paul II declared that "the Church has no authority whatsoever to confer priestly ordination on women", a judgment "to be definitively held". [cite:vt26_va_ordsac1994] On 3 June 2016, at the wish of Pope Francis, the Vatican raised Mary Magdalene's commemoration to a feast and recalled her old title, "apostle of the apostles". [cite:vt26_va_feast2016] The texts in which Mary teaches and Peter resents it were the texts the canon left out. The office that claimed Peter's succession has remained closed to women.
