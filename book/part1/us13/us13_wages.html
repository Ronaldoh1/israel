Minimum Wage, Hours and Pay Gaps

An average wage says what a typical post pays; it says little about the bottom of the labour market, where a legal floor does most of the work. This section compares the two countries' floors over time, the hours people work, the leave the law guarantees them, and the gap between men's and women's pay. Each measure is taken from one series per country, and where the two countries' series are defined differently the difference is stated. Average pay against prices is the subject of Part Fourteen. [scene:us14_wages|Part Fourteen: wages against prices] [cite:us13_dol_minwage] [cite:us13_nii_minwage]

Two kinds of floor

The two minimum wages are built on opposite principles. America's federal minimum is a fixed dollar amount written into the Fair Labor Standards Act; it changes only when Congress passes, and the president signs, an amendment. The Labor Department's chart of the non-farm rate shows how irregular that has been: $1.60 from February 1968, $3.10 in January 1980, $3.35 in January 1981 and then no change for nine years, $3.80 in 1990, $4.25 in 1991, $4.75 in 1996 and $5.15 in September 1997, then another ten-year pause until three steps to $7.25 on 24 July 2009. [cite:us13_dol_minwage] As of October 2026 the federal rate is still $7.25: twenty states used it as their own minimum at the end of 2025, and by October 2026 it had gone more than seventeen years without an increase. [cite:us13_bp_states2026] [cite:us13_epi_history]

Israel's Minimum Wage Law, passed in 1987, ties the floor to pay in general. The monthly minimum is set at 47.5 per cent of the average wage and updated each 1 April; the hourly minimum is the monthly figure divided by 186. The law covers every employee aged 18 and over, part-timers in proportion to their hours, and, by its own terms, foreign and undocumented workers; minors receive a reduced rate, and an employer who fails to pay the minimum can face up to a year in prison or a fine. [cite:us13_kz_minwagelaw] Because the floor follows the average wage, it rises without a new vote. It has not always moved mechanically, however: the National Insurance Institute's table shows the monthly minimum at NIS 5,300 from December 2017 and unchanged at each April update from 2018 to 2022, before it resumed rising in April 2023. [cite:us13_nii_minwage]

A correction belongs here. A widely repeated figure gives Israel's minimum as NIS 6,247.67 "from 1 April 2026". The National Insurance Institute's own table shows that amount as the April 2025 rate; the rate from 1 April 2026 is NIS 6,443.85 a month, a rise of about 3.1 per cent. [cite:us13_nii_minwage]

What the floors are worth

A nominal rate frozen for seventeen years loses value every year that prices rise. The Economic Policy Institute calculated that the 1968 federal minimum of $1.60 was worth $12.50 in 2023 dollars and the 2009 rate of $7.25 only $10.33; by 2023 the federal minimum's buying power was at a 66-year low, 42 per cent below its 1968 peak. [cite:us13_epi_history] The federal figure is no longer the floor most American low earners face, because states have moved on their own. Thirty states and the District of Columbia set higher minimums; in 2026 twenty-two states and DC raised theirs, lifting the average state minimum from $11.18 to $11.51, with the District highest at $17.95. [cite:us13_bp_states2026] The result is a floor that depends on where a worker lives: $7.25 an hour in the twenty states that use the federal rate, more than twice that in the highest-paying jurisdictions.

Israel's floor is one national rate. Its height relative to typical pay has long been high by international standards. In 2010 the Taub Center put Israel's minimum at 58 per cent of the median wage, against an OECD average of 46 per cent and 34 per cent in the United States, while noting that Israel was "not a strict enforcer" of it. [cite:us13_taub_minwage2010] That ratio is a 2010 snapshot; the later direction for each country follows from the series above, since Israel's floor is pegged to the average wage and America's federal floor has not moved since 2009.

Hours and leave

The OECD's series of average annual hours actually worked per worker includes part-time workers and is published, in the OECD's own words, for comparing one country over time rather than levels between countries. [cite:us13_jil_hours] On that series Israel has worked more hours than the United States in every year shown, but the gap has narrowed: Israeli hours fell from 2,033 a year in 2000 to 1,957 in 2010 and 1,870 in 2025, while American hours moved from 1,832 to 1,772 and 1,800. The OECD average in 2025 was 1,736. [cite:us13_wiki_hours] A claim repeated in American commentary, that only Mexico, Costa Rica, Chile, South Korea and Israel work longer hours than the United States, does not hold for the 2025 table, in which eleven countries, among them Greece, Croatia and the Czech Republic, show more hours than the United States. [cite:us13_wiki_hours] The OECD revises the series: the edition reproduced by Japan's labour institute in August 2025 gave the United States 1,880 hours in 2000 and 1,796 in 2024, so figures from different editions should not be mixed. [cite:us13_jil_hours]

Leave law differs in kind. American federal law sets no paid vacation at all: the Labor Department states that the Fair Labor Standards Act "does not require payment for time not worked, such as vacations", which are left to agreement between employer and employee, with exceptions for some federal contracts. [cite:us13_dol_vacation] Israel's Annual Leave Law of 1951 does set a minimum, but a low one: 10 days a year until the first increase in the law's history, to 12 days from 1 January 2017 for employees with less than four years' seniority, rising each year after that. Most OECD countries guarantee 20 days or more. [cite:us13_toi_vacation] Paid parental leave, the larger difference between the two systems, is compared in Part Eighteen. [scene:us18_family|Part Eighteen: family policy]

The gap between men's and women's pay

The OECD measures the gender pay gap as the difference between men's and women's median earnings for full-time employees, as a share of men's. In the United States it narrowed steadily from the mid-1970s to the early 2010s and has changed little since 2014: 19.0 per cent in 2005, 18.8 per cent in 2010, 17.7 per cent in 2020 and 17.3 per cent in 2024. The OECD average in 2024 was 10.1 per cent, down 8.4 points since 2000. [cite:us13_jil_gendergap] [cite:us13_oecd_lmf15] Israel is one of the countries the OECD lists with gaps of 20 per cent or more. [cite:us13_oecd_lmf15] The Shoresh Institution found that Israeli women's median monthly income in 2021–2023 was 76.9 per cent of men's, a gap of 23.1 per cent that was wider than in 35 of the 38 OECD countries and wider than fifteen years earlier, and traced much of it to pay falling for women after children are born while men's rises. [cite:us13_jpost_shoresh]

The two figures are not on the same footing. The OECD notes that Israel's data are monthly earnings and America's weekly; a monthly figure counts differences in hours worked within full-time employment that an hourly measure would remove, so it tends to show a wider gap. [cite:us13_oecd_lmf15] What the record supports is narrower than a ranking: both countries' gaps are well above the OECD average; America's has been flat for a decade; and Israel's, on the Shoresh measure, has widened.
