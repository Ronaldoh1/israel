Every Word Blacked Out

Not every redaction is a cover-up, and the book has to show the difference before it criticises any of them. The law requires the department to hide the names of victims and to withhold images of child sexual abuse. A black bar over a girl's name is the law working. A black bar over a powerful man's name, for no listed reason, is the law being broken. The record contains both, and it also contains the reverse of what the law intended.

What was hidden

In the first release in December 2025, CBS News counted at least 550 pages with every word blacked out, including a 119-page document labelled "Grand Jury-NY" redacted in full. The department posted a largely unredacted version of that document the next day. [cite:ep9_cbs_550] In February 2026 Representative Laura Friedman reviewed the files the department had made available to Congress as unredacted and found hundreds of pages still entirely blacked out; she was told that was how the FBI had sent them over. [cite:ep9_friedman] Republicans and Democrats alike said the release did not comply with the law. [cite:ep9_cbs_550]

One photograph shows how a single file moved. Among at least fifteen files removed after the December release was a picture of the president with Epstein, Maxwell and Melania Trump. The department said prosecutors had flagged it to protect victims, then reposted it "without any alteration" after finding no victims in it. [cite:ep9_time_photo] The sequence is documented. Why the picture was flagged in the first place is not.

What was exposed

The January 2026 release did the opposite of what the law asked. A review by the Wall Street Journal of 47 victims' names found 43 left visible, more than two dozen of them minors at the time, searchable alongside details such as home addresses. The department said it takes victim protection very seriously, had redacted thousands of names and was correcting pages; the Deputy Attorney General said the errors affected ".001% of all the materials". [cite:ep9_wsj_43] In March the department confirmed it had taken 47,635 files offline to add redactions, saying none had been deleted. [cite:ep9_salon_47k] The people the law was written to protect were exposed, while the names of men investigated alongside Epstein stayed behind black bars. [cite:ep7_cbs_dea2026]

What a name in the files means

The clearest lesson came from Congress itself. After reading the unredacted files, Representatives Khanna and Massie said six men were "likely incriminated", and Khanna read their names on the House floor. Two were well known. The department responded that the other four had no connection to Epstein at all: they appeared in a photo line-up assembled years earlier, as people chosen at random. [cite:ep9_reason_six] [cite:ep9_li_lineup] A name on a page is not the same as knowing why it is there. The book names none of the four.

What is still withheld

About half of the roughly six million pages the department reviewed have not been published. [cite:ep9_rollcall_final] In its February 2026 letter to Congress listing the officials and public figures named in the records, the department also cited legal privileges that are not among the grounds the Act allows. [cite:ep8_san_bondi2026] [cite:ep8_govinfo_act] Still unpublished, among other things, are the unredacted DEA memo naming fourteen people investigated with Epstein and foreign-language documents now under court-ordered review.
