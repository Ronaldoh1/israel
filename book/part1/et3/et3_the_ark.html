The Ark No One May See

At the centre of the Ethiopian story is an object that no outsider has seen and no one is permitted to examine. The Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion in Aksum claims to hold the original Ark of the Covenant, the chest that, in the Bible, held the tablets of the Law. According to the church's tradition the Ark was moved to the Chapel of the Tablet, beside the old church. [cite:et_wiki_zion] "At present", the Wikipedia article on the church states, "only the guardian monk may view the Ark". [cite:et_wiki_zion]

The guardianship is a closed succession. The guardian monk is appointed for life by his predecessor before the predecessor dies; if none is named, the monks elect one. Once appointed, the guardian is confined to the chapel of the Ark for the rest of his life. [cite:et_wiki_zion] No scholar, journalist or official has been allowed to verify the claim, and the church has never sought verification. The Kebra Nagast, written to legitimise the Solomonic kings, is the textual root of the claim that Ethiopia possesses the true Ark. [cite:et_wiki_kebra]

The restriction extends beyond the chapel. Women are not permitted to enter the Old Church at Aksum, a building that dates back in tradition to the fourth century under Ezana and was rebuilt and enlarged by the emperor Fasilides in the seventeenth century. [cite:et_wiki_zion] Haile Selassie built a new church beside it between the 1950s and 1964, which women may enter. [cite:et_wiki_zion] Every Ethiopian Orthodox church, moreover, has its own tabot, a consecrated tablet representing the Ark and the tablets of the Law; World History Encyclopedia notes that the tabots are concealed from all but priests. [cite:et_wh_solomonic] The holiest object in each church is, by rule, unseen by its congregation.

This book does not claim that the Ark at Aksum is or is not the Ark of Exodus. There is no way to test the claim, and the record has nothing to say about what lies in the chapel. What the record does show is the shape of the arrangement. The most sacred object in the tradition, the one that the founding book of the dynasty said made Ethiopia the heir of Israel, is held behind a single door, by a single man who never leaves, who chooses his own successor. It is the purest form of the gatekeeping this shelf has traced: an office that alone has access to the source, whose authority rests on the access, and whose claim cannot be checked by anyone outside it.

A sympathetic reading is equally available, and fairness requires stating it. For Ethiopian Orthodox believers the concealment is reverence, not control: the holy is set apart because it is holy, as the Holy of Holies in the Jerusalem temple was entered only by the high priest. The British Museum, which holds eleven tabots taken from Maqdala in 1868, keeps them off public display out of respect for their sacred nature, as Part Six shows. [cite:et_bm_maqdala] Both readings describe the same structure. The difference is whether one sees in it protection of the sacred or protection of the office that guards it. The documents cannot decide that. They can only show that the structure exists, and that it has lasted, in Aksum, for centuries.
