The Khazar Question: Who Held the Pen

The previous section tested the Khazar hypothesis against the genetic record. This section asks a different question, the one this volume asks of every story: who wrote it, when, and for what purpose. The Khazar story turns out to have passed through many hands, Jewish and non-Jewish, admiring and hostile, and following it from one writer to the next shows how a single historical claim can serve opposite agendas while the evidence underneath stays the same.

The story as it was told

The conversion of the Khazars is first told in medieval texts. Muslim geographers of the late ninth and tenth centuries describe Judaism among the Khazar elite, and two Hebrew documents of the tenth century, an exchange of letters attributed to the Spanish Jewish courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut and a Khazar king named Joseph, and a fragment found in the Cairo Genizah, tell the conversion story in detail. [cite:kz_medievalists_stampfer] In the twelfth century the Spanish Jewish poet Judah Halevi made it the frame of his philosophical dialogue, the Kuzari, in which a Khazar king chooses Judaism after hearing the case for each faith. [cite:kz_tablet_stampfer] For most of its history, then, the Khazar conversion was a story Jews told about themselves, with pride.

In the nineteenth century the story changed purpose. Some Jews in Hungary, keen to present themselves as long-rooted in the region, embraced the idea of Khazar ancestry. [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026] The historian Abraham Harkavy argued that the first Jewish communities of what became Russia came from the Black Sea coast and the Caucasus, and Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, writing his history of Jews in Russia in 2001, cited him alongside researchers who traced the early Jewish community of Kiev to Khazar captives settled in a quarter called "Kosary." Solzhenitsyn did not conclude that Russian or Polish Jews descend from the Khazars; for Poland he described Jews from western Europe invited by princes to settle. [cite:kz_solz_ch1] The English versions of his book now online are unauthorized translations, often loose; the first authorized one is due in 2028. [cite:kz_solz_center]

In 1976 the Hungarian-born writer Arthur Koestler, himself of Jewish origin, argued in The Thirteenth Tribe that the Jews of Eastern Europe descended largely from the Khazars. [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026] In 2008 the Tel Aviv University historian Shlomo Sand made the same argument part of a broader case in The Invention of the Jewish People, which spent nineteen weeks on Israel's bestseller list. Israeli historians answered sharply: Israel Bartal faulted his method, and Anita Shapira titled her review "The Jewish-People Deniers." [cite:kz_rd_sand] In 2012 the Israeli geneticist Eran Elhaik published the first genetic case for the theory, and in 2016 he, the linguist Paul Wexler and colleagues placed the ancestors of Ashkenazi Jews near villages named "Ashkenaz" in eastern Turkey and argued that Yiddish began as a Slavic language. A rebuttal in the same journal argued that their method cannot trace mixed populations back a thousand years and that Yiddish is, by every standard linguistic test, Germanic. [cite:kz_gbe_rebuttal]

The story also found other users. Writers hostile to Jews adopted it to claim that Ashkenazi Jews are "impostors" with no connection to the land, and the Anti-Defamation League reports its use has grown since October 2023. [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026] In February 2026 the commentator Candace Owens wrote that "the people currently occupying Israel are Khazarian Turks." [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026]

The answers from the record

Three kinds of evidence have been brought to bear, and they point the same way.

The texts. In 2013 the Hebrew University historian Shaul Stampfer re-examined every source for the conversion itself. He found some falsely attributed and others unreliable, and noted that a Byzantine patriarch's letter to the Khazars around 914 and an Arab envoy's report say nothing about it, while archaeology has turned up almost no distinctly Jewish objects in Khazar lands. "The silence of so many sources about the Khazars' Judaism is very suspicious," he wrote, and he concluded that the conversion was "a legend with no factual basis." [cite:kz_medievalists_stampfer] [cite:kz_tablet_stampfer] His view is itself debated, but it means the starting point of the theory, a mass Khazar conversion, is less certain than either its supporters or most of its critics assumed.

Modern genetics. Doron Behar and colleagues tested the theory in 2013 against a wide range of populations from the Caucasus and the steppe and found "no evidence" of a Khazar origin; the wider record, described earlier, shows Ashkenazi Jews to be a mix of Levantine and European ancestry. [cite:behar_2013_khazar] [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026]

Ancient DNA. Earlier studies had to use modern Armenians, Georgians and others as stand-ins for the Khazars. In 2019 researchers sequenced the genomes of nine people buried in elite Khazar-period mounds in southern Russia, from the seventh to ninth centuries. Their ancestry was mainly Central Asian and Turkic steppe, with Siberian and East Asian components and some from the Caucasus. The study found no significant genetic affinity with Ashkenazi Jews, and none of their maternal lineages appear among Ashkenazim today. [cite:kz_biorxiv_khazar] Their male lineages included haplogroups R1a, C2b, G2a, N1a, Q and R1b. [cite:kz_kornienko2021] Nine elite burials cannot speak for every Khazar, but this is the first direct look at the people the theory names, and it does not support it.

What the pen shows

Following the pen does not lead where either side of today's argument expects. The Khazar story was first told by Jews, revived by Jews in Hungary, argued by Jewish and Israeli scholars, and disputed by other Jewish and Israeli scholars, including the one who doubts the conversion happened at all. It was adopted by people hostile to Jews and rejected by Israel's defenders. The pen has been in many hands.

Two things in the record do match the pattern this volume tracks. The first is that the European half of Ashkenazi ancestry, openly published by Israeli and Jewish geneticists, has no place in the founding story as the state tells it: Israel's Declaration of Independence speaks of a people exiled from the land and returning to it, a story that is true of one strand of Ashkenazi ancestry but not of the other. [cite:declaration_israel_1948] [cite:la_carmi2014] The second is the response in 2026, when the theory was answered first with labels and only later, if at all, with the ancient DNA that answers it better. [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026] Israel's own Chief Rabbinate, meanwhile, has used DNA tests since about 2017 to verify the Jewish descent of some immigrants who want to marry, most of them from the former Soviet Union. [cite:kz_ynet_rabbinate] Testing DNA is not the line that was crossed in 2026. Making it a condition of the right to live in a land was.

Ancestry is a question the evidence can answer, and on the Khazar theory it has largely answered it. Whether a people's rights depend on their ancestry is a different question, and no evidence about the Khazars can settle it, for Israelis or for Palestinians.
