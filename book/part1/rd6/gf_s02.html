Charity for an Army: The Operating Budgets and Britain's Bill

Two assumptions often sit beneath accounts of the Mandate's last decades. The first is that the Jewish community's underground army was paid for secretly, by means no one can now trace. The second is that Britain held Palestine cheaply. Neither survives the documents. The defense money moved through budgets, a community levy and, by 1948, an openly advertised charity channel, all of which left a paper trail. Britain's own accounts, meanwhile, record a bill that by the end ran to about £100 million in three years. What the record does not contain is a single audited total for the army itself, and that gap is stated at the end of this section.

The clearest early view of the operating budget comes from an American diplomat. After the Zionist Congress of 1939, the United States consul in Jerusalem reported that the Jewish Agency's budget had been set at £720,000, about $74 million in today's money. Of that, £100,000 was earmarked for refugee settlement and £80,000 for what the budget itself called "the Jewish security service (defense)." [cite:frus_1939_ja_budget] The line is small, about one-ninth of the whole, but it is explicit: defense sat inside the Agency's budget, under its own heading, nine years before the state.

The budgets grew quickly during and after the Second World War. In 1944 the Jewish National Fund spent $10.8 million and the Jewish Agency and Keren Hayesod together $14.9 million of money raised in the United States, some $25.7 million in all. The United Palestine Appeal's budget for 1945 was $35.3 million. [cite:djn_1945_upa] In 1946 the Agency reported that it had received $12 million from the United States the previous year and asked for a budget of $56 million, about $0.93 billion in today's dollars. [cite:djn_1946_budget] In seven years the ask had moved from hundreds of thousands of pounds to tens of millions of dollars.

Inside Palestine, the community taxed itself for defense. In 1938 the Jewish National Council created Kofer HaYishuv, a levy on cigarettes, restaurant bills and luxuries, to fund central security and expand the Haganah. [cite:w_kofer] It was run by the community's own council, not by the Mandate government, and it gave the Haganah a revenue stream raised inside the country alongside the money that came from abroad.

By 1948, on this evidence, charity abroad and defense at home ran through the same channel. In June 1948 a community campaign in Detroit ran an advertisement telling donors that "the United Jewish Appeal is the official American agency for aid to Haganah," and that the only channel for aid to the Haganah was the local Allied Jewish Campaign. The advertisement warned that collecting money for the dissident militias was fraud. [cite:djn_1948_haganah] The same campaign that fed survivors in Europe and resettled refugees in America was, in its own words, the official channel for the Haganah. Charity and defense ran through the same pipe, and the advertisement said so in print.

The warning about dissident militias matters as well. It shows the mainstream campaign separating the Haganah from the armed groups outside it in what it told American donors, and naming one channel as the only legitimate one.

[cite:frus_1939_ja_budget] [cite:w_kofer] [cite:israelaffairs_italy] [cite:djn_1948_haganah] Each step is documented separately, and each left a record: the levy was public, the budget line was reported to a foreign consul, and the charity channel was advertised.

The other side of the ledger is Britain's. The cost of governing Palestine rose steadily under the Mandate. Civil government spending grew from £P1.9 million in 1922–23 to £P3.2 million in 1934–35, a rise of about two-thirds in twelve years; the Palestine pound was then at par with sterling. [cite:horowitz_1937] The garrison was a separate charge, paid by Britain: £1.5 million in 1923–24. [cite:league_report_garrison] In today's dollars those figures are roughly $161 million and $388 million for the civil budget and $129 million for the garrison.

The bill rose sharply at the end. By the British government's own account in its statement on the termination of the Mandate, the military forces in Palestine cost the British taxpayer about £100 million in 1945–47, roughly $6.7 billion today. [cite:hmg_termination_1948] Parliament debated keeping 80,000 to 100,000 soldiers in the country, and some 100,000 troops were deployed. [cite:hansard_1947_08] The human cost was about 750 British military and police killed over the period. [cite:nam_palestine] Measured in today's dollars, Britain's three-year military bill was larger than any single year of American giving, and larger than the whole of the wartime appeals of 1939–45.

Put together, the two sides of this ledger correct both assumptions. The Jewish community's defense was financed through documented channels: a budget line, a community levy, an arms network funded by American donors and, at the end, a national charity that called itself the Haganah's official American agency. The figures are partial, but the mechanism is on paper. Britain, for its part, did not hold Palestine cheaply. Its civil and garrison costs grew through the interwar years, and its last three years cost it about £100 million and some 750 lives.

The gap is the army's own total. No audited figure for the Haganah's budget is publicly available, and the documents gathered here do not allow one to be reconstructed: they show individual streams, not the sum of them, and some of those streams overlap with the campaigns described in [scene:gf_s01|the ledger of who paid]. The Jewish National Fund's full annual income series is likewise not available as an audited total.

The note above records the same limit. The Agency budgets of 1939 and 1944–46, the Mandate's civil spending, Britain's military cost and the defense channels are all documented. The Haganah's own budget is not, and this book gives no figure for it.
