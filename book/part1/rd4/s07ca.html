Roosevelt and Ibn Saud at Great Bitter Lake

On 14 February 1945, on his way home from the Yalta Conference, Franklin Roosevelt received King Abdulaziz Ibn Saud of Saudi Arabia aboard the USS Quincy in the Great Bitter Lake on the Suez Canal. [cite:brookings_quincy_75years] [cite:wiki_uss_quincy_ca71] The meeting was not a courtesy stop fitted into a crowded itinerary. Roosevelt arranged it himself, two months before his death and visibly declining. Ibn Saud, the founding monarch of Saudi Arabia, who reigned from 1932 to 1953, rarely left Arabia and had deep reservations about the journey, yet he travelled by ship specifically for the encounter. [cite:wiki_abdulaziz_saudi] Both men went to real, documented lengths to sit in the same room, and the principal subject between them was the future of Palestine.

Why an American president would make that effort has a plain answer in the record. By 1945 the Arabian American Oil Company, ARAMCO, had already established the core of the American–Saudi oil relationship. [cite:loc_aramco_oil_pamphlet] The meeting reflected an existing, high-stakes strategic partnership that the United States government understood clearly, and the Palestine question was becoming entangled with it. The sources establish that the relationship existed and mattered; they do not set out the full scope of ARAMCO's operations or the specific terms of the oil relationship at this date, and nothing here should be read as a claim about them.

The meeting ran for roughly five hours by one account and five and a half by another, longer than most of Roosevelt's wartime sessions with other heads of state. [cite:brookings_quincy_75years] [cite:quincy_meeting_1945] Roosevelt, a habitual chain-smoker, did not smoke throughout it, out of respect for the king's preference. [cite:quincy_meeting_1945] Its official record is a memorandum of conversation drafted by William Eddy, the United States Minister to Saudi Arabia, and Yusuf Yassin, the Saudi Deputy Foreign Minister. Both leaders signed it, and the State Department later published it in its own documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States. [cite:quincy_meeting_1945] The memorandum is a summary agreed by both sides, not a transcript. No complete stenographic record of the conversation survives, so specific exchanges quoted from the meeting beyond what the memorandum and the later correspondence document should be treated with caution.

The substance was contested, not ceremonial. Roosevelt argued the case for Jewish settlement in Palestine: one account describes him arguing for a Jewish state, the memorandum-based account for a Jewish national home. [cite:wiki_abdulaziz_saudi] [cite:quincy_meeting_1945] By multiple accounts, Ibn Saud pressed back directly and forcefully, describing continued large-scale Jewish immigration as an existential threat to Arab interests in the region. When Roosevelt raised the question of Jewish immigration, the king answered that Jews displaced by Nazi persecution should be given land in Germany, "in the country of their enemies," as he reportedly put it, rather than in Palestine at the expense of its existing Arab population. [cite:ibn_saud_bavaria_quote] In another rendering of the same exchange he proposed that the Jews be given a state in Bavaria. [cite:wiki_abdulaziz_saudi] [cite:brookings_quincy_75years] The exchange is well attested, but it is one moment in a five-hour conversation and does not capture the whole of the king's position.

What Roosevelt gave the king was a specific assurance. The United States government, he said, would make no decision altering the basic situation in Palestine without full, prior consultation with both Arabs and Jews, and would take no action hostile to the Arab people. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] He came away pleased. He later called the meeting "so outstanding a success," and told his daughter Anna that he had "learned more about the whole problem of Palestine and the Middle East by talking with Ibn Saud for five minutes than I could have learned in an exchange of two or three dozen letters." [cite:brookings_quincy_75years]

The king did not leave it at a spoken promise. About three weeks after the meeting he wrote formally to Roosevelt, asking him to oppose continued Jewish settlement in Palestine. [cite:ibn_saud_bavaria_quote] Roosevelt answered on 5 April 1945, one week before his death. In that letter he affirmed that he would "take no action, in my capacity as Chief of the Executive Branch of this Government, which might prove hostile to the Arab people." [cite:fdr_letter_ibnsaud] The letter reaffirmed in writing the commitment made aboard the Quincy, which closes any gap between an informal verbal impression and a deliberate written undertaking by the president himself. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] One account describes Roosevelt as taking the king's concerns seriously enough to respond in writing more than once in the weeks after the meeting; the letter of 5 April is the one whose words the sources quote. The wording also limits the promise: it is given in Roosevelt's own capacity as head of the executive branch. Whether such a letter was an isolated gesture or part of a pattern is taken up in [scene:s07c2|Roosevelt's actual position on Zionism], which shows that the State Department, when it confirmed the letter in October 1945, described it as one of several such letters to Arab leaders.

Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945. On 14 May 1948 his successor, Harry Truman, recognized the state of Israel eleven minutes after its declaration, without the prior consultation with Arab leaders that Roosevelt had promised. [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud] One account traces the recognition decision through Clark Clifford's domestic electoral calculation rather than through consultation with Arab heads of state; that process belongs to the next stage of this history and is told there. The change between the two presidents is documented. What the documents do not show is Truman's own state of mind about Roosevelt's specific commitment: whether he was personally aware of it, considered himself bound by it, or set out to break it. That he knew of it is a reasonable inference from the continuity of the presidency, but it is an inference, not a finding.

The meeting answers two common assumptions. The first is that American support for a Jewish state ran consistently and without a break from Roosevelt to Truman. The record shows a president who, two months before his death, gave the Arab head of state most affected a direct assurance, spoken and then written, that the United States would not act on Palestine without consulting the Arabs, and a successor who acted without that consultation three years later. The second is that Saudi opposition to a Jewish state in Palestine is a modern political position with no deep roots. The king's objection is on the record in February 1945, in a memorandum both leaders signed and in his own letter three weeks later, more than three years before the state existed. [cite:quincy_meeting_1945] [cite:ibn_saud_bavaria_quote]

How to read the gap between promise and outcome is where accounts part. One reading calls it a promise made and not kept: a deliberate commitment by a sitting president, in his own words, to the affected head of state, followed by an action that ignored it. A second, more cautious reading, taken by the account built on the memorandum, records the 1945 letter and the 1948 recognition as standing in direct and unresolved tension, and declines to adjudicate between them. [cite:fdr_letter_ibnsaud] The second reading has the stronger documentary footing on one point: the promise was Roosevelt's, made "in my capacity as Chief of the Executive Branch," and nothing in these sources shows that it bound his successor. The first has the stronger footing on another: the commitment was specific, written and repeated, and the later decision was taken without the consultation it described.

What the record cannot settle is what Roosevelt himself would have done. He died before the partition vote or the recognition decision came before him, and no evidence exists of how he would have weighed his promise to Ibn Saud against the domestic pressures his successor faced. Nor does any complete transcript allow the meeting's exchanges to be reconstructed word for word. This history documents the promise and the later departure from it, and stops there.
