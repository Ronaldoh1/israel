Acceptance, Rejection and the Two Escalations

A single sentence has long served as the summary of what followed the partition vote: Israel accepted, the Arabs rejected and started the war. In its fuller form, the familiar account holds that the Arab side rejected a peaceful UN compromise and immediately launched a war against a state that had only accepted it. The sentence joins two separate questions. The first is who accepted and who rejected Resolution 181. The second is how the fighting actually began. The documented record gives each its own answer.

On the first question the familiar account is accurate, and it deserves to be said plainly. The Jewish Agency accepted Resolution 181, reluctantly and with real misgivings about the plan's narrow borders. The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab League rejected the plan in its entirety and vowed armed resistance. Their spokesman, Jamal Husseini, told the United Nations that the partition line would be "nothing but a line of fire and blood." [cite:wiki_un_partition_plan] [cite:palquest_un_partition_1947] On who said yes and who said no, the record is one-sided.

The second question is where the compressed story slips. Violence escalated sharply beginning on December 1, 1947, within days of the vote. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] It took the form of a civil war inside Mandate Palestine between Jewish and Arab communities and their paramilitary forces. It was not yet a war between states. Documented attacks in these months ran in both directions. In early January 1948, a car bomb at the former Ottoman government house in Jaffa killed 26 people. On February 15, 1948, Haganah and Palmach forces attacked the village of Qisarya near Haifa, part of a documented pattern of Jewish paramilitary attacks on Palestinian towns and villages in the war's first months. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] [cite:aljazeera_remix_partition_transcript] These came well before the massacre at Deir Yassin, which this stage treats separately ([scene:ma_s01|Deir Yassin and the massacres of 1948]). None of this means Arab forces and communities were uninvolved in starting or continuing the violence. It means the violence of these months was two-sided.

The invasion by neighboring Arab state armies was a second and distinct escalation. It came on May 15, 1948, the day after Israel's declaration of independence, and the war ended in 1949 with Israel's territorial gains extending far beyond the lines of the UN plan. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] The invasion was real and significant, and its territorial consequences are documented. But it followed five and a half months of civil war already under way. It was not the war's starting point.

The documented sequence therefore has three parts: a one-sided vote; a two-sided civil war, with attacks from both communities; and, five and a half months later, a state-level Arab invasion. Each part is separately documented. The misleading effect comes from stitching them into one unbroken line from the vote to the invasion, so that the civil-war phase, with attacks such as Qisarya and Deir Yassin, drops out of view.

This does not argue that the Arab League's rejection was justified, or that the later invasion was unprovoked. Nor does it offer a final judgment on which side bears greater responsibility for the outbreak of the war. It establishes the shape of the sequence. Told in either direction, as a purely defensive story or as a purely aggressive one, the compressed version leaves out a real and documented phase of the conflict, and the reader is better served by the full order of events.
