The Crisis Opens, 13–19 May 1967

The Soviet warning with which the spring's Syrian story ends opened the crisis on Israel's southern front. In six days in May 1967 the position there was transformed. On 13 May the Soviet Union warned Egypt that Israel was about to attack Syria. By 19 May the United Nations Emergency Force, which had stood on the Egyptian side of the line, was leaving, and Egyptian troops were moving into Sinai. The familiar account tends to tell this week as a single movement, with one cause and one intention behind it. The documents tell it as a chain of separate decisions, taken in Moscow, Cairo, New York and Jerusalem, each by people who could see only part of the chain. Why the chain began, and what Nasser meant by the steps he took, are among the most disputed questions of the period. This section sets out what each document says, and where the historians who have read them part company.

The Soviet warning, 13 May. On 13 May 1967 the Soviet Union told Egypt that Israel was massing forces to attack Syria. According to the historian Hassan Elbahtimy, writing for the Wilson Center's Cold War International History Project, the warning spoke of about 11 brigades and came through at least three channels: to Anwar Sadat during a stop in Moscow, and in Cairo through Egyptian intelligence and through the Foreign Ministry. Elbahtimy writes that the information "proved to be false." [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy] The Library of Congress study of Egypt gives the warning differently: Israel, it says, was reported to have mobilized two brigades on the frontier, after which Nasser sent troops toward the border, and Syria followed. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt_m] The two accounts differ by a factor of more than five on the size of the force the Soviets reported, and the sources here do not explain the difference. They agree on what matters most for what followed: a Soviet report of an Israeli concentration against Syria reached Cairo on 13 May.

Fawzi on the Syrian border, 14 May. The next day General Muhammad Fawzi, Egypt's chief of staff, went to the Syrian border. His visit is recorded in a transcript of an interview he gave on 28 February 1997, held by the Liddell Hart Centre at King's College London, which covers the visit and the Soviet reports. [cite:rs_cw6_fawzi_kcl] The archive's catalogue description, which is all that was consulted for this book, does not state what Fawzi concluded about whether Israeli troops were massed there. [cite:rs_cw6_fawzi_kcl] That gap is important, and it should not be filled by assumption. What Egypt's chief of staff saw on the Syrian front on 14 May, and what he reported to Nasser, is not established here.

Into Sinai. Egyptian forces began to move into Sinai. The historians Tal and Laurens both read the deployment of 14 May as deterrence: a signal, meant to dissuade Israel from striking Syria, by moving force where Israel could see it. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_m] [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_m] In Jerusalem the movement was read in much the same way at first. Israel's cabinet heard on 17 May that Egyptian forces were moving into Sinai, and, according to the Israel State Archives' summary of its meetings, it worked on the assumption that the move was mainly for show, not for war. [cite:rs_cw6_isa_cabinet] On the same day, Laurens writes, two Egyptian reconnaissance aircraft flew over Dimona, whose reactor is the subject of earlier sections of this stage, without being detected. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_m]

The letter to Rikhye, 16 May. The next step was taken at 22:00 Gaza time on 16 May. Brigadier Eiz-El-Din Mokhtar handed the commander of the United Nations Emergency Force, Major-General Indar Jit Rikhye, a letter from General Fawzi. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d6] [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] The letter said that the troops of the United Arab Republic, as Egypt then called itself, were concentrated in Sinai to act if Israel attacked any Arab country, and it asked UNEF to withdraw from its observation posts along the border. Fawzi's text read:

I request that you issue your orders to withdraw all these troops immediately.

[cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d6] [cite:rs_cw6_a6730]

U Thant, the Secretary-General, answered the same evening. Any such request, he replied, had to come to him directly from the government of the UAR, not from its army to a field commander. On receiving it he would "order the withdrawal of all UNEF troops from Gaza and Sinai," and would inform the General Assembly at the same time. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d6] Fawzi had asked for the force to leave the border posts. U Thant's reply spoke not of the posts but of all UNEF troops, in Gaza and in Sinai.

Riad's request, 18 May. The formal request came from Egypt's foreign minister, Riad. It reached U Thant at noon New York time on 18 May. The UAR, it said, "has decided to terminate the presence of the United Nations Emergency Force." [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] [cite:rs_cw6_s7906] U Thant consulted UNEF's Advisory Committee. The committee did not ask for a session of the General Assembly. He replied to Egypt that evening. [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] [cite:rs_cw6_s7906]

The force and its foundation. What was leaving was a force of 3,378 men on 15 May 1967, drawn from seven countries: Brazil, Canada, Denmark, India, Norway, Sweden and Yugoslavia. It had been stationed only on the Egyptian side of the line, because Israel had refused to have it on its own side. [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] That fact shaped the legal argument that followed. A force that stood on one country's territory alone depended on that one country's consent.

U Thant set out his reasoning in his reports to the General Assembly and the Security Council. UNEF, he argued, rested on the consent of the host country. A peacekeeping force could not function without that country's "continuing consent and co-operation," and the movements of UAR troops had in any case already made the force unworkable on the ground. [cite:rs_cw6_s7906] [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] He also addressed a private note that Hammarskjöld had written in 1957 about the terms of UNEF's presence. That note, U Thant wrote, "cannot affect in any way the basis for the presence of UNEF." [cite:rs_cw6_s7906] [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] The documents consulted for this book give U Thant's ruling on the note, not the note's own text, and this book does not characterize it beyond his words.

The mechanism is clear from these documents. The power to end UNEF's presence lay with Egypt, as host. The Secretary-General judged that he had no power to keep a force on territory whose government had withdrawn its consent, and his reply to Fawzi spoke of withdrawing the whole force. The Advisory Committee could have asked for the General Assembly to meet; it did not. Israel, which had refused the force on its own side of the line, had no part in the decision. Whether U Thant could or should have delayed, consulted more widely, or offered a partial withdrawal is a question the record frames but does not answer, and his own reports are the case for the decision he made.

The dates. The dates of the withdrawal request are given differently in the secondary literature, and the differences are worth stating because they change how the sequence reads. The United Nations record, and the State Department's editorial note on it, date Fawzi's letter to 16 May and the formal UAR request to 18 May. [cite:rs_cw6_a6730] [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d6] The Library of Congress study gives 16 May; GlobalSecurity gives 18 May; Fanack gives 19 May. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt_m] [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_m] [cite:rs_cw6_fanack_m] The UN record is the primary source, and it shows that the two dates belong to two different documents, a military letter and a governmental request. Accounts that give one date have chosen one of the two, or, in Fanack's case, a date that matches neither.

U Thant's report of 19 May. On 19 May U Thant reported to the Security Council. Two of his findings bear directly on the warning that had begun the week. The observers of the UN Truce Supervision Organization, he wrote, had "confirmed the absence of troop concentrations" on both sides of the Syrian line. [cite:rs_cw6_s7896] [cite:rs_cw6_s7906] The concentration the Soviet warning described was not there for the UN's observers to see. He also wrote that statements attributed to senior Israeli officials had been "so threatening as to be particularly inflammatory," and that the situation was "more menacing" than at any time since the autumn of 1956. [cite:rs_cw6_s7896] [cite:rs_cw6_s7906] The report thus cut in two directions at once. It found no troop concentrations on the Syrian line of the kind the warning described, and it found statements attributed to senior Israeli officials particularly inflammatory. It did not say which of the two had weighed more in Cairo.

Kosygin and Eshkol. The question of the Soviet warning was carried into the next fortnight in a direct exchange between the two governments. On 26 May Kosygin wrote to Eshkol, urging him to "resort to all measures to prevent a military conflict." Eshkol replied on 1 June that "there were no Israeli concentrations at the Syrian border," and that he had invited the Soviet ambassador to visit the frontier and see for himself. [cite:rs_cw6_kosygin_eshkol] Israel's denial and the UN observers' finding therefore point the same way.

Why did Moscow send the warning? If the warning was false, the obvious question is why it was sent, and on this the historians divide three ways. The first position is that of Ginor and Remez, as Elbahtimy summarizes their work: they portray the Soviet Union as manipulating events toward war. [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy] The second is Elbahtimy's own. Working from Egyptian minutes and testimony, he finds Moscow acting as a force for restraint once the crisis was under way, and taken aback by how events developed; but he allows that the false report may still have set off the chain of decisions that led to the closure of the Strait of Tiran. [cite:rs_cw6_elbahtimy] The third is David Tal's: the warning was false, and its motive remains unexplained. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_m]

Each position can point to something in the record. On the manipulation thesis, the documented fact is that the Soviet Union passed to an ally, through three channels at once, a military report that the UN's observers did not confirm. Elbahtimy's restraint thesis rests on the Egyptian record of what Soviet officials actually said once the crisis had begun. Tal's agnosticism rests on the absence, in the sources he used, of any document that states the motive. The documents consulted for this book do not decide among them, and this book does not. Elbahtimy and Tal both treat the warning as false, a point the UN observers' report of 19 May and Eshkol's letter of 1 June both support; how Ginor and Remez treat its accuracy is not stated in Elbahtimy's summary.

What did Nasser intend? The second dispute concerns Egypt. One reading, shared by Tal, Laurens and the Library of Congress study, is that Nasser's move into Sinai and his request to remove UNEF were deterrence on Syria's behalf: a show of force to dissuade an Israeli attack on his ally, which escalated in part because UNEF left faster than he expected. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_m] [cite:rs_cw6_laurens_m] [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt_m] On this reading the steps were meant to be seen, not to be used, and Nasser's later steps were, in Tal's words as summarized here, partly forced by how fast UNEF left.

The other reading begins from Egypt's own words. Fawzi's letter of 16 May did not describe a defensive posture alone; it said that Egyptian troops were concentrated in Sinai to act against Israel if Israel attacked any Arab country. [cite:rs_cw6_frus19_d6] And Nasser's own public statements in the days after 19 May went considerably further, as the next section shows. On this reading the request to remove the only international force between the two armies was a step whose consequences Egypt's leaders could foresee, whatever they intended by it. Both readings use Fawzi's letter; they differ on whether its conditional, "if Israel attacked," is the operative part.

What the record does not settle. Four things remain open after the documents of this week have been read. Why the Soviet Union sent a report that the UN's observers did not confirm is not established by any document consulted here. What General Fawzi concluded on the Syrian border on 14 May is recorded in an interview transcript whose contents this book has not seen. Whether Nasser, in asking for UNEF's withdrawal from the border posts, expected or wanted the whole force to leave is a matter of inference from his later conduct, not of record. And why the Soviet warning spoke of 11 brigades in one account and two in another is not explained. Three days later Nasser announced the closure of the Strait of Tiran, the subject of [scene:cw6_tiran_june4|the stage's last section].
