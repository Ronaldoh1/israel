The Magus, the Queen and the Golden Dawn

Theurgy, in Britannica's definition, is "the art of compelling or persuading a god or supernatural power to act or refrain from acting"; the word comes from Greek words meaning "work of the gods". [cite:hm_brit_theurgy] It was practised by some of the late Platonist philosophers, "who believed miracles could be worked", and it aimed at "persuading a god to reveal himself and grant salvation, healing, and other requests"; beside its more magical procedures, "a higher and more intellectual theurgy was also practiced". [cite:hm_brit_theurgy] Ficino's interest in "statue animation" belongs to this line. [cite:hm_sep_ficino]

The most famous English practitioner was a royal adviser. John Dee, born in London in 1527, became "a scientific and medical adviser" to Elizabeth I after her accession in 1558; in 1577 he publicly advocated a "British empire"; and he built at Mortlake what was said to be England's largest private library, of more than four thousand books and manuscripts. [cite:hm_brit_dee] He had already been jailed under Mary I, in 1555, on the charge of being a conjurer, though he was soon released. [cite:hm_brit_dee] With the medium Edward Kelley, he held séances to converse with angels, and between 1583 and 1589 the two travelled mainly in Poland and Bohemia. [cite:hm_brit_dee] At Rudolf II's court the angels "could only be seen and heard by Edward Kelley"; accused of conjuring up the dead, Dee fled Prague in 1586. [cite:hm_quil_rudolf] Back in England, friends interceded with the queen, who made him warden of Manchester College in 1596, but his last years were marked by poverty. [cite:hm_brit_dee]

Three centuries later the Hermetic arts were organised as an order. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn opened its Isis-Urania temple in London in 1888. Its founders, William Robert Woodman, William Wynn Westcott and Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers, were all Freemasons. [cite:hm_wiki_gd] Its members included W. B. Yeats, Maud Gonne, Florence Farr, Arthur Machen, Arthur Edward Waite, Pamela Colman Smith and Aleister Crowley. [cite:hm_wiki_gd] Its first order taught Hermetic Qabalah, the elements, astrology, tarot and geomancy; its second order taught magic, including scrying, astral travel and alchemy. [cite:hm_wiki_gd]

The order broke over authority. By late 1899 adepts in London were dissatisfied with Mathers's leadership and his friendship with Crowley. When the London officials refused Crowley initiation into a higher grade, Mathers initiated him himself in Paris on 16 January 1900; London demanded an investigation, Mathers refused, and the temple declared its independence. Yeats resigned; the order split into rival bodies and was dissolved in 1903. [cite:hm_wiki_gd] An order founded on documents of uncertain origin, as Part Three showed, came apart over who held the right to admit whom to its secrets. [cite:hm_wiki_cipher]
