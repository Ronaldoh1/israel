Lebanon, 1978–2000: Invasion, Security Zone, Withdrawal

Lebanon enters the Greater-Israel argument through a river and a long occupation. The river is the Litani, which some accounts describe as the historic Revisionist Zionist claim line in the north. The occupation is the south Lebanon "security zone" Israel held from 1985 to 2000. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] The Hezbollah volume of this library tells the military and political history of those years in detail; this section does not retell it. It asks a narrower question of that record: what Israel's governments said they wanted in Lebanon, whether any of them claimed Lebanese territory, and how the occupation ended. The period after 2000, including the positions Israel has held since 2024, belongs to Part Nine. [scene:gi9_lebanon|Part Nine: south Lebanon since 2024]

The Litani in 1919: water, not a frontier

The documented origin of the Litani in Zionist planning is the Zionist Organization's statement to the Paris Peace Conference of 3 February 1919, a document of the mainstream movement rather than of the Revisionists. Its proposed northern boundary started "at a point on the Mediterranean Sea in the vicinity south of Sidon" and ran inland along the watersheds of the Lebanon foothills and the slopes of Mount Hermon. On the river itself it asked that "some international arrangement must be made" to protect the riparian rights of the people living south of the Litani, and it called the Hermon "Palestine's real 'Father of Waters'", which "cannot be severed from it." [cite:gi8_bu_zo1919] The request, in other words, was a northern line drawn with water in mind and an arrangement for the Litani's waters, made to the peace conference that drew the Mandate borders. The statement and its map are examined in Part Two; the description of a "traditional Revisionist" claim to the Litani is not supported by the sources reviewed and should not be repeated. [scene:gi2_map1919|Part Two: the 1919 map]

1978: Operation Litani

On 11 March 1978 gunmen who had come from Lebanon by sea attacked Israel's coastal road; 38 civilians, 13 of them children, were killed, and Fatah claimed the attack. [cite:hz1_wiki_coastal] On the night of 14–15 March some 25,000 Israeli troops crossed into Lebanon and within days held the south up to the Litani, except Tyre. Lebanon told the Security Council it had no connection with the attack, and on 19 March the Council adopted Resolutions 425 and 426, calling on Israel to withdraw and creating the UN Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL). [cite:hz1_unifil_bg] Estimates of the Lebanese and Palestinian dead run from about 1,100 to about 2,000. When Israel withdrew it handed its positions not to UNIFIL but to the militia of Major Saad Haddad, which it armed and paid. [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] [cite:hz1_csm_haddad] The full account is in the Hezbollah volume. [scene:hz1_1978|The Hezbollah Architecture: 1978]

1982: the forty-kilometre aim and Beirut

After the shooting of Israel's ambassador in London, Shlomo Argov, on 3 June 1982, Israel launched Operation Peace for Galilee on 6 June. The IDF's own account gives the objective as pushing the PLO "40 km to the north"; the Menachem Begin Heritage Center says the operation was designed to make "the forty miles surrounding the border" free of armed groups, and that the IDF "was forced to continue north all the way to Beirut." [cite:hz2_idf_lw1] [cite:hz2_begin_pfg] Lebanese police counted 19,085 killed between 4 June and 31 August 1982, a figure that cannot be independently verified; the Israeli army gave 446 soldiers killed by mid-November. [cite:hz2b_csm_dec82] In September Lebanese Phalangist militiamen killed hundreds of civilians, by some counts thousands, in the Sabra and Shatila camps, which Israeli troops had surrounded. Israel's state commission of inquiry, chaired by Supreme Court President Yitzhak Kahan (a commission, not a ruling of the Supreme Court), found Israel indirectly responsible and Defence Minister Ariel Sharon personally responsible for "ignoring the danger of bloodshed and revenge." [cite:hz2b_jvl_kahan] [scene:hz2_invasion|The Hezbollah Architecture: the 1982 invasion]

What Israel sought from Lebanon at the end of the war was set down in a document. The agreement of 17 May 1983 between Israel and Lebanon declared the state of war "terminated", described the border as inviolable, and provided for Israeli withdrawal within eight to twelve weeks, a "Security Region" in the south, and a liaison committee with American participation. Syria's president rejected it, and Lebanon repudiated it on 5 March 1984. [cite:hz2b_jvl_may17] [cite:hz2b_wiki_may17] [cite:hz2b_majalla_may17] The terms Israel signed concerned security arrangements inside Lebanon, not a change of the border.

1985–2000: the security zone

On 14 January 1985 Israel's cabinet voted to withdraw in three phases, the last along the border, leaving a zone in which Haddad's successor force, the South Lebanon Army (SLA), would operate with Israeli backing. [cite:hz2b_jvl_jan85] Sidon was handed to the Lebanese army in February and Tyre in April, and by 10 June 1985 most Israeli troops had left, with a smaller force staying in the zone. [cite:hz2b_wiki_slc] Human Rights Watch put the zone at about 850 square kilometres, roughly a tenth of Lebanon, with about 150,000 people in 162 villages and towns. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] The Congressional Research Service described it as about nine miles deep, which places it south of the Litani, not along or beyond the river as some accounts have it. Israel funded and equipped the SLA, paid its members about $500 to $800 a month, and lost roughly 250 soldiers there between 1985 and April 2000. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] The zone's prison at Khiam, run by the SLA with Israeli security-service involvement, is documented in the Hezbollah volume. [scene:hz4_zone|The Hezbollah Architecture: the security zone]

The stated purpose of the zone was protection of northern Israel, and Israeli officials said early on that the occupation might produce the opposite. Yitzhak Rabin, as defence minister in February 1985, warned that if Israel replaced PLO terrorism with Shiite terrorism "we have done the worst" in its struggle against terrorism. [cite:hz3_djn_rabin85] The sources reviewed record no Israeli civilian settlement in the zone and no application of Israeli law to it, the two steps taken on the Golan.

May 2000: withdrawal to the Blue Line

Domestic pressure ended the occupation. The Four Mothers movement, formed in 1997 after 73 soldiers died in a helicopter collision on their way to Lebanon, campaigned for withdrawal, and Ehud Barak promised to "bring the boys home" by July 2000. [cite:hz4_csm_mothers] He ordered the pullout on 23 May, and the last soldier left early on 24 May, weeks ahead of schedule; the SLA collapsed, and about 1,500 of its members fled to Israel. [cite:hz4_merip_2000] [cite:hz4_crs_sla] The UN Secretary-General confirmed on 16 June 2000 that Israel had complied with Resolution 425. The one disputed patch, the roughly 25 square kilometres of the Shebaa Farms, the UN placed in the Syrian Golan rather than in Lebanon, so that its status falls under the Golan resolutions, not under 425; Hezbollah's leader, Hassan Nasrallah, said the withdrawal would not be complete until the farms were liberated. [cite:hz4_crs_shebaa] [cite:hz4_merip_2000] [scene:hz4_withdrawal|The Hezbollah Architecture: the 2000 withdrawal]

What the record shows

In Lebanon between 1978 and 2000, Israel invaded twice, reached Beirut, and held a tenth of the country for fifteen years through an allied militia it paid. Each step was justified in the government's own words as security for northern Israel, and the 1983 agreement Israel signed treated the international border as inviolable. No law, platform or government statement reviewed claims Lebanese territory for Israel; the 1919 Litani wording was a water arrangement put to the peace conference, and the zone was evacuated in 2000 and certified as such by the UN. [cite:hz2b_jvl_may17] [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] [cite:hz4_crs_shebaa] The argument that the invasions and the zone expressed territorial designs on south Lebanon is an inference; on this period the documents point the other way. Whether the positions held since 2024 change that picture is the question Part Nine takes up. [scene:gi9_lebanon|Part Nine: south Lebanon since 2024]
