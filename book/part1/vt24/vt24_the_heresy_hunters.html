The Heresy Hunters: How the Teachers of Gnosis Were Written Out

For about sixteen centuries, almost everything known about the Valentinians, Basilideans and Sethians came from the people who wrote to defeat them. That fact shapes every claim in this Part. It also makes the heresy hunters a subject in their own right, because the methods they developed to exclude these teachers (a list of bishops, a rule of faith, a closed set of books, and a legal argument about who owned the scriptures) became the frame of the institutional church. None of them were Roman officials, and for most of the period Rome was one church among several. The first state action came only in the fourth century, from an emperor.

Irenaeus of Lyon: the list of bishops

Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, wrote the five books now called Against Heresies about 180, to expose what he called knowledge "falsely so called", the phrase of 1 Timothy 6:20. [cite:vt24_ah1_pref] Book One summarises the Valentinian system and then traces a genealogy of error from Simon Magus through Basilides, Carpocrates and others. [cite:vt24_ah1_23] His decisive move came in Book Three. Instead of arguing only over doctrine, he argued from structure: the apostles had appointed bishops, and their successors could be traced "to our own times". He gave the Roman list, from Linus to Eleutherius, "in the twelfth place from the apostles", and wrote that "every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its preeminent authority". [cite:vt24_ah3_03] Whatever the merits of a given teaching, a teacher outside that line had no claim to speak for the apostles. Irenaeus also argued that there could be four gospels and only four; that argument and the formation of the list are in [scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|Part Twenty-Three].

Tertullian: a plea from Roman law

Tertullian of Carthage, writing about 200, took the argument further in The Prescription Against Heretics. A praescriptio in Roman civil procedure was a plea that ended a case before its merits were heard. Tertullian applied it to scripture: he opposed "admitting them to any discussion of the Scriptures", because the scriptures belonged to the churches founded by the apostles and the heretics had no standing to argue from them. "Our appeal, therefore, must not be made to the Scriptures." [cite:vt24_praescr] The same work's account of the heretics' loose offices and women teachers, quoted in [scene:vt24_how_they_lived|the previous section], is part of the case: disorder was itself presented as proof of illegitimacy. [cite:vt24_praescr41]

Hippolytus, Origen, Plotinus

The Refutation of All Heresies, written in the early third century and attributed to Hippolytus or to an unknown author scholars call Pseudo-Hippolytus, argued that each heresy was a disguised Greek philosophy: Simon and Valentinus were made pupils of Pythagoras. Books four to ten were lost until a manuscript was recovered from Mount Athos in 1842; it gives independent accounts of the Naassenes, Peratae, Sethians, Valentinus and Basilides. [cite:vt24_wiki_refutation] Origen of Alexandria, defending Christianity against the pagan critic Celsus about 248, described an Ophite diagram of the heavens he had seen himself and called its formulas absurd. [cite:vt24_origen_cc6] The attack did not come only from Christians. The pagan philosopher Plotinus wrote a treatise known as Against the Gnostics (Ennead II.9) objecting to their contempt for the cosmos and its maker, their multiplication of divine realities, a salvation that asked no moral effort, and their magic. Porphyry, his student, names Zostrianos and Allogenes among the texts circulating in the school. [cite:vt24_bmcr_plotinus]

Epiphanius: the medicine chest

Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, began his Panarion in 374 or 375. Its title means a medicine chest, a stock of remedies against the poisons of 80 heresies, each compared to a snake. Its value is that it quotes documents found nowhere else, including Ptolemy's Letter to Flora; its weakness is that the author's zeal sometimes kept him from examining what he condemned, and he admitted writing on one heresy from hearsay. [cite:vt24_wiki_panarion] [cite:vt24_wiki_flora]

The state steps in

The change from argument to law came with Constantine. An undated edict preserved by Eusebius addressed "ye Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites, Paulians, ye who are called Cataphrygians", forbade them to assemble "not in public merely, but in any private house or place whatsoever", and ordered their houses of prayer "made over without delay to the catholic Church". [cite:vt24_vc364] [cite:vt24_vc365] Eusebius adds that "search should be made for their books", and that afterwards many members of these groups sought admission to the catholic church, some sincerely and some, he says, by pretence; he does not say the books were burned. [cite:vt24_vc366] This is the earliest documented state order that names a Gnostic group, and it is covered in its imperial setting in [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Part Twenty-One]. Theodosius I's law of 380 declared that the gatherings of heretics "shall not receive the name of churches", and the laws on heretics collected in book 16 of the Theodosian Code limited privileges to Catholics and fined heretics; one, against the Manichaeans, fined their leaders and had the houses where they taught seized. [cite:vt24_cth_ect] [cite:vt24_cth_osu] Athanasius of Alexandria's Easter letter of 367 warned his churches against apocryphal books as "an invention of heretics", without ordering them destroyed; [cite:vt23_na_festal39] [scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|Part Twenty-Three] sets out its text and [scene:vt25_the_jar|Part Twenty-Five] the hypothesis linking it to the burial of the Nag Hammadi codices.

What the record does not show

The record shows exclusion by argument, by episcopal discipline and, from Constantine onward, by imperial law, including a search for heretics' books. It does not show any Valentinian or Sethian teacher executed by order of a bishop, or any ancient church order to burn Gnostic gospels by name. Book-burning orders in this period are documented against Arius's writings after Nicaea and against the Manichaeans by Diocletian, a pagan emperor. [cite:vt23_na_socrates1] [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] The Gnostic texts mostly vanished in a quieter way: once excluded from public reading and denied buildings, they stopped being copied, and survived only where someone hid or kept them, as at Nag Hammadi, or where an opponent quoted them in order to refute them. A sixteen-century silence, broken in 1945, is the result.

Why the bishops' side prevailed: Pagels and her critics

The best-known scholarly explanation is Elaine Pagels's The Gnostic Gospels (1979), which won the National Book Award. She argued that orthodox doctrines did institutional work: the insistence on a bodily resurrection witnessed by named apostles "serves an essential political function" by grounding the authority of their successors, and the Gnostic claim to direct knowledge offered "the theological justification for refusing to obey the bishops and the priests". She also concluded that the orthodox churches' openness to all comers and their communal structure were real strengths, and that "ideas alone do not make a religion powerful". [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Critics have pressed back. Henry Chadwick found her lacking in "full rigour", especially on women; Hyam Maccoby said she idealised the Gnostics and passed over their elitism and hostility to the body; Raymond Brown judged the book too sympathetic to them. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Both points stand on the record: the heresy hunters did build an authority structure that a doctrine of direct knowing could not easily coexist with, and the teachers of gnosis did divide humanity into the spiritual few and the rest. How that tension plays out across the next fifteen centuries, and Ron's broader reading of it, is weighed in [scene:vt31_two_economies|Part Thirty-One] and [scene:vt32_verdict_two|Part Thirty-Two].
