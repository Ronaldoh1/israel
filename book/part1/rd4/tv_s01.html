How Did They Get Here? Ships, Citizenship and a Law of 1938

Zionist political work in the United States raises practical questions that are easy to skip past. Who were the people doing it? How did they travel, and under what legal status did they operate? A common assumption is that foreign agents slipped into the country in secret and worked outside the law. On almost every count, the record points in a different direction.

Start with citizenship. The leaders of American Zionism in this period, Louis Brandeis, Rabbi Stephen Wise and Rabbi Abba Hillel Silver, were citizens of the United States. Their political advocacy was the advocacy of citizens, carried on legally and in the open. Two figures central to the movement's later history had American chapters of their own. Golda Meir, then Golda Meyerson, grew up in Milwaukee and emigrated in 1921; she would later become Israel's prime minister [cite:w_golda]. David Ben-Gurion, expelled from Ottoman Palestine, lived in New York from 1915 to 1918. There he recruited volunteers for the Jewish Legion, and there he married [cite:w_bengurion].

Then there is travel. For most of this period, crossing the Atlantic meant crossing it by ship. Visiting leaders such as Chaim Weizmann came by ocean liner. Regular transatlantic passenger flights began only in 1939, with Pan Am's flying boats [cite:w_boeing314].

Finally, there is the law. Before 1938 no American statute required people working on behalf of foreign principals to register with the government at all. That changed when Congress passed the Foreign Agents Registration Act in 1938 [cite:doj_fara]. The gap is real and documented.

During the war the FBI watched the Bergson group, led by an Irgun member working under an assumed name; that story is told in [scene:fa_s01|Foreign Agents Before There Was a State]. The law was first tested against the movement's main American apparatus much later, in a 1962 order to the American Zionist Council. For the period covered here, then, the picture is not one of secret entry and illegal operation. It is one of American citizens organizing openly, of foreign visitors arriving by ocean liner, and of a registration law that did not exist until 1938.
