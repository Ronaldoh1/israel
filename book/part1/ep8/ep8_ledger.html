Fourteen Ordinary Rules

No single law protected the people around Epstein. What protected them was a stack of ordinary rules, most of them written for good reasons: to keep courts out of prosecutors' choices, to protect people who are never charged, to keep investigations secret, to let civil cases end. Each is defensible on its own. The test this section applies to each one is the same: did the rule work as written, so that the problem is the rule itself, or was it bent? Those are different claims, and they need different evidence.

Who holds the keys

Read down the last column and a pattern appears. Of the fourteen gates, ten are opened or closed, wholly or partly, by the executive branch: the Justice Department, the Treasury, the Bureau of Prisons or the president. Courts are kept out of most of those decisions by long-settled doctrine. The victims' own entry point closes before charges are filed. And the one mechanism designed to move the decision outside a conflicted executive, the independent counsel, has not existed since 1999. [cite:everycrsreport_iclaw]

The book calls this discretion stacking. It does not need a plan to work. When the people near the top of a network include, or are close to, the people holding these keys, nothing in the structure forces a different outcome, and so none happens. That is why it lasts. It would protect the friends of any administration, of either party, in the same way.

The pattern is not unique to this case. It needs at least two other documented cases before it can bear weight, and it has more. The Hastert case shows a statute of limitations turning child abuse into a bank-structuring charge. [cite:ep8_npr_hastert2016] The Iran-Contra pardons show the pardon power ending an investigation as it turned towards the president. [cite:washpost_weinberger_pardon] The Saturday Night Massacre shows that when a president moves against an investigator, what stands in the way is individual refusal and public anger, not a legal wall.
