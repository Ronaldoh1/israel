"Never Again" and the Classroom

A sentence many readers have heard runs roughly as follows: Israel was the world's answer to the Holocaust, the place created so that it could never happen again. It is one of the founding stories many people learned as fact, and it is worth checking in two halves, because the record shows two things at once: the plan came first, and the Holocaust then shaped what followed. This section takes the order of events first, then what American schools actually teach about the Holocaust and who supplies the material, and finally the history of the phrase "Never Again" itself, which has carried two different meanings for most of its life.

The first half concerns timing. The political program for a Jewish state was in print long before the Holocaust. Theodor Herzl's Der Judenstaat of 1896 weighed Palestine against Argentina as the territory for a political answer to European antisemitism, thirty-seven years before Hitler took power in Germany. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] The Sixth Zionist Congress voted in 1903 to investigate British East Africa, and the Seventh formally rejected it in 1905, so Palestine was confirmed by the movement's own choice against a real alternative. [cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress] In November 1917 Britain committed in writing to "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people." [cite:balfour_letter] At San Remo in 1920 the Allied powers assigned Britain the Mandate for Palestine and carried the Balfour language into an international agreement. [cite:san_remo_wiki] The League of Nations approved the Mandate in July 1922, and it took legal force in September 1923; its Article 6 bound Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and "close settlement by Jews on the land." [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] By 1933, then, the movement had a written program, a settled territorial choice, a great-power pledge, its international ratification and a binding legal mandate. The program, the pledge and the Mandate predate Hitler's rise by between eleven and thirty-seven years. [scene:s07a|Five dates that predate the Holocaust] sets out each date in full.

The second half is just as documented. The Holocaust changed the pace and the public demand. The Biltmore Program of May 1942 called openly for a "Jewish Commonwealth," the movement's first explicit, public statehood demand; it is best read as the culmination of the forty-six years of institution-building before it, not as their origin. [cite:biltmore_wiki] [cite:biltmore_medoff] Reading it that way does not minimize the Holocaust's effect, which was real: the catastrophe reshaped the world's willingness to act. "Never again" describes why the powers finally moved. It does not describe when the planning began.

The scale of what had happened is itself the best-documented fact in this story, and it bears on the classroom questions below. The historian Raul Hilberg's demographic breakdown produced an estimate of 5.1 million Jewish deaths; Lucy Dawidowicz's 1975 research in the birth and death records of prewar European cities produced 5,933,900. [cite:wiki_destruction_european_jews] Yad Vashem's Book of Names project, still under way, has identified about 4.8 million victims individually through testimony and archives. [cite:wiki_book_of_names] Independent methods, using different material, converge on the same range. Auschwitz alone, operating from May 1940 to January 1945, received at least 1.3 million people, of whom at least 1.1 million were murdered there, the largest toll at any single site among more than 44,000 camps and places of incarceration. [cite:wiki_auschwitz_camp] [cite:ushmm_evidence_documentation] Soviet troops liberated it on 27 January 1945, after the SS had driven most of the remaining prisoners onto death marches; the date has been marked since 2005 as International Holocaust Remembrance Day. [cite:wiki_auschwitz_liberation] The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum describes the Holocaust as the best-documented genocide in history, and it lists the core claims of denial: that the six million deaths never occurred, that Nazi Germany had no official extermination policy, and that the gas chambers of Auschwitz-Birkenau never existed. [cite:ushmm_evidence_documentation] The documentation is told in full in [scene:hol_s05|six million, and how that number is known].

The years 1945 to 1948 are where the Holocaust and Palestine actually met, and they met through the survivors. In 1945 Earl G. Harrison's team visited 30 displaced-persons camps in Germany and Austria. They found Jewish survivors held under armed guard behind barbed wire. Harrison wrote:

"we appear to be treating the Jews as the Nazis treated them, except that we do not exterminate them."

He delivered the report to President Truman on 24 August 1945, and the White House released it on 29 September. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_harrison] Harrison reported that most Jewish displaced persons wanted to go to Palestine, and he cited the Jewish Agency's petition for 100,000 additional immigration certificates. Truman asked Prime Minister Clement Attlee to admit 100,000. Attlee refused. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_harrison] The decision over entry to Palestine sat with Britain as the Mandatory power, and Truman's request was refused. Truman's own lever was American immigration law. His directive of 22 December 1945 gave displaced persons preference within the existing quotas, and about 22,950 entered the United States over two years, roughly two-thirds of them Jewish. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_harrison] The directive worked inside the existing quotas, so the 100,000 Truman asked Britain to admit stood against some 22,950 admitted by his own country over two years.

That refuge argument became the core of how support for the new state was presented to Americans. In 1948 the case rested on humanitarian grounds, with some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in Europe, on Christian restorationist belief, and on the new state's presentation as a democracy. The national-security establishment argued the reverse: the State Department, the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary and the CIA warned that American backing would harm American interests. Hostile framing of Arabs in pro-Zionist publicity drew heavily on the Grand Mufti's documented wartime collaboration with Hitler, a real episode used as a broad brush; the organized, well-funded anti-Islam network in the United States is documented mainly from the 2000s. [cite:cap_fear_inc] The "strategic asset" argument came later, growing after 1967 and formalized under President Reagan in 1981. That story belongs to [scene:sell_s01|how Israel was sold to Americans]. Among the founding narratives this library checks, "born of the Holocaust" fails in the same way: the Holocaust accelerated support; it did not create the program.

Turn now to the American classroom. The federal instrument is the Never Again Education Act, H.R. 943, signed on Friday, 29 May 2020. It requires the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum to develop and disseminate resources that improve awareness and understanding of the Holocaust, and it authorizes Holocaust education programs for prospective and current teachers and educational leaders. [cite:rs_ch01_wh_naea] The museum received $2 million under the Act in fiscal year 2025. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed]

The fullest account of what the states do is a report by the Government Accountability Office, delivered on 7 July 2026 to the House and Senate appropriations subcommittees for Labor, Health and Human Services and Education: GAO-26-108023, K–12 Education: How States and the U.S. Holocaust Museum Support Holocaust Education, prepared under a provision in Senate Report 118-84. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] GAO counted 51 jurisdictions, the District of Columbia among them. Forty-three have academic standards that explicitly mention the Holocaust or laws that require it to be taught: 35 include it in their standards and 25 require it by law. Eight have neither: Kansas, Montana, Nevada, North Dakota, Pennsylvania, Vermont, Washington and Wyoming. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed]

The requirements vary widely. Arizona's law requires at least three class periods, or the equivalent, on at least two occasions between the seventh and twelfth grades. Florida's standards run from fifth through twelfth grade. New Hampshire's mention the Holocaust in a single world-history standard. Michigan's law covers grades 8 to 12 and names both the Holocaust and the Armenian Genocide. GAO gives no typical number of hours; state officials told it that teachers "had to choose which parts of the Holocaust to cover." [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] The history of these mandates is mostly recent. According to Echoes & Reflections, which tracks them, the earliest were California's in 1985, for grades 7 to 12; Illinois in 1990, expanded in 2005; New Jersey in March 1994; and New York in 1996, amended in 2022. Most of the others date from 2016 onward. [cite:rs_ch01_er_map]

Who supplies the material matters as much as who mandates it. Per GAO, 35 states use materials from local or state Holocaust museums and centers, 34 from the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, and 20 from state Holocaust commissions. Ten of the 48 states that responded write their own materials, and none of them requires schools to use them. Of 70 teachers who acquired materials, 56 found them on their own. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] The main gap GAO found was teacher preparation. Only 3 of 48 states require Holocaust-specific teacher training. Forty-one of 48 called teacher preparedness very or somewhat challenging; 39 cited insufficient time, 37 funding and 34 misinformation. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] The mandate has spread widely; the training, and the choice of materials, rest largely with teachers.

A survey of young adults followed in early 2020. The Claims Conference commissioned it, and Schoen Cooperman Research compiled it, from 1,350 Americans aged 18 to 39. According to J. Weekly's account of it, 63% did not know that six million Jews were murdered: 37% chose six million and 15% chose two million. Thirty-six percent could not name a camp, 12% said the number who died had been "greatly exaggerated," 90% said the Holocaust happened, and 49% had seen Holocaust denial online. The Berkeley sociologist Samuel Lucas cautioned that "not sure" answers and the wording of questions are hard to interpret, and that social-desirability bias could make denial look smaller than it really is. [cite:rs_ch01_jw_claims] His caution runs both ways: some answers are hard to read, and the denial figure may understate denial.

One curriculum GAO names is Echoes & Reflections, which it identifies as a partnership of the Anti-Defamation League, the USC Shoah Foundation and Yad Vashem. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] According to J. Weekly's report of its launch in 2005, it was announced by the ADL, the Shoah Visual History Foundation and Yad Vashem, which the paper described as "Israel's Holocaust Remembrance Authority." It offered ten lessons with maps, photographs, timelines and primary sources, and 51 video testimonies drawn from the Shoah Foundation's 52,000 interviews. Yad Vashem supplied the historical expertise; the ADL handled outreach to schools and state officials. [cite:rs_ch01_jw_er2005] Yad Vashem's own status is set by law. It had operated privately since 1946. The Yad Vashem Law was brought to the Knesset on 18 May 1953 and passed on 19 August 1953, making it a body of the State of Israel. Its mandate includes representing the State at national and international commemorations, and the Knesset limited it to the Holocaust and the destroyed communities, not the rebuilding of the homeland. [cite:rs_ch01_yv_law] A body of the Israeli state is therefore one of three partners in an American curriculum, and its legal remit is the Holocaust and the destroyed communities, not the rebuilding of the homeland.

The program's lesson "Antisemitism after the Holocaust," for middle and high school, lists this learning objective: "Identify current features of antisemitism including Holocaust denial and distortion, and demonization of Israel." [cite:rs_ch01_adl_lesson] Whether that belongs in Holocaust education became an open fight in 2025. On 5 July delegates at the National Education Association's Representative Assembly narrowly adopted a new business item, NBI 39, saying the union would not use, endorse or publicize ADL materials or programs. Because it called for a boycott, it went to the union's executive committee, which rejected it on 18 July 2025. [cite:rs_ch01_njea_nbi39] [cite:rs_ch01_edweek_nea] The two positions deserve to be stated at their strongest. The ADL lesson treats demonization of Israel as one current feature of antisemitism, alongside denial and distortion, and the NEA's leadership kept the ADL as a partner; its president, Becky Pringle, said the boycott "would not further NEA's commitment to academic freedom, our membership, or our goals." [cite:rs_ch01_adl_lesson] [cite:rs_ch01_edweek_nea] The item's sponsor, Judy Greenspan, and the teachers behind it argued that the ADL's definition of antisemitism folds criticism of Israel into antisemitism and has made real antisemitism harder to track and fight. [cite:rs_ch01_edweek_nea] The union's delegates and its executive committee came down on opposite sides within two weeks, which is a fair measure of how unsettled the question is.

The phrase at the center of all this has its own history. According to the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, its first use is murky. JTA traced it most likely to postwar Israel: it appeared in secular kibbutzim in the late 1940s and in a Swedish Holocaust documentary in 1961. Some trace it further back, to Isaac Lamdan's 1926 Hebrew poem "Masada," with its line "Never shall Masada fall again!" [cite:rs_ch01_jta_never]

Its English currency, by JTA's account, came from Meir Kahane, who founded the Jewish Defense League in 1968 and titled his 1972 manifesto Never Again. For Kahane it meant "that if it happens again, it won't happen in the same way," and he used it to justify acts of terror against antisemitism. After his assassination in November 1990, Sholom Comay, president of the American Jewish Committee, said Kahane "must always be remembered for the slogan Never Again." [cite:rs_ch01_jta_never] The phrase later took on a universal meaning. Barack Obama said in 2011 that it should be "not just a phrase but a principled cause." The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum made it the theme of its 2013 Days of Remembrance. Elie Wiesel wrote in 2012 that it "becomes more than a slogan: It's a prayer, a promise, a vow." [cite:rs_ch01_jta_never] In 2010 Benjamin Netanyahu used both senses. At Auschwitz he said, "never again will we allow the hand of evil to sever the life of our people"; in the guestbook of the Rwanda genocide memorial he wrote, simply, "Never again." [cite:rs_ch01_jta_never]

That is the second dispute, and again both sides have a case. On the particular reading, set out by the scholar Shaul Magid, Kahane's "Never Again" was a call to militancy as the only act of prevention, and leaders have used the phrase to mean the protection of the Jewish people specifically. On the universal reading, Holocaust museums, Wiesel and others use it as a vow against genocide anywhere, and it has been taken up for refugees, for Japanese-American internment and for other causes; the historian Hasia Diner argues that objecting to such uses is not, by itself, reasonable. [cite:rs_ch01_jta_never] Neither reading owns the phrase. Netanyahu's two uses in one year show that the same speaker can mean both.

The record leaves real gaps. GAO's report, the most complete federal account of Holocaust education, does not mention Israel at all. [cite:rs_ch01_gao_holed] No national study measures how classrooms connect the Holocaust to Israel, whether through the Echoes & Reflections lesson objective or otherwise, so any claim that American schools teach the Holocaust as the reason for Israel, or that they do not, goes beyond what is documented. The survey of young adults measured knowledge of the Holocaust, not beliefs about Israel, and its own critic questioned how far even its knowledge figures can be read. The origin of the phrase itself remains unsettled. What the record does settle is the order of events: the plan came decades before the Holocaust, and the Holocaust, through the survivors in the camps, then shaped the years from 1945 to 1948.
