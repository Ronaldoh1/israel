Unions: the Histadrut and American Labour

In 1980 the two countries' labour movements could hardly have been more different. Israel's Histadrut was not only a union federation but an employer, a bank owner and the gateway to most people's health care; America's unions were private associations bargaining firm by firm under federal law. Both lost most of their reach in the decades that followed, by different routes. This section traces the decline in each country with each country's own measure, and states where the two measures cannot be set side by side. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] [cite:us13_bls_union2025]

Israel: from state-builder to union

The Histadrut, the General Federation of Labour, was founded in Haifa in December 1920 to represent Jewish workers in Mandatory Palestine; it began admitting Arab workers only in the 1950s. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] Through its holding company, Hevrat HaOvdim, it owned industrial conglomerates, the shipping line ZIM, Bank Hapoalim until the state took over the banks after the 1983 bank-share crisis, and the Clalit sick fund, which covered about 70 per cent of Israelis in the mid-1980s. It still employed about 280,000 people in 1989. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] Membership was close to universal because health care came with it: in 1983 the Histadrut counted about 85 per cent of wage earners, and the sociologist Michael Shalev found that until the early 1990s it negotiated for 85 per cent of all wage earners through framework agreements for the public and private sectors and spoke for about two-thirds of Israeli adults. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] [cite:us13_shalev_labor]

That position was also a role in governing. In 1985 the Histadrut signed the wage freeze that, with spending cuts and a fixed exchange rate, ended the hyperinflation; it was a party to the plan, not merely consulted. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] [scene:us11_turn|Part Eleven: the 1985 plan] Within a decade the structure came apart. The Labour Party lost control of the Histadrut in its May 1994 elections, and the National Health Insurance Law, in force from 1995, ended the rule that tied Clalit care to membership. [cite:us13_shalev_labor] Membership fell from about 1.8 million, some 80 per cent of the workforce, to about 200,000 in 1994–95; the new leadership sold assets outside its union work. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] The health law's side of this change is told in Part Sixteen. [scene:us16_systems|Part Sixteen: health systems]

The Histadrut rebuilt part of its base as an ordinary union: about 650,000 members in 2005 and 570,000 in 2017, with about 800,000 claimed more recently. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] Measured as a share of employees, density was 27 per cent in 2016. The OECD's 2017 profile of Israeli bargaining put private-sector density at 10–20 per cent and collective-agreement coverage at 20–30 per cent of employees, with bargaining mostly at company or sector level and not coordinated. The labour ministry can extend a sector agreement to a whole industry, but the OECD described extensions as "uncommon". [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] [cite:us13_oecd_cb_israel]

The United States: half the density of 1983

American bargaining runs on the National Labor Relations Act of 1935, under which a union bargains for a workplace or firm once it wins recognition there, and represents everyone in that unit, members or not. There is no general mechanism to extend an agreement across an industry, so coverage stops at the edge of each organised firm; in a 2017 comparison of 21 OECD countries the United States ranked last in collective-bargaining coverage. [cite:us13_eg_bargaining] Section 14(b) of the Act allows states to ban union-security clauses, so that workers covered by a contract can decline to pay toward it; 26 states had such "right-to-work" laws after Michigan became, on 30 March 2024, the first state in 58 years to repeal one. [cite:us13_littler_rtw]

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' consistent series begins in 1983, when 20.1 per cent of wage and salary workers, 17.7 million people, belonged to a union. By 2024 the rate was 9.9 per cent; in 2025 it was 10.0 per cent, or 14.7 million members. The split by sector is stark: 32.9 per cent of public-sector workers were members in 2025 against 5.9 per cent in the private sector. A further group is covered by contracts without joining, so 11.2 per cent of workers, 16.5 million, were represented by unions. Full-time union members had median weekly earnings of $1,404, against $1,174 for non-members, a raw comparison that does not adjust for occupation or industry. [cite:us13_bls_union2025] The 2025 figures omit October, when the federal shutdown stopped the survey. [cite:us13_bls_union2025] Before the BLS series, about one American worker in three was a union member in 1960. [cite:us13_eg_bargaining]

The two declines had different engines. Israel's density fell in a few years when the state cut the link between union membership and health care and the Histadrut ceased to be a conglomerate; it was a change of law. America's fell over four decades under a stable legal framework in which organising happens one workplace at a time, alongside the spread of right-to-work laws. [cite:us13_shalev_labor] [cite:us13_eg_bargaining] [cite:us13_littler_rtw] The result is a similar order of magnitude in both countries now, with Israel's coverage higher on the OECD's last estimate. The Israeli series has a gap: no official annual density series from 2016 to 2026 was found for this book, so Israel's current density is stated only as a range.

The political strike

The Histadrut has also used the national general strike, including over questions that were not about pay. It called one in February 2012 on behalf of subcontracted workers, settled four days later. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] In late March 2023, after the prime minister announced the dismissal of the defence minister during the fight over the judicial overhaul, its chairman Arnon Bar-David called a general strike; it was called off the same day when Benjamin Netanyahu agreed to pause the legislation. [cite:us13_wiki_histadrut] [scene:us2_courts|Part Two: the courts and the 2023 overhaul] On 2 September 2024, after the bodies of six hostages were recovered in Gaza, the Histadrut struck again to press the government on a ceasefire and hostage deal; Ben Gurion airport halted flights for several hours, and the Labour Court ordered the strike ended by 2.30 in the afternoon, which the Histadrut obeyed. [cite:us13_npr_strike2024]
