Black Sabbath: Britain Cracks Down

It is often said that relations between Britain and the Jewish community of Mandate Palestine deteriorated gradually, without a single sharp turning point. The record identifies one. On Saturday, June 29, 1946, Britain launched Operation Agatha, a coordinated raid across Mandate Palestine. Because it fell on the Jewish Sabbath, the community it struck named it "Black Sabbath."

The scale was very large. Roughly 17,000 British troops and police searched Jewish settlements, kibbutzim and offices simultaneously. More than 2,700 people were arrested, among them senior officials of the Jewish Agency. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] The operation was a direct response to escalating attacks on British installations by the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi. Its targets, however, went beyond the armed groups. By arresting the Agency's own senior officials, Britain struck at the institutions of the mainstream Zionist leadership itself. The sources give the operation's overall scale and its targeting of the leadership; they do not provide a full inventory of every site searched or every person detained.

Part of what was at stake was paper. British forces seized a substantial cache of documents from the Jewish Agency's headquarters in Jerusalem. The material was believed to include evidence linking the Agency's mainstream leadership to Haganah operations. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] If that link could be shown, it would undo the careful separation the movement had maintained between its political leadership and armed resistance, the same kind of separation that had been built, on the other side of the Atlantic, into the procurement network described in [scene:s07d|the Sonneborn meeting]. The exact contents of the seized documents are not specified in the sources; what is documented is what they were believed to contain and why that mattered.

The two names for the operation say something in themselves. "Agatha" was the official British codename, and there is no reason to think it carried any special meaning; codenames of the period were typically assigned without symbolic intent. "Black Sabbath" was the name given by the community whose settlements and institutions were searched, and it records how the event was experienced and remembered. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha]

The raid's most important consequence followed quickly and along a traceable line. The Irgun believed the seized documents were being processed at the King David Hotel in Jerusalem. The hotel's southern wing housed the Mandate Secretariat, the central administrative offices of the British government in Palestine, and served as regional British military headquarters, while hotel business continued in the rest of the building. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] In Irgun planning, the building was therefore both a symbol of British authority and the physical location of evidence against the Jewish Agency's leadership. Irgun intelligence assessed that the Agatha documents were being held in the Secretariat wing specifically. The documented objective of what followed was to destroy that evidence and to strike the administrative apparatus that had just carried out the mass raid and arrests. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

Irgun planning for the attack began in direct response to Black Sabbath. The bombing took place on July 22, 1946, twenty-three days after the raid. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The chain ran from raid to seizure, from seizure to an intelligence assessment, from that assessment to the choice of target, and from there to the attack.

Tracing that chain is not the same as excusing what came at the end of it. Ninety-one people died in the bombing, many of them with no connection to the administrative functions being targeted. Nor was retaliation for Black Sabbath necessarily the only motive; the planning and stated objectives of the attack are examined more fully in the next section. Explaining why an act happened and judging it are separate tasks, and this section attempts only the first.
