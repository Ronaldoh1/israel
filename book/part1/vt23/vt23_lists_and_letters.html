Lists and Letters: How the Books Were Sorted, c.180 to 1546

Part Fourteen set out the milestones of the Christian canon and corrected the claim that the Council of Nicaea chose the Bible. [scene:vt14_canon|Who Chose the Bible] This section goes one layer down, into the documents themselves: who wrote each list, where, for what audience, and in what words. The pattern that emerges is that most of the sorting was done in letters, sermons and handbooks written by individual bishops and scholars in Lyon, Alexandria, Caesarea, Carthage and Bethlehem, and only at the end by councils. The see of Rome was one voice among several for most of this period, and at one point was itself the doubter. The first binding definition of the full list by the Roman Church came in 1546. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon]

Why four gospels: Irenaeus, c.180

The earliest surviving argument that there must be exactly four gospels comes from Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, in his work Against Heresies, written around 180. He wrote that "it is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are", and supported the number by pointing to "four zones of the world" and "four principal winds", and to the four-faced cherubim. [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] The argument is theological, not historical: it does not say how the four had been chosen, only that four was fitting. In the same passage Irenaeus rejected a book that the followers of Valentinus called "the Gospel of Truth", which he described as "comparatively recent" and "totally unlike" the gospels handed down from the apostles. [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] A Coptic text known by that title was among the books found at Nag Hammadi in 1945. [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi] [scene:vt25_sayings_gospels|The Nag Hammadi gospels]

The Muratorian fragment

The oldest surviving list of New Testament books is a damaged Latin text named after the scholar Muratori, who discovered it. It survives in a single copy, in poor Latin, and was probably drawn up in Greek. Most scholars date the list to about 170 to 200, partly because it calls the Shepherd of Hermas recent; a minority date it to the fourth century. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat] Its contents show how open the edges still were. It accepts the Wisdom of Solomon, a book now in the Old Testament of Catholic and Orthodox Bibles, which it says was "written by the friends of Solomon in his honour". It accepts two revelations, John's and Peter's, adding that "some of us are not willing that the latter be read in church". It says the Shepherd was written "very recently, in our times" and "ought indeed to be read", but not publicly in church. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger]

The fragment also names what it rejects. Letters to the Laodiceans and the Alexandrians, "forged in Paul's name" to support the teaching of Marcion, are refused because "it is not fitting that gall be mixed with honey". The list ends by accepting "nothing whatever" of Arsinous, Valentinus or Miltiades, and rejecting Basilides. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger] Hebrews, James and the letters of Peter do not appear in the surviving text, though the beginning of the page is lost, so their absence is not proof of rejection. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger]

Origen and Eusebius: the scholars' shelves

Origen of Alexandria, the most prolific Christian scholar of the third century, called the four gospels "the only indisputable ones in the Church of God under heaven". He counted the Old Testament books "as the Hebrews have handed them down" at twenty-two, and openly recorded doubts: 2 Peter was "doubtful", not everyone accepted 2 and 3 John, and as for who wrote Hebrews, "God knows". [cite:vt23_na_eus_he6] Commenting on the opening of Luke, he contrasted the Church's four gospels with the several held by "heresy", naming gospels of the Egyptians, of the Twelve, of Basilides, of Thomas and of Matthias. [cite:vt23_bt_origen_luke]

A story preserved by Eusebius shows how local the decisions were. Serapion, bishop of Antioch around 200, first told the congregation at Rhossus in Syria that if a Gospel of Peter was the only thing dividing them, "let it be read". After reading the book himself and finding additions he judged unorthodox, he wrote against it as falsely ascribed to Peter. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he6] One bishop, one town and one book: this is the scale at which much of the early sorting happened.

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine and the first historian of the Church, wrote around 325 the most systematic account. In Book III of his Church History he sorted the books into four shelves. Recognised: the four gospels, Acts, the letters of Paul, 1 John, 1 Peter and, "if it seem proper", Revelation. Disputed but "recognized by many": James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2 and 3 John. Spurious: the Acts of Paul, the Shepherd, the Apocalypse of Peter, the Letter of Barnabas, the Teachings of the Apostles and the Gospel according to the Hebrews. And a fourth group "put forward by heretics": the Gospels of Peter, Thomas and Matthias and the Acts of Andrew and John, which he said should be "cast aside as absurd and impious". [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] Elsewhere he called Paul's fourteen letters "well known and undisputed", while noting that some rejected Hebrews, "saying that it is disputed by the church of Rome". [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] In the early fourth century, then, Rome was the church holding back on one of the books now in every Bible.

Athanasius, 367

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, used his Easter letter of 367 to give his churches a list. It counts twenty-two Old Testament books, including Baruch and "the epistle" with Jeremiah but not Esther, then the twenty-seven books of today's New Testament. "These are fountains of salvation", he wrote. A second group, Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Teaching of the Apostles and the Shepherd, was not in the canon but appointed to be read by new converts. Books called apocryphal he dismissed as "an invention of heretics". [cite:vt23_na_festal39] The letter warns; it orders nothing burned, as Part Fourteen showed. [scene:vt14_canon|Part Fourteen on Athanasius]

Africa writes to Rome

The Council of Hippo in 393 is the first council known to have accepted the present New Testament list, and councils at Carthage repeated it in 397 and 419. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] The canon as preserved in the African code of 419 is headed "That nothing be read in church besides the Canonical Scripture", which shows what a canon was for: a rule about public reading in worship. Its Old Testament includes Tobit, Judith, two books of Maccabees and "five books of Solomon", and a separate canon allows the stories of martyrs to be read on their feast days. The bishops ordered the list sent to "our brother and fellow bishop, Boniface", the bishop of Rome, and to other bishops, so that they might confirm it. [cite:vt23_na_carthage] Rome was asked to agree with Africa, not to decide for it. The claim that a council at Rome under Pope Damasus had already issued the list in 382 rests on the Gelasian Decree, which Ernst von Dobschütz judged a pseudonymous compilation of 519 to 553; the attribution remains disputed. [cite:vt23_wiki_gelasian] [cite:vt14_wiki_rome382]

Jerome's doubts

Jerome, the scholar who made the Latin translation later called the Vulgate, worked from the Hebrew where he could, and the Hebrew Bible did not contain several books that Greek-speaking Christians read. In the preface known as the Prologus Galeatus (391–392) he put Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the Shepherd outside the canon, "among the apocrypha". [cite:vt23_wiki_galeatus] In his preface to the books of Solomon he wrote that the Church reads Judith, Tobit and Maccabees "but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures", and reads Wisdom and Sirach "for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines". [cite:vt23_ccel_jerome_solomon] He translated Judith anyway, and in its preface gave a reason that touches this book's earlier parts: the Council of Nicaea, he said, "is read" to have counted Judith among the scriptures. [cite:vt23_gallagher_htr2015] No canon of Nicaea records any such decision; it is a report, not a document. [cite:vt23_owiki_otcanon] [scene:vt22_myths_tested|Nicaea's myths tested]

From Florence to Trent

Jerome's doubts did not disappear. Cardinal Cajetan, before the Reformation hardened positions, and Cardinal Girolamo Seripando at Trent itself, are named among Catholic churchmen who followed Jerome in setting these books apart. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero] The Council of Florence approved a full list on 4 February 1442. The Council of Trent, on 8 April 1546, made the list an article of faith under anathema, by 24 votes to 15 with 16 abstentions. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] [cite:vt14_trent_s4] Twenty years later, in 1566, the theologian Sixtus of Siena coined the word "deuterocanonical", meaning of second-stage recognition, for the books whose standing had been argued over. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero]

The record therefore shows a long sorting carried out by named people in many cities, with the centre of the list stable early (four gospels and Paul's letters) and the edges argued over for centuries. Which books fell off those edges, and who pushed them, is the subject of the next section. [scene:vt23_left_out|What was left out]
