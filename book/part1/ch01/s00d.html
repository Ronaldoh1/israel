Two Symbols and a Flag

Most people who see the flag of Israel take its six-pointed star to be the ancient sign of the Jewish people, carried onto the flag as it has always been. The documents tell a more layered story. Two symbols have a claim to represent Jewish tradition, and they are of very different ages. The older one, the seven-branched lampstand called the menorah, reaches back to the Temple. The younger one, the six-pointed star called the Magen David, or Shield of David, became a distinctively Jewish emblem in the Middle Ages. The flag carries the younger. That choice was made by a political congress in 1897 and confirmed by the new state in 1948, and both decisions are on the record.

The menorah is the older of the two by a wide margin. Exodus 25:31–40 describes it as the lampstand of the Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary of the wilderness narrative; it later stood in the Temple in Jerusalem. [cite:exodus_25_bible] It is the oldest continuous Jewish symbol. [cite:wiki_menorah] Its oldest visual record is in stone, in Rome: the Arch of Titus, completed in 81 CE, shows the lampstand carried off in the sack of the Second Temple. [cite:wiki_arch_titus] The image that survives, in other words, is a Roman one, carved to record the sack of the Temple that had housed the lampstand.

The six-pointed star has a different history. As a shape it is old and widespread, appearing in Hindu and Mayan settings and in decorative art across many centuries, in contexts that have nothing to do with Judaism. [cite:unpacked_star_of_david_history] As a distinctively Jewish symbol it is medieval, not ancient, and it is absent from the Jewish iconography of antiquity. [cite:wiki_star_david] Its first documented official Jewish use comes from fourteenth-century Prague. The sources describe that use in two ways: as the seal of Prague's Jewish community, and as a flag. In 1354, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles IV granted the Jewish community of Prague the right to bear its own flag, red with a yellow Star of David. [cite:wiki_star_david]

Even then the star was one emblem among several, not the emblem. It carried Kabbalistic associations in some traditions, but it did not become widely recognized as the Jewish symbol until the modern period. The spread came between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, driven by European Jewish communities that wanted a graphic emblem to stand alongside the Christian cross and the Islamic crescent. [cite:unpacked_star_of_david_history] The standard scholarly account is Gershom Scholem's 1949 monograph, Magen David: The History of a Symbol, which documents the specifically modern, and specifically Zionist, role in fixing the hexagram as the Jewish symbol. [cite:scholem_star_david] On that account, a Jew asked before the seventeenth century to name the symbol of the Jewish people would have pointed to the menorah, not the star. By the late nineteenth century the star was spreading across Ashkenazi Europe as a practical emblem, a modern graphic answer to a modern need rather than a recovered ancient sign. Theodor Herzl himself, according to several historians, called it around this time a "meaningless symbol of Judaism."

The flag itself began before the state, and before the Zionist Congress. In 1885 David Wolffsohn created a blue-and-white banner with a central Magen David for the Bnei Zion association in Boston, taking its colors from the tallit, the Jewish prayer shawl. When the First Zionist Congress met in Basel in August 1897, Wolffsohn proposed that this design serve as the movement's banner. [cite:basel_program] [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] It was not the only design on offer. Herzl's own Der Judenstaat, published the year before, had proposed a white flag with seven gold stars, standing for a seven-hour working day. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Herzl's idea never gained traction. What flew in the Congress hall was the Wolffsohn design: two horizontal blue stripes on white, with a blue Magen David between them. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] Wolffsohn later succeeded Herzl as president of the World Zionist Organization.

Why the star, and not the older lampstand? The sources give two explanations, and they are not quite the same. Scholem argued that the star was chosen precisely because it did not carry the heavy religious weight of the menorah. It was "available" for a secular political project: the movement needed a Jewish symbol, but one that did not tie its politics to rabbinic authority, and the star fit where the Temple's lampstand would not have. [cite:scholem_star_david] The more cautious reading starts from the movement's make-up. By the 1890s the Magen David had become the closest thing to a widely recognized, non-sectarian Jewish emblem, and the Zionist movement drew members from many religious and secular backgrounds. Unlike ritual objects tied closely to synagogue practice, the star worked as a legible, adaptable political sign. On this reading, practical legibility, more than any documented theological reasoning, is what the record actually supports as the reason for its selection. Both readings agree on the outcome: a symbol with modern movement associations, rather than one anchored to the Temple cult, became the movement's flag.

The state was declared on 14 May 1948. [cite:declaration_israel_1948] In the weeks that followed, the Provisional Council of State had to adopt a flag as a matter of administrative necessity. The Basel banner, by then fifty-one years old and already the recognized symbol of the Zionist movement worldwide, was the obvious default. The flag was formally confirmed on 28 October 1948. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel]

That outcome was not reached without argument, and the argument is the most revealing part of the record. Before simply taking over the movement's flag, the Provisional Council tried to create a distinct state flag. Its stated reason was a concern about Jews abroad: Diaspora communities already flew the Zionist flag at their own events, and officials worried that once the same banner became a foreign state's official flag, those communities would face accusations of dual loyalty. A government-preferred alternative, a seven-stars design by Otto Wassisch, was considered and rejected. In July 1948 the Foreign Minister, Moshe Sharett, cabled Jewish leaders across the Diaspora to ask their view. The answer came back overwhelmingly in favor of keeping the existing Zionist flag rather than adopting a new one. [cite:crwflags_israel_flag_1948] [cite:wjc_flag_unveiled_1948] So the flag was the low-friction choice in one sense, because it required no new design. In another sense it was a considered one: the objection was raised by the decision-makers themselves, an alternative was tested, and the people the objection was meant to protect were asked, and chose continuity.

The menorah did not disappear. It became the state's emblem, placed on the state seal by the Provisional Council of State in 1949, a year after the flag decision. [cite:wiki_menorah] That process was longer and more contested than the flag's. The selection committee rejected outright a first round of 450 proposals from 164 applicants. A second public competition followed. The winning design, by the Shamir brothers, was sent back for revision twice: once to add the word "Israel," and once to replace their own modern rendering of the menorah with the specific historical version carved on the Arch of Titus. [cite:nli_emblem_israel_blog] The emblem, in other words, was made to copy the lampstand as Rome had recorded it in 81 CE.

Nearly 1,300 years separate the menorah's oldest surviving image and the star's first documented Jewish use, and the newer symbol took the flag. None of this makes either symbol less real. Both are genuine Jewish symbols with genuine histories, and a symbol can become deeply meaningful regardless of its age. Nor was anything hidden: the Zionist movement never concealed that the state flag was its own 1897 banner, and the flag's history is public and uncontested. What the record settles is narrower. The common claim that the star is the ancient symbol, on the flag as it always was, overstates its age. The flag's lineage runs through a nineteenth-century political movement, not through the Temple period.

An interpretation, not a finding. What follows is a reading of the documented sequence above, and it should be weighed as one. One reading holds that the choice encodes the state's sense of its own origin. A state that understood itself as continuing a religious tradition might have led with that tradition's oldest symbol. This one put the political movement's 1897 emblem on its flag, the face it shows the world, and placed the Temple's lampstand on its seal, the stamp of administration. On this reading the flag signals that the state understands itself to continue the political project of Herzl and the Basel Congress, rather than the ancient Temple cult, which is consistent with a founding that was political, secular and modern. [cite:basel_program] An analogy sometimes used is a company with a founding logo and a later campaign design: the newer design can be entirely legitimate, but calling it "the company's timeless symbol" would be inaccurate.

The same reading goes a step further. Displayed in synagogues, schools and community centers, a flag that joins the movement's emblem to Jewish religious life may reinforce, through repetition rather than argument, the idea that the political project of 1897 and a religious tradition of millennia are one thing. That is the distinction between Zionism and Judaism examined in [scene:s00c|the next chapter's section on the movement and the religion], made here through an image instead of a claim. It is a plausible mechanism, not a measured effect. Most people who display the flag do so out of a real connection to Israel or to Jewish identity, not as part of any messaging effort, and this reading assumes nothing about any individual's intent. Many Israelis and Jews abroad experience the flag as holding both the political founding and the older tradition at once, which is an equally defensible way to read it. And the 1948 record itself cuts both ways: the very worry about conflation that this reading describes was raised by the state's own founders, who tested an alternative, consulted the communities most affected, and kept the movement's flag at their request.

What the documents do not settle is motive. They show which symbol is older, which one the movement chose, and the dated sequence by which the state confirmed it. They do not show that the choice was made to diminish the menorah or the Temple tradition. Scholem's argument about the star's secular availability and the narrower argument from practical legibility both remain on the table, and the record available here does not decide between them.
