The Gospel of Judas and the Price of a Codex

The most expensive Gnostic book in history spent sixteen years in a bank safe deposit box on Long Island. Its journey from an Egyptian cave to a television documentary is the clearest case in this book of ancient knowledge turned into a commodity, and every stage of it is documented in dollars.

A codex of papyrus leaves was found in the 1970s in a burial cave in Middle Egypt, near El Minya. [cite:vt26_wiki_tchacos] [cite:gn_nbc_judas] It contained four texts, among them a Gospel of Judas, whose title Irenaeus had mentioned around 180 when he described a group that said Judas "alone, knowing the truth as no others did, accomplished the mystery of the betrayal". [cite:vt26_wiki_tchacos] [cite:vt26_na_iren131] The codex was sold to a Cairo dealer known by the pseudonym Hanna. In 1980 it was stolen from his apartment the day after he showed it to a Swiss dealer and a woman who came with him; it was recovered in Geneva in 1982. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] Hanna's asking price was ten million dollars. He could not find a buyer, and he cut it to three million, which is what he asked when the Coptic scholar Stephen Emmel examined the codex in 1983. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas]

Unable to sell, Hanna took the codex to the United States, hidden in newspapers, and put it in a safe deposit box in a bank on Long Island, at Hicksville in New York, where it lay from about 1984 to 2000 and decayed. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] In April 2000 the Zurich dealer Frieda Tchacos Nussberger bought it for what the Associated Press called "hundreds of thousands of dollars". Yale University declined to buy it. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] The dealer Bruce Ferrini then took custody, giving post-dated cheques totalling 2.5 million dollars, and damaged the papyrus further by putting it in a freezer; when he went bankrupt the cheques proved worthless, and the codex came back to Tchacos in 2001 with pages missing. [cite:gn_nbc_judas]

The final buyer was the Maecenas Foundation for Ancient Art, which paid 1.5 million dollars for the gospel and related documents in 2001, with Tchacos providing the credit. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:vt26_wiki_tchacos] The foundation sold the rights to publish and exhibit the text to the National Geographic Society for one million dollars. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] Restorers reassembled some thousand fragments, radiocarbon dating placed the papyrus between 220 and 340, and in April 2006 National Geographic unveiled its translation. [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] The headline was a rehabilitated Judas: the disciple who alone understood Jesus and betrayed him at his request.

The headline did not survive scrutiny. In December 2007 the scholar April DeConick argued in the New York Times, and in her book The Thirteenth Apostle, that the translation had mistranslated key words: that the Greek daimon meant "demon" rather than "spirit", that Judas was set apart "from" rather than "for" the holy generation, and that a negation had been dropped, so that Judas would not ascend after all. National Geographic replied that the issues were addressed in footnotes. [cite:vt26_getrel_deconick] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] The rival readings are still debated. What the episode showed beyond dispute is that when a television network has paid for exclusive rights, the first public reading of an ancient text is shaped by a team working under a publication deadline, before other scholars can see the original. The foundation said the codex would be given to the Coptic Museum in Cairo once the profits from publication had been realised. [cite:gn_nbc_judas]

Six years later the market produced a fake. On 18 September 2012 Karen King of Harvard Divinity School announced a small papyrus fragment in Coptic in which Jesus says "my wife". Radiocarbon tests placed the papyrus around 741, and linguists showed its dialect had died out long before; in June 2016 the journalist Ariel Sabar, in The Atlantic, identified the owner as Walter Fritz, a former Egyptology student in Florida, and discredited the provenance he had supplied. King then conceded that the evidence "presses in the direction of forgery". [cite:gn_wiki_gjw] A text claiming that Jesus was married was exactly the kind of "suppressed gospel" the earlier discoveries had taught the public to expect. The forgery shows what that expectation could be worth to someone willing to fake one.
