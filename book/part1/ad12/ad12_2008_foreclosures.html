2008: How the Housing Crash Took the Middle's Main Asset

For most American families the home is the Dream's largest single asset, and in the first decade of this century it was also the asset most families borrowed most heavily against. When house prices fell nationally after 2006 and millions of mortgages failed, the loss fell hardest on the households whose wealth was mostly one house, and hardest of all on Black and Hispanic families who had been sold more expensive loans than similar white borrowers. This section follows that sequence with the record: how the loans were priced, what the Justice Department alleged and settled, how many homes were lost, how much wealth went with them, and who was rescued afterwards. [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|The wealth gap] Why the bubble formed at all is disputed, and Part One sets out the official inquiry's view and the main dissent. [scene:ad1_who_sells_it|Part One: who sells the Dream]

Pricing: when equal credit got unequal loans

A subprime loan is a mortgage made to a borrower judged a higher risk, usually at a higher interest rate and with higher fees; regulators track "high-cost" loans as those priced well above the rate on safe government bonds. Charging more to a borrower who is genuinely riskier is a designed feature of lending, known as risk-based pricing. Charging more to a borrower of the same risk because of race or national origin is a departure from that design and is illegal under federal fair-lending law. [cite:ad12_doj_countrywide] The record shows the second happened at scale. Patrick Bayer, Fernando Ferreira and Stephen Ross studied home-purchase loans in seven metropolitan areas from 2004 to 2007 using credit-bureau data. After controlling for credit scores and other risk factors, Black buyers were 105 per cent more likely and Hispanic buyers 78 per cent more likely than white buyers to have a high-cost mortgage. About 60 to 65 per cent of that difference came from which lenders borrowers ended up with, since Black and Hispanic buyers were more often served by high-risk lenders, and 35 to 40 per cent from those lenders treating equally qualified borrowers differently. [cite:ad12_bfr2018]

Two of the largest lenders settled federal cases over exactly these practices. On 21 December 2011 the Justice Department announced a $335 million settlement with Countrywide Financial, by then a subsidiary of Bank of America, its largest residential fair-lending settlement to that date. The government alleged that more than 200,000 African-American and Hispanic borrowers had paid higher fees and interest rates than non-Hispanic white borrowers between 2004 and 2008 because of their race or national origin rather than their creditworthiness, and that thousands had been placed in subprime loans when white borrowers with similar credit received prime ones. Attorney General Eric Holder said lenders should judge applicants on "creditworthiness, not on the color of their skin." [cite:ad12_doj_countrywide] On 12 July 2012 the department announced a settlement with Wells Fargo of more than $175 million, including $50 million in down-payment assistance, resolving allegations that about 4,000 African-American and Hispanic borrowers who came through mortgage brokers were steered into subprime loans between 2004 and 2008 and about 30,000 were charged higher fees and rates between 2004 and 2009. [cite:ad12_doj_wells] Both settlements resolved the government's allegations; neither release records an admission of liability. [cite:ad12_doj_countrywide] [cite:ad12_doj_wells]

The foreclosures

A foreclosure is the legal process by which a lender takes a home after the borrower stops paying. The Center for Responsible Lending, a non-profit lending-reform group, tracked loans made from 2004 to 2008 and reported in November 2011 that 2.7 million of those households had already lost their homes, with 3.6 million more at serious risk. About one in four Black and Hispanic borrowers had either been foreclosed on or was seriously behind on payments, against about 12 per cent of white borrowers. The gap did not close with income: among higher-income borrowers, 10 per cent of Black and 15 per cent of Hispanic borrowers had lost their homes, against 4.6 per cent of white borrowers. [cite:ad12_crl_lostground] Because loan pricing itself differed by race at equal credit, higher foreclosure rates among Black and Hispanic borrowers cannot be read as a simple measure of their risk; part of the risk was in the loans they were given. [cite:ad12_bfr2018]

Geography added to the loss. The Pew Research Center found that Hispanic families were hit hardest partly because many lived in California, Florida, Nevada and Arizona, where house prices fell furthest. [cite:ad12_pew2011] What happened to the foreclosed homes afterwards, including bulk sales to investors and resales on contracts for deed in mostly Black neighbourhoods, is covered in Part Six. [scene:ad6_eviction_and_traps|Part Six: eviction and housing traps]

The wealth that went with them

Because home equity was a larger share of Black and Hispanic wealth, the same fall in house prices took a larger share of it. On the Census Bureau's Survey of Income and Program Participation, Pew found that median household net worth between 2005 and 2009, in 2009 dollars, fell 16 per cent for white households (from $134,992 to $113,149), 53 per cent for Black households (from $12,124 to $5,677) and 66 per cent for Hispanic households (from $18,359 to $6,325). The share of households with zero or negative net worth rose to 35 per cent for Black and 31 per cent for Hispanic households, against 15 per cent for white households. [cite:ad12_pew2011] On the Federal Reserve's survey, median wealth fell by about the same proportion for each group between 2007 and 2010, roughly 27 per cent, but the recovery split: from 2010 to 2013, the white median rose 1.4 per cent while the Black median fell a further 23 per cent and the Hispanic median 21 per cent. [cite:ad12_fed_2019race]

The Distributional Financial Accounts show the scale for the bottom half of all households, whatever their race. Their combined net worth was $1.45 trillion in the third quarter of 2005 and $331 billion in the third quarter of 2010, in current dollars, a fall of 77 per cent ($331 billion ÷ $1,453 billion = 0.23). [cite:ad12_fred_b50nw] Their share of all household net worth fell to 0.4 per cent at the end of 2010 and stayed there into 2012. [cite:ad12_ad_bottom50] For half the country, a decade of home equity had been almost entirely borrowed against or lost.

Who was rescued, and what it cost

The federal response to the crisis is often summarised as a rescue of banks and not of homeowners. The record supports part of that claim and corrects another part. The Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), enacted in October 2008 and run under two administrations of different parties, was authorised at $700 billion and disbursed $444 billion, about $410 billion of it by March 2011. In its final report, issued in April 2024, the Congressional Budget Office estimated the program's total cost at $31 billion. Transactions with financial institutions other than AIG yielded a net gain to the government through dividends, interest and capital gains; the cost came largely from aid to AIG, aid to the car industry, and foreclosure-prevention programs, which were grants to help homeowners rather than investments to be repaid. The last remaining investment was repaid in September 2023. [cite:ad12_cbo_tarp]

The asymmetry the record does show is one of timing and form. Financial institutions received capital quickly and repaid it once markets recovered, so the public was made whole on that part of the rescue. Homeowners received grants and modification programs that came later and reached fewer families than were losing homes, and a family that lost its house in 2009 did not get its equity back when prices recovered. [cite:ad12_cbo_tarp] [cite:ad12_crl_lostground] Comparisons that set the peak value of all emergency lending commitments against the money spent on homeowners mix different measures and overstate the gap; the documented contrast is between capital that was returned with interest and household equity that was not.

Where it works as promised, and the dispute over cause

The recovery did come for many. The bottom half's combined net worth rose from $331 billion in 2010 to $1.81 trillion in 2019 and $4.28 trillion in the second quarter of 2026. [cite:ad12_fred_b50nw] Between 2016 and 2019 the Black median rose 32.6 per cent and the Hispanic median 64.9 per cent. [cite:ad12_fed_2019race] The rescue of the financial system, judged on its own terms of repayment, worked. [cite:ad12_cbo_tarp] How the bubble was caused remains contested. The Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission's majority concluded that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac "contributed to the crisis but were not a primary cause" and that government housing policies "were not a significant factor." [cite:ad1_fcic_angelides] A dissenting commissioner, Peter Wallison of the American Enterprise Institute, argued that government housing policy was "the sine qua non of the financial crisis." [cite:ad1_hw_wallison] The pricing findings above stand under either account: whatever drove the volume of lending, the documented settlements and studies concern how the loans were priced for borrowers of equal credit. [cite:ad12_bfr2018] [cite:ad12_doj_countrywide]
