The Map Makers

Every ten years, after the census, each state with more than one House seat redraws its congressional districts. In most states the legislature draws the lines, which means the politicians who will run in the districts help to choose the voters who will judge them. Drawing the lines to favour one party is called partisan gerrymandering. This section traces the practice from its name to the Supreme Court's 2019 decision that federal courts will not police it, and then maps the round of redrawing in 2025 and 2026 that changed congressional maps in the middle of a decade, on both sides.

An old practice with a founder's name

In 1812 Elbridge Gerry, then governor of Massachusetts, signed a bill that created a sprawling state senate district shaped to help his Democratic-Republican Party against the Federalists. On 26 March 1812 the Boston Gazette printed a cartoon of the district as a winged, clawed monster, and its label joined Gerry's name to "salamander". [cite:dt3_npr_gerry] The word has outlived the dispute. The technique it names is simple: pack the other party's voters into a few districts it wins by huge margins, and crack the rest across districts where they fall just short, so that a party can win a majority of seats with a minority of votes, or a large majority of seats with a narrow majority of votes.

Rucho: the federal courts step back

On 27 June 2019 the Supreme Court decided Rucho v. Common Cause by five votes to four. Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Kavanaugh, held that claims of partisan gerrymandering present political questions that federal courts cannot decide, because those courts are "not equipped to apportion political power as a matter of fairness". The opinion noted that the Constitution gave districting to state legislatures, "expressly checked and balanced by the Federal Congress". Justice Kagan, for the four dissenters, wrote that the maps before the Court "debased and dishonored our democracy". [cite:dt3_bp_rucho]

Two maps were before the Court, one drawn by each party. North Carolina's 2016 congressional map, the district court had found, was drawn by Republican legislators to subordinate voters who were not Republicans. [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] The companion case, Lamone v. Benisek, concerned Maryland's 2011 map, drawn by a Democratic-led state government. Its 6th District was redrawn to remove about 70,000 registered Republicans, and the Republican who had held the seat for two decades lost in 2012 by more than 20 points. The Court disposed of both cases the same way. [cite:dt3_wiki_benisek] The same rule now applies to both parties: federal judges will not strike down a map for being too partisan.

Rucho closed the federal courthouse; it did not close every door. Four years later, on 27 June 2023, the Court decided Moore v. Harper by six votes to three. North Carolina's Republican legislators had argued that state courts had no power to review the legislature's rules for federal elections, an idea known as the independent state legislature theory. The Court rejected it: a legislature created by a state constitution remains bound by that constitution, including review by the state's own courts. [cite:dt3_ts_moore] [cite:dt3_brit_moore] After the two decisions, the routes against a partisan map are a state constitution enforced by state courts, a state ballot measure that hands map-drawing to a commission, or an act of Congress. Those routes, and the evidence on independent commissions, are weighed in [scene:dt7_structure_fixes|Part Seven].

The mid-decade war, 2025–2026

Maps are normally redrawn once a decade. In August 2025 Texas broke that pattern. Its legislature passed a new map, signed on 29 August, that Ballotpedia projects will gain Republicans five seats; the Supreme Court allowed it to be used for 2026 on 4 December 2025. California answered through its voters, who on 4 November 2025 approved Proposition 50, a legislature-drawn map projected to gain Democrats five seats. Other states followed. As of Ballotpedia's update of 28 September 2026, new maps in nine states are in effect for the 2026 election. Republican-led legislatures drew new maps in North Carolina, Florida, Tennessee and Louisiana, and Alabama returned to a 2023 legislative map after the Supreme Court lifted a block on it; Ohio's map came from the redistricting commission its constitution requires; Utah's came from a judge, who chose a map submitted by plaintiffs. Ballotpedia projects a net gain of about nine seats for Republicans across the nine maps. [cite:dt3_bp_redistrict2026]

Three attempts failed. Missouri's legislature passed a map projected to gain Republicans one seat, but it is blocked for 2026 pending a referendum petition, and the 2022 map is in use. Virginia's voters approved a constitutional amendment on 21 April 2026 to allow a map aimed at ten Democratic seats and one Republican, up from six and five, but the Virginia Supreme Court voided the effort on 8 May 2026 and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to intervene. In Indiana, the House passed a new map and the state Senate rejected it on 11 December 2025. [cite:dt3_bp_redistrict2026] Both parties redrew where they held power, and both were stopped in some places: by voters and courts in Missouri and Virginia, and by members of the majority party in Indiana.

The seat projections are estimates, not results; the 3 November 2026 election will show what the maps actually produced. What can be measured now is the shape of the field. Cook's 2026 index puts the median House seat at R+3, two points more Republican than before the redrawing, and calls the new map "less elastic". [cite:dt3_cook_pvi2026] Whether that skew is read as a correction or a distortion depends on which earlier maps one takes as the baseline, and that is a judgment, not a measurement. The design fact does not depend on that judgment: in most states, those who hold the legislature choose the districts, and no federal court will review the choice for partisanship. [cite:dt3_bp_rucho]

The test

Peter Thiel wrote in 2009 that he had "little hope that voting will make things better"; Curtis Yarvin told the New York Times in January 2025, "I don't believe in actually voting at all". [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] [cite:de1_audit_nyt_voting] This part's facts bear on the claim beneath those lines, that the vote is too weak to govern. Documented: under the rules now in force, a general-election vote for the House is weak in most districts. About 16 seats were rated toss-ups in May 2026, most seats are settled in primaries attended by about one eligible voter in five, and the lines are drawn by the incumbents' own parties without federal judicial review. [cite:dt3_pw_tossups] [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] Not supported: that this weakness belongs to democracy as such. Each mechanism here, the plurality rule, sore loser laws, closed primaries and legislative map-drawing, is a specific American rule that other democracies do without, and each can be changed by a state court, a ballot measure or Congress. [cite:dt3_wiki_duverger] [cite:dt3_ts_moore]

The record also cuts against this book's own thesis. Most of the loss of competitive seats comes from voters sorting themselves by party, which no rule about maps or primaries can reverse. [cite:dt3_cook_decimated] Primary turnout stays low even where anyone may vote. [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] And when Californians and Virginians were asked directly, majorities approved partisan redraws for their own side. [cite:dt3_bp_redistrict2026] Probable, on this part's evidence: changing the rules would widen voters' choices and slow the map war, but it would not by itself produce the competitive, broadly attended elections the reformers' case assumes.
