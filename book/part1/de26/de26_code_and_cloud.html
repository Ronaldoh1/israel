Code and Cloud: Who Holds the Servers

Almost none of the tools in this book run on computers their users own. They run in "the cloud," which means rented space in data centres operated by a handful of companies; a government agency that uses a cloud service is renting someone else's machines, storage and network. Three layers sit on top of one another, and keeping them apart prevents a common error. The cloud host owns the buildings and machines. A software platform, such as Palantir's, runs on those machines and organises the customer's data. The customer, an agency, decides what goes in and who may look at it. Hosting is not access: a company that rents out the machines does not thereby read what is stored on them, any more than a landlord reads his tenants' letters, and the contracts and law that govern each layer are different. [cite:de26_reg_oracle] [cite:de26_ng_jwcc] A fourth layer sits beside them: the code platforms and app stores where software itself is written, stored and distributed. This section takes each in turn.

The government cloud: a few firms

Most American defence and intelligence computing now runs on four or five companies. In November 2020 the Central Intelligence Agency awarded its Commercial Cloud Enterprise contract, reported as "worth tens of billions of dollars" over about fifteen years, to Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, Google, Oracle and IBM, for the CIA and sixteen other intelligence agencies; Amazon had been the agency's cloud since 2013. [cite:de26_dcd_c2e] On 7 December 2022 the Pentagon awarded its Joint Warfighting Cloud Capability contract, with a $9 billion ceiling, to Amazon, Google, Microsoft and Oracle, for services "across all security domains and classification levels." It replaced JEDI, a single-vendor plan worth up to $10 billion that had been tied up in litigation and was cancelled in 2021. [cite:de26_ng_jwcc] The first award was made under the first Trump administration and the second under Biden; the move from one vendor to several was a deliberate choice to avoid dependence on a single company.

Before an agency may use a commercial cloud service it needs an authorisation under FedRAMP, the federal security-review programme run by the General Services Administration. In March 2025 GSA announced "FedRAMP 20x," which replaces annual paper assessments with automated, near-real-time security indicators; in its first phase, services were authorised without an agency sponsor, and as of October 2026 the programme is in its third phase, with a pilot for the highest-sensitivity tier planned for 2027. [cite:de26_fedramp_20x] Faster authorisation is the stated purpose; whether automated checks catch what manual reviews did is not yet shown either way.

The concentration cuts both ways. In September 2025 Microsoft announced that it would "cease and disable specified" subscriptions of Israel's Ministry of Defence after reporting that intercepted Palestinian phone calls were stored on its Azure cloud, stating that it does not provide technology to facilitate mass surveillance of civilians; the episode is set out in [scene:ow9_israel|The One-Way Mirror]. [cite:ow4_tc_msft] One provider's decision changed what a government's intelligence service could do. The same lever, held by a different company or pressed by a different government, could work the other way.

Oracle and Palantir: hosting, not access

In April 2024 Oracle and Palantir announced a partnership under which Palantir would move workloads for its Foundry platform to Oracle's cloud and make its Gotham and AI platforms deployable across Oracle's infrastructure, including "air-gapped regions for defense and intelligence customers," meaning data centres physically cut off from the public internet. Even so, the reporting noted, it was "up to the customer which cloud to deploy on," and some Palantir systems, such as its contract with England's health service, run on Amazon and Microsoft. [cite:de26_reg_oracle] The partnership puts Palantir's software on Oracle's machines. It does not give Oracle the data its government customers load into Palantir, and no document shows that it does; Palantir's own architecture and its claim that customers control their data are tested in [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Nine].

Drawn as a stack, the layers show where each kind of power sits: the agency decides what goes in, the platform organises it, an authorisation gate decides which services may hold it, and the host owns the machines.

The code platforms

GitHub, owned by Microsoft, is where most of the world's open-source software and much private code is stored. In 2019 its employees learned that ICE held a licence for GitHub's product, worth about $200,000, which was up for renewal. The chief executive, Nat Friedman, wrote to staff that GitHub and Microsoft opposed policies such as family separation but that the company "can't be held responsible" for how customers use its products; it renewed the licence and donated $500,000 to immigration nonprofits, and at least five employees quit. [cite:de26_bhr_github] What became of the licence after 2019 is not on the public record. GitHub also publishes the requests governments send it to remove content. It reported 98 such requests for all of 2025 and 708 for the first half of 2026, and said the rise was "largely" a change in how it counts, now including duplicates and every type of request, "not a corresponding increase in content removals." [cite:de26_gh_transparency]

The app store as a gate

For software meant to reach ordinary people, the gate is the phone's app store. ICEBlock was an iPhone app that let users report, anonymously, where they had seen immigration agents. On 2 October 2025 Apple removed it and similar apps after the Justice Department asked; Attorney General Pam Bondi said, "ICEBlock is designed to put ICE agents at risk just for doing their jobs," and Apple said it acted on information from law enforcement about safety risks. [cite:de26_audacy_iceblock] The app's developer, Joshua Aaron, who said it had more than a million users, sued Bondi, Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem and other officials in December 2025, alleging that pressuring Apple and threatening him violated the First Amendment. [cite:de26_abc_iceblock_suit] In a parallel case brought for the creators of a Chicago Facebook group and an app called Eyes Up, Judge Jorge Alonso of the Northern District of Illinois found in April 2026 that their injuries were "likely traceable to government-coerced enforcement," calling the officials' statements "intimations of a threat," and preliminarily barred the Justice and Homeland Security departments from pressing Apple and Facebook to keep the plaintiffs off. [cite:de26_reason_rosado] The government's position is that the apps endanger officers; the plaintiffs' is that reporting what agents do in public is protected speech. The pressure on a private platform from officials, known as "jawboning," is the same legal question raised about the previous administration's dealings with social-media companies, which reached the Supreme Court in Murthy v. Missouri in 2024 without a ruling on the merits ([scene:de11_twitter_x_xai|Part Eleven]). [cite:de11_scotus_murthy]

What this layer means for the worst case

The record shows a small number of companies holding the machines on which the state's data and software run, and a small number holding the gates through which software reaches the public. It shows one provider cutting off a foreign defence ministry, and one government persuading a platform to remove an app aimed at its own agents, a removal a federal judge has since found likely coerced. Concentration makes these firms points of leverage for whoever holds power, and the documented examples run in both directions. What the record does not show is any cloud host reading an agency's data, or a code platform handing over users' code to immigration enforcement; the worst-case extension, a few firms refusing service to the government's critics at its request, has one documented instance at the app-store layer, and it is in court. [cite:ow4_tc_msft] [cite:de26_reason_rosado]

If it were connected

The cloud is the floor every other layer stands on, so the join that matters here is access to what is stored. Not supported: a cloud host reading or passing on its government customers' data; the record shows hosting contracts, Oracle's partnership with Palantir leaves the data to the customer, and Microsoft said its own review of Israel's use did not access the customer's content. [cite:de26_reg_oracle] [cite:ow4_tc_msft] Documented, once: ordinary office software carrying a government dataset to a vendor, when ICE passed a Medicaid file to Palantir over a Microsoft Teams chat in January 2026, outside a court's limits; the channel was a chat window, not a designed interface. [cite:de27_npr_medicaid] Documented as well: officials asking an app store to remove software aimed at their agents, and the store complying. [cite:de26_audacy_iceblock] Possible, with no document: the same lever used to deny a named critic a service, or a code platform's records on a user reaching ICE through the kind of administrative subpoena DHS has served on Google and Meta ([scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Thirty]). The first would need only a request and a willing company, the second only a subpoena; what stands in the way is a company's refusal, notice to the user, and, since April 2026 in Chicago, an injunction against government pressure on platforms. [cite:de28_eff_letter] [cite:de26_reason_rosado]
