The Isaiah Scroll and the Text of the Bible

The most famous of the scrolls is the Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy of the book of Isaiah, one of the four the archbishop sold. It is about 7.34 metres long, holds 54 columns of text and dates from around 125 before Christ; radiocarbon tests give a range of 356 to 103 before Christ. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] It is "approximately 1000 years older" than the oldest Hebrew manuscripts of Isaiah known before its discovery. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] It is kept today in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah]

What it showed about the text was reassuring and surprising at once. It contains all sixty-six chapters of Isaiah in the same order, and its text is generally consistent with the Masoretic text, the standard Hebrew text on which most Bibles are based. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] It also contains "more than 2600 textual variants". Most are small, such as differences of spelling, single words and word order, though some are significant, and other Isaiah copies from the caves vary much less. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] A thousand years of copying had changed the words of Isaiah in detail but not in substance.

Some of the significant variants touch theology. In Deuteronomy 32:8, the standard Hebrew text says that God divided the nations "according to the number of the sons of Israel". A copy of Deuteronomy from the caves reads "sons of God" instead, as the ancient Greek translation also does. [cite:ds_logos_deut328] The difference matters. "Sons of God" places the nations under heavenly beings, the divine council that Enoch's Watchers belong to; "sons of Israel" removes them. The English Standard Version now prints "sons of God" in its text, and most other translations note it in a footnote. [cite:ds_logos_deut328] An older reading, preserved in a cave, has entered modern Bibles because of the scrolls.

The lesson for this shelf is precise, and it cuts against two opposite claims. The scrolls do not show that the Bible was rewritten beyond recognition; Isaiah is still Isaiah. Nor do they show that the text never changed; scribes altered spellings, words and, in a few places, meanings, and some of those changes, like the one in Deuteronomy, have theological weight. [cite:ds_wiki_isaiah] [cite:ds_logos_deut328] Like the canon itself, the text had a history before it was fixed, and the caves preserve a moment in that history.
