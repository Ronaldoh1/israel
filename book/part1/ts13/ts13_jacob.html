Jacob's Night: The Documented Inner Readings

Reading the story of Jacob as a story about the soul is not a modern idea, and it is not confined to esoteric writers. It is one of the oldest ways the passage has been read, and the readers who developed it were among the central figures of Judaism and Christianity. This section sets out what four of those traditions documentably said: the Alexandrian Jewish philosopher Philo, the Christian theologian Origen, the Kabbalah, and modern rabbinic commentary. It then turns to a later Christian image, the "dark night of the soul", which some readers have brought to the story. Each reading is reported as its author wrote it. None is presented as what Genesis "really" means; the point is that the inward reading has a long and named history.

Philo: Israel as "the one who sees God"

Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher writing in Greek in the first century CE, read the books of Moses as an account of the soul's progress as well as a history. In his treatise On the Change of Names he explains the renaming directly: Jacob means "a supplanter", while Israel signifies "the man who sees God". The one who practises virtue, he writes, strives "acutely and clearly to behold the living God". [cite:ts13_philo_mut] The etymology is Philo's own; it does not match the Hebrew roots set out in the previous section, and it is best read as a teaching device, an interpretation dressed as a derivation.

The classicist Marta Alesso, in a study of every use of the name in Philo, calls this etymology new to biblical interpretation and notes that the Church Fathers later repeated it. In Philo, Israel is "the one who sees God", "the seeing race", or simply "the seer"; his praise of sight carries echoes of Plato. Alesso also shows that Philo widened the term: Israel was for him a nation (ethnos) and also a kind (genos), a philosophical category of those who see. [cite:ts13_alesso_philo] Two things are documented together here and should be kept together. Philo read Israel as a condition of the soul, and Philo also remained a Jew who wrote of Israel as a people bound by God's law. The inward reading was for him an added level, not a replacement for the people.

Origen: three senses of scripture

Origen of Alexandria, the third-century Christian theologian, inherited the allegorical method and made it a rule. In book four of On First Principles he wrote that "as man consists of body, and soul, and spirit, so in the same way does Scripture": the simple are edified by its "flesh", the obvious sense; those who have advanced, by its "soul"; and the perfect, by the "spiritual law". [cite:ts13_origen_dp] He applied the method to Israel itself. Quoting Paul's phrase "Israel after the flesh", he argued that there is also an Israel "according to the Spirit", a "race of Jews inwardly", and that Jerusalem should be read as the heavenly city. [cite:ts13_origen_dp]

Origen's reading has to be reported with its consequence. In it the bodily Israel becomes a "type" of a spiritual one, and that move can be used to argue that the church has taken Israel's place, the position its critics call replacement theology, which Christian Zionist organisations such as the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem warn against by name. [cite:ts11_icej_catc] The inward reading is therefore not innocent by default. Read within Philo it deepens a people's self-understanding; read within a Christian polemic it can be turned against that people. Part Five records how the Scofield Reference Bible and its dispensational successors set themselves against exactly this spiritualising, insisting that the Christian does not inherit "the distinctive Jewish promises". [cite:ts5_sac_rom11] [scene:ts5_notes|Scofield's notes] That dispute returns in the last section of this part.

Kabbalah: Jacob at the centre of the tree

Medieval Jewish mysticism built a different inner map. Kabbalah describes ten sefirot, aspects through which the divine is manifest, arranged as a tree. Two of them stand opposite each other: Chesed, loving-kindness, expansive giving, and Gevurah, strength or restraint. Between and below them sits Tiferet, "beauty, glory, adornment", the sefira of balance and integration, whose task is to hold the expansive and the restrictive together. In the astrological correspondences the tradition gives it, Tiferet is the sun. [cite:ts13_wiki_tiferet]

Kabbalah assigns the three to the three patriarchs. Abraham is Chesed, Isaac is Gevurah, and Jacob is Tiferet. In the teaching of Isaac Luria, known as the Ari, as translated by Moshe Yaakov Wisnefsky, Jacob corresponds to Tiferet because he reconciles the two before him: "Jacob reconciles the first two". [cite:ts13_chabad_luria] The writer Efraim Palvanov, setting out the same scheme, puts it plainly: "Jacob was Tiferet", and connects it to Micah 7:20, which pairs truth with Jacob and kindness with Abraham; he cites the Zohar's assignment of water to Chesed, fire to Gevurah and air, the balancing element, to Tiferet. [cite:ts13_palvanov] Read this way, the man who becomes Israel is the one who holds two opposed forces in balance. That is a documented teaching of Jewish mysticism, and it is the closest thing in the traditional sources to the "integration" the IS-RA-EL lens speaks of. It differs from that lens in one respect worth noting: the opposed pair in Kabbalah is kindness and restraint, while the feminine aspect of the divine is assigned to a different sefira, Malkhut, paired with the masculine cluster around Tiferet. [cite:ts13_wiki_tiferet]

Modern rabbinic commentary: the struggle with oneself

Reading the wrestle as an inner struggle is also current in mainstream Jewish teaching. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, in his weekly commentary on the Torah reading, noted that the opponent's identity is disputed: the Torah calls him a man, Hosea an angel, the rabbinic sages identify him as Samael, Esau's guardian angel, and Jacob believed he had seen God. Sacks read the night through Jacob's whole life, in which he had repeatedly tried to be his brother. That night, he wrote, Jacob "throws off the image of Esau, the person he wants to be", and from then on "is content to be himself". His conclusion was a teaching about integration: "We have to be at peace with ourself before we can be at peace with the world." [cite:ts13_sacks_be] This reading keeps Israel as the name of a people; Sacks tied it to Jewish identity and leadership. But it locates the decisive event inside the man.

A later frame: the dark night of the soul

The phrase most often used for this kind of ordeal today is Christian and Spanish, and far younger than Genesis. John of the Cross (1542–1591), a cofounder of the Discalced Carmelites, was imprisoned by his own order in Toledo from the night of 3 December 1577 and escaped on 15 August 1578. [cite:ts13_brit_john] [cite:ts13_nwe_john] The spiritual term "dark night of the soul" takes its name from his poem "Noche oscura" and the prose commentary he wrote on it in the 1580s, in which the night stands for the hardships of detachment from the world on the way to union with God. He was canonised in 1726 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1926. [cite:ts13_nwe_john] [cite:ts13_brit_john]

The sources consulted for this section do not show John applying the image to Jacob, and no early reader is documented doing so here. The pairing is a later reader's move, made among others in this platform's own reading, which describes Jacob's night as an ordeal in darkness completed at the coming of light. It is a fair description of the shape of Genesis 32, alone, at night, wounded, renamed at dawn, but it is a frame brought to the text, and it is labelled as such in this volume.

The pattern across these readings is consistent. Each takes the night at the Jabbok as a transformation, each places the change in the person before it places it in history, and none of them, except where Origen's method was turned into polemic, uses the inward reading to deny the people who bear the name. That is the documented ground on which the next section's lens stands, and from which it then departs.
