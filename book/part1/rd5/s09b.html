Acceptance, Rejection and the Two Escalations

One sentence has long served as the summary of what followed the partition vote: Israel accepted, the Arabs rejected and started the war. In its fuller form the familiar account holds that the Arab side rejected a peaceful UN compromise and at once launched a war against a state that had only accepted it. That sentence joins two separate questions. The first is who accepted and who rejected Resolution 181. The second is how the fighting began, and between whom. The record gives each its own answer, and the answers are not the same shape.

On the first question the familiar account is accurate, and it should be said plainly before anything else. The Jewish Agency accepted Resolution 181, reluctantly, with real misgivings about the plan's narrow borders. The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab League rejected the plan in its entirety. Jamal Husseini, speaking for the Arab Higher Committee, told the United Nations that the partition line would be "nothing but a line of fire and blood." [cite:wiki_un_partition_plan] [cite:palquest_un_partition_1947] On the narrow matter of who said yes and who said no, the documented record is one-sided. The arguments each side made, and the contested figures behind them, are set out in [scene:s09|the section on the vote].

The second question is where the single sentence starts to mislead. Violence escalated sharply from 1 December 1947, immediately after the vote. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] What followed for the next five and a half months was not yet a war between states. It was a civil war inside Mandate Palestine, between the Jewish and Arab communities and their paramilitary forces, fought while Britain still formally governed the country. The neighbouring Arab states' armies did not cross a border until 15 May 1948.

The forces on the Arab side were of two kinds, and they did not work together. The Arab Liberation Army was created by the Arab League and led by Fawzi al-Qawuqji. Its forces were volunteers, in the historian Magdalena Pogońska-Pol's description "few in number, and poorly armed." [cite:rs_rd5_lodz_2019] The Army of the Holy War (Jaysh al-Jihad al-Muqaddas) was made up of Palestinian volunteers and was subordinate to the Husayni family. The two acted without cooperating, and, Pogońska-Pol concludes, neither stopped the Jewish forces. [cite:rs_rd5_lodz_2019] The Army of the Holy War's commander was Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni. He re-entered Palestine across the Egyptian border in December 1947 and, after the Arab Higher Committee proclaimed a jihad, re-formed and commanded the force. His explosives unit blew up the Jewish Agency building in Jerusalem; the source does not give the date. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_abdelqader]

The American government's own historians describe the fighting of these months in terms that favor the familiar account. The State Department's Office of the Historian describes irregular Palestinian Arab bands, attached to Arab Liberation Army units, attacking Jewish cities, settlements and forces, with the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi fighting back. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war] That is the strongest documentary support for the reading that the Arab side began the fighting and the Jewish side responded.

Other documented attacks run the other way. In early January 1948 a car bomb at the former Ottoman government house in Jaffa killed 26 people; the material available here gives the toll but does not name who planted the bomb. On 15 February 1948 Haganah and Palmach forces attacked the village of Qisarya near Haifa. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] [cite:aljazeera_remix_partition_transcript] The sources this book draws on place Qisarya within a documented pattern of Jewish paramilitary attacks on Palestinian towns and villages in the war's first months, well before the massacre at Deir Yassin, which is told in [scene:ma_s01|Deir Yassin, the Hadassah convoy and Tantura]. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] That is the strongest documentary support for the reading that a two-sided war began at once.

The decisive struggle of the spring was over the road to Jerusalem. At the end of March 1948 Abd al-Qadir al-Husayni went to Damascus to seek arms from the Arab League's Military Committee. Jewish forces took al-Qastal, the village overlooking the Jerusalem–Jaffa road. He was killed on 8 April 1948 leading the counter-attack. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_abdelqader] Read one way, the episode compresses the Palestinian predicament of these months: a local commander who had to travel to the League's Military Committee in Damascus to ask for weapons, and who died on the road he was trying to hold.

Before the Mandate ended, a state army had already entered the fighting in one place. The Arab Legion of Transjordan attacked the Gush Etzion bloc of Jewish settlements; its defenders surrendered, and some were massacred. [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_seminar_cw] The source, the historian Avi Shlaim's seminar notes, does not give the date or the number killed, and this book does not supply them.

The second escalation came on 15 May 1948, the day after Israel's declaration of independence, when the armies of neighbouring Arab states invaded. The war they entered ended in 1949 with Israel's territory extending far beyond the lines of the UN plan. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] The invasion is real, documented and significant; its course, the Arab League's own stated reasons for it and the forces on each side are told in [scene:rd5_invasion|the Arab states' war]. Its place in the sequence is the point here. It followed five and a half months of civil war already under way. It did not start the fighting.

Set against the record, both popular stories lose something. The reading that "the Arabs rejected and attacked" is supported on the vote, which was a clean Arab rejection backed by a public threat of "fire and blood," and it has support on the fighting: the Office of the Historian describes Arab bands attacking and Jewish forces fighting back, Abd al-Qadir's men bombed the Jewish Agency building, and the Arab Legion attacked Gush Etzion before the Mandate ended. What it cannot carry is the claim that the war began on 15 May with an unprovoked attack on a purely defensive new state. By then the country had been at war for five and a half months, with documented Jewish paramilitary attacks on Arab villages and towns among the events of those months.

The reading that "a two-sided war began at once" is supported by the dates: violence from 1 December, attacks documented in both directions, Qisarya in February, the Jerusalem road in April. What it cannot carry is any suggestion that the rejection was incidental, or that Arab forces were merely reactive. The Arab Higher Committee had promised resistance in advance, and the Army of the Holy War and the Arab Liberation Army were organized to fight. Treating the sequence as a single, uninterrupted chain from the vote to the invasion, in either direction, skips over the civil-war phase that the record documents: a one-sided vote, then a two-sided civil war, then a state-level invasion.

Some things this record does not settle. It does not establish who fired first in the opening days of December, and the sources here do not attempt to. It does not give a count of the dead in the civil-war months, on either side. It leaves the perpetrator of the Jaffa car bomb unnamed in the material available, and leaves the date of the Jewish Agency bombing and the date and toll of Gush Etzion unstated. Nor does it deliver a verdict on responsibility: it does not argue that the Arab League's rejection was justified, or that the later invasion was unprovoked. It establishes the shape of the sequence, and leaves the moral weighing of each side's decisions within it to the reader.
