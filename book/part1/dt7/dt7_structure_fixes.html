Structure: Maps, Primaries, Terms and the Rolls

The second group of fixes changes the machinery rather than the money: how electoral votes are awarded, who draws district lines, who may vote in a primary, how long a legislator may serve, and how people get onto the voter rolls. Most of these can be made without touching the Constitution, which is why most of them have been tried somewhere. That also means there is evidence on each, and the evidence is more modest than either supporters or opponents usually claim.

The popular-vote compact

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact is an agreement among states to give their electoral votes to the winner of the national popular vote. It uses the power the Constitution already gives each state legislature to decide how its electors are chosen, so it needs no amendment on its supporters' reading. It takes effect only when its members together hold a majority of the 538 electoral votes. When Virginia's governor signed it on 13 April 2026, the compact reached 18 states and the District of Columbia, with 222 electoral votes, 48 short of 270. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia] [cite:dt2_crs_faq2026]

Its legal footing is disputed. Critics argue that it needs the consent of Congress as an interstate compact, that it would in effect abolish the House's role in choosing a president when no candidate wins a majority, that its bar on states withdrawing in the last six months of a presidential term may itself be unconstitutional, and that replacing the Electoral College requires an amendment. Its defenders, among them the law professors Akhil and Vikram Amar and Representative Jamie Raskin, answer that the Constitution gives states plenary power over how they appoint electors. [cite:dt7_wiki_npvlaw] Patrick Valencia, now Iowa's deputy solicitor general, calls it an attempt to "usurp the constitutionally required electoral procedures," and supporters expect a court challenge if it ever reaches 270. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia] Public support depends on party: 63 per cent of Americans favoured a popular-vote system in 2024, including about 80 per cent of Democrats and 46 per cent of Republicans. [cite:dt2_pew_ec2024] The amendment route was tried once in earnest; in 1969 the House passed direct election 339–70, and the Senate failed twice to end a filibuster in 1970. [cite:dt2_wiki_bayh]

Independent redistricting commissions

Since the Supreme Court ruled in 2019 that federal courts cannot hear claims of partisan gerrymandering, the remedies left are state courts, state law and commissions. [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] [cite:dt3_ts_moore] Arizona's voters created an independent commission by ballot measure in 2000; its five members include four chosen by legislative leaders from a pool of nominees, who then pick a fifth from a party other than theirs. On 29 June 2015 the Supreme Court held 5–4 that voters may take redistricting away from a legislature this way; Chief Justice Roberts, dissenting, argued that the Constitution's word "legislature" means the legislature. Ballotpedia lists eight states with independent commissions. [cite:dt7_bp_airc]

The evidence on what commissions do is suggestive, not conclusive. A 2023 study of House elections from 1982 to 2018 by Matthew Nelson found that, compared with maps drawn by legislatures, maps drawn by independent commissions were associated with roughly half the odds of the incumbent party winning and more than twice the odds of a competitive race, decided within ten points; commissions made up of party appointees showed no significant effect. The author states that this is association, not proof of cause. [cite:dt7_nelson_ps] FairVote, a reform group, examined California's first commission map in 2013 and found it more legitimate but not more competitive: five competitive districts in 2008 and five in 2012, with voters increasingly sorted by party. [cite:dt7_fv_ccrc]

Commissions are also only as durable as the voters who create them. On 4 November 2025 California's voters approved Proposition 50 by 65 to 35 per cent, setting aside the commission's congressional map for a legislature-drawn one through 2030, aimed at five more Democratic-leaning seats, in answer to Texas's new map drawn for Republicans. [cite:dt7_bp_prop50] [cite:dt3_bp_redistrict2026] The Fair Representation Act, introduced in Congress in July 2025, would ban mid-decade redistricting nationally; it has not advanced. [cite:dt6_fra_text]

Open and top-two primaries

Turnout in primaries is low everywhere and somewhat higher where independents may vote: in 2022 it averaged 24.5 per cent of eligible voters in states with open systems and 20.7 per cent in closed ones, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center. [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] A top-two primary goes further, putting all candidates on one ballot and sending the two leaders, of any party, to the general election. The claim made for it is that it elects more moderate legislators. The best study of the two states that use it, by Eric McGhee and Boris Shor, found "an inconsistent effect": some moderation among California Democrats, partly overlapping with new district lines and term limits, and "virtually no effect" in Washington. [cite:dt7_mcghee_shor]

Massachusetts votes on a top-two primary for most state and congressional offices on 3 November 2026 as Question 3. Its campaign had raised about $7.16 million by 20 October, against about $195,000 for the opposition; it is backed by two former governors, one from each party, and opposed by both state parties, the Working Families Party and several unions, and a court challenge was filed. [cite:dt7_bp_maq3] The ballot-box record so far is unfavourable to reformers: in 2024 voters rejected open-primary or ranked-choice measures in Colorado, Idaho, Nevada, Arizona, Montana, Oregon and South Dakota. [cite:dt6_bp_2024results]

Term limits

Term limits cannot reach Congress without an amendment. In U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton in 1995 the Supreme Court held 5–4 that states may not add to the qualifications for Congress set in the Constitution, striking down limits that nearly half the states had sought to impose. [cite:dt7_fed_thornton] For state legislatures, where limits are allowed, the largest study, by the National Conference of State Legislatures with the Council of State Governments and a group of political scientists, found that limits shifted power towards governors, staff and lobbyists, reduced cooperation across party lines, and did not deliver the gains for women's representation their proponents had promised. NCSL's Karl Kurtz concluded that limits had "done more to limit rather than enhance the effectiveness of the legislative branch," and also that "the public doesn't have much of an appetite for repeal." [cite:dt7_stateline_tl]

Automatic registration and votes at sixteen

Automatic voter registration signs eligible people up when they deal with a state agency, usually the motor-vehicle office, unless they decline. A study prepared for the Center for American Progress estimated that Oregon's system, the first, added about 116,000 people in 2016 who would have been unlikely to register otherwise, of whom about 40,000 voted, a gain the reporting called "relatively modest." [cite:dt7_opb_avr] A later study of Oregon found that people registered this way were far less likely to join a party and that "very little partisan mobilization" followed. [cite:dt7_eurek_avr] The critics' case is administrative: in 2018 California's motor-vehicle department disclosed about 23,000 registration errors and then about 1,500 more wrongful registrations, including non-citizens; the records were cancelled, the secretary of state sought an audit, and no report showed that any of those people voted. [cite:dt7_gov_dmv]

Lowering the voting age to sixteen has been tried only in local elections. Takoma Park, Maryland, did it first, in 2013; turnout among 16- and 17-year-olds was 44 per cent that year against 10 per cent for all voters, and 69 per cent against 54 per cent in 2020, although only 191 young people were registered in 2020 and the city had moved its elections to even-numbered years, which may have helped. [cite:dt7_wm_vote16] Elsewhere voters have been divided: Oakland approved votes at sixteen for school board elections in 2020, San Francisco narrowly rejected the idea that year and in 2016, and California voters rejected letting 17-year-olds vote in primaries by ten points; the San Francisco Chronicle argued it was inconsistent with higher age limits for smoking and military service. [cite:dt7_74_vote16]

The test

The claim under test is that democracy has failed and cannot be repaired by more of it; Thiel wrote in 2009 that he had "little hope that voting will make things better." [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] Documented: each fix in this part has been tried somewhere in the United States, adopted in most cases by voters themselves, from New York City's charter and Seattle's levy to Arizona's commission. [cite:dt7_nyc_match] [cite:dt7_bp_seattle] [cite:dt7_bp_airc] Probable: that matching funds broaden who gives and that independent commissions make more races competitive, on studies that are careful but not conclusive. [cite:dt7_malbin_parrott] [cite:dt7_nelson_ps] Not supported: that democracy cannot reform its own rules, since these reforms are the record of it doing so.

The record also cuts against this book's thesis. The measured effects are small: a few more competitive districts, an inconsistent shift towards moderation, a modest rise in registration. None of the studies here shows any fix restoring trust in government or closing the gap in responsiveness described in [scene:dt1_where_they_are_right|Part One]. Voters rejected most of the reform measures put to them in 2024, 18 states have banned ranked-choice voting, Missouri by popular vote, and California's voters set aside their own commission's map when the other party redrew in Texas. [cite:dt6_bp_2024results] [cite:dt6_bp_bans2026] [cite:dt7_bp_prop50] The Supreme Court, meanwhile, removed another limit on money in June 2026. [cite:dt7_pac_nrsc] A fuller democracy is available in pieces; whether the pieces would answer the critics' charges is the question for [scene:dt8_their_logic|Part Eight].
