## How the map was built

The map is the book's own record, laid out at once instead of in order. Nothing goes on it from who someone was; ties come only from what the documents say they did, and the same rule applies to every node on every side.

### Every name in the book becomes a node
Each person, organization, government, document and event the book's stages name is a node you can open, with who it was, what it did and where the book discusses it.

### Every tie carries its sentence and its source
A tie is an act: funded, founded, signed, voted, sold, armed. Each one shows the sentence that states it and the source behind it, so any line on the map can be checked in a click.

### Every tie is graded by how directly its source states it

- Verified, 1,338: the cited page states the act directly. Counts in full.
- Reported, 201: stated indirectly or in someone else's account. Counts a little over half.
- Named together, 2,076: only appear on the same page. Kept for searching, count for nothing.

A further 11 ties rest on a single source and are marked as still to be checked; they count for nothing until a second source confirms them.

### New findings are added under the same rules
In November 2026 the map gained the network before Herzl (eight nodes from 1836 to 1896), a search for central-bank ties, and three new measures: legitimacy, people and land held. Each addition was checked against its sources, and two common errors were corrected on the way.

## The nine things a state needed

To weigh a removal, the map needs a measure of what it took to found a state. It uses nine, each drawn from the record, and each counted from the year it began to be built. Money counts twice, because every other need ran on it. Great powers count by the size of their economies in 1950: the United States 6.6 times France, the Soviet Union 2.3, Britain 1.6, China 1.1, every other state once.

Each need follows the same curve: the first part of anything counts for more than the last. That is why, in the tables above, money can fall to two-thirds of what the full record gave it and cost only a few points. The larger losses usually come from the years of work that would have to be done again.

## How a removal is weighed

Taking a node off the map is simple. Deciding what falls with it follows a short list of rules, the same for every node, each taken from how the record itself worked.

### What no longer connects is lost
A tie counts only while it still reaches the bodies that founded the state: the Zionist Organization, the Jewish Agency, the Haganah, the provisional government, Resolution 181 and the Declaration of Independence. What is cut off takes its years of work with it.

### Someone has to pursue the goal
If the Zionist Organization, the Jewish Agency and the Jewish National Council are all gone, every need counts as empty. Outside support remains, but on this record none of it set out to build a state on its own.

### Documents need the bodies that issued them
A declaration, treaty or resolution falls when every body that issued or signed it is removed, and a document written into another falls with it: the Mandate carried the Balfour Declaration.

### The UN vote needs two-thirds
Resolution 181 passed 33 to 13 with 10 abstentions. A removed state that voted yes counts as abstaining; if the yes votes fall below twice the no votes, the resolution fails.

### Blocs follow their leaders
The Americas, Western Europe and Moscow's allies moved with the power they depended on. If a leader is removed, each member keeps its vote only at the independence its bloc showed in the 1947 vote.

### Key decisions are read as decisions
Seven acts were one person's or one cabinet's call, from the Balfour Declaration to American recognition. Their chance is the documented push over push plus resistance; a successor's own documented position counts, and one with none counts as an even chance.

### Lost time is rebuilt at the original pace
What is left rebuilds at the speed each need was first built, scaled to how much of it remains: half a need rebuilds at half the speed.

### Waiting costs 2% a year
The window of 1947 and 1948 would not stay open: the end of the Mandate, the UN vote, the sympathy that followed the Holocaust, and American and Soviet support at the same time.

## Reading a result

Every removal is read five ways: the chance of a founding on time, in 1948; by 1958; by 1978; ever; and whether the movement itself survives. The starting figure of about 93.2% is a calibration, not a finding. It says only that on the full record the state was founded, with a margin left for what the record does not hold. The verdict reads the chance on time first, then the chance ever.

- **Happens on time**: 85% or more in 1948

- **Still likely, but delayed**: 70% or more in 1948, or 75% or more ever

- **Possible, with great difficulty**: 55% or more ever

- **Unlikely**: 30% or more ever

- **Not possible on this record**: below 30% ever

The figures are not predictions. They are a disciplined way of asking which parts of the documented record the founding depended on.

## What the map cannot know

**It measures the founding, not the war.** The question is whether a state is declared on 14 May 1948, not whether it survives what followed.

**It counts only what is documented.** Someone who would have stepped in but left no record cannot be a substitute.

**It weighs a cause only through what it caused.** The pogroms of 1881 and the May Laws now count through the movements they set off: without the pogroms the map reads 83% in 1948. Ideas with no documented route to an act, such as the writings of Hess and Kalischer, still score nothing; a zero means the map cannot measure them, not that they did not matter.

**Legitimacy is a judgment about weight.** No document says how much public sympathy weighed beside money or arms. The map counts it like the other needs, and the tests above vary it.

**It weighs opposition only through decisions.** Opponents appear with their documented acts, as resistance in the decisions they tried to stop, not as a network of their own.

**It cannot weigh persuasion that left no tie.** Newspapers and propaganda shaped beliefs, but a belief counts only where a document shows it moving a decision.
