Seven Founding Narratives, Checked

Many stories about Israel's founding are taught as settled history. This section gathers seven of them and sets each beside the documented record presented elsewhere in this book. Six are familiar parts of the founding account. The seventh comes from the opposite direction, and it is included on purpose: the standard that checks the founding slogans must also check the conspiracy myths about who paid.

The first is the slogan "a land without a people for a people without a land." The familiar version stops there. The count does not. Britain's own census of 1922, taken two years into the Mandate, recorded 757,182 people, about 11 percent of them Jewish. The slogan did not describe the country.

The second holds that Palestinians left because Arab leaders told them to. The monitored broadcasts of 1948 showed no general evacuation order, and Israel's own archives point to attacks, expulsions and fear as the main causes of flight. That correction is traced in [scene:np_s01|how the flight was told].

The third is the image of a miracle against impossible odds. A CIA assessment from 1948, and later research, describe a war fought in two phases that Israel ended with roughly a two-to-one advantage in manpower. The victory was real. The odds at its end were not those of the familiar image.

The fourth holds that Israel was born of the Holocaust. The program, the British pledge and the Mandate all came before Hitler, by between 11 and 37 years; the dates 1896, 1917, 1920 and 1922 mark them. The Holocaust sped up support for a state. It did not create the program.

The fifth holds that Israel accepted partition, the Arabs rejected it, and the Arabs then started the war. The vote did split as described. But the fighting began as a two-sided civil war months before the Arab armies entered.

The sixth holds that the land was bought fair and square. In 1947 about 7 percent of the land was Jewish-owned. Purchases were made legally from absentee owners. But under Ottoman land law those purchases evicted the tenant farmers who worked the land, and later confiscations followed, among them those under the absentee property law of 1950.

The seventh comes from the other side: the claim that the Rothschilds, or a few Jewish bankers, paid for it all. The documented money came overwhelmingly from mass small-donor giving, with more than a million American contributors in 1948 alone. Edmond de Rothschild, the Paris Rothschild, did fund early colonies, but he opposed Herzl. The founding was not bankrolled by a handful of financiers, as the ledger in [scene:gf_s01|who paid for it all] shows in detail.

Taken together, the seven checks do not add up to a single verdict. Some founding claims are simply contradicted by the record, as with the empty land and the orders to flee. Others are partly true but misleading as usually told, as with the partition vote, the legal land purchases and the military victory. And the myth of a few bankers fails just as the slogans do, against the same kind of evidence. The record corrects in every direction. That is what makes it worth trusting.
