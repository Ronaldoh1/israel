True Wealth and Public Wealth: What Is on the Books

The wealth of "the Vatican" is usually given as a single large number. The documents allow a more exact answer. Some assets are on published books, with a year and an auditor or a responsible office attached. Some are deliberately left off or carried at a symbolic value. A third group, much the largest, belongs to other legal owners and was never the Holy See's to count. The popular totals mostly blur these three groups together. This section separates them and then traces two of the best-known totals to where they came from.

Who owns Church property: one Church, many legal owners

Canon law answers the ownership question directly. Canon 1256 provides that "under the supreme authority of the Roman Pontiff, ownership of goods belongs to that juridic person" that lawfully acquired them. A diocese, a parish, a religious order, a Catholic university or hospital foundation is each its own juridic person (a legal entity under Church law), and in almost every country it is also a separate legal person under national law. [cite:vt9_cic_1254] Canon 1273 makes the pope "the supreme administrator and steward of all ecclesiastical goods". That gives him authority over how Church goods are managed. It does not make the Holy See their owner, and it does not put a diocese's buildings on APSA's balance sheet. [cite:vt9_cic_1254] Money moves in the other direction by rule: canon 1271 asks bishops to help supply what the Apostolic See needs, in proportion to their dioceses' means. The Holy See's 2015 accounts recorded 24 million euros from that source. [cite:vt9_cic_1254] [cite:vt9_hs_2015]

The large national flows of Church money follow the same rule. The Italian otto per mille, more than a billion euros a year, goes to the Italian Bishops' Conference, not to the Holy See, and it does not pass into the Holy See's accounts except through contributions like those under canon 1271 and Peter's Pence. [scene:vt3_otto_per_mille|Part Three] follows the Italian money. [cite:vt3_teleborsa_8x2026]

What is on the published books

Four bodies now publish figures, each for itself. The IOR holds 5.9 billion euros of clients' money and 815.3 million euros of its own capital (2025). [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] APSA reported net equity of 2,686 million euros and total assets of 3,337.5 million (2025), including investment property at 2,211 million and a gold holding equal to 29 per cent of its own portfolio. [cite:vt9_apsa_bil2025] Peter's Pence took in 57.6 million euros in 2025. [cite:vt9_vn_pp2025] The Holy See's consolidated statement for 2024 gave total assets of 6,326.7 million euros and net equity of 4,277.0 million, hospitals included. [cite:vt9_hs_fs2024]

These figures cannot be added together. Since 2022 the Holy See's funds have been kept at the IOR as a rule (exclusively until Leo XIV's rescript of October 2025), and APSA, by its own description, carries out its financial operations through the IOR, so adding the two would count some of the same money twice. [cite:vt9_ewtn_rescript2022] [cite:vt9_cna_coniuncta] [cite:vt9_vn_apsa_mandate] The IOR's 5.9 billion also includes the savings of religious congregations and other Church bodies that are separate owners under canon 1256. [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] [cite:vt9_cic_1254] The consolidated statement is the nearest thing to a total for the Holy See, and even it does not say exactly which bodies it covers. [cite:vt9_hs_fs2024]

What is carried at a symbolic value

The largest gap between book value and any imaginable market value is the Holy See's art and its historic buildings. In 2005 the Associated Press reported that St Peter's Basilica and the Sistine Chapel were listed in the Holy See's accounts at a symbolic one euro each, the officials' reasoning being that such works are "a treasure for all humanity" and cannot be sold. Its other real estate was then valued at about 700 million euros. [cite:vt9_ap_2005_deficit] Marketplace in 2023 quoted a Vatican accountant from 2004 to the same effect. [cite:vt9_marketplace_2023] The 2024 consolidated statements give no valuation of the historic, artistic or cultural heritage at all. "Historic Patrimony" appears only as a cost, an expense line of 19.58 million euros for its upkeep under the apostolic mission. [cite:vt9_hs_fs2024] The accounts treat the heritage as a cost to maintain, not as an asset that could be sold. Whatever such works might fetch is therefore not part of any published Holy See figure, and an estimate of it, such as the "10–15 billion dollars" sometimes given for the art, is a guess about a sale the owner says will never happen. No document supports any such figure.

Two famous numbers, traced

"73 billion dollars." This figure circulates as a total for "Holy See assets". Its likely origin is a Marketplace explainer of 10 February 2023, which estimated the Catholic Church's assets at "at least $73 billion". Marketplace reached that total by adding a few unrelated published estimates: diocesan assets in Australia (extrapolated from the state of Victoria) and in Germany, Trinity Church's portfolio in New York, and the IOR's and Holy See's own figures. It said plainly that dioceses are separate and that the real figure could not be known. [cite:vt9_marketplace_2023] Most of the total was therefore not the Holy See's. One of its pieces, Trinity Church Wall Street's portfolio of about 6 billion dollars, is not even Catholic: Trinity is an Episcopal parish. [cite:vt9_npq_trinity] As a measure of "Holy See assets", the number fails on its own source's terms.

"177 million acres." This figure, often given with "larger than Texas" and "the world's second-largest landowner", is a claim about land held by all Catholic entities worldwide, not by the Holy See. It circulates through business and lifestyle journalism, among them Bisnow in 2015 (citing a 2011 Business Insider list of landowners) and Benzinga in 2022, and none of these gives a method. Bisnow itself called the true figure "virtually impossible to calculate". [cite:vt9_bisnow_2015] [cite:vt9_benzinga_land] Other writers give other numbers: David Owen, in a New Yorker profile in 2021, put Church holdings at about 200 million acres, and Gerald Posner, author of God's Bankers, at about 277,000 square miles, which is the same 177 million acres expressed differently. [cite:vt9_longreads_owen] [cite:vt9_marketplace_2023] The cartographer Molly Burhans, who was mapping Church land with Vatican permission, found that no comprehensive inventory existed. [cite:vt9_benzinga_land] With no inventory and no stated method, the figure is an estimate repeated from one article to the next, not a measurement. This volume does not use it.

The wider ranges that circulate, such as "a Catholic institutional network of 300 billion to 1 trillion dollars" or IOR assets of "2 to 8 billion dollars, never published", fail for the same reasons. They have no source, they mix separate owners, and in the IOR's case they are out of date: the IOR has published its figures every year since its first annual report.

What can be said

The defensible statement is narrow. On its own published books, the Holy See is an institution with net equity of about 4.3 billion euros (2024), a property and investment arm worth about 2.7 billion on its own valuation (2025), and a bank holding about 5.9 billion euros of other people's money (2025). It has an operating deficit and a pension fund that its own head has described as seriously imbalanced. [cite:vt9_hs_fs2024] [cite:vt9_apsa_bil2025] [cite:vt9_ior_ar2025] [cite:vt9_ap_pension] Beyond those books lie art and buildings it says it will never sell, and a worldwide body of Church property that belongs, in law, to thousands of other owners. A larger single figure for "the Vatican's wealth" can be produced only by counting those things as the Holy See's, and the documents do not support doing so.
