Where the Promise Holds

A book that tests a promise has to say first where the promise is kept. The American Dream as taught says that a person who works hard can earn a decent living, that the country's ordinary families live well by the standards of the world, that newcomers can rise, and that children can do better than their parents. Each of those claims has a documented side on which it holds. This section sets them out plainly, with the numbers, before the rest of the book examines the mechanisms where the record parts from the story. Nothing later in the book cancels what is established here; where a later part finds the promise failing, it fails in a named place, for a named group, through a named mechanism, beside the record set out below.

Incomes that are high by the world's standards

The typical American household is richer today than at any time since the government began measuring it. The Census Bureau's real median household income, meaning the income of the household exactly in the middle once all households are lined up from poorest to richest, adjusted for rising prices, was $87,460 in 2025, up 2.6 per cent in a year and the highest since the series began in 1967. After federal and state taxes and the value of tax credits, the median was $76,060. [cite:us14_census_story2025] Over the long run the gain is real but uneven: from 1967 to 2025 household income before taxes rose about 56 per cent at the median and at the 10th percentile, and about 121 per cent at the 90th. [cite:us14_census_p60] The household in the middle is better off than its counterpart of a generation ago; the household near the top gained about twice as much.

Compared abroad, the American middle is near the top. On the OECD's measure of median disposable household income, adjusted for household size and converted at purchasing-power rates so that a dollar buys the same basket in each country, the United States stood second among member countries in 2021 at about $46,600, behind only Luxembourg and ahead of Norway, Switzerland and Canada. [cite:ad2_oecd_median] The same organisation reports that the top fifth of Americans earn about eight times as much as the bottom fifth, a wider spread than in most rich countries; a high middle and a wide spread are both true at once. [cite:ad2_oecd_bli]

Work that keeps a family out of poverty

The taught version says that full-time work is a way out of poverty, and for the poverty line as officially defined it is. In 2025 the official poverty rate was 10.2 per cent, 34.5 million people; among people who worked full time all year it was 1.6 per cent. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] The child poverty rate, 13.4 per cent, and the rate for Hispanic Americans, 13.9 per cent, were both the lowest ever recorded. [cite:us8_census_2025] On the Census Bureau's broader Supplemental Poverty Measure, which counts taxes, benefits and costs such as medical bills, poverty was 13.1 per cent, and Social Security alone kept 28.8 million people above that line. [cite:us18_census_p60290] Public policy can move these numbers quickly: when the child tax credit was enlarged for 2021, supplemental child poverty fell to 5.2 per cent, and it rose to 12.4 per cent in 2022 after the larger credit expired. [cite:us18_scripps_ctc] The federal tax system also leans toward lower earners: the Congressional Budget Office finds that average federal tax rates fell for every income group between 1979 and 2022, most of all for the lowest fifth, whose individual income tax rate was about minus 10 per cent once refundable credits are counted. [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] Part Fourteen tests how well the official poverty line itself measures need. [scene:ad14_poverty_line|How the poverty line is drawn]

Children of immigrants rise

The immigrant chapter of the story holds over more than a century of data. Linking millions of fathers and sons from the 1880s to the present, Ran Abramitzky, Leah Boustan, Elisa Jácome and Santiago Pérez found that children of immigrants from nearly every sending country have higher upward mobility than children of parents born in the United States, both in the age of Ellis Island and today, even though the countries of origin and the immigration laws have changed completely. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer] The size of the advantage is steady: sons of immigrant fathers at the 25th percentile of income ended up 3 to 4 percentile points higher than comparable sons of American-born fathers in the 1880–1910 cohorts, and about 5 points higher in modern data. Part of the advantage comes from immigrant families settling in places with better prospects for children. The exceptions are few and named: in modern data, children of immigrants from Haiti and Jamaica do not show the advantage. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_nber] Part Thirteen follows these groups in detail. [scene:ad13_immigrants_latinos|Immigrants and Latino Americans]

The record for Hispanic Americans as a whole points the same way. Raj Chetty, Nathaniel Hendren, Maggie Jones and Sonya Porter, working from linked tax and Census records, conclude that Hispanic Americans "are moving up significantly in the income distribution across generations" because their rates of upward mobility are relatively high, in contrast with Black Americans, whose rates are substantially lower than white Americans'. [cite:ad2_oi_race_hisp]

Gains for Black children, and places where the ladder works

The racial gap in mobility is one of the largest failures this book documents, but it is narrowing for recent cohorts. Comparing children born in 1978 with those born in 1992, Chetty, Will Dobbie, Benjamin Goldman, Sonya Porter and Crystal Yang found that Black children's adult earnings rose at every level of parental income, and that the earnings gap between white and Black children from low-income families shrank by 30 per cent. The same study found the gap between rich and poor white children widening by 30 per cent, and similar patterns in schooling, test scores and mortality. [cite:ad2_changing_opp] Part Thirteen sets these gains beside the wealth record, which moved the other way. [scene:ad13_black_americans|Black Americans and the Dream]

Where a child grows up matters as much as the national average. In the commuting zone around San Jose, a child born into the bottom fifth of incomes had about a 12.9 per cent chance of reaching the top fifth; around Charlotte, about 4.4 per cent. The places with higher mobility had less residential segregation, less income inequality, better primary schools, more social capital and more stable families. [cite:ad2_land_opp] In those places the promise of rising from the bottom is kept at roughly three times the rate of the places where it is not. [scene:ad3_relative_and_place|Relative mobility and place]

The generation the promise described

For Americans born around 1940, the taught version was close to a literal description of life. About 90 per cent of children born that year grew up to earn more than their parents at the same age, after adjusting for inflation; for sons compared with fathers, 95 per cent. [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] [cite:ad2_oi_fading] That generation came of age as the Servicemen's Readjustment Act, the GI Bill, signed on 22 June 1944, paid for veterans' schooling and backed their mortgages. In 1947 veterans made up 49 per cent of college admissions; by the time the original programme ended in July 1956, 7.8 million of the 16 million veterans of the Second World War had used its education or training benefits, and between 1944 and 1952 the Veterans Administration backed nearly 2.4 million home loans. [cite:ad2_va_gibill]

The same record carries its own limit. The benefits were federal, but colleges, lenders and much of their administration were local, and in the segregated South they were not equally open. Sarah Turner and John Bound found that the war and the GI Bill together raised college attainment by about 0.4 years for Black men born outside the South, comparable to white men's gain, but brought few gains to Black men born in the South, where about a hundred colleges were open to them and few counsellors served them; for Southern men the bill "exacerbated rather than narrowed" the racial gap. [cite:ad2_turner_bound] Part Six follows the housing side of that story. [scene:ad6_redlining_to_suburbs|From redlining to the suburbs]

More for less in goods

Everyday goods take a smaller bite than they once did. In 1960 Americans spent about 17 per cent of their disposable income, the income left after taxes, on food; in 2025 they spent 9.7 per cent, including meals out. [cite:ad2_ers_food1960] [cite:ad2_ers_food] Air-conditioning, a luxury in the 1960s, was installed in 49 per cent of new single-family homes completed in 1973 and in 89 per cent by 2008. [cite:ad2_census_chars] The average hides the bottom: in 2024 the poorest fifth of households spent 33 per cent of their pre-tax income on food, the richest fifth 6.4 per cent. [cite:ad2_ers_food] The goods that grew cheaper are mostly manufactured and farmed; the costs this book follows in later parts, housing, college, health care and child care, are mostly services, and several of them moved the other way. Even there the record is mixed: the average net tuition paid at a public four-year college by in-state students, after grants, fell from a peak of about $4,450 in 2012–13 to about $2,300 in 2025–26, measured in 2025 dollars. [cite:us14_cb_trends2025] [scene:ad7_college_cost_pell|What college costs]

What this record does and does not show

The record above is not a verdict on the whole promise; it marks where the promise is kept. Three limits run through it. First, the averages are high partly because the top is very high: the same Census series that shows record median income shows the gap between the 90th and 10th percentiles widening from 9.2 times in 1967 to 13.1 times in 2025. [cite:us14_census_story2025] Second, the mobility record is two records. The chance of out-earning one's parents fell from about 90 per cent to about 50 per cent, but the chance of climbing from the bottom fifth to the top fifth stayed roughly where it was, about 8 or 9 per cent, for children born between 1971 and 1986. [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] The ladder did not get steeper; the steps moved apart, and the economy's growth was shared less evenly. Third, gains for one group do not settle the record for another. Part Three measures the mobility record in full, and the next section explains the measures needed to read it.
