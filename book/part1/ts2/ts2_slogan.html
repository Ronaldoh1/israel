"A Land without a People": The Phrase and Its Myths

The restorationist strand left one legacy that outlived every institution it built: a phrase. "A land without a people for a people without a land" is among the most quoted lines in the argument over Palestine, usually as a Zionist slogan that denied the existence of the Arab population. Its documented history is different on three counts. It was coined by Christians, not by Zionists; the clergyman who first published it had travelled in the country and knew it was inhabited, and the first Zionist to use it dropped it within a few years; and how widely Zionists later used it is a matter on which named historians disagree. Shorter accounts appear in [scene:bc_s05|Restoration before Zionism] and [scene:rd1_arabquestion|the Arab question]. This section sets out the chain in full.

The chain of wording

The earliest wording that the literature cites comes from Richard Watson, a Methodist minister who died in 1833, in a sermon: "There is a people without a country, and a country without a people." [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] Accounts that follow Diana Muir in the Middle East Quarterly (Spring 2008), name Alexander Keith as the first to publish the phrase. Keith remains the first well-documented printed use, but not the first use. [cite:ts2_muir_meq] [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout]

Keith was a Church of Scotland minister who travelled to Palestine in 1839 on behalf of his church. In The Land of Israel According to the Covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob (1843) he described the Jews as "a people without a country" and their land as, in great measure, "a country without a people". [cite:ts2_muir_meq] [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] The compressed form, "a land without a people and a people without a land", first appeared in an 1844 review of Keith's book in a Scottish Free Church magazine. [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] Muir points out that Keith knew the country was inhabited: he had travelled through it, and returned about five years later with his son, George Skene Keith. [cite:ts2_muir_meq]

Shaftesbury used a variant with a different noun. In a letter of July 1853 he described Greater Syria as "a country without a nation" that needed "a nation without a country", and he wrote the same idea in his diary. [cite:ts2_wiki_shaftesbury] [cite:ts2_muir_meq] The sources disagree about the recipient: Muir names Palmerston, though she gives him Aberdeen's family name, George Hamilton Gordon, while the encyclopaedic accounts name the Prime Minister, Lord Aberdeen. [cite:ts2_muir_meq] [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] The library's [scene:bc_s05|Restoration before Zionism] chapter is right to date the phrase to 1853; an account that places it within the 1839–40 episode is not. Shaftesbury returned to the image in 1875, at a meeting of the Palestine Exploration Fund, as "a country without a people". [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] His 1887 biography by Edwin Hodder carried the wording to a wide readership. [cite:ts2_muir_meq]

The first recorded use by a Zionist came from the writer Israel Zangwill, in the New Liberal Review of December 1901: "Palestine is a country without a people; the Jews are a people without a country." [cite:ts2_muir_meq] Zangwill did not hold the line. In a speech in New York around 1904 he said that "Palestine proper has already its inhabitants", and by 1917 he was writing that the country held 600,000 Arabs. [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout]

Three claims about the phrase, tested

Three claims circulate about the phrase, and they need to be separated because the evidence for each is different. The first is that it was a Zionist coinage. On the record it was not: every documented use before 1901 is by a Christian writer, and the earliest are by restorationist clergy. [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] [cite:ts2_muir_meq]

The second is that it was a widely propagated Zionist slogan. Here the historians divide. Muir, writing for a journal published by the Middle East Forum, searched seven major American newspapers up to 1948 and found "Jewish national home" more than 3,000 times and Zangwill's wording four times, all before 1906; she found no Zionist organisation or leader that adopted it, and notes that it does not appear in Herzl's books, letters or diary. She concluded that the "widely propagated" claim "should be retired" unless new evidence appears. [cite:ts2_muir_meq] Adam Garfinkle, in Jewcentricity (2009), wrote that early Zionists who validated the phrase did not do so "easily or for long". On the other side, the Israeli historian Anita Shapira describes it as common among Zionists of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and Boaz Neumann, citing among others a book by David Ben-Gurion and Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, finds pioneer writings full of descriptions of Palestine as empty and desolate. Rashid Khalidi calls it a "widely propagated Zionist slogan". [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] The newspaper count measures the phrase in the American press; it does not settle how often it was used in Hebrew or in the settlements, which is where Shapira and Neumann locate it. The claim is therefore contested, not settled in either direction.

The third is about meaning: whether the phrase claimed the land was empty. Muir and the historian Gertrude Himmelfarb read "people" in the nineteenth-century sense of a nation, so that the phrase denied the inhabitants national standing rather than existence; Shaftesbury's own noun, "nation", fits that reading. [cite:ts2_muir_meq] [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] Edward Said read it as a way to "cancel and transcend an actual reality", the Arab population. Nur Masalha argues that Zangwill and Chaim Weizmann did not mean it literally but treated the inhabitants as not "worth considering". Gudrun Krämer puts the middle position in one line: "Palestine contained people, but not a people." [cite:ts2_wiki_landwithout] The two readings differ less than the dispute suggests. On either one, the phrase described a country with inhabitants and declined to count them as the people whose claim mattered.

For this volume the significance is the direction of travel. The phrase began as a restorationist reading of prophecy and geography, made by Christian clergy, the best documented of whom had seen the country and its people, and passed to a Zionist writer half a century later, who abandoned it within a few years. Its later life as an emblem of the conflict, used by critics of Zionism and disowned or reinterpreted by its defenders, belongs to the politics of the twentieth century, and the volume's ledger of myths and settled points returns to it in [scene:ts14_myths|Part Fourteen].
