The Closings: Who Shut the Temples and the Schools, 391–c.537

The end of the ancient mysteries is often told as a single act: "the Church" closed the mystery schools and burned their knowledge. The record shows a sequence of separate acts over a century and a half, carried out by different people with different powers. Emperors issued laws. A bishop of Alexandria used those laws to lead the destruction of a temple. A Gothic army, not acting for any church, wrecked Eleusis. A mob led by a church reader killed a philosopher. Popes and bishops turned temples into churches. A sixth-century emperor ended philosophical teaching at Athens and sent a general to shut the last Isis temple on the Nile. Each act is set out below with the actor who carried it out and the document that records it. The laws of Theodosius themselves, Cunctos populos of 380 and the bans on sacrifice of 391 and 392, are set out in Part Twenty-One and summarised here only as the frame. [scene:vt21_church_and_empire|Church and Empire]

The legal frame, 391–392

A law of 391 in the Theodosian Code (16.10.10) ordered that no one go to the sanctuaries or walk through the temples; another (16.10.11) closed the temples; a third, in 392 (16.10.12), banned all sacrifice and worship of the old gods, down to burning incense to the household gods in a private house. Historians including Malcolm Errington question how far these laws were enforced, and the archaeologists Luke Lavan and Michael Mulryan found only four of 43 temple destructions reported in written sources confirmed on the ground. [cite:vt21_wiki_theodpagan] [cite:vt21_wiki_paganpers] The laws made the mysteries illegal. They did not by themselves end them. What ended particular sanctuaries was local action, and local action is where the actors can be named.

The Serapeum, Alexandria, 391

The church historian Socrates Scholasticus, writing in Constantinople before 450, records the sequence in Alexandria. "At the solicitation of Theophilus bishop of Alexandria the emperor issued an order" for the demolition of the city's temples, and put Theophilus in charge of it. Theophilus cleared out the Mithraeum and put its mysteries on public display to ridicule them, then destroyed the Serapeum, the great temple of the god Serapis. Pagans, led by philosophers, fought back; there were deaths on both sides. The images of the gods were melted down into vessels for the church, which Socrates says were for the relief of the poor; one statue was kept on display so that, in Theophilus's words, pagans could not later deny that they had worshipped such gods. [cite:vt33_socrates_516] Rufinus, writing about 402 and possibly a witness, describes a soldier, at the bishop's urging, striking the colossal statue of Serapis on the jaw with an axe; it was broken up and burned. The site became a martyr's shrine and a church. [cite:vt33_romana_serapeum]

The actors are documented on the Christian side by Christian writers, which is unusual: they recorded it as a triumph. The bishop asked; the emperor ordered; the bishop directed; soldiers and crowds carried it out. What the Serapeum did not contain, on the evidence, was the Great Library, a point taken up at the end of this section. [cite:vt33_socrates_516] [cite:vt33_ou_library]

Eleusis, 392–396

The Eleusinian Mysteries were ended in two stages, by two different actors. Theodosius's laws of 391–392 forbade the cult; the sanctuary may nevertheless have continued to operate for some years. [cite:vt33_livius_eleusis] Then, in 395–396, the Visigothic king Alaric invaded Greece and his forces ravaged Attica, "destroying the Telesterion", the hall of initiation, "which was never to be rebuilt". [cite:vt33_wiki_telesterion] Alaric's Goths were Christians of the non-Nicene, "Arian" kind, and no source says they acted on any bishop's instruction. [cite:vt33_wh_eleusis]

The only near-contemporary account of the end comes from the pagan writer Eunapius, who had himself been initiated at Eleusis by the last legitimate hierophant, whose name, he says, it was not lawful to tell. Eunapius reports that this hierophant prophesied the ruin of the temples, and that afterwards a man from Thespiae, who held the rank of "pater in the ritual of Mithras", took the office. Alaric then passed through Thermopylae "as easily as though he were traversing an open stadium". Eunapius blamed the "impiety" of "men clad in black" cloaks, who entered Greece with Alaric, for opening the way. He does not name them in that passage. Earlier in the same book, describing the destruction of the Serapeum, he complains that monks had been brought into the sacred places and that any man "who wore a black robe" could act as a tyrant, which is why the Alaric phrase is commonly taken to mean monks. [cite:vt33_eunapius_hierophant] [cite:vt33_audit_eunapius_monks] What is disputed is how much of the destruction is owed to the laws and how much to the invasion; what Eunapius's account adds is that by the 390s the line of hierophants had already been broken and a Mithraic "Father" was presiding at Eleusis, a sign of how far the old cults had drawn together under pressure.

Hypatia, Alexandria, March 415

Hypatia, a mathematician and philosopher who taught in what Socrates calls "the school of Plato and Plotinus", was killed in Alexandria in March 415, during Lent, in the fourth year of Cyril's episcopate. Socrates gives the cause as politics: a rumour spread among the Christian population that she was preventing the prefect Orestes from being reconciled with the bishop. A crowd led by "a reader named Peter", a minor church officer, dragged her from her carriage to the church called the Caesareum, stripped her and killed her with tiles, then burned her remains. Socrates judged that the affair "brought not the least opprobrium, not only upon Cyril, but also upon the whole Alexandrian church". [cite:vt33_socrates_715]

Whether Cyril ordered it is disputed, and the sources divide along lines of loyalty. Socrates, a Christian, blames the crowd's zeal and the bishop's reputation but does not say Cyril gave an order. Damascius, a pagan philosopher of the next century, says Cyril envied her and plotted her death. John of Nikiu, a seventh-century Egyptian bishop, is hostile to her, repeats the charge that she practised magic and praises Cyril. [cite:vt33_romana_hypatia] The leader of the crowd is named in the record; whether anyone instigated it is not settled by it. The popular claim that Hypatia was the last librarian of Alexandria has no ancient source. [cite:vt33_romana_hypatia]

Temples into churches

Many sanctuaries ended not by demolition but by conversion. In Athens, the Parthenon was rebuilt inside as a three-aisled church of the Virgin Mary, the Theotokos, around the middle of the sixth century, and later served as the city's cathedral. [cite:vt33_byzleg_parthenon] In Rome, on 13 May 609, Pope Boniface IV, with the permission of the emperor Phocas, dedicated the Pantheon to Mary and all the martyrs, bringing in twenty-eight carriages of martyrs' bones from the city's cemeteries. [cite:vt33_chi_pantheon] Beneath the basilica of San Clemente in Rome lies a Mithraic sanctuary of about 200 CE, built in the courtyard of an apartment block; by the late fourth century the lower levels had been filled in and a church built over them, existing by 392, when Jerome mentions it. The mithraeum was found again in the 1860s by the Irish Dominican prior Joseph Mullooly. [cite:vt33_wiki_sanclemente]

Justinian: Athens, about 529

The end of the Platonic school at Athens rests on one sentence. The chronicler John Malalas, writing in the sixth century, recorded that the emperor Justinian sent a decree to Athens "ordering that no one should teach philosophy nor astrology". The decree itself is undated and was probably issued between 527 and 529. A law in Justinian's Code allowed only those of the orthodox faith to teach and draw a public salary, and it was aimed at Jews and heretics as well as pagans; it names no school. [cite:vt33_hannam_academy] The historian Agathias, writing about a generation later, tells how seven philosophers, among them the last head of the school, Damascius, and Simplicius, went to the Persian court and returned after a clause in the peace treaty of 532 protected them. That story rests on Agathias alone. [cite:vt33_hannam_academy]

How much the decree did is debated. Edward Watts argues that Malalas's notice is the only direct ancient statement on the subject, that the measure is best explained by local politics in Athens, and that contemporaries did not treat the closing as remarkable. [cite:vt33_watts_jrs] Alan Cameron argued that Justinian may only have cut public funding. No formal decision to suppress the philosophical schools of Alexandria is recorded, and Alexandria remained a leading centre of philosophy until the Arab conquest of Egypt in the seventh century. [cite:vt33_hannam_academy] What is documented is that an emperor forbade pagans to teach and that the Athenian succession from Plato, which Proclus had headed, ends in the record at this point.

Philae, about 535–537

The temple of Isis on the island of Philae, at Egypt's southern frontier, outlived the others; Procopius says peoples from beyond the frontier, whom he calls barbarians, had kept its sanctuaries up to his own time. Procopius also records its end: Justinian decided to tear the sanctuaries down, and his commander Narses "tore down the sanctuaries at the emperor's order", put the priests under guard and "sent the statues to Byzantium". [cite:vt33_procopius_philae] The buildings were not in fact pulled down. An inscription in the inner sanctuary records that it became a church of St Stephen under Bishop Theodore, and the last dated pagan graffiti on the island are from about 457. [cite:vt33_lemans_philae_church] Jitse Dijkstra's study of Philae argues that the old cult there was already dying of its own accord, its festival gone by the mid-sixth century, so that Justinian's order closed a temple whose priests were already few. [cite:vt33_gozzoli_dijkstra]

The Library of Alexandria: a myth graded

No account of the closings escapes the Library of Alexandria, and the popular version, that a single fire (lit by Caesar, by Christians or by Arabs) destroyed the wisdom of the ancient world, does not survive the record. Plutarch says the library burned in the fire Caesar started in 48 BCE, but another ancient account may refer only to warehouses. The papyrologist Roger Bagnall considers the war under the emperor Aurelian in the 270s the most probable occasion of large-scale damage, though no contemporary source mentions the library. Edward Gibbon in the eighteenth century blamed the Christian sack of the Serapeum in 391. The story that Arab conquerors in 641 used the books to heat the city's baths first appears six centuries later and is widely regarded as fiction. Bagnall's own conclusion is that "gradual processes of neglect and decay" did as much harm as any single act. [cite:vt33_ou_library] The Serapeum's destruction in 391 is documented; that it destroyed the Library is not.
