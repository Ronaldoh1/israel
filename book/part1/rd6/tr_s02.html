Truman After the Money: Retreat, Pledge, and a Threat Not Kept

American recognition in May 1948 did not settle Washington's policy toward the new state. Over the next two years Harry Truman's decisions shifted, sometimes in Israel's favour and sometimes against it, and the dates of those shifts are the best guide to what drove them. Two accounts of the period circulate. One says that once the donors had paid, Truman did whatever Israel wanted. The other says money had nothing to do with it. The dated record supports neither.

The first decision of the summer cut against the new state's supporters. On 25 June 1948 Truman signed the Displaced Persons Act "with very great reluctance," saying that it "discriminates in callous fashion against displaced persons of the Jewish faith." [cite:truman_institute_dpact] He did not leave it there. He pressed Congress for two years, until the act was amended in 1950. [cite:wjc_dpact]

That same summer, Truman privately wanted Israel to stop taking territory beyond the partition lines, and he was troubled by its refugee policy. He pressed neither point. The historian John Judis documents that Truman backed away in part because his nearly broke whistle-stop campaign needed $100,000 from two donors, Abraham Feinberg and Ed Kaufmann. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] The fuller story of that transaction belongs to [scene:s07f2|the section on Abraham Feinberg].

The pull of the campaign showed again in September. On 21 September 1948 Secretary of State Marshall endorsed the plan of the murdered UN mediator, Bernadotte, which would have given the Negev to the Arab side. On 28 September the White House aide Clark Clifford was upset that Marshall had approved it without the President's endorsement, and the camp of Truman's opponent, Thomas Dewey, tried to exploit the episode. [cite:jvl_clifford_bernadotte] [cite:djn_marshall_bernadotte_1948] Truman backed away from the plan.

On 24 October 1948, ten days before the election and two days after a statement by Dewey, Truman went further. He quoted the Democratic platform's pledge of full recognition and of revising the arms embargo "to accord to the State of Israel the right of self-defense." He also told government agencies to speed up Israel's loan applications. [cite:ucsb_truman_oct24_1948] On 29 November he assured Weizmann that Israel would keep the Negev.

The pledges were then kept. An Export-Import Bank loan of $100 million was set aside for Israel, and on 31 January 1949 the United States granted de jure recognition, full legal recognition of the state. [cite:jvl_truman_marshall_dejure] [cite:docsteach_dejure_1949]

Then the pressure returned. On 28 May 1949, while talks were under way at Lausanne, Truman had a secret note delivered to David Ben-Gurion. It said the United States was "seriously disturbed" by Israel's position on territory and refugees. It said Israel should offer territorial compensation for land it held beyond the partition lines. And it warned that if Israel kept rejecting the UN resolution of December 1948, "a revision of its attitude toward Israel has become unavoidable." [cite:jvl_truman_note_may1949] [cite:djn_1977_truman_note] Israel rejected the demands in June. The threatened revision never came. In May 1950 the United States joined Britain and France in the Tripartite Declaration on the region.

Laid out by date, the sequence has a clear shape. The retreat from the Bernadotte plan and the October pledges came just when money and votes were most needed. After the election Truman both delivered and pushed back, and then did not enforce his own warning. That is neither a president who did whatever he was asked nor one untouched by his campaign's finances. What the documents establish is timing, and one moment in which a historian names financial need as part of the reason a president stopped pressing a point he cared about.
