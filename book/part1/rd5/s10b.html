The Actual Military Balance of 1948

On July 1, 1948, with the war still raging, an American government report tried to count the armies in the field. Two popular stories about the military balance of that war circulate side by side. The more familiar holds that Israel was a tiny, vastly outnumbered nation that defeated overwhelming Arab armies through sheer will. The second, told in reply, holds that Israel won only because international sympathy, not real capability, tipped the scales. The military record supports neither. It shows a balance that shifted during the war, in stages, and ended close to the reverse of where the underdog story begins.

That July report is the most useful single document, because it was written while the fighting went on. The sources describe it in one place as a CIA report and in another as a State Department report. It put Israeli forces at roughly 90,000 in total, with about 35,000 used for active operations. It also stated that the Arab armies were stronger than Israel in artillery and tanks at that point, while fielding a wide variety of small arms. [cite:frus1948_military_situation] A report written before anyone knew the outcome carries a credibility that later reconstruction cannot fully match. Its limit is that it is a snapshot of one moment, not an account of the whole war.

Historians describe the whole war in two distinct phases. From the invasion on May 15 until the first truce in June 1948, documented accounts describe Israeli forces as initially inferior in both numbers and arms. That early disadvantage was real, and the record does not dismiss it. After the truce, new recruits and arms supplies, including the Czech shipments described earlier in this part of the book ([scene:cz_s01|the Czech arms]), allowed Israel to seize the initiative decisively. One historical anthology states that for most of the rest of the war, excluding that initial period, "the Jews were superior in personnel, equipment, logistics and organization." [cite:jvl_israel_studies_1948]

By the later stages the reversal was plain in the numbers. Documented accounts state that Israel's army outnumbered the combined Arab armies by roughly 2 to 1. The Arab states reinforced their own forces as the war went on, but historians document that they never matched the pace of Israeli growth in men and arms. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] That final advantage does not mean the outcome was inevitable or easy. It means the war's end looked very different from its opening weeks.

Why, then, has the image of overwhelming odds proved so durable? Part of the answer lies in its sources. Many of the most widely repeated figures for the "tiny, outnumbered Israel" account trace back to estimates from John Glubb, commander of Jordan's Arab Legion during the war. Historians who have worked on the documented order of battle note that, as commander of one of the fighting armies, Glubb had a direct and plausible incentive to inflate Israeli numbers and play down Arab ones. [cite:decolonizepalestine_military_balance] This is a reason for caution, not proof of deliberate falsification. But it is a caution rarely mentioned when his figures are cited as neutral fact.

Set against the full record, both popular versions fail. The underdog story holds only for the war's opening weeks, and even there it leans on one interested commander's disputed figures. The sympathy story fares no better. It has to ignore a dated military reversal, the arms deliveries that came with it, and a final 2 to 1 advantage that owed nothing to international goodwill and a great deal to logistics, organization and weapons that arrived and were used.
