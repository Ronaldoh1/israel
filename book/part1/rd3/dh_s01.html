Jacob de Haan and the First Political Killing

Jacob Israël de Haan was born in the Netherlands in 1881. A poet and a lawyer, he came to Palestine in 1919 as a newspaper correspondent and taught at the government law school in Jerusalem, the Mandate's law classes [cite:w_dehaan]. In 1924 he was shot dead in Jerusalem. His life there shows that organized Jewish dissent from Zionism existed inside Palestine, that it was carried to the British and to Arab leaders, and that on at least one occasion it was answered with gunfire.

De Haan arrived sympathetic to Zionism. Over time he crossed to the other side of the argument. He became the political spokesman of the Haredi, or strictly Orthodox, community and its most effective political voice in English, writing for British newspapers and lobbying Mandate officials [cite:w_dehaan].

The community he spoke for was the "old Yishuv," the Orthodox Jewish population of Jerusalem, Safed, Hebron and Tiberias, which had lived in the country for generations before political Zionism appeared. Its leaders, including Rabbi Yosef Chaim Sonnenfeld, rejected a secular Jewish state on religious grounds. They also objected to the Zionist Organization speaking in their name. Agudat Israel, founded in 1912, gave that objection an organized form [cite:w_dehaan]. De Haan's case to the British was precise, not sweeping. The Zionist Organization, he argued, was one political movement among Jews, not the representative of all Jews in Palestine. For a community whose presence in Jerusalem long predated the Zionist movement, that distinction was the whole point [cite:w_dehaan].

His boldest step was diplomatic. De Haan sought a separate understanding between Orthodox Jews and Arab leaders. His premise was that Jews who did not seek a state need not be in conflict with the Arab majority. In 1924 he met the Hashemite leadership in Transjordan. He was preparing to travel to London with an anti-Zionist Orthodox delegation when he was killed [cite:w_dehaan].

On 30 June 1924, as he left evening prayers at a synagogue on Jaffa Road in Jerusalem, de Haan was shot three times. He died soon afterward. No one was charged. The Zionist leadership publicly condemned the killing; Orthodox leaders blamed Zionist circles. For sixty years the question of who had ordered it remained formally open [cite:w_dehaan].

Part of the answer came in 1985. Avraham Tehomi, by then an old man, had once been a member of the Haganah, the Zionist movement's armed wing, and later co-founded the Irgun. On Israeli television he said that he had shot de Haan on the Haganah's orders. He named the source of the order as Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, who later became Israel's second president and had died in 1963 [cite:w_dehaan]. That last claim rests on Tehomi's word alone, and some historians dispute it. The confession itself stands on firmer ground: the man who fired the shots said the killing was a Haganah operation [cite:w_dehaan]. Historians widely describe it as the first political assassination in the Jewish community of Palestine.

The familiar account of the years before 1948 treats Jewish opposition to Zionism as marginal and peaceful, something that simply faded away. De Haan's story does not fit it. Organized Jewish opposition to Zionism existed inside the country. It was argued openly, to British officials and to Arab leaders. And, by its gunman's own admission, the movement's armed wing killed its most effective spokesman. What the record does not establish is a settled chain of command above the gunman. Beyond Tehomi's word, who gave the order remains an open question.

The killing also marks a point in the wider story. Tehomi would go on to help found the Irgun, one of the rival armed organizations traced later in [scene:s07e2|the paramilitary line]. By then the first political assassination in the Jewish community of Palestine had already taken place. Its target was a Jew who argued that Zionism did not speak for all Jews in the country.
