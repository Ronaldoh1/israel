The Idea Appears, 1862–1885

The familiar account holds that Zionism is millennia old and that Jews always yearned for the land of Israel. Part of that is true: the hope of return lived for centuries in prayer, as the previous section showed. But a prayer is not a program. Zionism as a written political idea appears in the documents between the 1860s and the 1880s, in scattered proposals that answered a modern problem, modern European antisemitism. The record of those decades also shows two things the familiar account leaves out. When persecuted Jews in the Russian Empire could choose where to go, the great majority chose America. And the largest Jewish religious movement in the United States formally rejected a return to Palestine in 1885.

The first of the proposals came from an unlikely author. Moses Hess (1812–1875) was a German-Jewish philosopher and an early, influential figure in socialist thought. In the 1840s he collaborated and corresponded with Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, and some historians credit him with introducing Marx to communist ideas before Marx had fully formulated his own. [cite:wiki_moses_hess] In 1862 Hess published Rome and Jerusalem: The Last National Question. Its argument was that Jewish assimilation into European society had failed to end antisemitism, and that Jews therefore needed national self-determination. It proposed, explicitly, a socialist Jewish commonwealth in Palestine, joining Hess's two commitments, socialism and Jewish nationalism, in a single dated text. [cite:wiki_moses_hess]

Almost no one listened. The book sold poorly and drew little serious engagement from socialist or Jewish intellectual circles. Mainstream European Jewish thought in the 1860s and 1870s was moving toward emancipation and integration, not national separation, and Hess's socialist nationalism belonged to neither camp. He died in 1875 a minor and largely unread figure. [cite:wiki_moses_hess] Only after the Zionist Congress of 1897 was he rediscovered and placed in the movement's own origin story as a founding intellectual, a common pattern in political movements, which canonize an ignored forerunner once a later effort succeeds. His actual influence on the events of 1897 was close to nil. There is no strong documented evidence that Herzl, who reached his own position chiefly through the Dreyfus Affair, was significantly influenced by Hess's book before writing his own. [cite:wiki_moses_hess] Hess is best read as an early, isolated voice making a real proto-Zionist argument thirty-five years before the movement organized, not as the hidden origin of a plan that then unfolded on schedule.

The pressure that turned scattered ideas into a wider response came from Russia. The Romanov Empire was home to roughly five million Jews, the largest Jewish population in the world, confined by law to the Pale of Settlement. In March 1881 Tsar Alexander II was assassinated. Waves of pogroms followed across the Pale: organized mob violence against Jewish communities, in many cases with the complicity of local officials. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] Under his son Alexander III, who reigned from 1881 to 1894, the state then wrote the hostility into law. The May Laws of 1882 formally restricted where Jews could live, what they could study and how they could earn a living. Further decrees imposed quotas on Jewish admission to universities and barred Jews from rural areas. [cite:wiki_may_laws] This was antisemitism as state policy: specific, named legal restrictions with a date, not a vague climate of prejudice. The pogroms and the May Laws were separate pressures, and the record keeps them apart. The law was the legislated escalation; the pogroms that came before it were often the more immediately violent cause of flight, and not every emigrant's decision can be traced to the law itself. [cite:wiki_may_laws][cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire]

The wave of 1881–1884 was only the first. A second, from 1903 to 1906, included the Kishinev pogrom of April 1903, which killed 49 people, wounded hundreds and became an international scandal. [cite:wiki_kishinev_pogrom] The third, during the Russian Civil War of 1917–1921, was by far the deadliest, with an estimated 100,000 to 250,000 Jews killed in Ukraine and the surrounding regions. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] Those later waves belong to later parts of this library. They are named here because the three should not be flattened into one: the death tolls differ by orders of magnitude, and the founding texts of the 1880s answered the first wave, not the later ones.

The first territorial answer came from Odessa. Leon Pinsker (1821–1891) was a physician who had been decorated by the Tsarist state earlier in his life for public service and who had supported Jewish integration into Russian society: a model of the assimilated, patriotic Russian Jew. [cite:wiki_leon_pinsker] The pogroms of 1881 and the May Laws of 1882 convinced him that integration offered no safety. His pamphlet Auto-Emancipation, published in 1882, argued that antisemitism was a permanent, almost pathological feature of the nations among whom Jews lived as a perpetual minority. Jews would never be granted true equality as guests; they needed a territory of their own, where they would be the hosts, and they should stop relying on the goodwill of host nations. [cite:wiki_auto_emancipation] The argument was political, territorial and secular. It answered a safety problem; it did not invoke a scriptural promise.

Pinsker did not specify Palestine. He considered any suitable territory. One account documents him as open to alternatives including Argentina; another says he at points leaned toward a location in North America or Argentina rather than Palestine. [cite:wiki_leon_pinsker][cite:wiki_auto_emancipation] He and other early organizers of the Hovevei Zion societies discussed several possible territories before the movement's center of gravity settled on Palestine, a choice reinforced by the religious and historical resonance that a purely territorial safety argument did not by itself require. [cite:wiki_leon_pinsker] At its written origin, then, political Zionism was territorially agnostic. Herzl's Der Judenstaat would weigh Argentina and Palestine again in 1896, and the debates over a British offer of East African territory came later still. Exactly when Palestine became fixed is itself given differently in the sources: one account places it with Herzl and his Congress, another, the account of Herzl's later negotiations, with the movement's Russian base in 1905. Either way, the Palestine focus was a decision taken later, not the starting premise.

The strongest form of the case Pinsker made deserves to be stated as he made it. Its premise was empirical: an assimilated, decorated, loyal subject had watched the state that decorated him legislate against his community within a year of mob violence against it. Its conclusion, that Jews could be safe only where they were not a minority, was a judgment about the future. The record of 1881–1882 supports the premise. The conclusion is the political argument that the rest of this library follows, together with the costs it would carry for the people already living in the land Pinsker's successors chose.

The people fleeing the Empire made their own choice, and it is the clearest evidence the period offers. Roughly two million Jews left Imperial Russia in the pogrom era. The sources give the window differently: 1881 to 1914 in one account, 1881 to 1924 in another. Documented estimates put 90–95 percent of these emigrants in the United States and about 3 percent in Ottoman Palestine. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire] These shares are estimates, and they rest on a single reference source; they should be read as orders of magnitude, not as counts. Even so, the order of magnitude is unambiguous. On the source's own reckoning, those who could choose chose America over Palestine by a factor of about thirty to one. Jewish communities in New York, Chicago, Philadelphia and Boston grew from modest pre-1881 numbers into the multi-million American Jewish diaspora that exists today. The Lower East Side, not the colonies of the First Aliyah, was where most of the exodus went. [cite:wiki_pogroms_russian_empire]

What does that choice show? It shows that people fleeing for their lives went, overwhelmingly, to the country that offered civic equality and economic opportunity, rather than to the land the liturgy held sacred. It is the central evidentiary difficulty for any account that treats Palestine as the natural or inevitable destination of persecuted European Jews in this period. It also explains why Zionism had to be built as a political movement: the ancient longing, whatever its place in prayer, did not by itself move populations toward Palestine. What the choice does not show is that the 3 percent were unimportant. The settlements that smaller group founded, counted in the next section, shaped much of what followed. One further consequence is a matter of interpretation rather than record, and is offered as such: the demographic and financial center of world Jewry moved to the United States in exactly these decades, and when the political-Zionist project needed a great-power patron in the twentieth century, the community that had chosen America became central to securing one. The migration that bypassed Palestine helped, two generations later, to make the American relationship with Israel possible.

Opposition had a formal voice within three years of Pinsker's pamphlet. In 1885 American Reform Judaism adopted the Pittsburgh Platform, which stated:

"We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine..."

The sentence continues, in the platform's text, to reject "the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state." [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] This was the founding-generation position of what was then the largest Jewish religious denomination in the United States, adopted three years after Auto-Emancipation and twelve years before the Basel Congress. It was not this book's characterization of Reform Judaism but the movement's own words. Nor was it permanent: Reform Judaism embraced Zionism only in the mid-twentieth century, a documented later reversal. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] It was one of several Jewish objections to the national idea present from its beginning, alongside the Orthodox reading of the Three Oaths set out earlier in this chapter, and the socialist and cultural objections told in the section on Dreyfus and Herzl.

Two more facts belong to these decades. The first concerns words. "Semitic" was coined in 1781 by the German scholar August Ludwig von Schlözer, from Shem, son of Noah in Genesis 10. It named a language family, including Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic and Amharic, and its speakers, and it carried no political charge. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] That changed in 1879, when Wilhelm Marr coined "antisemitism", three years before Pinsker's pamphlet. The modern political vocabulary that later arguments would turn on was being assembled in the same narrow window in which the earliest Zionist texts were written. The overlap is one of timing; no causal link between the two is documented. The full history of both words is told in Volume I, in [scene:s00b|Semitic is not Jewish] and [scene:s00b0|the word 'antisemitism'].

The second fact concerns the land. Palestine was not empty. Its majority population in this period were Palestinian Arabs, speakers of a Semitic language. Two peer-reviewed population-genetics studies, Nebel and colleagues in 2000 and Behar and colleagues in 2010, describe them as descended from populations continuously present in the region since antiquity. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000][cite:behar_2010_nature] The colonies of the First Aliyah were founded on land that was already inhabited.

It is often said that this population was named nowhere in the founding literature of political Zionism. That claim needs testing, and it does not survive in its strongest form. It holds for the earliest written texts as they are summarized here: neither Hess's argument nor Pinsker's, as the sources describe them, addresses the people already living on the land. That absence is a real fact about those texts, though it is not by itself proof of malice. But the absence was noticed from inside the movement, and early. Ahad Ha'am proposed a cultural rather than a political Zionism. [cite:wiki_ahad_haam] He raised exactly this concern in writing in 1891, less than a decade after Pinsker. And Herzl's own diary for 12 June 1895, published only after his death, contains a passage about spiriting "the penniless population" across the border, whose meaning and reference scholars dispute; it is examined, with both readings, in [scene:s03|Dreyfus and Herzl]. The defensible statement is narrower than the common one: the inhabitants were absent from the first proto-Zionist programs, and their presence was raised in writing within the movement within a decade.

Several questions the record available here leaves open. It does not give the exact years over which the two million left, since its two accounts end in 1914 and in 1924, and the 90–95 percent and 3 percent shares are estimates from one reference source, not counts. It does not quote Pinsker's pamphlet on the question of territory, so how far he leaned toward North America or Argentina, rather than simply leaving the question open, rests on two summaries that word it differently. It records Ahad Ha'am's 1891 concern but not the text in which he raised it. And it does not establish any line of influence from Hess to Pinsker or from either to Herzl: the ideas appeared one after another, in answer to the same pressures, but the documents do not show one man reading and building on the other.
