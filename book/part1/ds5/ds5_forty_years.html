Forty Years Behind a Door

In 1953 G. Lancaster Harding, the director of Jordan's Department of Antiquities, and Roland de Vaux appointed an international team of scholars to publish the scrolls. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] The scrolls from Cave 1 appeared in a reasonably timely fashion, in 1955 and 1956, and those from the minor caves in 1962. [cite:ds_vq_pubcontro] The great hoard from Cave 4, about 575 manuscripts, was another matter. [cite:ds_jta_pirated] The first official volume of Cave 4 texts did not appear until 1968. [cite:ds_vq_pubcontro] For the first forty years, according to the Israel Antiquities Authority, "fewer than a dozen scholars" controlled the study of the fragments, and their small numbers "prevented speedy publication". [cite:ds_iaa_discovery]

The team also controlled the tools. Between 1957 and 1960 four scholars compiled a concordance listing every word in the non-biblical texts, with the document it came from and the words around it; the team kept it secret until 1988. [cite:ds_jta_pirated] Scholars outside the team could not see the unpublished texts at all. Britannica records the critics' charge that the official editors "monopolized access to the scrolls". [cite:ds_brit_dss]

The end began with a scandal. In 1990 the team's chief editor, John Strugnell of Harvard Divinity School, gave an interview to the Israeli newspaper Ha'aretz, published on 9 November, in which he was quoted as calling Judaism "a horrible religion" and "originally racist". [cite:ds_jta_strugnell] Fellow editors removed him in December, citing his health; others told The New York Times of a drinking problem. [cite:ds_jta_strugnell] A senior team member, Eugene Ulrich, called the remarks "totally repugnant" and admitted that "there has been too tight a grip on the scrolls". At that point, between 35 and 45 per cent of the texts in the editors' hands were still unpublished. [cite:ds_jta_strugnell]

Two outsiders broke the lock. Ben-Zion Wacholder, a professor at Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati, and his doctoral student Martin Abegg used a computer to rebuild texts from a copy of the secret concordance, and in September 1991 the Biblical Archaeology Society, edited by Hershel Shanks, published the result. Shanks said it broke "the lock a small group of scholars have hitherto maintained on the scrolls". [cite:ds_jta_pirated] The official team called its reliability "highly questionable"; both sides agreed it was no more than about 80 per cent accurate. [cite:ds_jta_pirated] The same year it emerged that a duplicate set of photographs had been deposited for safekeeping at the Huntington Library in California, and the library opened them to researchers. The official restrictions were lifted. [cite:ds_vq_pubcontro] [cite:ds_brit_dss]

After that the work went quickly. Under a new chief editor, Emanuel Tov, appointed by the Israel Antiquities Authority, about a hundred scholars worked on the texts, and most of the official editions were published by 2001. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] One fight remained. The Israeli scholar Elisha Qimron sued Shanks for publishing his reconstruction of a scroll without permission, and in August 2000 Israel's Supreme Court ruled that while no one could own the ancient text, Qimron's deduction of the roughly forty per cent that was missing came from his "creative depths" and was his copyright; Shanks was fined $10,000. [cite:ds_abc_qimron] The court drew the line exactly where this shelf has drawn it: the ancient words belong to no one, but the reconstruction, the scholar's reading of them, can be owned.

The pattern of The Canon appears here in a modern form. A small group held a body of sacred texts; access depended on membership; the tool that would have let others read them was kept secret for thirty years; and the gate opened not because the holders chose to open it, but because outsiders copied the key and a library released the photographs.
