The Passfield White Paper and the "Black Letter"

In the wake of the violence of 1929, Britain set out to learn what had caused it. Two British inquiries found that land sales and immigration were creating a landless Arab class. London acted on their findings. Then, within four months and under political pressure, it effectively withdrew the policy. British policy in Palestine is sometimes described as moving in one steady direction until the White Paper of 1939. The years 1930 and 1931 show otherwise. They offer the clearest case before 1939 of the Zionist movement reversing a British policy decision in a matter of months.

The first inquiry was the Shaw Commission, sent to investigate the 1929 violence. Its report of March 1930 found that the fundamental cause was Arab fear, economic and political, of being displaced through Jewish immigration and land purchase. It recommended that Britain clarify its policy on both [cite:w_shaw].

The second inquiry went further. Sir John Hope Simpson surveyed land and agriculture and reported in October 1930. He found that a large proportion of rural Arab families lacked enough land to live on. Evictions after land sales were producing a landless class. And without development, there was no margin for further Jewish agricultural settlement [cite:w_hopesimpson]. His report is the most detailed British land study of the Mandate period.

On that basis the Colonial Secretary, Lord Passfield, issued a White Paper, a formal statement of government policy, in October 1930. It tied immigration to Arab as well as Jewish unemployment and tightened control over land transfers [cite:w_passfield]. It was the first serious attempt to slow the settlement machinery the Mandate had set in motion.

The response was swift. Chaim Weizmann resigned as president of the Jewish Agency in protest. Senior Conservative and Liberal politicians attacked the policy in Parliament and in the press as a breach of the Mandate, and the press campaign was intense. In a closely watched by-election in Whitechapel, a constituency with a large Jewish electorate, Labour's vote fell [cite:w_passfield]. All of this pressure landed on a minority Labour government.

On 13 February 1931 the Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald, sent Weizmann a letter that was read into the record of the House of Commons. It "interpreted" the White Paper in a way that removed its restrictions. Arab leaders called it the "Black Letter" [cite:w_passfield]. A policy grounded in two official inquiries had been effectively withdrawn within four months of its publication.

What followed can be measured. Jewish immigration rose sharply, from about 4,000 in 1931 to roughly 30,000 in 1933 and to over 60,000 in 1935, its peak for the Mandate period. The reversal was not the only cause. The restrictions had been lifted, and from 1933 the Nazi takeover in Germany, described in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse], added its own push. The Arab Revolt began the following year, in 1936.

The episode matters less for its outcome than for what it reveals about how policy was made. London's own inquiries had identified a problem and recommended limits, and London's own government had adopted them. Then political pressure in London, applied through a resignation, parliamentary attack, the press and an electoral setback, overrode those findings.
