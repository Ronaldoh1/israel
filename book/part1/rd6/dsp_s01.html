Where the Refugees Went, and the Diaspora That Came Before Them

Two Palestinian migrations are often run together, and the confusion costs both of them their history. The first began under the Ottoman Empire, some seventy years before the state of Israel existed, and carried families from a handful of towns around Bethlehem to the Americas. The second was the flight and expulsion of 1948, which carried some 700,000 people across the new armistice lines into the neighbouring Arab states and into the parts of Palestine that Israel did not hold. A common assumption holds that the Palestinian communities of Latin America are made up of 1948 refugees who found open doors there. The record shows an older population, a different legal status, and, in El Salvador at least, doors that were not open.

Palestinian emigration to the Americas began in the 1870s. Most of the early migrants were Christian families from Bethlehem, Beit Jala and Beit Sahour. They travelled on Ottoman papers, and so in their new countries they were long called "Turcos", Turks. [cite:palquest_sv] The movement continued into the 1930s. Its scale, relative to the towns that sent it, was large: El Salvador's population of Bethlehem descent is described today as several times larger than Bethlehem's own. [cite:palquest_sv]

Chile became the largest centre. Its Palestinian-descended population is the largest anywhere outside the Arab world, with estimates commonly running into the hundreds of thousands. In 1920, nearly three decades before the Nakba, the Santiago football club Club Deportivo Palestino was founded; it plays in Chile's national first division. [cite:w_palchile] [cite:w_palestino]

El Salvador shows how little the doors were open. Its Palestinian community, estimated at roughly 70,000 to 100,000 people and overwhelmingly of Bethlehem origin, is described as the third-largest in Latin America after Chile and Honduras. In 1921 the Salvadoran state declared Arabs and Chinese to be "pernicious" races, a documented act of exclusion written into policy. Salvadoran elites looked down on the "Turcos" as peddlers. The community built its standing in commerce despite that discrimination, and in time rose to economic and political prominence. [cite:palquest_sv]

The rise reached the presidency twice. Antonio Saca won the presidency in 2004, and his opponent, Schafik Handal, was also of Palestinian descent. Nayib Bukele was elected in 2019; his paternal grandparents had emigrated from Jerusalem and Bethlehem in the early twentieth century, and his father became a well-known imam. [cite:palquest_sv] [cite:jweekly_bukele] Bukele's own politics on Israel are pro-Israel, a reminder that descent does not decide a person's position. [cite:jweekly_bukele]

The refugees of 1948 were a different population, uprooted at a different date, and held a different legal status. The 1948 war resulted in approximately 700,000 Palestinians fleeing or being expelled from their homes, the event Palestinians call the Nakba, "catastrophe". They settled in Gaza, which was under Egyptian administration; in the West Bank, under Jordanian administration; and in Lebanon, Syria and Jordan itself. [cite:npr_hamashistory] From 1950 the United Nations Relief and Works Agency, UNRWA, registered them as refugees. Treating the earlier emigrants as Nakba refugees erases both histories: that of the Ottoman-era families who built communities under discrimination, and that of the 1948 refugees, whose displacement remains unresolved.

Where the 1948 refugees stood depended on which state they had reached. Jordan was the exception. Under its 1954 nationality law, it gave citizenship to most of the Palestinians who had come after 1948. [cite:jns_arab_citizenship] [cite:adl_refugees] According to the sources cited here, it hosts more than 2 million registered Palestinian refugees, most of them citizens, although Human Rights Watch has documented arbitrary revocations of that citizenship. [cite:jns_arab_citizenship] [cite:adl_refugees]

Lebanon took the opposite course, and the choice was made in Beirut. Palestinian refugees in Lebanon have never been eligible for Lebanese citizenship as a class, lack political rights, and have had no access to most state-provided health and education services, a policy that has persisted from 1948 to the present according to European Union Agency for Asylum assessments current through 2025. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy] The application was not uniform in the early years. Middle- and upper-class Palestinians, and Christian Palestinians in particular, could obtain Lebanese citizenship, and some did, so that religion and class shaped individual outcomes inside a restrictive framework. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy]

The reasoning was stated openly. Lebanon's political system allocates parliamentary seats, the presidency and the premiership by religious sect, under the power-sharing formula of the 1943 National Pact, later revised by the Taif Agreement of 1989. Lebanese officials and analysts across several decades have named the central concern: that naturalizing a refugee population that was overwhelmingly Sunni Muslim would upset that sectarian balance. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy] The restrictions that followed are only partly dated in these sources. Palestinians were barred from dozens of professions outside the camps, and a property law of 2001 barred them from owning real estate. These measures were justified as preventing tawteen, permanent settlement. [cite:fmr_lebanon] [cite:aj_lebanon_property] According to the sources cited here, Lebanon counts about 400,000 registered refugees, roughly 10 percent of its population. [cite:fmr_lebanon] The same sources record twelve official camps run by UNRWA and more than 150 informal ones, on land that, in Amnesty International's description, has "barely changed" in seven decades despite the growth of the population living on it. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy] The longer story is told in [scene:ilb_s03|Lebanon's refugee policy].

Egypt and the Gulf states offered residency without a path to citizenship. Behind these different national policies sat a common line. The Arab League instructed its members not to grant citizenship to the refugees, "to avoid dissolution of their identity and protect their right of return". Its Casablanca Protocol of 1965 later promised refugees the right to work and to travel, but it was unevenly applied. [cite:fmr_lebanon]

Two readings of these policies run side by side, and each has a real case. The Arab League's own stated reason was protective: to give the refugees citizenship elsewhere would dissolve their identity and give up the claim that they should go home. [cite:fmr_lebanon] Critics, including Israeli and pro-Israel analysts, argue that the same policy kept the refugees in limbo as a political tool. The record shows that the states did not act as one. Jordan absorbed refugees as citizens; Lebanon restricted them, and gave as its reason its own sectarian balance as well as the politics of return; Egypt and the Gulf offered residence only. The argument has continued to the present. After 7 October 2023, Egypt's President el-Sisi and Jordan's King Abdullah refused to take in people from Gaza; analysts noted that, historically, Palestinians forced to leave have not been allowed to return, and Egypt warned of militants in Sinai. [cite:pbs_egypt_refugees] The full treatment is in [scene:sq_s20|the question of why Arab states did not absorb the refugees].

The camps also became a political base. The displacement of 1948 is the foundational fact around which every later Palestinian political movement organized. [cite:npr_hamashistory] The Palestine Liberation Organization was founded in 1964, and Fatah, Yasser Arafat's secular nationalist movement, became its dominant faction by 1969. [cite:npr_hamashistory] That history, and what came after it, belongs to a later volume and is traced in [scene:hma_src_s02|Palestinian political origins].

What the record here does not settle is the weight of motive. The documented policies are clear, and so are the reasons each government gave. Whether the League line served the refugees' own interest, or chiefly the interests of the states that adopted it, is a judgment the sources cited here do not make. Nor do these sources give a count, for the 1950s, of how many refugees reached each host state.
