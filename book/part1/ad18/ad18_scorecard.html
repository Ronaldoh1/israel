The Scorecard: Seven Promises, Graded in the Open

A ledger records; a grade judges. The difference matters, and this section keeps it visible. A grade on a promise is not a fact found in a dataset in the way a poverty rate is. It is a judgment built from facts, and it is only as sound as the rules used to build it. So the scorecard below follows one discipline throughout: every sub-score is tied to a named figure from a named source; every rule that turns a figure into a score is printed; every weight is disclosed; and the arithmetic is shown, so that a reader who weighs things differently can recompute the result from the same numbers. The aggregate grade at the end is this book's editorial synthesis, not a discovery. The method follows the one the companion volume used to grade two national systems, adapted to grade one system against its own promises. [scene:us22_method|US System vs Israel System: how the scorecard grades]

The seven promises

The promises graded are the ones the Dream has been sold on, in the words of curricula and presidents of both parties traced in Part One: that a child will have a better life than his or her parents; that a working family can own a home; that steady work brings a secure job and a decent living; that a job brings health care; that college is a ladder; that a working life ends in a secure retirement; and that the rules are fair. [scene:ad1_as_taught|Part One: the Dream as taught] [cite:ad1_obama_2011] [cite:ad1_clinton_1992] [cite:ad1_bush_2002] Each is graded on four sub-scores, and the grade asks only whether the promise was kept on its own terms. It does not ask whether a given policy was good for the country, a question that depends on values no score can settle.

The four sub-scores and their rules

Fulfilment asks whether the promise is kept for those it was made to, and uses five status labels: delivered (4 points), meaning it holds on its own terms for about three in four or more; delivered with modification (3), meaning it holds for most but at a materially higher cost or in a narrower form; partial (2), meaning it holds for roughly half to two-thirds; attempted but blocked (1); and not delivered (0). Breadth asks whether the promise holds across groups: it divides the rate of the worst-off documented group by that of the best-off on the same measure, and scores 4 at 0.90 or above, 3 from 0.75, 2 from 0.60, 1 from 0.40 and 0 below. Reach asks whether the share for whom the promise is within reach has grown or shrunk since the earliest comparable point: 4 if it improved by 5 per cent or more, 3 if it changed by less than 5 per cent either way, 2 if it fell by 5 to 15 per cent, 1 if by 15 to 30 per cent, and 0 if by more than 30 per cent. Durability asks how the promise is backed: 4 if by a federal statute funded for the long term, 3 if by statute with a dated funding gap or enacted cuts, 2 if partly by statute and partly by employer or market choice, 1 if mainly by market or employer choice or if key protections were repealed or vacated in the last two years, and 0 if by nothing.

The weights, 40, 30, 20 and 10 per cent, put most weight on whether the promise is kept for the people it was made to and least on legal form. They are a choice. A reader who thinks breadth should count as much as fulfilment, or that durability matters more in an era of rules repealed within a year, can change them; the tests at the end show how much the result moves when they do.

The grades

The arithmetic for the first row shows how every row works. The promise of a better life than one's parents is partial: about half of children born in the early 1980s out-earned their parents, which scores 2. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] On breadth, a Black child born to the bottom fifth reaches the top fifth 2.5 per cent of the time against 10.6 per cent for a white child; 2.5 divided by 10.6 is 0.24, below 0.40, which scores 0. [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] On reach, the share fell from about 92 to about 50 per cent between the 1940 and 1984 birth cohorts, a fall of 46 per cent (42 divided by 92), which scores 0. On durability, the outcome is set by the market and backed by no statute, which scores 1. The weighted sum is 0.4 × 2 + 0.3 × 0 + 0.2 × 0 + 0.1 × 1 = 0.9; multiplied by 2.5, it gives 2.3 out of 10 (2.25 rounded).

Three rows need a word on the figures chosen. For a secure job, the fulfilment figure is a household's ability to meet a small emergency, the measure the Federal Reserve's annual survey uses for financial security, set beside the ALICE count of households below a local cost-of-living budget, a measure built by a United Way research group and higher than the official poverty count. [cite:ad9_shed2025] [cite:ad14_alice_national] Against those, official poverty among full-time, year-round workers was 1.6 per cent in 2025, which would grade the promise delivered on the narrowest reading. [cite:us18_crs_pov2025] For health, the reach figure joins a Census estimate for 2009 with the 2025 release, and survey methods changed in between, so the size of the gain is approximate; the uninsured share fell from nearly 17 per cent to 7.9 per cent, and any gain of 5 per cent or more in the insured share scores 4, about half the gain measured here. [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] [cite:us16_census_2025] For fair rules, the reach figure is a single mechanism, the spread of mandatory arbitration, measured by a survey of employers run by Cornell University for the Economic Policy Institute, a labour-aligned research group; it is the clearest long-run measure of access to court the volume found, and it is narrower than the promise it stands for. [cite:ad15_epi_colvin]

Costs and distribution, reported but not scored

Under this scorecard's rules, the cost of a promise and who gains or loses from it are reported beside the grade rather than folded into it, because how much a reader should mind a higher price depends on what it buys. They are the strongest reasons to read the two highest grades with care. The home that 65 per cent of households own costs about 5.0 years of median income against 3.2 in the 1990s, and ownership among householders under 35 was 35.2 per cent in mid-2026. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] The health coverage that all but 7.9 per cent had for at least part of 2025 costs $15,474 a person a year and $28,303 for a family plan at work. [cite:us16_cms_nhe2024] [cite:ad8_kff_ehbs2026] On distribution, the 2025 budget law, scored by the Congressional Budget Office, lowers resources for the poorest tenth of households by about $1,200 a year and raises them for the top tenth by about $13,600, and the Social Security trust funds that carry the retirement grade are projected to pay about 83 per cent of scheduled benefits from 2034 unless Congress acts. [cite:ad11_tr_pl11921] [cite:ad10_crs_tr26]

Testing the result

A grade that flips under a reasonable change of rules is a grade about the rules. This one was recomputed four ways. Fulfilment alone, the sub-score closest to a plain fact and the single most defensible number, sums to 16 of a possible 28 points (2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2), or 5.7 out of 10. With the four sub-scores weighted equally, the seven promises total 51 of a possible 112 points, or 4.6. If the secure-job promise is graded on full-time workers' poverty, fulfilment rises to 4, that promise's grade to 7.0, and the aggregate to 5.0 (35.0 divided by 7). If the mobility promise is graded on the most favourable alternative estimate, 68 per cent out-earning their parents, reach becomes a 26 per cent fall and scores 1, that grade rises to 2.8 and the aggregate to 4.8.

Every test keeps the aggregate between 4.6 and 5.7 out of 10, and none lifts it to 6.5, the band this scorecard treats as a promise kept. That is the finding of the scorecard, and it is a finding about a synthesis: by this book's disclosed rules, the American Dream as a bundle of promises is about half kept.

Which promises hold, for whom, and which fall short

Read by band, the seven grades fall into three groups. Two promises hold, with modification, at 6.5 or above: health care, at 6.8, and a home, at 6.5. Both reach most households, and both are carried by federal law built for the purpose; both cost far more than they did, and the home promise is harder to reach for the young and for Black households. Two promises hold for some, between 4.0 and 6.4: a secure retirement, at 5.3, where Social Security keeps its part and the private account leaves the low-paid behind, and a secure job, at 5.0, where real incomes have risen while more than a third of adults could not cover a $400 emergency with cash or its equivalent. Three fall short on these measures, below 4.0: fair rules, at 3.8; college as a ladder, at 3.5, where the degree still pays its holders but the ladder's lowest rungs are harder to reach for children from poor families; and a better life than one's parents, at 2.3, the promise on which the Dream was first defined and on which the record has moved most. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] [cite:ad10_census_a3]

The grades also show where the promise works as taught. Every promise scores at least 2 on fulfilment: none is a promise that was never kept. Three score the top mark on reach, a secure living, health care and retirement, where the share for whom the promise is within reach is larger today than at the earliest comparable point. The low grades come not from absence but from narrowing: the promises still hold for many, and less often for those who started lower. Part Seventeen's families show what those bands look like in a single household's budget.
