"Transfer" Enters the Record

Alongside its partition map, the Peel Commission's July 1937 report made a recommendation about people. It proposed an "exchange of population," modelled explicitly on the Greek-Turkish exchange of 1923. Approximately 225,000 Arabs would be moved out of the territory allotted to the Jewish state, and about 1,250 Jews out of the territory allotted to the Arab state. The report stated that, in the last resort, the exchange should be compulsory. It was the first official British document to put population transfer in writing. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

A common assumption holds that the idea of removing the Arab population from a future Jewish state originated with Israeli extremists after 1948. The record places it earlier and higher. It appeared first not in the program of a fringe group but in the report of a British royal commission, a decade before the war of 1948. The numbers themselves show how lopsided the proposed exchange was: roughly 225,000 people on one side of the ledger and roughly 1,250 on the other.

How Zionist leaders reacted in private is documented in their own papers, and here the record becomes genuinely contested. David Ben-Gurion wrote to his son Amos in October 1937. In that letter he welcomed partition as a stage toward a larger state and discussed the Arab population. His diary and letters from the same period discuss compulsory transfer as a possibility. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] One sentence in the Amos letter, however, has become the subject of a long dispute. It has been quoted as an expression of support for expelling Arabs; read differently, from the handwritten Hebrew, it has been taken to say the opposite. Historians have disagreed over the wording, and some, including Benny Morris, have changed their positions on it over time. This book does not resolve that dispute. The exact meaning of that sentence remains a matter on which serious historians differ.

What is not in dispute is the response of the movement's highest body. The Twentieth Zionist Congress, which met in Zurich in August 1937, accepted partition in principle as a basis for negotiation, and it did not reject the transfer clause. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] The Arab Higher Committee, for its part, rejected the Peel report outright. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

The distinction between those two kinds of evidence matters. Whether any particular leader's private words amounted to an endorsement of compulsory transfer is a question on which the sources do not speak with one voice, and it should be stated as open. That the concept itself was on the table is a different question, and the answer is clear. Compulsory transfer entered the official record in a British royal commission report in 1937, and it was discussed, not rejected, by the Zionist movement's own Congress. It was a live subject at the top of the movement eleven years before the displacement of 1948.

The finding is limited, and it should be read that way. It does not establish what any individual intended to do a decade later, nor does it explain by itself what happened in 1948. What it does establish is narrower and firmer: the idea of moving a population to fit a partition map was not invented in the fighting of 1948. It had been written down, in an official document, by the power that governed the country, and it had been debated by the leadership of the movement that would found the state.
