The Teachers: Basilides, Valentinus and Ptolemy

The second-century church had two kinds of leader, and the Gnostic story is in large part the story of the losing kind. The bishop presided over a congregation, held its funds and claimed descent from the apostles. The teacher ran a school, as philosophers did, gathering students who chose to come, writing commentaries and passing on an interpretation. Teachers were not paid by a congregation, and their authority depended on their learning and the loyalty of their students. Three of them shaped what the opponents called Gnosticism, and each is known first through what others wrote about him.

Basilides taught in Alexandria in the first half of the century. Irenaeus attributes to him a vast cosmos of 365 heavens, whose chief he names as Abraxas, and a striking account of the crucifixion: Simon of Cyrene, who carried the cross, was crucified in Jesus's place while Jesus "stood by and laughed". Salvation, Basilides taught in this account, "belongs to the soul alone", and his followers ate meat offered to idols "without any hesitation". [cite:vt24_ah1_24] Irenaeus is a hostile witness, and his summary cannot be checked against Basilides' own books, which are lost. What the report makes clear is the kind of teaching that worried the bishops: a salvation of the soul that made martyrdom and outward observance matter less.

Valentinus came from Egypt to Rome and became the most influential Christian teacher of his generation. His school produced the most sophisticated theology of the period, and his followers spread from Rome to Alexandria and beyond. Tertullian preserves the reason, as he saw it, for the break: Valentinus "had expected to become a bishop", but another man obtained the office on the strength of having suffered for the faith, and Valentinus left the church in anger. [cite:vt24_tert_adval] The detail shows how close the rival forms of authority came. The greatest teacher of the age wanted the office, and the office went to a man whose qualification was not learning but endurance under persecution.

Ptolemy, one of Valentinus's leading students, is the only one of the three whose own words survive complete from before the Nag Hammadi find. His Letter to Flora, written to a woman who had asked him about the law of Moses, is preserved in full by Epiphanius, who quoted it in order to refute it. [cite:vt24_wiki_flora] [cite:vt24_ect_flora] The letter is calm, careful and pastoral. Ptolemy divides the law into three parts: the pure law of the Ten Commandments, "which the Savior came not to destroy but to complete"; laws mixed with injustice, which were set aside; and the symbolic law of offerings, circumcision, the sabbath and fasting, whose "names remained the same but the content was changed". [cite:vt24_ect_flora] The giver of that law, he explains, is neither the perfect God nor the devil but "the demiurge and maker of this universe", an "intermediate" figure who "can properly be called just". [cite:vt24_ect_flora]

The letter corrects the caricature at once. Ptolemy's creator is not a monster but a lesser, just administrator, and his reading of the law is a reasoned argument about scripture, the kind of argument any Christian teacher might make. It is also addressed to a woman, as a teacher to a student, without reference to a bishop. The Gnostic schools taught women and, as the next section shows, gave some of them roles that the bishops' churches were closing.

The teachers lost because their kind of authority could not be institutionalised as the bishops' could. A school depended on a teacher; when he died, it divided. A bishopric passed from holder to holder, kept its property and its lists, and could be backed by law. By the time Constantine's edict named "Valentinians" among the groups whose meeting houses were to be handed over, the teachers had no office to defend them. [cite:vt24_vc365] Their students had to choose between the church that held the buildings and a school that held only ideas.
