Who the Black Bars Protect

The survivors have asked for one thing above all, and it is simple to state. Hide who they are. Do not hide who did it to them. The law Congress passed says the same thing. It allows the department to withhold victims' names and personal details. It forbids withholding or redacting anything because it would cause "embarrassment, reputational harm, or political sensitivity", and it names who that protection is not for: "any government official, public figure, or foreign dignitary". It goes further, requiring a list of every government official and politically exposed person named in the records, with no redaction allowed on those grounds. [cite:ep8_govinfo_act]

The January 2026 release ran the other way. Survivors' names, some of them of girls who were minors at the time, were left readable, while names of men around Epstein were blacked out. [cite:ep9_wsj_43] [cite:ep7_cbs_dea2026] The survivors who have spoken publicly said so plainly. Annie Farmer called the release "beyond careless". Jess Michaels, looking at heavily blacked-out FBI records, said the redactions appeared to be "protecting someone, something". Marina Lacerda called it a double standard: the powerful men's names hidden, the survivors' left in the open. Lisa Phillips put the whole argument in one line: victims' identities should be protected, but protections meant for survivors should not be used to hide the people who enabled the abuse. [cite:ep9_ms_survivors2026] A protection written for the abused was being used, in effect, for the people around their abusers.

What is actually stopping them

Nothing physical. The department holds the records, has already published millions of pages and has the technical means to black out a victim's name while leaving the man's beside it. What stops it is a decision, and the record shows what that decision rests on.

The department applies its own long-standing policy against publicly identifying people it has not charged. The current wording of that rule, in the Justice Manual, was updated in February 2024, under the previous administration. It tells staff not to disclose the identity of uncharged people without senior approval, weighing their "privacy, safety, and reputational interests" against whether the public has "a significant need to know". [cite:ep9_emptywheel_jm2024] The rule has a real purpose. A person named in a prosecutor's file but never charged has no trial at which to answer, and the release itself showed the danger: four men read out on the floor of the House as "likely incriminated" turned out to be strangers from a police photo line-up. [cite:ep9_li_lineup] A tip in a file is not proof, and the department says many tips about prominent men were not credible. [cite:ep8_abc_cocon_memo]

But that is an argument for releasing the records with care, not for hiding them. Congress weighed the reputations of the powerful and decided, almost unanimously, that for these files they do not justify secrecy. A departmental policy does not override a statute, and whether the department's redactions comply with the Act is now before the federal court in Washington. [cite:ep9_clearinghouse_phang] The law does not ask the department to call anyone a predator. It asks it to publish what its own records say about the men who were there, and let the documents speak, while keeping the girls' names out of it.

What justice would take

Disclosure is not the same as justice, and the book should be honest about the gap. A name in a released file convicts no one. What would bring the survivors justice is what has been missing at every stage of this story: a prosecutor willing to charge. The law allows it. There is no time limit on federal charges of child sex trafficking, a 2015 amendment made the men who pay for sex with a child liable as traffickers, and no one but Epstein and Maxwell has ever been charged. [cite:ep8_lii_3299] [cite:ep8_lii_1591] [cite:ep8_abc_cocon_memo] Releasing the files would let the public, the press, Congress and the survivors' own lawyers test that evidence. Charging is a decision only the Justice Department, or a state prosecutor, can make. Both decisions sit with the same small group of officials, and so far both answers have been no.
