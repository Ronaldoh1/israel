The Wool and the Way

The name Sufi probably comes from ṣūf, wool, after the woollen garments of the early ascetics; Sufis were also called "the poor". [cite:sf_brit_sufism] The movement grew out of early Islamic asceticism, which developed as "a counterweight to the increasing worldliness" of the expanding Muslim community. [cite:sf_brit_sufism] Sufis opposed the dry legal reasoning of the jurists, but they still observed the religious law scrupulously. [cite:sf_brit_sufism]

Sufis find the root of their path in a famous report about the angel Gabriel. In it, a stranger questions the Prophet about religion, and the answers divide it into three dimensions: islam, the Five Pillars of practice; iman, the articles of faith; and ihsan, a word often glossed as "doing what is beautiful". [cite:sf_wiki_gabriel] Ihsan is defined in the version recorded by Muslim, on the authority of Umar, as "That you worship Allah as if you are seeing Him, for though you don't see Him, He, verily, sees you." [cite:sf_wiki_gabriel] The inner path of Islam takes that third dimension as its whole subject: the life of a person who worships as if seeing.

The goal of the path has a technical name. Fana is "the complete denial of self and the realization of God": the Sufi who has purified himself of worldly concerns "has 'annihilated' his individual will and 'passed away' from his own existence". Beyond it lies "the more sublime state of baqāʾ (subsistence)", reached when the Sufi regains full consciousness. [cite:sf_brit_fana]

The path was taught person to person. At first, mystical life centred on a master and a few disciples. The Persian Abu Saʿid ibn Abi al-Khayr, who died in 1049, laid the foundations of a monastic system, and real orders appeared from the twelfth century; ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani, who died in 1166, founded the first and still most important one. [cite:sf_brit_orders] A disciple swore allegiance to a master and received the khirqa, the frock, which made him part of the "chain of mystical succession and transmission". Each order's chain, its silsila, differs, but each traces back through the early master Junayd to the Prophet. [cite:sf_brit_orders] The community gathered at a centre, called a khanqah, dargah or tekke, around the shaykh; most initiates lived ordinary lives and joined the mystical services at set times. With the orders, Sufism moved beyond "the way of the chosen few" and reached the masses. [cite:sf_brit_orders]

The chain of transmission is the Sufi form of a structure this shelf keeps meeting. Irenaeus traced the authority of the bishops through a line of succession back to the apostles; the Sufi orders traced the authority of their shaykhs through a line of masters back to the Prophet. [cite:cn_iren_3_3] [cite:sf_brit_orders] Both lines guaranteed that what was taught was authentic, and both made the teacher the gate. The difference is that the Sufi chain was many chains, one for each order, with no single office at the top.
