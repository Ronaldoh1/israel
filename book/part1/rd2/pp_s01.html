The Palestinian National Movement: Seven Congresses and a Demand for Elections

The first Palestine Arab Congress opened on 27 January 1919. Its 27 delegates came from Muslim–Christian Associations across the country, organizations that brought the two communities together, and they sat until 10 February. Between 1919 and 1928, seven such congresses met, in Jerusalem, Jaffa, Haifa and Nablus. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jvl_palestine_arab_congress] The record of these meetings does not fit the familiar claim that Palestinians had no leaders and no political demands before Israel existed. From 1919 Palestinian Muslims and Christians organized together, chose an executive, sent delegations to London and put a clear demand to Britain: an elected government.

Their program was consistent. The congresses called for Palestine to be part of the independent Arab state Britain had promised during the war. They asked for "a national government responsible to a representative assembly," chosen by the country's Arabic-speaking inhabitants. They rejected the Balfour Declaration and mass Jewish immigration. [cite:wiki_palestine_arab_congress] [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive] In effect, the Palestinian leadership was asking for the principle set out in the League's own Covenant: that the wishes of the inhabitants should weigh in the choice of who governed them.

In December 1920 the third congress, meeting in Haifa, elected a nine-member Arab Executive Committee. Its chairman was Musa Kazim al-Husseini. Earlier that year, after the riots of April 1920, the British had dismissed him as mayor of Jerusalem. In his place they appointed Raghib al-Nashashibi, head of a rival leading family. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] For years afterward, rivalry between factions aligned with the Husseini and Nashashibi families divided Palestinian politics. Only in 1928 did a congress briefly form a united front. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim]

Musa Kazim led delegations to Winston Churchill, in Cairo and in Jerusalem, and to London in 1921 and 1922. They were rebuffed. In a telegram he concluded that the cause would not find justice "at the hands of the British government which is under Zionist influence." [cite:wiki_musa_kazim] That was his judgment, written after his delegations had been turned away. It records how the Palestinian leadership had come to see the mandatory power.

Britain never recognized the Arab Executive. The contrast with its treatment of the Zionist movement was stark. The Mandate gave formal standing to the Zionist Organization's Jewish Agency, while the Palestinians' own elected executive went without official status for as long as it existed. [cite:palquest_musa_kazim]

By the early 1930s the leadership was under pressure from within as well. In 1932 the Executive launched a fund known as the Piaster Project to keep land in Arab hands. That same year the more radical Istiqlal, or Independence, party began attacking the established leadership for its inaction, a challenge that continued into 1933. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

In October 1933 Musa Kazim joined a protest against immigration in Jaffa and was injured by British forces. He died in March 1934. The Arab Executive dissolved that year. In 1936 a new body, the Arab Higher Committee, took its place. [cite:fhfpal_musa_kazim] [cite:wiki_musa_kazim]

These events run past 1922, but they belong here because they began here. The revolt that broke out in 1936 came after some fifteen years of congresses, petitions and delegations asking for representative government. All of them were addressed to a mandatory power that recognized one national movement in Palestine and declined to recognize the other. [cite:jerusalemstory_arab_executive]
