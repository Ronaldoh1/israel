Before Herzl: The First Network, 1836–1896

Herzl founded the political movement, the one with a congress, a program and an organization, but he was not the first to imagine a return, and he did not start from nothing. By 1896 there was already a network: rabbis who had argued for settlement, a Paris organization that ran an agricultural school near Jaffa, donors in Europe and Russia, and a legal society in Odessa that funded colonists. This section follows the ties between them, because the companion network map now holds them, and because they show what the movement inherited when Herzl arrived.

A letter to a stranger, 1836

The first recorded attempt to enlist a Rothschild in settling the land came from a rabbi, not a banker. In 1836 Zvi Hirsch Kalischer, a rabbi in Thorn, wrote to Amschel Mayer Rothschild of the Frankfurt house, a man he had never met. The letter was his first attempt to act on a new reading of redemption, and its theme was that God works through prominent men, with Rothschild cast as the instrument. [cite:rdn_myers_letter] According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, Rothschild's reply was noncommittal, and Kalischer turned next to Moses Montefiore; that correspondence has not survived. [cite:rdn_ej_kalischer] The letter came twenty-six years before Kalischer's book of 1862, told in [scene:ideo_s01|the move from prayer to program].

Two cases and an organization, 1840–1870

Two cases of persecution shaped the first international Jewish body. The Damascus affair of 1840, a blood-libel charge ended by the mission of Montefiore and Adolphe Crémieux, is told in [scene:ch01_hatred|the history of the old hatred]. The second came in 1858. On 24 June papal police in Bologna took six-year-old Edgardo Mortara from his family on the orders of the city's inquisitor, because a former servant said she had secretly baptised him as an infant; under papal law a baptised child had to be raised by the Church. [cite:rdn_wiki_mortara]

In May 1860 the Alliance Israélite Universelle was launched in Paris, the first modern international Jewish organization. Its founding members met at the house of Charles Netter, who became its treasurer. The Encyclopaedia Judaica traces its origin to both cases: Damascus renewed the urge for Jewish solidarity, and Mortara intensified the drive for self-help on a world scale. Crémieux was its president from 1863 to 1880; he was not among its founders, as some summaries say. [cite:rdn_jvl_aiu] [cite:rdn_jvl_netter] After an Ottoman decree of 1870 confirmed the land, Netter founded the Alliance's agricultural school at Mikveh Israel, near Jaffa, and ran it for four years. [cite:rdn_jvl_netter]

The Alliance was not a Zionist body. Netter himself publicly opposed Jewish immigration to Palestine in March 1882, changed his view later that year, met the Bilu settlers at Rishon LeZion on a visit that August, and died a month after arriving. [cite:rdn_jvl_netter] After the First World War the Alliance opposed Zionism outright, and it took a pro-Zionist position only in 1945. [cite:rdn_jvl_aiu]

From societies to a legal network, 1881–1896

The pogroms of 1881 and the May Laws of 1882, told in [scene:s02|the idea appears], set off the Lovers of Zion societies. In 1890 the Russian government permitted their work to be organized legally in Odessa as the Society for the Support of Jewish Farmers and Artisans in Syria and Palestine, known as the Odessa Committee. Leon Pinsker was its first chairman, followed by Abraham Gruenberg in 1891 and Menahem Ussishkin in 1906. It supported colonists and land buyers, the Carmel winegrowers' cooperative and the Geulah land company, and made the first donation toward a plot for the Hebrew University. [cite:rdn_jvl_odessa] Before the first Zionist Congress it counted more than 4,000 members, funded mostly by charities registered in Europe and the United States. [cite:rdn_wiki_odessa]

What the map shows about this network

On the network map, removing this whole network before 1897, all twenty-one of its people, bodies and events, leaves the founding still possible, but only with great difficulty: about 72 percent on time against about 93 for the full record, with about 12 years of work lost. Nearly all of that drop comes from the Lovers of Zion and the money and settlement around them; removing the Lovers of Zion alone lowers the chance to about 80 percent, with about 8 years lost. The thinkers who first argued for a return, Hess, Kalischer and Alkalai, move the figure not at all, and neither do the pogroms, the May Laws, Damascus or Mortara. [scene:rd7_tests|The tests] explain why: the map counts only ties the record states, and no document states that these ideas or these cases caused a later act in a way the map can count. That makes them unscoreable, not unimportant.

The record therefore supports a narrower statement than either common account. Herzl did not invent the idea of a state, and he did not build its first network; he gave an existing network a political program and an organization. Persecution set the early movement going. Money and settlement gave it something to build on.
