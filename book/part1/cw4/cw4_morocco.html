Morocco's Jews, 1956–1962: The Ban, the Egoz and the Start of Operation Yachin

Morocco's Jewish community was one of the oldest and largest in the Arab world, present for some two thousand years and numbering in the hundreds of thousands in the 1940s. Most of it emigrated after 1948, mostly to Israel. [cite:w_yachin] The years before 1961 are told in [scene:oa_s03|Volume II's account of Morocco], and the wider departures of Jews from Arab and Muslim countries in [scene:s00e2|the history of the Jews of the Arab world]. This section takes up the years between Moroccan independence in 1956 and the end of 1962, when emigration to Israel moved from a forbidden, clandestine traffic to a secret agreement between Israeli negotiators and the Moroccan monarchy, with money changing hands.

It is sometimes said that relations between Morocco and Israel began with the agreements of 2020. The record of 1961 shows something different: emissaries of Israel's government negotiating directly with a Moroccan prince and a Moroccan minister, and an agreement, kept secret, under which tens of thousands of Moroccan Jews left for Israel with their government's quiet consent. [cite:w_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] That was not diplomatic recognition, and it was not public. It was a working relationship, conducted through secret negotiators and a front organization.

The ban. After Morocco became independent in 1956, emigration to Israel was officially banned. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] The sources disagree on when the prohibition became formal. Aish and the website Point of No Return place the ban from independence in 1956; the Wikipedia article on Operation Yachin, which draws on works by the historian Michael Laskier and by Bialer, Abecassis and Szulc, dates the formal prohibition to 1959, under pressure from the Arab League. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] The two dates may describe different things, an obstruction in practice from 1956 and a formal ban from 1959, but the sources used here do not say so, and the difference is left as they give it.

King Mohammed V's own position, as the sources report it, was that he preferred Moroccan Jews to stay, and he barred foreign action to encourage them to emigrate. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] That was the position of a newly independent state toward a part of its own citizenry, and it can be read in more than one way: as a sovereign's refusal to let a foreign state recruit his subjects, as an accommodation with Arab League policy, or as an obstacle to people who wished to leave. The sources support each element without settling their weight.

People left anyway. Between 1956 and 1961 about 30,000 Jews left Morocco clandestinely; another count gives 29,400. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Most went through the Mossad's underground. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin]

The Egoz, 10 January 1961. The cost of that underground was paid at sea. The Egoz, formerly the Pisces, was a converted British naval vessel. According to Aish and Point of No Return, it had made 12 trips carrying more than 300 Jews from northern Morocco to Gibraltar. On 10 January 1961 it sank about ten miles off the coast. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Those two sources give 44 dead, among them the Mossad's envoy on board, Chaim Tzfarti. Twenty-two bodies were never recovered, sixteen of them children's. Three crew members escaped in the only lifeboat. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] One passage of the Wikipedia article, describing the "Pisces tragedy," gives 46 dead instead. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] The sources used here do not explain the difference of two.

On one reading, the sinking showed what the ban had produced: families travelling at night on a small vessel, under an intelligence service's direction, because the legal road was closed.

A new king and a negotiation. Hassan II acceded to the throne on 26 February 1961, and the formal ban ended around this time. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Negotiations followed. According to the reported accounts, Isser Harel assigned Alex Gatmon to open them in early May 1961. Morocco's negotiators were Prince Moulay Ali and the Labour Minister, Abdelkader Benjelloun. HIAS served as the front organization. A compromise was reached by the end of July 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin]

The mechanism had three parties, each with a defined role. On Israel's side, Harel and Gatmon negotiated, and the Mossad had already run the clandestine route. The Moroccan monarchy, through a prince and a minister, granted permission. HIAS gave the arrangement a public face that was neither Israeli nor Moroccan. None of the three was the emigrants themselves, whose departure was the object of the agreement.

The money. The agreement is reported to have provided for a down payment of $500,000, then $100 for each emigrant up to the first 50,000, and $250 for each emigrant after that. [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] According to Szulc, HIAS financed about $50 million of the operation's costs. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] Historians, as summarized in the account carried elsewhere in this library, record that Israel, together with American Jewish organizations, made payments to the Moroccan government to allow the departures. [cite:w_yachin]

The flow of money can be stated plainly, within the limits of the sources. It came from Israel and from HIAS. It moved through a secret agreement, not a published treaty. It went to the Moroccan state, in a lump sum and then per person, so that the payment grew with every departure. What Morocco's government gained was money and, as the sources give Hassan II's motives, the chance to appear favourable to the West. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] What Israel gained was immigrants. What the community bore is the subject of the dispute below.

The operation begins. Operation Yachin began at the end of November 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Emigrants left by air and by sea from Casablanca and Tangier and travelled via France and Italy. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] This chapter ends in 1962, while the operation was still running. It continued until the spring of 1964, and its total therefore reaches beyond this period: estimates of departures range from 90,000 to 97,000, and Abecassis gives 97,005 for 1961–64. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] The account of Morocco carried elsewhere in this library gives about 97,000. [cite:w_yachin] The result for the community is plain in its numbers: Morocco's Jewish population, 162,420 at the start of 1961, had fallen to about 53,000 by 1967. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Israelis of Moroccan descent became one of the largest communities in Israel. [cite:w_yachin]

Rescue or paid transfer? The same events are read in two ways, and each reading has evidence behind it. The first treats Yachin as a rescue. In this account, carried by Point of No Return, Moroccan Jews faced a ban on leaving and a hostile climate, and the agreement opened a closed door. [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] Laskier's scholarship supports part of this case: he notes propaganda in the nationalist press and arrests, and Wikipedia's summary reports his judgment that the threat to the community was "not entirely unsubstantiated." [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] The Egoz, on this reading, shows what the alternative to an agreement was.

The second reading treats Yachin as a negotiated, paid emigration that emptied one of the oldest Jewish communities in the Arab world. Simon Levy, a Moroccan Jew of the left, described the departures as a trauma. The historian Susan Gilson Miller called them a "monumental national tragedy." [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] In this view the community's end was arranged between two states, each acting for its own reasons, with payment per head, and the loss was Morocco's as well as the community's.

The readings do not exclude each other. The ban and the hostile press are documented; so are the payments and the collapse of the community's numbers. What divides them is the weight given to each, and the question of choice: how far the departures were what the emigrants wanted, and how far they were produced by pressure at home and by an agreement made over their heads. The sources used here do not record the emigrants' own reasons in their own words, and no generalization about Moroccans, or about Moroccan Jews, can be drawn from the positions of the officials and writers named above. Each spoke for himself or herself, or for a government.

What the record does not settle. The agreement's terms rest on secondary and advocacy sources, and on Wikipedia's citations of Laskier, Bialer and Szulc; no primary text of the agreement, and no scholarly text of its terms, was among the sources fetched for this section. The payment figures, $500,000 down, $100 and $250 per emigrant, should therefore be read as reported, not as established from the document. [cite:rs_cw4_wiki_yachin] [cite:rs_cw4_aish_egoz] [cite:rs_cw4_pnr_yakhin] The date of the formal ban, 1956 or 1959, remains in conflict, as does the Egoz's death toll, 44 or 46. The total of the operation is an estimate with a range, 90,000 to 97,000. And how the payments were made, to whom in the Moroccan state, and how they were recorded is not documented in these sources. What is documented is the sequence: a ban, a clandestine flight that cost lives at sea, a new king, a secret negotiation, a priced agreement, and from the end of November 1961 an organized departure that within three years had carried most of the community away.
