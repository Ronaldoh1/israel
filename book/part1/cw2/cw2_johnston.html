The Johnston Mission, 1953–1955: Dividing the Jordan

The suspension of aid over Banat Yaacov stopped a canal. It did not answer the question the canal had raised: who would use the water of the Jordan, and on what terms. Washington's answer was an envoy. For two years, from October 1953 to October 1955, Eric Johnston travelled between Israel and its Arab neighbours with a plan to divide the river among the four states that shared it. The engineers on both sides accepted his figures. The governments did not sign them. And yet the figures outlived the failure: they were never ratified, but they were broadly observed, and the United States later tied its money to them.

The mandate, 16 October 1953. On 16 October 1953, immediately after the Qibya raid and while American aid to Israel was being withheld over the canal works, President Eisenhower announced that he was sending Johnston "as my personal representative with the rank of Ambassador" to discuss "the mutual development of the water resources of the Jordan River Valley on a regional basis." The statement gave the reason in terms of people rather than water: "some hundreds of thousands of Arab refugees" lacked adequate support, and a plan for the valley could give "a substantial proportion" of them an economic base on the land. [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_johnston] From the first day, then, the mission was two things at once: a water-sharing scheme and a refugee-resettlement scheme, presented as a single development project.

A State Department telegram of the same month explained why Washington was moving now. An engineering study made by the Tennessee Valley Authority for UNRWA, the UN agency for Palestine refugees, had "just been completed." Its findings "cannot be carried out without political agreement on the part of the riparian states." The best prospects for settling refugees lay in the East and West Ghor. And "Israel's unilateral action in commencing diversion of water in the Demilitarized Zone below Lake Huleh" had raised tension. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_johnston_tel] The same telegram noted a condition that would weigh on Israel: the plan would require it to relinquish "exclusive territorial control over the outlets to Lake Tiberias." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_johnston_tel] The engineering required a political concession from every party, Israel included. How the canal works came to a halt is told in [scene:cw2_banat_yaacov|the Banat Yaacov suspension].

Four plans. Johnston did not bring a finished agreement; he brought a starting point, and each side answered it with its own. The starting point was the Main plan, drawn up by Charles T. Main for the TVA on UNRWA's commission. Its authors stated that it did not "consider political factors or attempt to set this system into the national boundaries now prevailing." [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] Israel answered with the Cotton plan, which brought a second river, the Litani, into the system and raised Israel's share accordingly. The Arab League's Technical Committee, under Egyptian leadership, answered in 1954 with an Arab plan that excluded the Litani and rejected storage in the Sea of Galilee. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] The final compromise, the Unified or Johnston plan, came out of the negotiations among these three.

The competing allocations, as tabulated by the water-diplomacy analysts Aaron Wolf, Joshua Newton and Matthew Pritchard in the AquaPedia case study, are set out below in millions of cubic metres a year. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan]

The table shows how far apart the opening positions were and where the compromise landed. Israel's own plan asked for more than three times what the Main plan had given it, 400 million cubic metres of it from the Litani. The Arab plan gave Israel less than half the Main figure. The Unified plan returned Israel almost exactly to the Main plan's number, took the Arab plan's figures for Syria and Lebanon unchanged, and gave Jordan somewhat less than the Main plan but more than either of the other two. Two small inconsistencies in the sources should be noted. AquaPedia gives Israel's share under the Main plan as 394 in one place and 393 in another. And it gives Israel 400 under the Unified plan while also noting that Israel's actual share, as the residue of the river after the others had taken theirs, was estimated at about 409 on average. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan]

That residue was the heart of the design. The State Department's later summary of the Unified plan gives fixed allocations to the Arab states and a residual right to Israel: Jordan 720 million cubic metres (377 from the Yarmuk, 100 from the Jordan and 243 from the side wadis); Syria 132 (20 from the Banias, 22 from the Jordan and 90 from the Yarmuk); Lebanon 35, from the Hasbani; and Israel 25 from the Yarmuk, with the remaining waters of the Jordan available to it. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_unified_plan] The arrangement gave the three Arab states certainty and Israel the variable remainder, which in an average year came to more than its nominal figure.

Technical agreement, 1955. By the summer of 1955 the engineers had done their part. Johnston signed a memorandum of understanding with Israel on 5 July 1955, and one with the Arab riparian states' Technical Committee on 11 October 1955. He was told that the plan was technically acceptable to Israel, Jordan, Syria and Lebanon. The United States offered to pay two-thirds of the costs of development. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_unified_plan] [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] The October memorandum was signed by the Arab riparian states' own technical body.

Political refusal, October 1955. The decision did not rest with the engineers. The Arab League's political organs did not endorse the plan. According to the State Department, the League's Political Committee returned it to the Technical Committee for "further consideration." AquaPedia dates the League Council's decision not to accept it to October 1955, citing "the political implications of accepting." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_unified_plan] [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] The Jewish Virtual Library says the mission was abandoned in late 1955. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_johnston]

Why it failed. The sources offer two kinds of explanation, and they are not mutually exclusive. The first is political. A shared water regime, formally agreed, would have meant a final arrangement with Israel, and accepting it implied recognition of a state the Arab governments did not recognize. AquaPedia's analysts record that Iraq and Saudi Arabia urged Lebanon, Syria and Jordan not to accept; the State Department's summary, in returning the plan for "further consideration," points the same way. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_unified_plan] The second is substantive. The same analysts summarize the Arab objections as suspicion that the plan was designed to Israel's benefit, and fear that water carried to irrigate the Negev would enable more immigration and further expansion. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] On that reading the refusal was not only about recognition but about what Israel would do with the water.

Both cases have evidence behind them. The political reading is supported by the sequence: the engineers agreed and the politicians declined, and Iraq and Saudi Arabia pressed the riparian states to refuse. The substantive reading is supported by the design itself, in which Israel received the residue of the river, an amount that in an average year exceeded its nominal figure, and by the fact that Israel's own Cotton plan had sought far more. Against any simple picture of Arab obstruction stands one further fact the analysts record: Nasser had been an active advocate of the plan. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] Egypt had led the Arab League committee that produced the Arab plan, and its leader had argued for agreement. One reading is that the decision turned on politics among the Arab states as well as on the merits of the water; the sources here do not settle it.

Israel's side of the failure is less fully documented in these sources. Israel signed its memorandum in July 1955. The record does not show how its government regarded the plan's requirement that it give up exclusive control of the lake's outlets, or whether it would have ratified a final agreement on those terms had the Arab states accepted. What it shows is that Israel's own opening claim was the largest of the four, and that the compromise brought it back to roughly the engineers' original figure.

What survived. Although never ratified, the allocations were generally adhered to, and the United States tied later funding for water projects in the basin to them. [cite:rs_cw2_aquapedia_jordan] The Jewish Virtual Library says that about 200,000 refugees could have been settled on irrigated land in Jordan under the plan. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_johnston] That figure is the Jewish Virtual Library's, not a planning document's, and the sources here do not say how it was reached. The water dispute on the Syrian front, which the plan was meant to defuse, went on regardless, and from 1964 turned into a struggle over the river's headwaters, told in [scene:sy_s02|the demilitarized zones and the water war]. Water also reappeared in the larger Anglo-American settlement proposals of 1955, told in [scene:cw2_alpha|the search for a settlement].

The record leaves several questions open. The date of the Arab League's final decision rests on AquaPedia alone; the State Department's summary gives none. The reasons for the refusal come to us chiefly through Western analysts' summaries of Arab positions, not through the Arab governments' own statements, which are not among these sources. The refugees in whose name the mission was launched do not appear in the record as a party: neither their own views of resettlement in the Ghor nor the relation of the plan to the right of return is addressed in these documents. And whether a plan that the engineers of four states accepted could have been carried through by their governments in any political climate of 1955 is a question the record does not answer.
