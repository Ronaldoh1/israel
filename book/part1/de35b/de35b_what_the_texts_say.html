What the Texts Say About Choosing the Ruler

A democracy settles the question of succession on a calendar. On a fixed day, voters choose who governs, and on a later fixed day they may choose someone else. The texts examined in this book reject that arrangement, and they are explicit about it. What they put in its place, and what they leave out, can be read in their own words. [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight] examined the texts' answer to a ruler who goes wrong, the board's cryptographic keys, and Albert Hirschman's distinction between exit and voice. This section looks at the step before removal: who picks the ruler in the first place, who counts in that choice, and what the authors said when asked how a bad ruler would be replaced.

Moldbug's design: shares, a board and a manager

The clearest statement of the design is a passage from Moldbug that Nick Land reproduced in the first instalment of his 2012 serial. "To a neocameralist, a state is a business which owns a country," it begins. Ownership is divided into "negotiable shares," each paying a fraction of the state's profit, and "Each share has one vote, and the shareholders elect a board, which hires and fires managers." The business's customers, it continues, are its residents. [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Neocameralism, Moldbug's coinage, means exactly this: a state run as a joint-stock company, with the chief executive answering to owners rather than to voters. [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six] traces how the idea developed.

Moldbug presented the joint-stock form as a cure for monarchy's oldest weakness. Writing on 25 July 2007, he said it would eliminate "the hereditary principle which caused so many problems for so many monarchies." [cite:de35b_le] In "Patchwork: A Positive Vision", posted on 13 November 2008, he described a world broken into small states, "each governed by its own joint-stock corporation without regard to the residents' opinions." The remedy he offered the residents was departure: "If residents don't like their government, they can and should move." He summed up the design in one line: "The design is all 'exit,' no 'voice.'" [cite:de35b_le] Exit here means leaving; voice means speaking up, voting or protesting to change things from inside. The resident's opinion is not an input to the choice of ruler at any stage.

Land's gloss: who gets the shares

Land's own summary, which follows the quoted passage, is more specific about who the first shareholders would be, and it is the part of the design most often skipped. The first step, he wrote, is "to squash the democratic myth that a state 'belongs' to the citizenry." The point of neocameralism is "to buy out the real stakeholders in sovereign power." To find them, "the entire social landscape of political bribery ('lobbying')" must be "exactly mapped," and the administrative, legislative, judicial, media and academic privileges that such bribes buy must be "converted into fungible shares." Voters are not wholly excluded: "Insofar as voters are worth bribing," their portion of sovereignty "will be estimated with appropriate derision." [cite:de35b_land_ebook]

Once that conversion is done, Land continued, the owners "can initiate rational corporate governance, beginning with the appointment of a CEO," and the state's interest becomes "the maximization of long-term shareholder value." Residents, whom he calls "clients," no longer need to take "any interest in politics whatsoever. In fact, to do so would be to exhibit semi-criminal proclivities." If the state fails to deliver value for its taxes, which he calls "sovereign rent," residents "can notify its customer service function, and if necessary take their custom elsewhere." His summary is four words: "No voice, free exit." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] On the same page he quotes Patri Friedman, the seasteading advocate, saying that free exit is so important "that we've called it the only Universal Human Right." [cite:de35b_land_ebook]

Read plainly, the passage answers the question this part asks. The ruler is chosen by the owners of shares; the shares go to those who already hold political influence, measured by what it costs to buy; the ordinary voter's share, if any, is discounted; and the resident's only formal role after the transfer is as a customer who may complain or leave. Land wrote these sentences as a summary of Moldbug's position rather than as a manifesto of his own, but he wrote them without objection, and the serial as a whole endorses the turn from voice to exit. [cite:de35b_land_ebook]

The transition: one vote to end voting

The texts do allow one election, at the start. In the ninth part of his "Gentle Introduction" series, Moldbug described a party that wins power and holds it "only as long as it takes to hire a qualified administrator," whom he imagined as "an experienced corporate CEO, perhaps." [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de35b_le] In a talk to technology workers in 2012 he put the same idea more bluntly: "If Americans want to change their government, they're going to have to get over their dictator phobia," and the country needed "a national CEO [or] what's called a dictator." [cite:de6_pein_2014] The election in this sequence is a means of transferring authority to someone who is not then subject to election. The texts describe no second vote.

The question put directly, January 2025

In January 2025 the New York Times interviewer David Marchese put the removal question to Yarvin in its oldest form. He contrasted good monarchs with bad ones, Marcus Aurelius with Nero, and listed the dictators of the twentieth century. Yarvin replied: "Your question is the most important question of all." He went on to discuss the twentieth century, democracy's own "mixed bag" record and the Holocaust as a new kind of evil. In the exchange he did not describe any mechanism by which a bad ruler would be removed. [cite:de35b_nyt]

Two other remarks in the interview are often quoted out of context, and both should be read precisely. Asked why he trusted chief executives, Yarvin said the median Fortune 500 chief executive would outperform the present government, so one could "just pick one at random and put him or her in charge of Washington." [cite:de35b_nyt] That is an argument about the competence of corporate managers as a class, not a proposed selection rule. Asked whether he votes, he said no, that he does not believe "in actually voting at all," and that voting provides "this almost pornographic stimulus," a sense of status and relevance like supporting a sports team. [cite:de8_audit_nyt] [cite:de35b_nyt] He also said that any American monarch would have to "be the president of all Americans," reaching out to his opponents rather than demonising them. [cite:de35b_nyt] In a CNN interview in 2025 he described the monarchical bet as concentrating power "in a single individual and then just hope somehow that this is the right individual." [cite:de6_cnn_2025]

The one removal mechanism attributed to the design, a board holding cryptographic keys able to disarm the state's weapons, rests on two secondary accounts: Ava Kofman's New Yorker profile and a reviewer's summary of Patchwork. The primary wording of the 2008 posts on that point has not been located, and the mechanism should be read as a paraphrase until it is. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] [cite:de8_kossina_patchwork]

Thiel: escape rather than selection

Peter Thiel's writing does not propose a way of choosing a ruler at all. In "The Education of a Libertarian", published by Cato Unbound on 13 April 2009, he wrote: "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible," and proposed seeking "an escape from politics in all its forms" through cyberspace, outer space and seasteading. In a follow-up of 1 May 2009 he added: "While I don't think any class of people should be disenfranchised, I have little hope that voting will make things better." [cite:de10_cato] [scene:de10_education_of_a_libertarian|Part Ten] reads the essay in full. In practice Thiel has used elections rather than tried to escape them: according to Forbes, as summarised by Ballotpedia, he gave $35 million across 16 federal races in 2022. [cite:de9_audit_bp_2022] He has said he did not think Yarvin's ideas would "work." [cite:de6_cnn_2025]

Taken together, the texts answer two of the three questions and leave the third open. Owners choose; residents leave. Who removes a chief executive who refuses to go is the question Yarvin called the most important of all, and it is the one the texts do not settle. The next section turns from the texts to the record of systems that have actually had to answer it.
