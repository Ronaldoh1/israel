Syria in 1949: The Offer, the Coups and the Demilitarized Zones

A familiar account holds that Syria rejected every chance at peace with Israel from 1948 onward. The record of 1949 complicates it. For a few months that year Syria's ruler offered Israel a peace treaty, a meeting at the highest level, and the resettlement of a large share of the Palestinian refugees inside Syria. Israel's prime minister declined, the ruler was overthrown and executed, and the armistice frontier the two states left behind, the border for the next 18 years, became the setting of two decades of clashes. Each part of that sequence is documented, and so is the conduct of both sides along the line.

Syria's army had entered the war in May 1948 and fought on the northern front. When the fighting stopped, Syrian forces held small areas west of the international border. On 30 March 1949 Syria's elected government was overthrown by an army officer, Husni al-Za'im, in the first of three coups that year. Declassified records and historians document contacts between US officials and Za'im before the coup; how far the United States directed it is debated. [cite:w_1949_syria_coup]

Armistice talks between Syria and Israel opened on 5 April 1949. In the course of them, Za'im offered a peace treaty and military cooperation, proposed a meeting with Ben-Gurion or with Moshe Sharett, and offered to resettle 250,000–300,000 Palestinian refugees in Syria. The border he proposed would have run through the middle of Lake Hula and the Sea of Galilee. [cite:tf_zaim] [cite:ecf_zaim] [cite:majalla_zaim] The offer is documented in Israeli archives and by historians. [cite:tf_zaim] [cite:ecf_zaim] Its scale is clearest against the numbers at Lausanne a few months later, where Israel offered to take back 100,000 refugees as part of a final peace: Za'im was offering to absorb two and a half to three times as many himself.

Ben-Gurion was unwilling to stake policy on him, in part from distrust of Za'im's ties to Washington. [cite:majalla_zaim] The Israeli case for caution rests on documented facts. Za'im had seized power by force less than a week before the talks opened, his contacts with American officials before the coup are on the record, and the border he asked for ran through the middle of both lakes. A treaty with a ruler who had come to power that way might not outlast him, as events soon showed. The case on the other side rests on documented facts too. The offer included a treaty, a summit and a resettlement figure larger than anything Israel itself put forward on refugees that year, and it would have settled, for a large number of refugees, the question that the Lausanne conference could not. The record shows that the offer was made and that it was declined. It does not show whether it could have been carried out.

The question was soon closed. In August 1949 Za'im was deposed and executed in Syria's second coup of the year, and the offer died with him. [cite:ecf_zaim] The armistice had already been signed on 20 July 1949, the last of the four agreements negotiated under Ralph Bunche. [cite:rs_rd6_nobel_bunche] Under its terms, the areas Syria evacuated west of the international border became demilitarized zones. How the four agreements were made, and what they did and did not settle, is told in [scene:rd6_armistices|the Rhodes armistices].

The zones were small strips of land whose sovereignty the armistice left unsettled, and they became the fault line of the frontier. In a meeting in 1953–54, recorded by the State Department, Moshe Dayan told American officials that Israel claimed sovereignty over Jewish-owned lands inside the zones, that "someone must keep the area alive," and that Israel did not want Syria to have direct access to the Jordan River. [cite:frus_dayan_dmz] The conduct of both sides along the line is documented. Syrian forces fired from the heights on Israeli farmers and fishermen. Israel sent armoured tractors to cultivate disputed land in the zones and carried out reprisal raids.

The map traces the line from Za'im's offer forward. Two later developments belong to the following volumes and are given here only in outline. In the 1960s the dispute turned to water: Israel built its National Water Carrier, the Arab League backed a plan in 1964 to divert the headwaters of the Jordan, and Israeli strikes hit the diversion works between 1965 and 1967. And in an interview given in 1976 and published in 1997, Dayan said that at least 80 percent of the clashes had been provoked by Israel, which sent tractors into the zones until the Syrians fired. [cite:w_dayan] That account is his own, given long after the events; others contest it, and it does not cancel the documented Syrian fire on farmers and fishermen. It does show that the familiar picture of clashes caused entirely by shelling from the Golan is incomplete. The fuller story of the frontier to 1967 is in [scene:sy_s02|the demilitarized zones and the water war].

Syria's three coups of 1949 opened a decade in which the region's old order gave way. Egypt's Free Officers, the movement Nasser led, overthrew King Farouk in July 1952. [cite:w_1952_egypt] In August 1953 the CIA and MI6 removed Iran's elected prime minister. [cite:w_1953_coup] After Egypt nationalized the Suez Canal in 1956, Britain, France and Israel invaded, and US and Soviet pressure forced them to withdraw. [cite:w_suez] Iraq's monarchy was overthrown on 14 July 1958. [cite:w_1958_iraq] By the end of the decade, military or royal autocrats, Cold War alignments and outside intervention had become the pattern of the region's politics. The record ties that pattern to the coups of named officers and, in Iran, to the CIA and MI6, not to local culture or religion alone. Za'im's coup was the first of the decade.

Three things remain unsettled. The first is the degree of American involvement in Za'im's coup, which the declassified record documents as contacts and historians still debate as direction. [cite:w_1949_syria_coup] The second is whether his offer was a serious basis for peace or the gesture of a ruler whose hold on power proved brief; the record establishes the terms but cannot establish what would have followed acceptance. The third is responsibility for the clashes in the demilitarized zones. Dayan's statements of 1953–54 and 1976 are on the record, and so is Syrian fire on civilians; the dispute over which side bore more responsibility continues. [cite:w_dayan]
