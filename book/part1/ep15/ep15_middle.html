The People in the Middle

Take Epstein and Maxwell out of the network map and ask which remaining points hold the most of it together. The answer is not a president, a prince or a billionaire. It is the 2008 agreement and the Justice Department that signed it; Lesley Groff, his executive assistant for eighteen years; the apartment building on East 66th Street that housed his staff and his guests; and Paul Morris, the banker who carried his account from JPMorgan to Deutsche Bank and later wrote to him from Bank of America. [cite:ep3_ta_morris2026] Groff was one of the four women named and protected in the 2008 agreement and has never been charged. [cite:ep2_cbs_coconspirators2019] Morris is not accused of any crime.

The same pattern runs through the estate. Darren Indyke, Epstein's long-time lawyer and co-executor, formed and controlled two Epstein-linked companies, Hyperion Air and SLK Designs; his attorney says Indyke did not socialise with Epstein and rejects any suggestion that he facilitated abuse. [cite:ep15_cbs_dea_ice2026] Indyke and the accountant Richard Kahn settled a victims' class action without admitting liability, and they still manage what remains of the money, as Part Ten sets out. [cite:ep10_findlaw_estate2026] Their own bank accounts at TD Bank were closed only in 2019, after the arrest. [cite:ep3_wsj_firms] On the map the two of them, their bank and Epstein's Virgin Islands company form a closed loop: a structure that holds together without him.

This is the blind spot the network makes plain. Coverage has followed the famous names because they are famous. The documents show the structure running through the people who scheduled, banked, drafted and stored: the ones who would know where the computers went, who approved each wire and which visitors came when. Some have spoken to congressional investigators. None has been asked these questions in open court.
