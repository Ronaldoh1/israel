How the Square Mile Governs Itself

Most of the City of London's government looks, at first sight, like any British local council. It collects business rates, manages social housing, cleans the streets and carries out the functions of a London borough under the same statutes. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts] The differences lie in who sits on its councils, how they get there, and what else the Corporation does with the wealth and standing it has accumulated over eight centuries. This section takes those in turn: the offices, the electorate, and the reach.

The offices. At the top is the Lord Mayor, who serves a one-year term and chairs both of the City's governing courts. Nobody can become Lord Mayor without first having been elected an Alderman and then having served as one of the City's two Sheriffs. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013] The City is divided into 25 wards. Each ward elects one Alderman for a six-year term, and together they form the Court of Aldermen. The ward residents and business voters also elect the 100 members of the Court of Common Council, the main legislative and executive body, which sits with the Aldermen and holds powers similar to those of a London borough council. Common Council elections take place every four years, and they have traditionally not been fought on party lines. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013]

The two Sheriffs and the Lord Mayor are not chosen by the ward electorate at all. They are chosen by the liverymen of the City's livery companies, the descendants of its medieval craft and trade guilds. The liverymen elect the Sheriffs each Midsummer Day, and each September they meet in Common Hall to choose the next Lord Mayor from among the eligible Aldermen, usually by acclamation, though a contested vote is possible. Travers counted 108 livery companies when he described the system in 2013. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013] [cite:dv2_wci_lordmayor] The Corporation's most visible office, in other words, is filled through a franchise that has no counterpart anywhere else in British public life: membership of a guild.

The Lord Mayor's year is spent to a large extent representing the City at home and abroad. Day-to-day political leadership is usually associated instead with the chairman of the Policy and Resources Committee, who is often described as the Corporation's de facto political leader and who represents the City among the leaders of London's councils. According to the sources consulted for this book, Chris Hayward has held the post since 5 May 2022, succeeding Catherine McGuinness. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation]

The electorate. The feature of the City's government that most surprises outsiders is who gets to vote for it. In every other British local election, the right to vote follows residence. Businesses voted in local elections elsewhere too until 1969, when that franchise was abolished everywhere except here. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] In the City it was not only kept but widened. The City of London (Ward Elections) Act 2002 "greatly increased the business franchise," and a reform taking effect in 2003 redrew the wards to give the residential areas more representation while extending the business vote at the same time. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013]

The rule is set by the size of the workforce. A business based in the City may appoint one of its employees as a voter for every five staff, up to ten voters, and one further voter for every fifty staff beyond that. Residents each have one vote. [cite:dv2_wiki_2025_election] The appointed voters have to meet conditions of service in the City, and the qualifying date is 1 September of the election year. [cite:dv2_gresham_travers_2013] [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation]

The effect on the balance of the electorate is large, and the figures available come from sources of different kinds. A reference summary puts the business vote at about 24,000 in 2009, far above the number of resident voters. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] For 2013, the Green Party, which campaigns to abolish the business vote, put it at some 32,000 against about 9,000 resident votes; those are a campaigner's figures. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] The Corporation itself counts about 8,600 residents and 614,500 people working in the City, one in every 52 workers in the United Kingdom. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts] The sources used for this book do not give the split between resident and business voters for the most recent election, and the reader should treat the 2009 and 2013 numbers as indications of scale rather than current counts.

That most recent election was held on 20 March 2025, for all 100 seats of the Court of Common Council. Forty-five of the hundred councillors were returned without a contest, in 14 of the 25 wards. Independents won 83 seats, Labour 7 and the local group Temple and Farringdon Together 10. Turnout, where there was a contest, ranged from 11 percent in Farringdon Without to 49 percent in Portsoken and Vintry. [cite:dv2_wiki_2025_election] Nearly half the governing council, in other words, was chosen without any vote being cast.

The reach. A body with that electorate also holds wealth and functions well beyond those of a council of 8,600 residents. For most of its history the Corporation kept one of its main funds, City's Cash, a private endowment built up over centuries, out of public view. In December 2012, after criticism of its lack of financial transparency, it disclosed that City's Cash held more than £1.3 billion; by March 2016 net assets were put at £2.3 billion. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] The Corporation reports property assets of about £3 billion across some 250 properties and spends about £130 million a year on heritage and culture. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts]

Much of that reach lies outside the Square Mile. The Corporation manages about 11,000 acres of natural habitat in London and the Home Counties, including Hampstead Heath, Epping Forest and Burnham Beeches. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts] [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] It runs London's wholesale markets, which it says support 10,250 jobs and move a million tonnes of produce a year, and its Port Health Authority patrols 94 miles of the tidal Thames. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts] It controls three independent schools and owns the Guildhall School of Music and Drama. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation]

It also has its own police force. The Common Council is the police authority for the City, whose force is separate from London's Metropolitan Police. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] That small force has a national role far larger than its patch. Under an agreement with the Home Office, and with Home Office funding, the City of London Police is the National Lead Force for fraud: it sets the national strategy for policing fraud, coordinates fraud reporting across the country through Action Fraud, triages and allocates fraud cases nationally, and investigates the most significant and complex frauds. [cite:dv2_gov_nlf] It is also the National Police Chiefs' Council lead for economic, cyber and business crime. [cite:dv2_col_keyfacts] The force that polices the country's financial district also leads the country's response to financial crime.

The Corporation does not hide what it is for. Its own description of its purpose includes supporting the financial services industry and representing its interests. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] Each Lord Mayor serves as an international ambassador for the United Kingdom's financial and professional services, travelling abroad to represent the City. [cite:dv2_wci_lordmayor] Its critics read the same facts more darkly. The journalist George Monbiot argued that the Corporation's power helps to explain why the regulation of banks was scarcely better after the 2008 crash than before it. [cite:dv2_wiki_corporation] That is an argument, not a finding, and the sources used here do not measure the Corporation's effect on any particular piece of regulation.

What the record does establish is the shape of the arrangement. A local authority whose electorate is weighted toward the firms in its streets, which elects its figurehead through the guilds, which holds a large private endowment, which polices the nation's fraud, and which describes itself as an advocate for finance, sits at the center of one of the world's largest financial markets. None of that makes it a state. All of it makes it a very unusual kind of council, and it has one more feature that no other council in the country has: a permanent officer assigned to Parliament.
