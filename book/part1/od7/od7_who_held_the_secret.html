Who Held the Secret

The orders in this book had very different fates, and the differences are the finding. The Templars were destroyed by a king who owed them money, with a pope who absolved their leaders and dissolved them anyway. [cite:vt29_nwe_templar] [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon] [cite:vt29_ewtn_vienne] The Rosicrucians may never have existed as an order. [cite:ts13_wiki_rosicruc] The Freemasons were banned first by a Protestant state, then by the popes, and later by a totalitarian one. [cite:vt30_wiki_ineminenti] [cite:od_ushmm_nazi] The Illuminati were broken by a Catholic prince in under a decade. [cite:vt30_wiki_illuminati]

Three findings stand out. The first is that secrecy itself was the charge. The bull of 1738 reasoned that men who did no evil would not hate the light; the Bavarian edicts banned any society formed without permission; the Nazi decree called the lodges "hostile to the state". [cite:vt30_wiki_ineminenti] [cite:vt30_natgeo_weishaupt] [cite:od_ushmm_nazi] What the authorities could not tolerate was not a doctrine but a loyalty they did not administer. The second is that the orders were weaker than their legends. The one that held real money, the Temple, was destroyed in seven years by its debtor; the others were dissolved by edict. [cite:vt29_barber_cup1]

The third finding is the most important for this shelf. The legend of the all-powerful order was built by the orders' enemies, and it was turned, from 1806 on, into a weapon against a people who had nothing to do with any of them. [cite:vt30_wiki_simonini] [cite:vt30_wiki_judeomasonic] Where a lodge really did shelter a network of power, P2, the record names 962 individuals, a bank and a government; it does not name a people. [cite:vt30_wiki_p2] The rule this book follows, and this shelf follows throughout, is the one that separates history from that weapon: name the institution, name the individual, name the document, and never let a people stand in for any of them.
