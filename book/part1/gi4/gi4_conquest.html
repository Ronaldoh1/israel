Six Days, and What to Do With Them

The war itself is told elsewhere in this library. The crisis that led to it, from the closing of the Straits of Tiran to the eve of 5 June, ends the Cold War volume in [scene:cw6_tiran_june4|the Tiran crisis]; the war in the West Bank and the second Palestinian exodus are in [scene:wb3_war|the West Bank volume]; the occupation of Gaza and its first census are in [scene:gz2_1967|the Gaza volume]. This section takes up a narrower question, the one that matters for the phrase "Greater Israel": what Israel held when the fighting stopped, and what its government first said it would do with it.

What was taken

Between 5 and 10 June 1967 Israel took the Sinai Peninsula and the Gaza Strip from Egypt, the West Bank and East Jerusalem from Jordan, and the Golan Heights from Syria. A ceasefire arranged by the United Nations took effect on 10 June. The territory under Israel's control roughly tripled. By a later estimate, some 20,000 Arabs and about 800 Israelis were killed. [cite:gi4_history_sixday] In June Israel set up a military government for the civilian population of all four areas. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation]

The land did not come empty, and on the Golan the population largely disappeared. Israeli Military Intelligence estimated about 90,000 Syrian residents on the plateau, some 20,000 of them in the town of Quneitra, three days after its capture; the census Israel took on 10 August 1967 counted 3,936 in its final figures, most of them Druze in the villages of the north. Israel's position was that the residents had left; Colonel Shlomo Gazit, in Akevot's account of his meeting with the Red Cross, said their departure should not be seen as an expulsion. The International Committee of the Red Cross concluded that most had been "expelled either violently upon arrival of the armed forces, or under duress at a later stage," and in 1968 the Israeli diplomat Michael Comay wrote of "the expulsion of Arabs from Quneitra, which has been going on for several months." These accounts, all drawn from Israeli and Red Cross archives by the Israeli research institute Akevot, are set side by side here because they disagree. [cite:gi4_akevot_golan] Quneitra's later history and the Golan Heights Law of 1981 belong to [scene:gi8_golan|Part Eight].

The decision of 19 June 1967

Nine days after the ceasefire the government met to decide what it would offer. Its minutes, released by the Israel State Archives, record three different answers for three fronts. For Egypt and Syria, the morning session voted ten to nine to propose "peace treaties with Egypt and Syria, that include security arrangements, based on the international borders"; nine ministers backed a rival text that leaned toward keeping territory. In the afternoon a formula for peace with Egypt, and with Syria, "on the basis of the international border" was approved unanimously. [cite:gi4_cie_june19] In other words, on the day the question was first put, a bare majority of Israel's cabinet was prepared to return Sinai and the Golan in exchange for peace and security arrangements.

The other two fronts were treated differently. On Jordan and the West Bank the government could not agree and decided, for the time being, not to decide. On Gaza, the drafting committee, with one dissent, that of Moshe Kol, agreed that the strip should be annexed to Israel. [cite:gi4_cie_june19] East Jerusalem was not on offer at all: on 27 and 28 June Israel applied its law to about 70 square kilometres of East Jerusalem and the surrounding villages, a step the Security Council rejected, as [scene:wb3_jerusalem|the West Bank volume] sets out. [cite:wb_wiki_ejannex]

What happened to the 19 June offer is itself a matter on which accounts differ. The decision was passed to the United States in secret; Egypt and Syria gave no positive answer. [cite:gi4_aijac_1967] Both accounts of the 2012 archive release reviewed here say only that Israel "did not receive positive responses from Egypt and Syria"; AIJAC adds that the answer "would only come later in the year", at Khartoum. [cite:gi4_cie_june19] [cite:gi4_aijac_1967] Whether Washington ever conveyed the terms to Cairo and Damascus in a form those governments recognised as an offer is not established by the sources reviewed for this part, and the question is left open here.

The Arab states' own stated position followed in September. The Arab summit at Khartoum adopted the formula remembered as the "three noes": "no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with it." [cite:gi4_aijac_1967] On 22 November 1967 the Security Council unanimously adopted Resolution 242, which emphasized "the inadmissibility of the acquisition of territory by war" and called for "withdrawal of Israeli armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict," alongside the right of every state in the area to live within "secure and recognized boundaries." Israel and the Arab states read the absence of "the" before "territories" differently, a dispute the West Bank volume explains. [cite:wb_wiki_242]

Holding without deciding

The government's legal adviser on these questions warned early. In September 1967 the Foreign Ministry's legal counsel, Theodor Meron, advised that "civilian settlement in the administered territories contravenes the explicit provisions of the Fourth Geneva Convention"; days later the cabinet approved a settlement at Kfar Etzion, presented as an army outpost. [cite:wb_gorenberg_meron] Israel's governments came to argue that the Convention did not apply to the West Bank as a matter of law, since Jordan's annexation of it had not been recognized, while undertaking to observe its humanitarian provisions; critics answered that the Convention protects people rather than sovereign claims. [cite:wb_cow_israelview] The first decade of settlement was small: by Peace Now's count, 1,500 settlers lived in the West Bank outside East Jerusalem in 1972 and 4,400 in 1977. [cite:wb_peacenow_pop]

Three things can be stated as documented facts about mid-1967. First, Israel held, for the first time, almost all the land west of the Jordan that the Revisionist and maximal programmes described in [scene:gi1_versions|Part One] had claimed, together with Sinai and the Golan, which almost none of them had. Second, the government's first recorded answer was not "keep it all": it offered two of the five areas back on the basis of the international border, deferred the West Bank and kept East Jerusalem. Third, that answer was divided, ten votes to nine on the first ballot, so the question of how much to keep was open inside the government from its first week. The rest of this part follows the two answers that formed outside and inside the government: a movement that said none of it could be given up, and a Labor minister's plan to keep some of it and return the populated rest.
