The Other Diagnosis: Capture, Not Too Much Democracy

The symptoms set out in the previous section can be read in two ways. The Dark Enlightenment texts read them as proof that democracy itself is the disease: power hidden behind elections, and a state that grows because voters can be bought. A second reading, held by critics on both the left and the right, takes the same facts and draws the opposite conclusion. On this account the trouble is not that the public has too much say but that it has too little, because influence has been concentrated in the hands of those who can pay for it. If that reading is right, a cure that concentrates power further would make the disease worse. This section sets out the second diagnosis, the evidence for it and its weaknesses, and then asks what the two readings mean for the texts' proposed cure.

Money as the mechanism

Gilens and Page did not conclude that voters were powerless because democracy is weak by nature. Their finding was that economic elites and organised groups representing business carried the weight, and that mass-membership groups carried much less. [cite:ad15_jr_gilens] Their own reading, which they defended against every critique, is that this is a problem of unequal influence within a democracy, not proof that democracy cannot work. [cite:de7_gp_reply] The record of money in American politics since their study gives that reading something to stand on. On 21 January 2010, in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the Supreme Court struck down limits on independent political spending by corporations, holding that such expenditures do not give rise to corruption or its appearance. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] Federal lobbying reached a record of about $5 billion in 2025, up 14 per cent on the year before, according to OpenSecrets' analysis of disclosure reports. [cite:us3_os_lobby2025]

Spending is also concentrated in a small number of hands, on both sides. In the 2024 election cycle, OpenSecrets recorded more than $291 million in political giving from Elon Musk to Republican candidates, committees and outside groups. Timothy Mellon, Miriam Adelson, Richard and Elizabeth Uihlein, Kenneth Griffin and Jeffrey and Janine Yass each gave $100 million or more, all on the Republican side. On the Democratic side, Michael Bloomberg gave $64 million and Dustin Moskovitz $51 million. [cite:de7_os_donors2024] A disclosure record of this kind shows who gave; it does not show what any donor received in return, and that distinction runs through the rest of this book. But it does establish the structure the second diagnosis describes: a political system in which a few people can spend more than most candidates raise.

What is striking is that the Dark Enlightenment texts agree about the mechanism. Land's 2012 serial does not deny that money buys government; it says so more bluntly than any reformer. Winning elections, it states, "is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying." [cite:de7_land_de] Neocameralism, Moldbug's word for a state run as a joint-stock company, does not propose to stop the buying. In Land's summary, its first step is that "the entire social landscape of political bribery ('lobbying') is exactly mapped," and the privileges those bribes purchase are "converted into fungible shares" in the state. The sovereign's revenue is called by its name: taxes become "sovereign rent." [cite:de7_land_de] Both diagnoses, in other words, name the same mechanism. They part company over the remedy. One proposes to limit what money can buy; the other proposes to give those who already buy influence formal title to the state.

Techno-feudalism and enshittification

A second strand of the counter-diagnosis looks beyond elections to the economy that now surrounds them. The Greek economist Yanis Varoufakis argues in Technofeudalism: What Killed Capitalism (2023) that capitalism "has not been overthrown but has instead become something else." In his account the owners of digital platforms no longer compete in markets; they own the markets, and draw "cloud rent" from everyone who must pass through them, while users, whose clicks and data produce the value, become "cloud serfs." Not every reader accepts the label. Reviewing the book for the Washington Post, the scholar Leif Weatherby wrote that he was "not convinced" capitalism had died, while crediting Varoufakis with posing a real problem. [cite:de7_varoufakis_wapo]

The writer Cory Doctorow described the same dynamic from the user's side and gave it a name that the American Dialect Society chose as its word of the year for 2023: "enshittification." In Doctorow's account, platforms first serve their users well, then turn on those users to serve their business customers, then turn on the business customers to take the value for themselves, and finally decline. [cite:de7_ads_enshit] The pattern depends on one condition: that leaving is costly. Users stay because their contacts, their data or their livelihoods are locked in.

Both writers are making economic arguments, and neither is about the Dark Enlightenment texts. Their relevance here is structural. The texts' model state is a platform in the same sense: residents are "customers," the state's income is "rent," and the only remedy for bad service is to "take their custom elsewhere." [cite:de7_land_de] The techno-feudal critique is, in effect, a description of what happens to customers of a sovereign provider when exit is expensive. It reads the private platforms of the 2020s as a warning about the public one the texts propose.

Two diagnoses, one cure tested

The two readings can be laid side by side. Both start from the same symptom, a government that answers to some citizens far more than others. The Dark Enlightenment traces it to democracy and proposes to replace voting with ownership. The counter-diagnosis traces it to concentrated money and proposes to reduce what money can buy, through campaign-finance rules, disclosure, lobbying limits and, for platforms, competition law. Both cannot be right about the cause, and the evidence of the previous section does not settle which is. It is consistent with either.

The counter-diagnosis has its own weak points, and they should be stated as plainly as the texts'. The research reviewed in the previous section found that middle-income and affluent Americans agree most of the time, which limits how much of the gap money can explain. [cite:ad15_enns] [cite:de7_branham_ifs] A record of large donations is not proof that donations bought outcomes; disclosure shows timing and amounts, not motive. And the most direct reform the counter-diagnosis proposes, limiting independent spending, runs into the constitutional ruling in Citizens United, which only the Supreme Court or a constitutional amendment could undo. [cite:ad15_lii_cu]

Even with those limits, the comparison exposes the central weakness of the cure the texts propose. Yarvin has been candid about what it requires. Asked by CNN in May 2025 how his system would prevent a ruler from becoming a tyrant, he said that power must be concentrated "in a single individual" and that one must "then just hope somehow that this is the right individual, or close to the right individual." [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin] A cure that depends on hope is the opposite of formalism's own promise, which was to make power visible and accountable. If the counter-diagnosis is even partly right, and influence in America is already concentrated by money, then a state owned by its largest stakeholders and run by one executive would not reverse the concentration. It would complete it. Whether that state could at least be kept honest by its owners is the question of the next part.

The worst-case reading, tested

This part bears on the hypothesis in one direction only: what the texts' destination is and why it might appeal. Documented: the texts describe a real crisis of trust, and their own diagnosis names money and bribery as the mechanism of democratic politics; their stated remedy is to convert that influence into ownership of the state and to remove the vote. [cite:de7_land_de] [cite:de7_pew_trust2025] Documented: Yarvin in 2025 described the remedy as concentrating power in one person and hoping that person is the right one, and used Roosevelt's 1933 inaugural as his precedent for an executive taking power from a reluctant Congress. [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin] [cite:de7_yarvin_nyt2025] Where the record runs against the texts, it does so plainly: Roosevelt asked Congress conditionally, received specific statutes, and was checked by the courts and the Senate. [cite:de7_fdr_1933] [cite:de7_brit_schechter] [cite:de7_ncc_courtpack] Not supported in this part: any link between the diagnosis and the decisions of any government official. The fact that low trust and diffuse accountability are real makes an executive-centred cure easier to sell; it does not show that anyone is selling it.
