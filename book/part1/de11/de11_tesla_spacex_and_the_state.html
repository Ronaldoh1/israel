Tesla, SpaceX and the State

Musk is often described, by admirers and by himself, as a builder who succeeded in spite of government. The record shows something more interesting: two companies that grew in a long partnership with government, as its contractor, its borrower, its regulated party and, in Ukraine, its stand-in. That partnership predates his politics by two decades. It is set out here by instrument, with dates and amounts, because it is the background against which his later role in cutting government has to be read. Timing is reported as sequence; a contract that follows a donation is not, on that fact alone, a contract bought by it.

Tesla and the "founder" settlement

Tesla Motors was incorporated in 2003 by the engineers Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning. Musk led its first funding round in February 2004, investing $6.35 million, became its majority shareholder and joined its board as chairman. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Eberhard was pushed out in 2007, and Musk became chief executive in 2008. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Eberhard then sued him, and the case turned on who could call himself the company's founder. It was settled out of court in September 2009. The two men, together with JB Straubel, Tarpenning and Ian Wright, agreed to be described as the company's co-founders, and the terms were not disclosed. [cite:de11_af_settle] Each said something generous about the other: Musk, that "Without Martin's indispensable efforts, Tesla Motors would not be here today"; Eberhard, that Musk's contributions had been "extraordinary." [cite:de11_af_settle] A widely repeated line says Musk bought or litigated his way into the title; the record shows a settlement in which five people, Musk among them, agreed to share it.

SpaceX and its first customer

Musk founded Space Exploration Technologies in May 2002 with about $100 million of his own money and became its chief executive and chief engineer. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Its first rocket, Falcon 1, failed on its first three launches; after the third failure, on 2 August 2008, Musk accepted a $20 million investment from Founders Fund "as a precautionary measure." [cite:de11_space_ff] The customer that carried the company through was NASA. The space agency awarded SpaceX $278 million in 2006 and a $1.6 billion cargo contract in 2008, and by 2025 NASA said it had invested more than $15 billion in the company. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] SpaceX's president, Gwynne Shotwell, has answered critics of that dependence in three words: "We earned that." [cite:de11_wapo_38bn]

The public money, by instrument

The fullest public count was published by the Washington Post in February 2025, as Musk was the public face of the cuts described in Book Three. Using only public records, it found that Musk and his companies had received at least $38 billion in government contracts, loans, subsidies and tax credits over more than twenty years, nearly two-thirds of it promised in the previous five years, with a record $6.3 billion committed in 2024 alone and 52 continuing contracts that could pay another $11.8 billion. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] The count excludes classified work, so the true figure is higher. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn]

Three instruments account for the shape of that figure. The first is contracts, mostly from NASA and the Defense Department, for launches, cargo, crew and satellites. The second is loans: a $465 million Energy Department loan in 2010 helped Tesla build the Model S and buy its Fremont factory, and was repaid within a few years. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] The third is regulatory credits, which are not paid by government at all. Federal and state emissions rules let makers of zero-emission cars sell credits to other carmakers that fall short; Tesla earned $11.4 billion from such sales between 2014 and early 2025, about a third of its profits over the period. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] State and local incentives sit on top: a $1.3 billion Nevada package for the battery factory near Reno, shared with Panasonic, and $750 million from New York to SolarCity, which Tesla later bought. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn]

Musk's own statements about this money have varied. In 2015 he told the Los Angeles Times that "If I cared about subsidies, I would have entered the oil and gas industry," and in 2021 that "I'm literally saying get rid of all subsidies." [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] Those are his positions; the figures above are the record against which they can be read.

The national-security business

The defence and intelligence side has grown fastest. In March 2024 Reuters reported, from five sources, that SpaceX's Starshield unit was building a network of hundreds of spy satellites for the National Reconnaissance Office under a $1.8 billion contract signed in 2021, a network separate from the commercial Starlink system; the agency declined to discuss SpaceX's role. [cite:de11_reuters_starshield] On 4 April 2025, while Musk was in government, the Space Force awarded the most demanding national-security launches for the coming years: about $5.92 billion to SpaceX for 28 missions, $5.37 billion to United Launch Alliance for 19, and $2.39 billion to Blue Origin for 7. [cite:de11_ussf_nssl] The procurement had begun under the previous administration, and SpaceX was already the leading launch provider. On 24 April 2026 SpaceX was one of twelve companies to receive prototype agreements for the space-based interceptors of the planned Golden Dome missile shield, worth a combined $3.2 billion, with each firm's share undisclosed. [cite:de11_bd_sbi]

Regulation moved in the same direction after Musk had left government. An executive order of 13 August 2025 told the transport secretary to eliminate or speed up environmental reviews for rocket launch licences; Reuters wrote that SpaceX "potentially stands to be the single biggest immediate beneficiary," though it is not named in the order, which was signed months after Musk and Trump fell out. [cite:de11_reuters_spaceeo] SpaceX had complained for years about licensing delays. The order is a documented benefit; it is not documented as a reward.

Starlink in Ukraine: a private network in a public war

The clearest test of one man's control over infrastructure that states depend on came in Ukraine. After Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022 disrupted Ukrainian communications, Musk agreed to provide Ukraine with Starlink terminals worth millions of dollars. [cite:de11_snopes_crimea] In October 2022 he said SpaceX could no longer fund the service, which he put at $80 million so far, then walked the statement back; on 1 June 2023 the Pentagon confirmed that it contracts with Starlink for services in Ukraine, without giving the price. [cite:de11_dn_pentagon]

In September 2023 a CNN excerpt of Walter Isaacson's biography reported that Musk had secretly ordered Starlink turned off near Crimea in 2022 to thwart a Ukrainian drone attack on the Russian fleet. [cite:de11_snopes_crimea] Isaacson corrected the account the next day: "the Ukrainians THOUGHT coverage was enabled all the way to Crimea, but it was not." [cite:de11_cnbc_isaacson] Musk's own version is that there was "an emergency request from government authorities to activate Starlink all the way to Sevastopol," with "the obvious intent being to sink most of the Russian fleet at anchor," and that agreeing would have made SpaceX complicit in "a major act of war and conflict escalation." [cite:de11_fox_sevastopol] On the record, then, he did not switch the service off; he refused to switch it on, and a private company's owner made a decision about the reach of a military operation.

The dependence became a political argument in 2025. On 22 February Reuters reported, citing three sources, that American negotiators had raised cutting off Ukraine's Starlink access during talks over a minerals deal; Musk replied, "This is false. Reuters is lying." [cite:de11_dcd_minerals] On 9 March he called Starlink "the backbone of the Ukrainian army" and wrote that "Their entire front line would collapse if I turned it off," adding that "Starlink will never turn off its terminals." [cite:de11_aj_sikorski] Poland's foreign minister, Radosław Sikorski, replied that Poland paid about $50 million a year for the service and would look elsewhere if SpaceX proved unreliable; Musk told him to "Be quiet, small man," and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said no one had threatened a cut-off. [cite:de11_aj_sikorski] In February 2026 the same control ran the other way: Ukraine, with SpaceX's cooperation, allowed only verified terminals to operate, and Musk said the steps had likely stopped Russia's unauthorised use, which had reportedly included Starlink on attack drones. [cite:de11_up_whitelist]

The record here supports two plain findings. The American state, and its allies, came to rely for military communications and intelligence satellites on infrastructure owned and controlled by one man. And that man has, on the record, used his discretion over it once, in 2022, against an allied operation, and has said publicly that he will not use it as leverage. Whether that dependence is wise is a policy question that runs across administrations of both parties; it began before his politics changed and has deepened since.
