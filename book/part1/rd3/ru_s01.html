The Electricity Concession and the Grid

Land, labor and arms are the usual measures of how the Yishuv was built. Electricity belongs beside them. On 12 September 1921 the Mandate government signed the Auja (Yarkon) Concession. It granted the Jaffa Electric Company of Pinhas Rutenberg the exclusive right, for 70 years, to generate, distribute and sell electricity in the Jaffa district, using hydroelectric turbines on the Auja [cite:wiki_israel_electric]. It was a monopoly over a whole district, granted by the governing power to a single company.

The common view of the Mandate's public works is that they were neutral economic modernization, of benefit to everyone in the country. The concession tests that view. Its terms, the politics of its approval and, above all, the shape of the network it produced show something more particular: infrastructure that followed Jewish settlement and helped bind it together.

The concession had a legal footing in the Mandate itself. Article 11 allowed the administration to arrange with the Jewish Agency to construct or operate public works [cite:zaoerv_mandate_article11]. Rutenberg also had backing at the top. The High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel, and the Colonial Secretary, Winston Churchill, both supported him [cite:jc_turnberg_rutenberg]. The point of the arrangement, in mechanical terms, was that the British administration did not have to build or run a power system itself; it could grant the right to a concessionaire, and the Mandate named the body with which such arrangements could be made.

The grant did not pass unopposed. In 1922 the House of Lords passed an amendment to stop the concession. The House of Commons then overturned the Lords by a large majority, in a vote led by Churchill [cite:jc_turnberg_rutenberg]. The Lords had tried to stop the monopoly; the elected chamber chose to keep it. A challenge came from abroad as well. Euripides Mavromatis, a rival Greek concessionaire, contested the monopoly before the Permanent Court of International Justice. The sources used here record the challenge but not its outcome, so this book does not state one.

What was built did not match what was promised. The hydroelectric plant on the Auja was never built. When the grid switched on in 1923, it ran on diesel, in Tel Aviv [cite:jstor_electrifying_conflict] [cite:military_wiki_rutenberg]. The concession had also brought Rutenberg irrigation rights in the Auja valley, and those rights ended up benefiting only Jewish settlers [cite:jstor_electrifying_conflict] [cite:military_wiki_rutenberg]. A concession framed around hydroelectric power from the Auja produced a diesel network in the Jewish city and water for Jewish farms.

In 1923 Rutenberg founded the Palestine Electric Corporation. It built the country's only hydroelectric plant, not on the Auja but at Naharayim, on the Jordan. In 1961 the corporation was renamed the Israel Electric Corporation [cite:wiki_israel_electric]. The company founded under the Mandate thus carried on, under a new name, in the state.

The money in this arrangement moved through rights rather than cash. The Mandate government granted a monopoly over a whole district's electricity for 70 years; the concessionaire gained a protected market and, with it, the irrigation rights in the valley. The sources do not give the sums invested, the tariffs charged or the company's revenue, so the financial size of the monopoly cannot be stated here. What they do show is who gained the water and where the power went.

The fullest study of where the power went is the historian Fredrik Meiton's prize-winning work on electrification in Palestine. Meiton finds that the centralized grid knit Jewish settlements together while marginalizing and bypassing most Palestinian Arab communities [cite:mesa_meiton_award] [cite:wiki_israel_electric]. Another historian writes that the Yishuv's economic separatism "was given a tremendous push" by the 1921 monopoly. On this reading, electricity was statecraft: a network laid along the lines of Jewish settlement drew a map of a future state in copper and diesel, decades before any partition line was proposed.

The other reading deserves its strongest statement. The familiar account holds that the Mandate's development of Palestine was neutral economic modernization that benefited everyone, carried out by the means the Mandate text itself provided, a concession under Article 11. The Commons approved the concession by a large majority. Nothing in the sources shows that Arab communities were barred from connection by rule. The findings of Meiton and the second historian concern the shape and effect of the network, not a written policy of exclusion.

That is also where the record runs out. The sources here do not say how many Arab towns or villages were connected by 1936, or on what terms; whether the bypassing Meiton describes was planned or followed from where the company chose to build; or how the Mavromatis case ended. What is documented is the monopoly, the parliamentary fight that kept it, the plant that was never built, the diesel grid in Tel Aviv, the irrigation rights that served only Jewish settlers, and a company that passed intact from the Mandate to the state.
