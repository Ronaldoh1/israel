The Rules Before Epstein, 1973–1999

On the night of 20 October 1973, President Nixon ordered his Attorney General, Elliot Richardson, to fire Archibald Cox, the special prosecutor who had subpoenaed the White House tapes. Richardson refused and resigned. His deputy, William Ruckelshaus, refused and resigned. The third man in line, the Solicitor General Robert Bork, signed the order, and FBI agents sealed Cox's offices that night. [cite:history_snm] What stopped Nixon afterwards was not a law. It was public fury: hundreds of thousands of telegrams to Congress and a wave of impeachment resolutions. [cite:constitutioncenter_snm] Two officials said no, and the order was carried out anyway.

Congress answered in 1978 with the Ethics in Government Act, which created an independent counsel appointed by judges rather than chosen by the Attorney General, so that the executive branch would not decide whether to investigate itself. [cite:pbs_frontline_iclaw] It worked, under presidents of both parties. But it carried a built-in weakness: it expired unless Congress renewed it, which Congress did in 1983, 1987 and 1994. [cite:crs_speccounsel_history] And the Attorney General could still remove a counsel for cause. [cite:encyclopedia_ega1978]

The law left one older power untouched. On 24 December 1992, twelve days before the former defence secretary Caspar Weinberger was due to stand trial in the Iran-Contra affair, President George H. W. Bush pardoned him and five others. [cite:presidency_proclamation6518] The independent counsel, Lawrence Walsh, had disclosed days earlier that Bush himself held notes he had not produced, and that his inquiry was turning to the president. The pardons ended it. [cite:washpost_weinberger_pardon] [cite:walsh_report_ch28] A reform built to fix one route out of accountability had left another, written into the Constitution, fully open.

Then the law itself went. After Kenneth Starr's investigation of President Clinton, both parties had had enough of it. Janet Reno, Clinton's Attorney General, told the Senate in 1999 that her department had "reluctantly" concluded the Act did not serve its purpose, and Congress let it lapse on 30 June 1999. [cite:reno_testimony_1999] [cite:archives_speccounsel_records] The Justice Department replaced it with its own regulations, under which the Attorney General decides whether to appoint a special counsel and may remove one. [cite:everycrsreport_iclaw] That framework is still in force. The one mechanism designed to take the decision away from a conflicted executive has not existed since 1999, and both parties chose that.

None of this was written with Epstein in mind. It is the ground he stood on. Every step that follows, the deal in Florida, the files, the custody of the one person convicted, runs through decisions that sit with the executive branch and that no outside body can make it take.
