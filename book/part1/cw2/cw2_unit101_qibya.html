Unit 101 and Qibya, 1953: Retaliation Becomes Policy

Volume II followed the armistice lines through the years of crossings, killings and expulsions that followed 1949, in [scene:rd6_return_barred|how return was barred]. This section picks up the border in the late summer of 1953, when the Israeli army created a unit whose stated purpose was to strike across it, and when one of that unit's first large operations brought Israel a censure in the strongest language the Security Council used. The operation was Qibya. What happened there over one night in October 1953 is no longer seriously disputed. How it was described at the time, by whom, and with what knowledge is the more revealing story.

A unit for reprisals. According to the archives of Israel's Ministry of Defense, Ariel Sharon was appointed in August 1953 to establish and command Unit 101, created "to carry out reprisal operations in enemy territory." [cite:rs_cw2_mod_sharon] Walid Khalidi and Neil Caplan, who introduced and annotated the relevant excerpts of Moshe Sharett's diaries for the Journal of Palestine Studies in 2002, describe Sharon in 1953 as a 25-year-old major. [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] The unit was short-lived as a separate formation, but its purpose was written into its founding: it existed to answer attacks from across the line with attacks across the line.

It was in action within weeks. Khalidi and Caplan record that Sharon led an attack on the al-Burj refugee camp in Gaza in late August 1953 in which, according to the historian Benny Morris, twenty people were killed, among them seven women and five children, and twenty-two were wounded. [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] The camp lay in the Strip that Egypt administered, and its inhabitants were refugees.

Yehud, 12–13 October 1953. The raid on Qibya followed a killing inside Israel. On the night of 12–13 October 1953 a grenade was thrown into a house at Yehud (the Jewish Virtual Library spells the place Tiryat Yehuda), killing Suzanne Kinyas and her two children. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] The Jordanian–Israeli Mixed Armistice Commission, the body set up under the 1949 armistice to rule on violations, called that attack "intolerable aggression." Israel's representative at the United Nations, Eban, later reminded the Security Council of the Commission's words. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_spv632] The finding matters to Israel's case. The killing of a mother and two children at Yehud was not an Israeli claim alone; the armistice machinery itself, on which Jordan sat, had condemned it.

The decision. The response was planned in the army. According to Khalidi and Caplan, the planning options included "total destruction of the village and maximum harm to the villagers." Moshe Dayan authorized Sharon to choose among them, and the decision was to retaliate "in the firmest possible manner." [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] The Jewish Virtual Library quotes Sharon's diary: "Qibya was to be an example for everyone." [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] The target was a village across the armistice line, on the Jordanian side.

The night of 14–15 October. The force crossed on the night of 14–15 October 1953. How many houses it destroyed and how many people it killed is given differently by different sources, and the difference should be stated rather than smoothed. Khalidi and Caplan give forty-five houses blown up and sixty-nine villagers killed, all of them civilians, about three-quarters of them women and children; this is also the figure Volume II used. Glubb, the commander of Jordan's Arab Legion, counted sixty-six dead. The Jewish Virtual Library gives fifty homes and 69 civilians. [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] The sources therefore agree on the order of magnitude, on the fact that the dead were civilians, and, in two of three counts, on sixty-nine; they differ by five on the houses and by three on the dead.

Sharon later gave his own account. In his autobiography, Warrior, he wrote that he had not known the houses were occupied ("I couldn't believe my ears"), suggesting that families had stayed inside expecting only limited damage. [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] That account, written long afterwards, sits beside the planning option that Khalidi and Caplan record, and the reader can weigh the two. Neither source fetched here establishes what the men who set the charges knew of the houses' occupants that night.

What the Foreign Minister knew. The man who would have to defend the raid abroad had not been consulted on its scale. Moshe Sharett, Israel's foreign minister, wrote in his diary on 16 October 1953 that had he feared "such a slaughter" he "would have raised hell," and that "Horror gripped me" when he heard Jordanian radio describe the destruction. [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] The diary matters because it records what ministers discussed in private while the government said something else in public. The contest between Sharett and Ben-Gurion over retaliation, of which Qibya was an early instance, is the subject of [scene:cw2_sharett_ben_gurion|the leadership split].

Washington, 18 October. On 18 October 1953 the State Department expressed "the deepest sympathy for the families of those who lost their lives in and near Qibya." It described an attack "by Israeli forces," said that those responsible "should be brought to account," and announced that it had joined Britain and France in asking the Security Council to consider the frontier, with a direct report from General Vagn Bennike, the Chief of Staff of the UN Truce Supervision Organization. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_qibya_pr] The statement attributed the raid to Israel's forces a day before Israel's Prime Minister denied it. Washington was at that moment also withholding economic aid from Israel over a separate dispute on the Syrian front; Dulles insisted, two days later, that the deferral followed Bennike's ruling on the canal works and not "the more recent incidents," a distinction told in [scene:cw2_banat_yaacov|the Banat Yaacov suspension].

Ben-Gurion's broadcast, 19 October. On the evening of 19 October 1953 Ben-Gurion went on the radio. He denied "the absurd and fantastic allegation that 600 men of the Israel defense forces took part," and said that "not a single Army unit was absent from its base" that night. He attributed the raid to Israeli border settlers whose patience had been exhausted by the killings at Yehud. He regretted that innocent people had been killed, and said that "all the responsibility rests with the government of Transjordan." [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bg_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya] The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported the speech on 21 October under the heading that Ben-Gurion blamed the Qibya provocation on the Arab states' war policy. [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bg_qibya]

Sharett's diary shows that the settlers' story was a choice made in the cabinet, not a misunderstanding. Ben-Gurion insisted that the army not admit responsibility and that the government say border residents "had taken the law into their own hands." Sharett noted that it would not be believed. When the UN observer's account contradicted it, Ben-Gurion wrote in a marginal note that the observer's version was a "fantastic lie." [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] The public denial and the private record are therefore two different kinds of evidence. The broadcast is documented as what Israel's government said; the diary is documented as what its ministers knew. On the question of who carried out the raid, every source fetched for this section other than the broadcast, including Sharett's private record and Sharon's own later account, places it with Israel's armed forces. [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_qibya_pr] [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] [cite:rs_cw2_jvl_qibya]

Israel's case at the Council. At the United Nations, Israel's argument turned on what had preceded the raid. At the Council's 632nd meeting on 29 October 1953, Eban set the raid against the attacks that had preceded it and reminded the members that the Mixed Armistice Commission had itself called the Yehud killings "intolerable aggression." [cite:rs_cw2_spv632] The strongest form of that case, in its own terms, was this: Israeli civilians were being killed by men crossing from Jordanian-held territory; the armistice machinery could condemn such attacks but not stop them; and a government that answered only with complaints would be inviting more. Israel also asked for a conference with Jordan under Article XII of the armistice agreement. [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101]

Resolution 101, 24 November 1953. The Council answered on 24 November 1953, at its 642nd meeting. Resolution 101 found that the "retaliatory action at Qibya by Israeli armed forces" violated the cease-fire and was inconsistent with the armistice agreement and the United Nations Charter. It expressed "the strongest censure" of that action and called on Israel to prevent such actions in future. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_res101] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101] The resolution had a second part, often forgotten. It noted that there was "substantial evidence" of unauthorized crossings of the demarcation line, and it asked Jordan to strengthen its measures against them. It asked the Chief of Staff to report within three months, taking account of Israel's request for an Article XII conference. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_res101] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101] It was adopted by 9 votes to none, with Lebanon and the Soviet Union abstaining. [cite:rs_cw2_un_res101]

Read together, the two parts of the resolution summarize what the Council concluded. It did not accept Israel's denial; it named "Israeli armed forces" and censured them in the strongest language it had. Nor did it accept that the border's violence ran in one direction; it found substantial evidence of crossings from the Jordanian side and called on Jordan to act. On this reading, what it would not accept was that the second justified the first.

From unit to doctrine. Censure did not end the policy; it changed its form. In January 1954, according to the Ministry of Defense archive, Unit 101 was folded into paratrooper battalion 890 under Sharon's command. He later commanded Paratroopers Brigade 202, under which cross-border operations continued. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_sharon] A specialist unit had become the core of a regular formation. The raids that followed, at Nahalin, Gaza, Khan Yunis, Kinneret and Qalqilya, are told in their places later in this chapter.

The word "retaliation." Each party used the same word for its own purpose. Israel's defence establishment wrote it into the unit's charter: Unit 101 existed for "reprisal operations in enemy territory," a phrase that presented each raid as an answer to a prior attack. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_sharon] The Security Council used the word too, but as a charge: "retaliatory action ... by Israeli armed forces" was the thing it found to violate the cease-fire. [cite:rs_cw2_avalon_res101] In the Council's usage, calling an act retaliation identified who had done it and denied that it was lawful; in the army's usage it explained why it had been done. Ben-Gurion's broadcast avoided the word's official sense altogether, presenting the raid as the spontaneous reprisal of settlers rather than a state's act. [cite:rs_cw2_jta_bg_qibya] And the planning record shows that, in this case, the response was not measured against the act it answered: three people had been killed at Yehud; the options considered for Qibya included "maximum harm to the villagers." [cite:rs_cw2_jps_qibya] Whether a policy of this kind deterred attacks or multiplied them was the question that divided Israel's own leaders for the next three years.

The record leaves some things open. The exact wording of the operational order is not in the sources fetched here, beyond the planning options Khalidi and Caplan report; nor is the identity of the Yehud attackers, or the village they came from, if they came from one. The counts of houses and dead differ slightly among the sources and cannot be reconciled from them. And Sharon's later statement that he did not know the houses were occupied can be neither confirmed nor refuted from these documents. What is not open is who carried out the raid. The United Nations, the United States government, Israel's own Foreign Minister writing privately, and Sharon himself all place it with the army, against the account that Israel's Prime Minister gave the public on 19 October 1953.
