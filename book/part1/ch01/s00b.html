"Semitic": A Family of Languages

A common claim folds two statements into one sentence: that "antisemitic" means anti-Jewish, and that criticism of Israel is therefore antisemitic. The first half is historically sound. The second is the subject of a live, documented dispute among scholars and governments. Neither can be weighed properly until the root word is clear, because almost every later argument over the term depends on remembering, or forgetting, where it came from.

The root is Shem, in Hebrew שֵׁם, one of the three sons of Noah in the genealogy of Genesis 10. In 1781 the German scholar August Ludwig von Schlözer coined the term "Semitic" (semitisch) for the family of related languages that the biblical genealogy traced to Shem. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] Schlözer was working as a linguist, sorting languages by their relationships. His term was a classification of languages. It was not a racial category and not a religious one. Schlözer's framework grouped together the languages now called Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Ge'ez, Akkadian and Phoenician. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] He coined it more than a century before Zionism existed as a political movement, and long before the word became politically loaded in either direction.

The label works the way "Romance languages" does. That name, taken from Rome, covers French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Romanian, and nobody takes it to mean that the French and the Romanians are one ethnicity or share one religion. "Semitic" names a language family after a figure in Genesis in the same way. Through most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries it stayed a term of the comparative philology classroom, used as uncontroversially as "Indo-European" or "Sino-Tibetan" to group related tongues. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology]

The family as linguists describe it today includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Amharic (spoken in Ethiopia), Tigrinya, Maltese, Ge'ez, Syriac and Mehri, as well as Akkadian, which is extinct. Their speakers include Arabs, Jews, Ethiopians, Assyrians, Chaldeans and Maltese, and by the word's original meaning all of them are Semitic peoples. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] This is not a contested claim. It is the definition an introductory course in Semitic linguistics teaches in its first week. In that original sense the word applies equally to a Hebrew speaker, an Arabic speaker and an Amharic speaker.

Palestinian Arabs belong to this family on two separate grounds. The first is language: Palestinians speak Arabic, a Semitic language in the same family as Hebrew, Aramaic and Amharic. The second is descent. Peer-reviewed population genetics, including the Y-chromosome study of Nebel and colleagues (2000) and the genome-wide study of Behar and colleagues published in Nature in 2010, documents that Palestinian and Jewish populations in the Levant share substantial common ancestry, consistent with descent from populations that have lived in the region continuously since antiquity. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] Haber and colleagues' 2013 work on ancient Canaanite DNA points in the same direction. [cite:haber_2013_bronze] On this evidence, Palestinians and Israeli Jews share more common ancestry with each other than either does with populations elsewhere. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] [cite:haber_2013_bronze] The same body of genetic research is used in this book to trace the Ashkenazi, Sephardi and Mizrahi Jewish lineages [scene:s00b2|Jewish lineages and the genetic record]; here it is applied to the other population that draws on the same regional ancestral pool. Palestinian Muslims and Palestinian Christians alike are, in the word's own sense, a Semitic people.

It is worth being exact about what kind of claim this is. It is a linguistic and genetic fact, not an argument built to win a political point, and it does not by itself resolve any policy dispute between Israelis and Palestinians. It does rule out one rhetorical move. Actions against Palestinians cannot coherently be described as "pro-Semitic," and a political conflict between two Semitic-speaking populations cannot be described as a contest between Semites and anti-Semites. The word was never built to do that work.

Nor does the linguistic origin make later uses of the word illegitimate. Words change their meaning over time, and that is normal. The compound "antisemitism" took on its own meaning in the 1870s and 1880s, and that meaning, set by use rather than by the parts of the word, is hatred of Jews. In 1879 Wilhelm Marr, a German journalist and a Lutheran, not a Jew, made "antisemitism" the name of his movement against Jews, as a euphemism for Judenhass, Jew-hatred, meant to sound modern and scientific rather than crude and religious; the adjective was older, first written in 1860 by the Jewish scholar Moritz Steinschneider against a theory of Semitic inferiority. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] That history is told in full in [scene:s00b0|the history of the word "antisemitism"]. What matters here is its result. In its original meaning, antisemitism meant hostility toward Jewish people. It did not describe hostility toward Arabic speakers, and it never did. [cite:wiki_marr_1879]

The live dispute concerns a different extension of the word: toward political criticism of a state and of a political movement. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance's working definition of antisemitism, adopted in 2016, carries 11 attached examples; 7 of them describe criticism of Israel or Zionism as antisemitic under certain conditions. [cite:ihra_definition] Its critics read this as redirecting the word toward a new target. On 25 March 2021 the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism, signed by more than 200 scholars including Omer Bartov, Christopher Browning, Michael Rothberg and Noah Feldman, kept the core definition of antisemitism but named exceptions explicitly: boycott of Israel (BDS), criticism of settlements and criticism of Zionism as an ideology are not antisemitic. [cite:jerusalem_declaration] Marr's meaning of the word still stands; so does the 2016 extension, and so does the challenge to it. The three competing definitions, the legal uses made of the IHRA text, and the strongest case its supporters make for it are set out in [scene:s00b1|the word as a shield].

A widely repeated everyday version of this extension substitutes another phrase for the word itself. It holds that opposing or criticizing Israel denies Jews their right to national self-determination, and is therefore discriminatory, keeping a charge close to antisemitism without using the word. The formula appears in pro-Israel commentary, where a 2019 formulation by Bari Weiss is among the most widely cited, in the materials of hasbara (public-diplomacy) organizations, and in critiques of those materials alike. [cite:currentaffairs_snappy_hasbara] It is a current, documented instance of the argument, not a hypothetical one.

The Jerusalem Declaration has serious, named critics of its own, and a fair account has to hear them where the dispute is first raised. Dr. Dana Barnett, a researcher at Israel's Begin-Sadat Center for Strategic Studies, and analysts writing for NGO Monitor have argued in print that the Declaration's explicit exceptions do less to clarify than to shelter. In their reading, permitting comparisons of Israeli policy to settler colonialism or apartheid, and classifying BDS as ordinary non-violent protest, creates cover for anti-Zionist advocacy that can, in specific instances, cross into real antisemitism. [cite:besa_jda_barnett] [cite:ngomonitor_jda_bogus] So the argument runs in both directions. Some credentialed scholars hold that the IHRA definition reaches too far into legitimate criticism; other credentialed scholars hold that the Jerusalem Declaration reaches too far toward giving cover to the real thing. Both positions are in print under their authors' names.

Why this belongs at the start of the book is simple. If "Semitic" names a language family and all its speakers, and Palestinians are Semitic-language speakers descended from the region's continuous population, then the conflict cannot be framed as Semites against anti-Semites. It is a documented political dispute between a state, a political movement, and a displaced population native to the land, all of whom are Semitic in the word's original sense. Every later subject in this book, from funding and lobbying to the wars and the peace negotiations, reads differently once that conflation is cleared away: claims and counter-claims can be judged as a historical dispute between two real populations, rather than arriving with a framing that makes one side's grievance illegitimate before the facts are examined.

What this does not settle should be said as plainly. Clearing up the root word does not decide who is right in any of the specific disputes documented later. It does not show that a given criticism of Israeli policy is legitimate, or that it is not; that depends on what is said and at whom it is aimed. The genetic studies describe populations and shared ancestry; they settle no one's rights. And the argument between the IHRA definition and the Jerusalem Declaration remains open. This book does not resolve it; it records who holds each position, on what grounds, and leaves the reader to weigh them.
