What to Watch, and What Anyone Can Do

This book has spent most of its length on rules: how electors are chosen, how lines are drawn, how money moves and how ballots are counted. The rules can look fixed from a distance, as if they had been handed down and could only be endured or overthrown. Up close they turn out to be made and remade all the time, often by small numbers of people in ordinary places, and the next round of that work is already on the calendar. This last section sets out what is due to be decided, and what the record shows ordinary people can do about it.

3 November 2026

Four sets of decisions in this book are on the ballot on 3 November 2026, and their results are not yet known. In Alaska, Measure 2 asks voters whether to repeal the top-four primary, ranked-choice general elections and the "true source" rule requiring disclosure of the original donors behind political spending, all adopted by 50.55 per cent in 2020; a repeal failed by 49.88 per cent in 2024. The repeal is backed by President Trump, Representative Nick Begich, Governor Mike Dunleavy, the state Republican Party and Elon Musk, and opposed by the state AFL-CIO, the Libertarian Party and Unite America; by 2 October the opponents had raised $8.55 million and the supporters $2.52 million. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] In Massachusetts, Question 3 would replace party primaries with a single top-two primary from 2028; it is backed by former governors of both parties and opposed by both state parties. [cite:dt7_bp_maq3] In Montana, I-194 would bar corporations, unions and other entities from spending on candidate elections, parties and ballot issues. [cite:dt5_sosmt_2026] And voters in Honolulu, Irvine and Longmont decide whether to adopt ranked-choice voting for their own elections. [cite:dt6_fv_2026use]

The same standard applies to all four. None of them is a contest between democracy and its enemies. Each is a disagreement among people who all claim to be defending the vote, and in each case the coalitions cross the usual lines: a labour federation and a libertarian party on the same side in Alaska, Republican and Democratic former governors on one side in Massachusetts and both parties' organisations on the other. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] [cite:dt7_bp_maq3]

After the votes are counted

Some of what this book describes will change in the weeks after it is read. The compact needs states with 48 more electoral votes, and its supporters expect a court challenge if it gets them. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia] The DISCLOSE Act and the Fair Representation Act sit in a Congress where neither has the 60 Senate votes it would need. [cite:dt7_whitehouse_disclose2026] [cite:dt6_fra_text] The first federal elections since parties were freed to coordinate unlimited spending with their candidates will show, in the filings that follow, whether money moves from super PACs to parties or simply adds a channel. [cite:dt7_skadden_nrsc] Missouri's voters will eventually decide whether their legislature's new map stands, and the mid-decade maps drawn in 2025 and 2026 will go on being tested in court. [cite:dt3_bp_redistrict2026] Each of these is a place where a reader can check this book's claims against what happens next.

What anyone can do that the record shows works

The first thing is to vote in primaries. About one eligible voter in five took part in the 2022 primaries, and in the many districts that are safe for one party, the primary is where the seat is decided. [cite:dt3_bpc_turnout2022] [cite:dt3_cook_pvi2025] A primary electorate that small can be moved by relatively few people, which is the same arithmetic that gives organised groups and large donors their leverage there.

The second is to use the tools that already exist. A New York City resident's gift of up to $250 to a participating city candidate is matched eight to one with public money. [cite:dt7_nyc_match] Every Seattle resident receives four $25 vouchers each cycle, and in 2023 fewer than one in twenty used them. [cite:dt7_cascade_seattle] Those programs were built to make small donors count, and they count only when used.

The third is to take part in changing the rules themselves, which is less remote than it sounds. Alaska's repeal measure reached the ballot with 42,837 verified signatures; Montana's I-194 campaign submitted about 50,000, where 30,121 were required. [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] [cite:dt5_bp_i194] New York City's ranked-choice system was adopted by 510,153 votes in an off-year election, Maine's by a margin of about four points, and Arizona's redistricting commission by a ballot measure that the Supreme Court upheld fifteen years later. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] [cite:dt7_bp_airc] In Takoma Park, 132 sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds voted in 2020 under a rule their city wrote for them. [cite:dt7_wm_vote16] Signatures, testimony and votes on measures of this kind are where most of the reforms in this book came from, and where most of the reversals came from as well.

The fourth is to hold every side to the same standard. The spending that worries reformers on one side has its mirror on the other: Future Forward and MAGA Inc., Fairshake and the United Democracy Project, Musk and Bloomberg, teachers' union PACs and Meta's new super PAC. [cite:dt4_os_2024record] [cite:dt4_wiki_fairshake] [cite:pm_sludge_final2024] [cite:de7_os_donors2024] [cite:dt4_74_aft] [cite:dt5_techcrunch_metapac] A rule worth adopting is one a citizen would accept with the other party in power, and the measures on this year's ballots are a chance to apply that test.

The test

The book began with Thiel's "little hope that voting will make things better" and Yarvin's verdict that democracy is "very weak." [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] [cite:de1_nyt_interview] Against the routes and dates in this part, the grades are these. Documented: voters have changed the rules of their own elections by voting, in New York City, Maine, Alaska, Arizona and Seattle, and voters have also rejected reforms and adopted bans, in 2024 and before. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] [cite:dt7_bp_airc] [cite:dt7_bp_seattle] [cite:dt6_bp_2024results] Probable: that the city and state routes will keep producing change in both directions, while the federal and constitutional routes stay closed for now. Not supported: that the vote is powerless to change the system, since most of what this book describes as reform was done by it. Not supported either: that finishing the democratic design would by itself make government work well or win back trust; the reforms' measured effects are small, and the scorecard found six of the critics' eight charges beyond their reach. [cite:dt7_nelson_ps] [cite:dt8_pew_intl2026]

That is the book's answer to its own question. The failure the critics describe is real. The cause they name, democracy itself, is not shown by the record, and the design they propose in its place has never been tried. [cite:de8_land_de] The fuller democracy they never examine is not a theory either. It exists in pieces, in a ranked ballot in New York, a levy in Seattle, a commission in Arizona and a city election in Takoma Park, each adopted and kept by people who collected signatures, counted rounds, argued at public meetings and voted on the rules themselves. Like the checks described in [scene:de40_the_garden|The Garden], none of it works by itself, and all of it depends on people doing particular work. The work is open to anyone who wants to do some of it, and the next chance to do so is on the calendar.
