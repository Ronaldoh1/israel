The Record: Soldier, Politician, Debtor, Convert, Pastor

Cyrus Ingerson Scofield, the man whose notes framed the most widely used reference Bible of American fundamentalism, left two accounts of his life. One is the authorised biography, The Life Story of C. I. Scofield, written by his admirer Charles G. Trumbull and published in 1920, the year before Scofield died. The other is the paper record: Confederate service files, Kansas legislative journals, Department of Justice lists, court dockets and newspapers, assembled most fully by the historian D. Jean Rushing in a thesis for East Tennessee State University in 2011. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_rushing] This section follows the paper record, and notes where the authorised account departs from it. The record is unflattering in places. It is set out here as documented, with nothing added.

Michigan, Tennessee and the Confederate army

Scofield was born on 19 August 1843 in Lenawee County, Michigan, the youngest of seven children; his mother died three months after his birth. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] In February 1861, aged seventeen, he was staying with a married sister in Lebanon, Tennessee. When the war began he joined the local volunteers, who became Company H of the 7th Tennessee Infantry on 28 May 1861. [cite:ts4_rushing] On 8 April 1862 he was admitted to Chimborazo Hospital in Richmond for exposure. The next day the Confederate Conscription Act extended all enlistments by three years, and Scofield wrote to the Secretary of War, George Randolph, asking to be released: he was a native of Michigan, not a Confederate citizen, he had enlisted underage while visiting his sister, and he was worn down by exposure. [cite:ts4_rushing] The discharge came on 26 September 1862, on the ground that he was "an alien friend"; Rushing notes that only 373 such alienage exemptions were granted during the war. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield]

The discharge did not end his service. Confederate authorities soon ordered him to a camp of instruction at McMinnville. Instead he walked about 75 miles north to Union-held Bowling Green, Kentucky, took the oath of allegiance to the United States, and was paroled on 18 November 1862 at the age of nineteen. Rushing treats this as desertion. [cite:ts4_rushing] Four decades later, in 1903, the Dallas chapter of the United Daughters of the Confederacy awarded him the Southern Cross of Honor. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Trumbull's version has him serving through the whole war, under fire in eighteen battles, and decorated "for bravery at Antietam". Rushing found nothing unusual in the Confederate records for Antietam, and the documented service ends in 1862. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_rushing]

St. Louis, Kansas and the attorney's office

After the war Scofield went to St. Louis. On 27 September 1866 he married Leontine LeBeau Cerré, from a prominent French Catholic family of the city, in a civil ceremony; they had two daughters, Abigail and Marie Helene, and a son, Guy Sylvester, who died in December 1874. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] He read law in his brother-in-law's office and in late 1869 moved to Atchison, Kansas. In November 1871 he was elected to the Kansas House of Representatives as a Republican for Atchison County, chaired its Judiciary Committee, and the next year won a seat for Nemaha County. In 1873 he backed John J. Ingalls in the contest for the United States Senate. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield]

Ingalls, once seated, nominated him for federal office. President Ulysses S. Grant appointed Scofield United States Attorney for Kansas on 19 March 1873; he took the oath on 8 June and resigned on 20 December, about six months later. Five days before resigning he declined to prosecute the outgoing senator Samuel Pomeroy, on the day the Chicago Tribune reported affidavits in the Pomeroy bribery affair. [cite:ts4_rushing] The Department of Justice's own history of the Kansas office says he "was forced to resign from that position due to questionable financial transactions". [cite:ts4_doj_ks] A Kansas City Journal article of 28 December 1899, "A Preacher with a Past", said he had left owing thousands to Atchison Republicans, including money he had collected on Ingalls's behalf and kept. Biographers have also reported allegations of railway bribes and forged signatures on bank notes. None of the Kansas allegations was prosecuted, and they remain allegations. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Trumbull gives a two-year term and says Scofield left because politics was interfering with his law practice. [cite:ts4_trumbull]

The forgery case, 1877–1879

The documented legal trouble came after Kansas, and it was a case that never reached trial. In June 1877 a man named Jeptha Simpson said Scofield had obtained money from him with a note bearing the forged signature of Scofield's sister Emeline; Simpson sued in August, Emeline denied any part in it, and Scofield avoided service. [cite:ts4_rushing] Between November and December 1877 he was arrested in Milwaukee under the name "Charles Ingerson" over unpaid hotel bills. In April 1878 a second, nearly identical forgery complaint was filed in St. Louis. In October 1878 he was found in Horicon, Wisconsin, under the name "Cyrus Ingerson"; the Milwaukee Daily Sentinel exposed him on 3 October, he was held for St. Louis, and he entered the St. Louis jail on 8 October 1878. He "remained jailed much of the year". [cite:ts4_rushing] The Department of Justice history records the same fact in plain terms: he was "jailed in St. Louis on forgery charges". [cite:ts4_doj_ks]

On 6 November 1879 the court entered a nolle prosequi: the prosecution dropped the charges because it had "failed to produce enough evidence". A Topeka newspaper later reported that his "wealthy sister came forward" and settled the forged notes. [cite:ts4_rushing] Scofield was therefore jailed for much of the year before the charges were dropped, awaiting a trial that never came, and was never convicted and never sentenced. Whether he committed the forgeries is a separate question; the court record does not answer it, and the settlement report suggests why the case lapsed.

The family he left

In these same years Scofield left his wife and daughters. Leontine took the girls back to Atchison and went to work to support them; she later became the town's librarian. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_wiki_kellogg] By his own later account he had become a heavy drinker. [cite:ts4_trumbull] Leontine divorced him on grounds of desertion in 1883. He married Hettie Hall von Wartz soon afterwards (1883 in the biographer David Lutzweiler's dating, 1884 in Trumbull's), and their son Noel was born in 1888. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] [cite:ts4_trumbull] Trumbull's 1920 biography does not mention the first marriage or the daughters at all, and Scofield's will left nothing to them; his estate, including the Oxford royalties, went to Hettie and Noel. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_wiki_kellogg] There was one divorce, not two.

Conversion, ordination and Dallas

Scofield dated his conversion to 1879 in St. Louis. Trumbull names a lawyer, Thomas S. McPheeters, who read scripture to him until he said "I will", and quotes Scofield on his drinking: "Christ came in, and drink went out." [cite:ts4_trumbull] Rushing places a visit to a Washington Avenue mission after the charges were dropped in November 1879. [cite:ts4_rushing] By the autumn of 1879 he was helping in Dwight L. Moody's St. Louis campaign and serving as secretary of the city's YMCA, under the mentorship of the St. Louis minister James H. Brookes, whose place in the premillennial Bible-conference movement is told in [scene:ts3_niagara|Part Three]. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] Dallas Theological Seminary's archive records that he was licensed to preach in 1880. [cite:ts4_dts]

In 1882 he took charge of a small Congregational mission in Dallas with about fourteen members. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_dts] He was ordained a Congregationalist minister in October 1883, while Leontine's divorce suit was pending. [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] The church, later First Congregational and now Scofield Memorial Church, grew to more than five hundred members by 1895, when he left to lead Moody's church at East Northfield, Massachusetts; he returned to Dallas in 1902–03. [cite:ts4_dts] [cite:ts4_trumbull] He had no college or seminary training, a point Trumbull himself makes, and from the early 1890s he styled himself "Rev. C. I. Scofield, D.D." although no record shows any institution granting him the degree. [cite:ts4_trumbull] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] From 1902 he worked mainly on the reference Bible that Oxford published in 1909 ([scene:ts5_edition|Part Five]). He died in New York on 24 July 1921. [cite:ts4_dts]

The record answers what kind of man Scofield was before 1879 more fully than his admirers did: a deserter from one army who later accepted the other's honour, a federal prosecutor who left office under a financial cloud, a debtor jailed for much of a year on forgery charges that were dropped, and a husband who abandoned his family. It also shows a conversion that held, a pastorate that grew, and a working life after 1879 that drew no further charges. Neither half settles whether his reading of the Bible was right. A theology is tested against texts and arguments, not against its author's character, and the volume treats Scofield's notes that way in [scene:ts5_notes|Part Five].
