"Czechoslovak Arms Saved the State": The Pipeline of 1948

A familiar belief holds that the United States armed Israel from its founding. In 1948 it did the opposite. Washington kept its arms embargo in place, and the government that sold the Jewish side the fighters and rifles it used in the war was communist Czechoslovakia, acting with Soviet consent. Some of the money that paid for them was raised in the United States, but the weapons came from the East. The American military relationship with Israel that later defined its arms supply did not exist in 1948. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel]

The first contract. The pipeline opened before the state did. On 14 January 1948 the Jewish Agency signed its first arms contract with Czechoslovakia, coordinated through "Secretariat D" of the state engineering-works holding. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] [cite:wiki_operation_balak] Two dates frame it. It came four months before independence, at a time when the purchaser was not a state but the Agency that represented the Jewish community of Palestine. And it came three months before the United Nations Security Council imposed its own arms embargo on 17 April 1948, in Resolution 46. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] [cite:wiki_operation_balak] A third date sits awkwardly between them. Czechoslovakia became communist in the coup of February 1948, a month after the first contract, and sold with Stalin's consent. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] The sources record Soviet consent for the sales; they do not explain how Moscow's position related to a contract signed before the coup.

Operation Balak. The most striking deliveries came by air. From May to August 1948 a secret airlift, Operation Balak, ran from the Žatec airfield west of Prague, code-named "Zebra," to Ekron. It carried arms, ammunition and personnel, and it carried fighters: the Avia S-199, a Czech-built derivative of the German Messerschmitt Bf 109, dismantled and crated inside C-46 and C-54 transport aircraft. About 25 were delivered, and they formed 101 Squadron. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] [cite:machal_avia_s199] The airlift did not stay secret for long. A defector exposed the Zebra base, the United States pressed the United Nations to have it closed, and operations moved to Nikšić in Yugoslavia. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] [cite:machal_avia_s199] The episode shows Washington's posture plainly: it did not supply arms, and it acted to shut down the supply that was reaching the new state.

The rifles. More came by sea. The deliveries included some 35,000 German-pattern Karabiner 98k rifles and heavy weapons, many of them originally produced for the Wehrmacht. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] Many of the rifles of the new state's first war were German-pattern army rifles sold by a Soviet-bloc government.

The other side's suppliers. Czechoslovakia was not the only outside power arming a party to the war. Britain, according to the same sources, supplied aircraft and training to the Arab air forces, while the United States maintained its embargo. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] And the Soviet bloc's choice was not lasting. Within months, the record notes, it began shipping the same kind of weapons toward the Arab side. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] The Soviet diplomacy behind the bloc's position in 1947–48 is told in [scene:so_s01|the Soviet layer]; what the arms record shows is a convergence of interest for a period, not an alliance.

The price. In a 1952 report, the Czechoslovak Foreign Minister, Viliam Široký, put the receipts from the sales at almost $14.5 million. [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] That is the seller's own figure, given four years later, and it is the one the record can cite to a named official. The library's funding ledger shows how uncertain the true cost is. It gives a range for the Czechoslovak arms from $14.5 million to about $90 million, about $0.19 billion to $1.2 billion in today's dollars, and notes that estimates vary up to six-fold. It sets this beside the reported $50 million from Golda Meyerson's American tour, spent mostly on arms, $3 million in letters of credit from Nicaragua's Somoza for arms buying, and Al Schwimmer's roughly 30 aircraft, whose cost the record does not total. Its conclusion is that no audited grand total for the war's arms has been published.

The two ends of the Czechoslovak range each have a case. The low figure has a documentary source in the seller's foreign minister, and it measures receipts. The higher estimates in the ledger are not attributed in the sources used here to a named document, and this book cannot say what they include that Široký's figure leaves out. What is clear is the link between the two halves of the year's money. The reported $50 million that Golda Meyerson brought back from the United States financed weapons purchases, including those from Czechoslovakia, as told in [scene:us_s07|Golda Meyerson's tour]. [cite:reformjudaism_golda] [cite:jwa_golda_50m] American private money paid, in part, for Soviet-bloc arms.

The library's summary of the money behind the state sets these sums side by side: the reported $50 million from the 1948 tour; about $14.5 million paid for Czechoslovak arms, with Stalin's approval; and a $100 million US Export-Import Bank loan earmarked for Israel after recognition. Only the last came from the American government, and it was a loan for building the state, not weapons.

When American arms came. The aid ledger's timeline fixes the American side. From 1948 to 1961 US aid to Israel consisted of economic aid and loans only; Washington avoided selling arms for fear of alienating the Arab oil states. The first American military aid came in 1962, and then as loans. The larger military relationship that followed, from the first congressional earmark in 1971 to the airlift of 1973 and beyond, belongs to later volumes of this library.

Ben-Gurion's verdict. Ben-Gurion left no doubt about how he weighed the shipments. In 1968 he said: "Czechoslovak arms saved the State of Israel, really and absolutely." [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] It is the judgement of the man who led the war, given twenty years later. It sits beside his tribute to Meyerson's fundraising as "the money which made the state possible," and the two are not in conflict: the money, in part, paid for the arms.

What the record does not settle. The sources establish the first contract's date, the airlift's route and months, the number of fighters and rifles, and Široký's figure. They do not settle the true price, which ranges six-fold in the estimates, or itemize what was bought for it. They do not show how the January contract, signed before the communist coup, related to the Soviet consent recorded afterwards. They name the defector who exposed the Zebra base only as a defector. And Ben-Gurion's verdict, however weighty, is an assessment rather than a measurement of how much the Czechoslovak weapons, as against other factors, decided the war; the balance of forces is examined separately in this chapter.
