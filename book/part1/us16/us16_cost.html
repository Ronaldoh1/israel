What It Costs

The coverage laws described in the previous section are paid for very differently, and the bills differ in size by a factor of more than three. This section sets the spending of the two countries side by side over time, then looks at two costs that fall on households: medical debt in the United States, and the price of medicines in both. All international comparisons use the World Health Organization's spending series as published by the World Bank, so that both countries are measured on one definition. [scene:us16_systems|Who is covered and who pays]

What each country spends

The United States spends far more than Israel by any measure. On the World Health Organization's series, published by the World Bank, current health spending was 12.5 per cent of GDP in the United States in 2000, 16.2 per cent in 2010, a peak of 18.5 per cent in the pandemic year 2020 and 16.7 per cent in 2023. Israel's was 6.6 per cent in 2000, 6.9 per cent in 2010, 7.7 per cent in 2020 and 7.1 per cent in 2023. [cite:us16_wb_chegdp] Per person, in dollars adjusted for purchasing power, the United States spent $4,549 in 2000, $7,836 in 2010 and $13,473 in 2023; Israel spent $1,750, $2,097 and $4,033. The American figure was about 3.3 times Israel's in 2023, not the "nearly four times" sometimes quoted from mismatched bases. [cite:us16_wb_chepc] The national accounts give the latest figures in each currency: $5.3 trillion in 2024, $15,474 per person and 18.0 per cent of GDP by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' definition, which is wider than the WHO's; NIS 146 billion and 7.3 per cent of GDP for Israel. [cite:us16_cms_nhe2024] [cite:us16_cwf_israel] The American series goes back further: $353 a person in 1970, or $2,208 in 2024 dollars. [cite:us16_pkff_spending]

One common claim does not hold in the form usually stated. Households in Israel pay a larger share of health costs out of pocket than households in the United States: 20.5 per cent of current health spending in 2023 against 10.9 per cent, down from 29.5 and 15.1 per cent in 2000. [cite:us16_wb_oop] Because total American spending is so much higher, the amounts run the other way. Out-of-pocket spending in the United States was $1,632 a person in 2024; Israel's 19.9 per cent share of $3,941 a person in 2024, at market exchange rates, works out at about $780. [cite:us16_pkff_spending] [cite:us16_cwf_israel] That last figure is derived here from the Commonwealth Fund's numbers, not published as such.

Medical debt: what is measured and what is disputed

The best-measured household cost in the American system is medical debt; this book found no comparable Israeli survey, so the figures here are American only. Using the Census Bureau's Survey of Income and Program Participation, the Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker found that people in the United States owed at least $220 billion in medical debt at the end of 2021; about 20 million adults owed more than $250, 14 million more than $1,000 and 3 million more than $10,000. Eleven per cent of adults uninsured all year reported medical debt against 8 per cent of those insured all year, and 13 per cent of Black adults against 8 per cent of white adults. [cite:us16_pkff_debt] The often repeated claim that most American bankruptcies are medical is disputed. A 2018 study in the New England Journal of Medicine by Carlos Dobkin, Amy Finkelstein and colleagues found, using California hospital records, that hospitalisations caused about 4 per cent of bankruptcies among non-elderly adults, against a widely cited figure of about 60 per cent from surveys of people who had filed. [cite:us16_mit_bankruptcy] David Himmelstein, Steffie Woolhandler and Elizabeth Warren, authors of the earlier studies, replied that the method misses costs incurred outside hospital and illness in the family. [cite:us16_pnhp_reply] The book uses the measured debt, not either bankruptcy figure.

Medicines

The two governments set drug prices in different ways. Israel's Ministry of Health negotiates directly with manufacturers and fixes a maximum price for each prescription drug from the three lowest prices among seven reference countries; in 2024 it cut the maximum prices of 1,500 drugs by 3.6 per cent. Pharmaceuticals were 12.9 per cent of Israeli health spending in 2022, about $405 a person. [cite:us16_cwf_israel] In the United States Medicare paid no negotiated drug prices until 2026, as set out below. Prescription drugs were 8.8 per cent of national health spending in 2024; retail drug spending grew 7.8 per cent a year on average from 2020 to 2024. [cite:us16_pkff_spending] A RAND study of 2022 prices found American prices at 278 per cent of those in 33 other high-income countries across all drugs, and at 422 per cent for brand-name drugs before rebates and more than three times after estimated rebates. Unbranded generics were cheaper in the United States than in most of the comparison countries. [cite:us16_rand_drugs]

Medicare price negotiation began only recently. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 required the government to negotiate prices for some high-cost Medicare drugs. The first ten negotiated prices took effect on 1 January 2026; the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services estimated that they would have saved Medicare $6 billion, 22 per cent net, had they applied in 2023. Fifteen more, including Ozempic and Wegovy, take effect on 1 January 2027 with an estimated 44 per cent net saving, and a third round of 15 drugs was selected in January 2026 for 2028. The 2025 reconciliation law widened the exemption for drugs that treat rare diseases, which delayed the selection of Keytruda and Opdivo; the Congressional Budget Office put the cost at $8.8 billion over a decade. [cite:us16_kff_negotiation]

On spending, then, the gap is wide and long-standing, and it is not explained by households in Israel paying more out of their own pockets: they pay a larger share of a much smaller bill.
