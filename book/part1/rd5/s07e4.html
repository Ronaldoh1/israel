The King David Hotel Bombing

The bombing of the King David Hotel is sometimes remembered, where it is remembered at all, as a minor incident in a British administrative building. The record does not allow that description. It killed 91 people in a single attack. It was carried out by an organization whose commander later became Prime Minister of Israel. It hardened British opinion toward leaving Palestine. And it remains, on its own documented merits, one of the most consequential single events of the Mandate period.

The attack came on July 22, 1946, three weeks after the Black Sabbath raid. The Irgun, commanded by Menachem Begin, detonated explosives in the basement of the hotel's southern wing, the part of the building that housed the British Mandate Secretariat and the military command. The explosives were reportedly concealed in milk churns. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The 91 dead included British, Arab and Jewish civilians and administrative personnel. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] It was the deadliest single attack against the British during the entire Mandate period. The sources give the total toll and its composition across communities; they do not provide a full list of the individual victims.

As the previous section showed, the target was chosen for specific reasons. The Secretariat wing was the administrative and military center of British rule, and Irgun intelligence believed that documents seized from the Jewish Agency during Operation Agatha were being processed there. The bombing was intended both to destroy that evidence and to strike the apparatus that had carried out the raid.

Whether the people in the building were adequately warned is a question the historical record has not resolved. The Irgun's own account holds that warning calls were placed about 25 minutes before the detonation to the hotel switchboard, to the French consulate and to the Palestine Post newspaper, urging evacuation. British investigators and later historians have disputed whether any warning actually reached the hotel's management in time to act, or whether it was dismissed as a hoax. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] Both accounts are documented. Neither has displaced the other, and this book does not choose between them.

The man who commanded the Irgun at the time of the attack had a long political future. Menachem Begin later served as Israel's Prime Minister, from 1977 to 1983. [cite:wiki_menachem_begin] The line from commanding this operation to holding the country's highest office is a matter of record. Begin's own later justifications and public statements about the bombing are not covered in the sources used here, and nothing should be inferred about them from this account.

The reaction within the Jewish political leadership was public. The Jewish Agency, whose seized documents had helped bring about the attack, publicly condemned the bombing. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The condemnation was one visible instance of the tension between the mainstream political leadership and the paramilitary groups. The sources document the public statement, not the private views of individual officials or the internal debate behind its wording.

The bombing's effect in London was significant. It is documented as having markedly hardened British public and parliamentary opinion toward disengaging from Palestine altogether. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] In that way it contributed to Britain's decision in 1947 to hand the whole question to the United Nations. It was not the only cause of that decision; Britain faced several pressures at once. But it was one significant factor among them.

The consequence was that the future of Palestine moved from a British problem to an international one. When the United Nations took up the question in 1947, the Jewish Agency was ready with a prepared case, while the other party chose not to appear, a story told in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair].
