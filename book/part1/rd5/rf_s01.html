Objections at the Time

A familiar assumption holds that no one in the United States questioned lobbying on behalf of a foreign state until much later, in the era of AIPAC. The record shows that objections were raised as events unfolded, by Jewish organizations, by a CIA officer and by a Christian committee, and that a foreign parliament protested as well. The objections were made and published. They were also outweighed.

One organized opposition came from the American Council for Judaism, founded in 1942 and led by Lessing Rosenwald with Rabbi Elmer Berger. The Council argued that Judaism was a religion, not a nationality, and it opposed partition. In 1948 it objected to United Jewish Appeal funds being used "for political and foreign governmental purposes." [cite:djn_1948_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman] Its weight should not be overstated. The Council's own historian documents it as a small minority that mainstream Jewish organizations opposed, and Zionist sources describe it as a fringe group. [cite:kolsky_acj] [cite:jvl_acj_truman]

A more mainstream body also stopped short of endorsing a Jewish state. At the 1944 congressional hearings, the American Jewish Committee proposed an international trusteeship for Palestine instead of a Jewish commonwealth. [cite:djn_1944_hearings] Together with the Council, it shows that American Jewish opinion in these years was not a single position, and that alternatives to statehood were argued openly by Jewish institutions.

Criticism also came from within the national-security world. In 1948 Kermit Roosevelt Jr. published a 24-page pamphlet, "The Partition of Palestine: A Lesson in Pressure Politics." [cite:ptsem_kr_pamphlet] Roosevelt would later serve as the CIA's field commander in the 1953 coup in Iran. [cite:w_kermit_jr] That same year, together with Virginia Gildersleeve, former dean of Barnard College, he formed the Committee for Justice and Peace in the Holy Land. The committee warned that "extreme Zionist pressure" risked disrupting national unity. [cite:mosaic_cia_zionism] [cite:w_kermit_jr] Its argument was about the political process in Washington, the same concern that Reston had raised in the New York Times in 1946.

Objections came from abroad too. Iraq's parliament protested the congressional resolutions of 1944. [cite:djn_1944_marshall]

None of these objections changed the outcome. The pattern is the same one traced in the preceding sections on [scene:s07j|the warnings] and the CIA estimate: concerns were stated clearly, on the record, and in time, and were set aside. Formal action on the question of foreign agents came only much later, with a 1962 order under the Foreign Agents Registration Act and the Fulbright hearings of 1963.

The existence of these critics matters for two reasons. It refutes the idea that the question of lobbying for a foreign state was unthinkable before recent decades; it was posed in print in 1948. And it shows that the critics included Jewish organizations as well as Christian and governmental voices, so that the debate cannot be reduced to one community against another. It was an argument among Americans about how their government was making a decision, and in the years before 1948 one side of that argument consistently prevailed.
