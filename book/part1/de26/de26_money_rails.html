Money Rails: Watching Payments, Stopping Them

Every electronic payment runs on "rails," the networks of banks, card companies and settlement systems that move money from one account to another, and every rail keeps records. Two powers sit on those records. The first is to watch: banks must report certain transactions to the government. The second is to stop: an account can be closed, a payment refused, a balance frozen. Both powers are old, both have been used by administrations of both parties, and both are now being rebuilt for digital money. This section sets out what the law requires, what has been documented in practice, and how far the worst case would have to stretch it. The denial of commerce to particular people, from sanctions lists to emergency freezes, is the "denied" rung of the ladder in [scene:de35_the_consequence_ladder|Part Thirty-Seven] and is only pointed to here.

The watching power

The Bank Secrecy Act of 1970 requires banks and other financial institutions to file a Currency Transaction Report for cash dealings over $10,000 and a Suspicious Activity Report, or SAR, whenever they suspect a transaction may involve crime; the reports go to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, FinCEN, a bureau of the Treasury. Since United States v. Miller in 1976, the Supreme Court has treated bank records as information the customer handed to a third party, so agencies may search the reports without a warrant. The volume is large. In fiscal year 2024 institutions filed 4.7 million SARs, about 12,870 a day, and 20.5 million currency reports, and personnel of the Department of Homeland Security, ICE's parent, ran about 290,000 searches of the reporting in the same period. [cite:de26_aba_fincen] The customer is not told a SAR has been filed. The rule that protects location data after Carpenter ([scene:de26_counterweights|the last section of this part]) has not been extended to bank records.

The stopping power, both directions

Banks may close accounts largely at their own discretion, and governments of both parties have leaned on that discretion. In 2013, under President Obama, the Justice Department began "Operation Choke Point," aimed at keeping fraudsters out of the payment system; guidance from the bank regulator FDIC listed "high-risk" merchants that included payday lenders and sellers of firearms and ammunition. Critics said lawful industries were being cut off; the list was withdrawn in 2015, and on 16 August 2017 the Justice Department wrote that the initiative was "no longer in effect, and it will not be undertaken again." [cite:de26_cfslm_chokepoint] In December 2025 the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency reported preliminary findings that the nine largest national banks, between 2020 and 2023, had restricted service to whole sectors, among them firearms, coal, Arctic oil and gas, private prisons, payday lending, tobacco, adult entertainment, digital assets, and political action committees and political parties, often for reasons of reputation rather than financial risk. It was still reviewing nearly 100,000 complaints and had made no finding of political or religious debanking. [cite:de26_occ_debank]

On 7 August 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14331, "Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans." It defines "politicized or unlawful debanking" as restricting service because of a customer's political or religious beliefs or lawful business the provider disfavours for political reasons, and orders regulators to strip "reputation risk" from their guidance within 180 days and to review banks' past policies. Only religion-based debanking may be referred to the Attorney General. [cite:de26_fr_14331] The record therefore runs in both directions at once: pressure on lawful sectors under one administration, bank policies that restricted political committees and private prisons under the next, and an order against those policies under the third.

Programmable money

Digital money adds a third power: to stop a payment by code. A "stablecoin" is a cryptocurrency token designed to hold a fixed value, usually one dollar, backed by reserves the issuing company holds. It moves on a public blockchain, a shared ledger on which every transfer between addresses is visible to anyone, permanently. The issuer controls the token's software and can mark an address so that its tokens cannot move, a "freeze," or destroy them, a "burn." A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, would instead be money issued directly by the central bank in digital form. [cite:de26_fr_14178] [cite:de26_crec_genius]

The United States has rejected the second and written the first into law. Executive Order 14178 of 23 January 2025 states that agencies "are hereby prohibited from undertaking any action to establish, issue, or promote CBDCs," unless required by law. [cite:de26_fr_14178] The GENIUS Act, signed on 18 July 2025 as Public Law 119-27, did the opposite for private issuers. [cite:de26_crs_genius] Its text provides that a permitted stablecoin issuer "shall be treated as a financial institution for purposes of the Bank Secrecy Act," which brings SAR filing to stablecoins; that it must keep "technical capabilities, policies, and procedures to block, freeze, and reject specific or impermissible transactions"; and that it must have "the technological capability to comply, and will comply, with the terms of any lawful order." It defines a lawful order to include one requiring an issuer to "seize, freeze, burn, or prevent the transfer of payment stablecoins." [cite:de26_crec_genius] Implementing rules were still being written in 2026. [cite:de26_crs_genius]

The power is already used at scale by issuers acting on their own terms. Tether, which issues the USDT stablecoin, said in February 2026 that it had frozen about $4.2 billion linked to illicit activity and had worked with more than 310 agencies in 64 countries, including the Justice Department and the Secret Service; the figures are the company's own. [cite:de26_forklog_tether] On 23 April 2026 it froze more than $344 million in two addresses, working with the Treasury's sanctions office and American law enforcement; its chief executive, Paolo Ardoino, said, "USDT is not a safe haven for illicit activity." [cite:de26_decrypt_tether] Documented uses so far concern sanctions, fraud and crime. No freeze aimed at a person for political speech has been documented.

The theorists and the rails

The texts this book maps do not ask for this machinery. Nick Land's wish-list in The Dark Enlightenment calls for "Restoration of hard money (precious metal coins and bullion deposit notes) and abolition of central banking," and the "Dismantling of state monetary and fiscal discretion," a programme that points away from a state able to freeze accounts by code. [cite:de4_land_de] Curtis Yarvin's own answer to who controls a sovereign corporation is a board holding cryptographic keys to the state's weapons ([scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight]); a freeze switch built into money rhymes with that design, but rhyme is not provenance, and no document links the GENIUS Act, a banking statute passed by Congress, to either writer. [cite:de8_kofman_ny] [cite:de26_crs_genius]

Capability is not intent

The capability is documented: a reporting system that files thousands of reports a day and is searchable without a warrant, a history of sector-wide account closures under both parties, and a federal statute that requires every regulated stablecoin to carry a freeze-and-burn switch for lawful orders. The worst-case extension is plain to state: if payroll and retail moved onto stablecoins, a lawful order could stop a named person's money at once, with any hearing afterward. What the record does not show is that switch, or any other rail, used against a critic for speech. The same administration that legislated the switch also barred a central-bank digital currency and ordered regulators to stop reputation-based debanking, which cuts against the reading that the rails are being prepared as a political weapon. [cite:de26_fr_14178] [cite:de26_fr_14331] [cite:de26_crec_genius]

If it were connected

The money layer is already joined to enforcement at one point. Documented: DHS searches the banks' reports to FinCEN, about 290,000 times in fiscal year 2024, and the September 2025 domestic-terrorism memorandum tells the Treasury to guide banks on suspicious-activity reports about the funders of political violence ([scene:de30_nspm7_and_detachment|Part Thirty-Two]). [cite:de26_aba_fincen] [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] Possible, with no document: those reports, or the Treasury's own records through the "common API layer" Palantir has been building for IRS modernisation since September 2025, loaded into ICE's integration platforms beside its location and people-search data; and a stablecoin freeze order aimed at the addresses of a person on an enforcement list. The first would need an agreement between agencies, and the IRS records are behind the tax-privacy wall the courts enforced in 2025–26; the second would need a "lawful order" under the GENIUS Act and an exchange's records linking an address to a name. [cite:de27_treasury_api] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de26_crec_genius] Not supported: any rail, report or freeze used against a person for political speech; the documented freezes concern sanctions and crime.
