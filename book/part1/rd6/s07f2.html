Abraham Feinberg: A Donor, a Campaign, and a Decision

Abraham Feinberg's name does not appear in most popular accounts of 1948. The usual explanation of Truman's support for Israel rests on personal conviction and on political calculation about Jewish voters in general, with no documented case in which financial need changed a specific decision. The record contains one such case, and Feinberg stands at its centre.

Abraham "Abe" Feinberg was born in 1908 and raised in the Bronx, and he made his fortune in the hosiery business. From 1945 until 1968 he played a significant behind-the-scenes role in the relationship between the United States and Israel, serving as a confidant and key financier to three presidents: Truman, Kennedy and Johnson. He was also one of the original seventeen members of the Sonneborn Institute, the arms-procurement network formed in 1945. He died in 1998. [cite:emory_feinberg_thesis] [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb]

The 1948 campaign brought him to the centre. Truman's campaign was broke, and Thomas Dewey was widely expected to make him a one-term president. Truman needed $100,000, a very large sum in 1948, to fund a whistle-stop tour through the Midwest. Feinberg and the jewelry magnate Ed Kaufmann led the fundraising that produced that amount at that moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman] Feinberg later summed up his path to influence in his own words: "My path to power was cooperation in terms of what they needed — campaign money." [cite:prnewswire_feinberg_fbi]

The historian John Judis connects that money to a specific decision. In mid-1948, Truman wanted Israel to stop seizing territory by military force beyond the UN partition lines, and he was personally "disgusted" by Israel's refugee policy. He reportedly said that Jews had turned their own narrative on its head by denying Palestinians the right of return. Judis documents that Truman folded on these impulses in part because he needed the $100,000 from Feinberg and Kaufmann for the whistle-stop tour at that exact moment. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_truman]

The claim should be stated with its limits. It is a historian's sourced account, not an inference from general goodwill, and it names a donor, a sum, a moment and a policy objection that was dropped. It also says "in part." Judis does not argue that money was the only reason Truman stopped pressing, and the wider record of this period, examined in [scene:mn_s02|the question of money and politicians], shows other documented drivers of American policy that involved no payment at all. What the Feinberg case establishes, on Judis's documented account, is that financial dependency did, at least once, change a specific decision.

Feinberg's role did not end with the campaign. In 1952 US authorities ordered him to register as an agent of a foreign principal over Israel Speaks, a publication he ran; he shut it down to avoid registration under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. [cite:israellobby_feinberg_fbi] According to Avner Cohen's history Israel and the Bomb, from 1958 Ben-Gurion secretly made Feinberg his chief coordinator for raising private American funds for Israel's clandestine nuclear weapons program, directing his staff simply to "call Abe." [cite:nationalinterest_feinberg_bomb]

Read together, these episodes describe something more durable than a single campaign gift. Feinberg was a fixture across three administrations, connected to the arms network of 1945, to the campaign of 1948, and to the nuclear program from 1958. Truman's recognition of the state, eleven minutes after its declaration, sits inside this same broader financial relationship. It is not, on the record, a story of money acting as an abstraction. It is a named donor, a precise figure and a dated decision, documented as such.
