Tlon: Founding, Exit and Return

In 2013 Yarvin turned Urbit from a private project into a company. He founded Tlon Corporation in San Francisco to build it, naming the firm after a Jorge Luis Borges story, "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius," about an invented world whose internal logic slowly displaces the real one. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] Urbit, as Tlon described it, was a new personal computer reimagined from the ground up: its own programming languages, a peer-to-peer network, and a permanent cryptographic identity for each user, later recorded on the Ethereum blockchain in a registry called Azimuth. Its identity space was divided, like a feudal map, into 256 "galaxies," 65,280 "stars" and about four billion "planets." [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] How that address space was distributed and sold, and who holds it, is the subject of [scene:de23_address_space|Part Twenty-Three]. This section follows Yarvin's own place in the company: the money he raised, his departure in 2019 and his return in 2024.

The money

The outside money is the documented link between Yarvin and Peter Thiel, and its size has been reported in more than one way. Corey Pein, in his 2018 book Live Work Work Work Die, reported that Thiel's venture firm Founders Fund and Andreessen Horowitz, the firm led by Marc Andreessen, together invested $1.1 million in Tlon; whether that was each firm's stake or the combined round is not clear from the citation. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] Pein had reported Thiel's investment as early as May 2014, adding that Yarvin held the title "benevolent dictator for life" at the company, a term borrowed from open-source software culture for a project's founder who keeps the final say. [cite:de6_pein_2014] Kofman, writing in 2025, put the total investment in Urbit at about $30 million, without a breakdown. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] The two figures are not reconciled by any filing examined here; the first is a single seed-era round, the second a lifetime total that would include later investors and sales of address space. Both are reported, and neither is summed with the other.

Thiel's money reached Yarvin's circle by a second, earlier route. In 2011 Thiel gave $100,000 to John Burnham, who went on to co-found Tlon, a grant reported by Pein and by the historian Joshua Tait. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] The connection types are worth stating exactly, because they are often blurred. Founders Fund's investment in Tlon is a direct financial tie between a Thiel vehicle and Yarvin's company. It is not a payment to Yarvin for his political writing, and no document examined here shows Thiel paying for the writing. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso]

The product, for most of its life, reached few people. In September 2022 the magazine The Point estimated its active users at "a few thousand," and described its main software at the time as a bare-bones text message board. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] Its executives also spent effort keeping the project apart from its founder's politics: Josh Lehman, executive director of the Urbit Foundation, the non-profit that came to steer the network's core development, told the magazine Astra in 2022 that the hardest part of his work had been distancing Urbit from Yarvin. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit]

The exit, 2019

In January 2019 Yarvin left Tlon. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_kumin_mars] He explained his departure in a post titled "A Founder's Farewell." [cite:de5_kumin_mars] A line widely attributed to that post says he had always known Urbit could not succeed until it stopped being his and became the world's; the post is no longer at its original address, and the line is given here as a circulating quotation rather than a verified one. Whatever its exact words, the departure was presented, by him and by the company, as a founder stepping back so that a technical project could outgrow its author. [cite:de5_kumin_mars] The following year he returned to political writing under his own name.

The return, 2024–2025

The project ran short of money in 2024. A newsletter writer close to the community reported that the Urbit Foundation's board fired its executive director in about mid-2024, a step tied to the Foundation running out of funds, and that most of its staff quit in protest. [cite:de5_rettig] In August 2024 Yarvin announced, at an impromptu public meeting with investors and developers, that he was coming back "in a big way," in the words of Noah Kumin, a writer who had worked at Tlon. [cite:de5_kumin_mars] ARTnews and, later, Kofman reported that he returned without an official title as an informal "wartime C.E.O.," and that several of the project's senior employees resigned in protest. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] The phrase describes a familiar idea in Silicon Valley management writing: a founder who takes back personal control in a crisis and suspends ordinary consultation until the crisis passes.

The return did not end the turmoil. An account published in July 2025 described four executive directors at the Foundation in about a year, and a board fight in which the latest, Ryan Kneer, who had taken the post in November 2024 when Yarvin returned, fired Yarvin from the board while Yarvin was lobbying the board's other two members to fire Kneer. Kneer then called a no-confidence vote and appealed to the holders of Urbit's 256 galaxies, the network's top-level owners, to replace the board. [cite:de5_marbl] A second newsletter writer, who holds some Urbit address space himself, reported in late July 2025 that Yarvin was acting as executive director "by fiat," quoted him calling the period "real Roman times," and reported, without knowing the terms, that Yarvin had secured a $4 million investment from Andreessen Horowitz earlier that year in exchange for more address space. [cite:de5_rettig] Kofman, who had been travelling with Yarvin, reported that his newest plan was to market Urbit as something like an exclusive private club. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso]

These later accounts come from community newsletters, not from filings or major outlets, and they are reported here with that standing. No named outlet had reported the outcome of the galaxy holders' vote, or a settled structure for the Foundation, as of October 2026, and the account stops where the documented record does.

What the company record shows

The Tlon story establishes three things for this book. It documents the one financial tie between Peter Thiel and Yarvin: venture investment in a software company, beginning in 2013, alongside Andreessen Horowitz. That is a real connection of a specific type, and it is the strongest one in the record; [scene:de12_the_thiel_years|Part Twelve] and [scene:de14_network_map|the network map] place it among Thiel's other ties. It also shows that Yarvin's political writing and his company were kept apart in public, by him and by the company's later managers, and that the separation was imperfect: the Foundation's own director described distancing the project from him as the hardest part of his job, and conference organisers in 2015 and 2016 treated it as one. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] [cite:de5_dailydot]

Finally, the company's governance gave a small, real-world view of how its founder behaves when he holds power and it is challenged. In 2019 he stepped back; in 2024, in a funding crisis, he returned and took control without a title, and senior staff left; in 2025 the board and the founder tried to remove each other. It would overstate the case to call this a test of his political theory. A start-up's board fight is not a state, Urbit's members could leave at any time, and nothing in the record shows violence or coercion of any kind. But it is his own record, in his own organisation, and the pattern of a founder reclaiming personal authority in an emergency is one his political writing recommends for countries. [scene:de23_wartime_ceo|Part Twenty-Three] tells the full story, and [scene:de33_two_tests|Part Thirty-Three] asks what it shows beside the federal government's experience in 2025.
