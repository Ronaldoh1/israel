The Revision of 1984

The Pacts survived the regime that signed them. When the Italian Republic wrote its Constitution after the war, Article 7 declared that "the State and the Catholic Church are, each in its own order, independent and sovereign," that "their relations are regulated by the Lateran Pacts," and that "amendments to the Pacts accepted by both parties do not require a procedure of constitutional revision." [cite:vt3_cost_art7] The last clause shaped everything that followed. The Republic could change its relationship with the Church by negotiating with the Holy See and passing an ordinary law, without a two-thirds majority or a referendum; but it could not change the Pacts alone without touching the Constitution. The 1929 settlement therefore entered the republican order as a treaty to be renegotiated, not a statute to be repealed.

For more than three decades the Concordat stood largely as written, with its state religion, its bishops' oath and its religious teaching as the "foundation and crown" of public education. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] The revision came under a Socialist prime minister. Bettino Craxi took office on 4 August 1983 and put the file in the hands of his adviser Gennaro Acquaviva; on 28 January 1984 the Chamber of Deputies authorized him to conclude an agreement, by 338 votes to 67 with 30 abstentions. [cite:vt3_agi_1984]

Villa Madama, 18 February 1984

The agreement was signed in Rome on 18 February 1984, at Villa Madama, by Cardinal Agostino Casaroli, Secretary of State, for the Holy See and by Craxi for the Italian Republic, with an Additional Protocol. Parliament ratified it by Law 121 of 25 March 1985. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_agi_1984] It amended the Concordat, not the Treaty: Vatican City's sovereignty, the Financial Convention's settlement and the Treaty's Article 24 were not reopened.

The new text began where the Constitution did. Article 1 restated that State and Church are "each in its own order, independent and sovereign." [cite:vt3_l121_1985] The first point of the Additional Protocol then removed the principle on which the 1929 Concordat had rested: "The principle, originally referred to in the Lateran Pacts, of the Catholic religion as the sole religion of the Italian State is considered no longer in force." [cite:vt3_l121_1985] From 1984 Italy had no state religion.

The rest of the agreement followed from that change. Religious teaching stayed in state schools, but Article 9 gave every pupil, or every parent, "the right to choose whether or not to make use of" it; under the 1929 text it had been the "foundation and crown" of public education. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_lateran_text] Church marriages kept their civil effects under Article 8, on condition that banns were published and the marriage transcribed in the civil registers. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] Article 3 left the Church free to appoint its bishops and required only that the civil authorities be notified of appointments to diocesan sees; the text contains no oath, and the prior political check of 1929 disappeared. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_agi_1984] Rome kept a special mention, but the wording changed: Article 2 recognized "the particular significance that Rome, episcopal see of the Supreme Pontiff, has for Catholicism," in place of the state's 1929 duty to protect the city's "sacred character." [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_lateran_text]

Two further changes concerned money. Activities of Church bodies other than religion and worship became subject to ordinary taxation, and Article 7 set up a joint commission of the two parties to draft the rules on Church bodies and property. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_agi_1984] The commission's work replaced the congrua, the state's direct supplement to clergy incomes under Article 30 of the 1929 Concordat. [cite:vt3_lateran_text] [cite:vt3_agi_1984]

Law 222 of 1985: Church bodies and clergy pay

The joint commission's rules became Law 222 of 20 May 1985, "Provisions on ecclesiastical bodies and property in Italy and on the support of the clergy." [cite:vt3_l222_text] It did three things. First, it set up a network of institutes for the support of the clergy: one in each diocese, and a central institute established by the Italian Bishops' Conference (CEI). Each institute was to provide "the adequate and dignified support of the clergy who serve the diocese," at a level set by the CEI rather than by the state. [cite:vt3_l222_text] Second, from 1989 it allowed individuals to deduct gifts to the central institute from taxable income, up to two million lire a year. [cite:vt3_l222_text] Third, from tax year 1990 it allocated "a share equal to eight per thousand of personal income tax" among the state and the Catholic Church according to taxpayers' choices: the otto per mille. [cite:vt3_l222_text] For the transition years 1987, 1988 and 1989 the state paid the old contributions, increased by 5 per cent a year, to the CEI, and the earlier legislation on clergy pay was repealed. [cite:vt3_l222_text] How the eight per thousand works, and how much it now produces, is the subject of [scene:vt3_otto_per_mille|the next section].

The effect, by design, was to move the Church's Italian income out of the state's budget lines and into a mechanism that taxpayers direct. The state stopped paying priests. Priests are now paid by the Church's own institutes, which draw on parish income, deductible gifts and, above all, the share of income tax allocated to the Church. In 2024 the system supported 31,053 priests; full-time priests received between 1,049.60 and 1,981.12 euros a month, twelve times a year, and bishops 2,204.16 euros. [cite:vt3_icsc_2024] Of the total, 389 million euros came from the eight per thousand and just under 8 million from deductible gifts. [cite:vt3_icsc_2024]

The revision is often summarized as the moment Italy stopped being a Catholic state, and on the legal question that is exact. It is less exact as a summary of the relationship. The Holy See kept its treaty, its territory and its treaty properties; the Church kept religious teaching in state schools, civil effect for its marriages and a public source of income that, as the next section shows, now exceeds a billion euros a year. What changed was the basis. In 1929 the state had paid the clergy and declared a religion; after 1985 it declared none, and taxpayers chose each year where a share of their income tax would go. The Holy See's own representative signed the change; the Republic's prime minister negotiated it with a parliamentary mandate. [cite:vt3_l121_1985] [cite:vt3_agi_1984]
