Elections, Turnout and the Ratings Over Time

Both countries choose their governments in competitive elections whose results the losers have, in the end, accepted, and both are rated "Free" by Freedom House. [cite:us3_fh_il2026] [cite:us3_fh_us2026] The way they vote is very different, and so is the way their democratic ratings have moved since 2010. This section sets out the electoral rules, the turnout record and the main indices, with each figure tied to its definition and to the population it covers. Both countries go to the polls within a week of each other in 2026: Israel on 27 October and the United States, for its midterms, on 3 November. [cite:us3_aje_cec2026]

How each system votes

Israel elects its 120-member Knesset by nationwide proportional representation for four-year terms; voters choose a party list, not a local candidate. [cite:us3_fh_il2025] A list needs a minimum share of the valid vote to win any seat, and that threshold has been raised step by step: 1 per cent from 1951 to 1992, 1.5 per cent to 2003, 2 per cent to 2013 and 3.25 per cent since then. [cite:us3_taub_threshold] The last rise came in the Governance Law the Knesset passed on 18 March 2014, which the Institute for National Security Studies (INSS) warned at the time could cost the small Arab parties their representation unless they merged. [cite:us3_inss_threshold2014] The threshold has a measurable cost in votes. In November 2022 almost 420,000 ballots, 8.8 per cent of the valid vote and about ten seats' worth, went to lists that fell below it; the threshold that year was 154,854 votes. [cite:us3_taub_threshold] Because no party has ever won a majority alone, every government is a coalition; Part Two explains how coalitions are formed. [scene:us2_founding|Part Two: how the state is built]

The United States has no single national election system. Federal elections are run by the states, each with its own registration and identification rules, and House members are elected in single-member districts whose boundaries state legislatures usually draw. Freedom House's 2026 report recorded that several states had tightened registration and identification rules; that Texas, Missouri and North Carolina had redrawn their maps in 2025 to favour Republicans and California to add Democratic seats; that a March 2025 executive order requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register had been permanently blocked by a federal court in October, with appeals pending; and that the Federal Election Commission (FEC) had been left with two members, too few to do much of its work. [cite:us3_fh_us2026] The Supreme Court's ruling in Louisiana v. Callais on majority-minority districts was expected in 2026. [cite:us3_fh_us2026]

Turnout over time

Israel's turnout, measured as votes cast against the registered electorate, has fallen over the long run. It was 86.9 per cent in the first election in 1949, above 80 per cent through the 1960s, 78.5 per cent in 1981, 77.4 per cent in 1992 and 78.7 per cent in 1999. It then dropped to 68.9 per cent in 2003, 64.7 per cent in 2009 and 63.9 per cent in 2013, before recovering to 72.3 per cent in 2015. [cite:us3_jvl_turnout] The Taub Center describes three phases: above 80 per cent until the late 1960s, the high 70s from the 1970s to the 1990s, and the mid-60s to low 70s since the second intifada. [cite:us3_taub_threshold] In the five elections of 2019 to 2022, turnout was 68.5, 69.4, 71.3, 67.4 and 70.6 per cent; in 2022, 6,788,804 people were eligible. [cite:us3_jvl_cec] [cite:us3_i21c_2022]

Turnout among Arab and Druze voters, about one in five eligible voters, has been lower and has swung more widely: 49.2 per cent in April 2019, 59.2 per cent in September 2019, 64.8 per cent in 2020, when the Arab parties ran as one list, 44.6 per cent in 2021 and 53.2 per cent in 2022. [cite:us3_jvl_cec] [cite:us3_inss_arab2022] The swings followed the parties' alliances and splits and the rise in the threshold, which meant that a divided Arab vote risked winning nothing. [cite:us3_inss_threshold2014] One source, INSS in 2014, put 2013 turnout at "some 56 percent"; the Knesset-based series gives 63.9 per cent, and this book uses the latter. [cite:us3_inss_threshold2014] [cite:us3_jvl_turnout]

The American figures come from a different measure: the Census Bureau's survey of the share of voting-age citizens who say they voted. On that measure 65.3 per cent voted in 2024, 1.5 points fewer than in 2020 (about 66.8 per cent); in the 2022 midterms the figure was 52.2 per cent, the second-highest midterm turnout since 2000. [cite:us3_usafacts_turnout] [cite:us3_npr_2022] This book did not pull the Census series for the years before 2020, so it gives no American figure for 1980 or 2000. The two countries' numbers are not directly comparable: Israel's is an official count against the voter roll, the American one a survey estimate against the citizen population, and American midterms, unlike Knesset elections, do not choose the head of government.

Who may run

Israeli election law allows parties and candidates to be barred if they deny Israel's existence as a Jewish and democratic state, incite racism or support armed struggle against it. The rules have been used against far-right Jewish lists and against Arab lists and candidates, and the Supreme Court has overturned several of the Central Elections Committee's initial disqualifications. [cite:us3_fh_il2025] The pattern held in 2026. In September the committee, made up of party representatives, voted to bar the two main Arab lists, the Joint List (Hadash, Ta'al and Balad) and Ra'am, and two candidates, Ofer Cassif of Hadash and Sami Abu Shehadeh, Balad's leader. On 2 October 2026 the Supreme Court unanimously reinstated both lists and, by 7–2, Cassif. A majority signalled it would uphold the bar on Abu Shehadeh, the push against whom Al Jazeera attributed to National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, and he withdrew, calling the episode "a historical catastrophe for democracy". [cite:us3_aje_cec2026] The United States has no equivalent ideological bar on candidates; disputes over ballot access there turn on state filing rules and the courts.

The ratings over time

Freedom House scores countries out of 100. The United States scored 94 in 2010 and 83 in 2020, an 11-point fall that Freedom House attributed to corruption and conflicts of interest, weak transparency and punitive immigration and asylum policies. [cite:us3_fh_fiw2021] It stood at 84 in the 2025 edition and fell to 81 in the 2026 edition, covering 2025, its lowest since the series began; Freedom House cited "legislative dysfunction and executive dominance", pressure on free expression and the weakening of anticorruption safeguards. [cite:us3_afp_fh2026] [cite:us3_fh_us2026] Israel scored 73 in both the 2025 and 2026 editions, with full marks for its electoral process but 2 of 4 for the political rights of minority groups. [cite:us3_fh_il2025] [cite:us3_fh_il2026] Israel's score explicitly excludes what its army and settlers do in Gaza and the West Bank, which Freedom House rates separately at 2 and 22, both "Not Free"; Part Nine deals with the people living there. [cite:us3_fh_il2026] [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank]

The Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) project, which classifies regimes on hundreds of expert-coded indicators, moved both countries down a category within three years. Israel lost its status as a liberal democracy in 2023, the year of the judicial overhaul, and is now classed as an electoral democracy, still the only one in the Middle East and North Africa; it is not listed among the countries currently autocratising. The United States lost its liberal-democracy status in 2025, "for the first time in over 50 years", and joined the list of autocratisers, with an episode dated from 2023. V-Dem found that legislative constraints on the American executive lost about a third of their value in 2025, reaching their lowest point in over a century. [cite:us3_vdem2026] Inside Israel, the Israel Democracy Institute's annual survey recorded in 2025 that only 24 to 27 per cent of Jewish respondents and 12 per cent of Arab respondents rated Israeli democracy good or excellent, and that 65.5 per cent expected the coming election to be free and fair. [cite:us3_idi_2025]

The ratings measure different things and should not be added into one verdict. Freedom House and V-Dem rate the institutions governing a country's citizens; neither score for Israel covers the roughly five million Palestinians in the occupied territories, and neither American score covers the effect of American policy abroad. What they share is direction: by both measures, both systems were rated less free in 2026 than a decade earlier, the United States by the larger margin on Freedom House's scale.
