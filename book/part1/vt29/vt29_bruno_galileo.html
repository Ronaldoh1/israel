Bruno and Galileo: The Stake and the Abjuration

Two trials of the Roman Inquisition, thirty-three years apart, became the standard proof that the Church fought science: Giordano Bruno burned in 1600 and Galileo Galilei forced to abjure in 1633. In the platform's earlier material they also appear as proof of something else, the suppression of inner and Hermetic knowledge; the Sophia Vatican module says Bruno was burned "for explicitly advocating inner spiritual work over institutional religion". The record of each trial is now largely available. It shows two different cases, and neither fits either slogan cleanly. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo]

Giordano Bruno: the charges

Bruno was born at Nola, near Naples, in 1548, entered the Dominican order at seventeen and was ordained in 1572. He left Naples when an indictment was being prepared against him and spent the next years wandering through Europe, writing on memory, magic and cosmology. He argued that the universe is infinite and has no centre, that the stars are suns with planets of their own, and that those worlds may be inhabited. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno]

On 22 May 1592, after the Venetian patrician Giovanni Mocenigo, who had brought him to Venice as a tutor, denounced him, the Venetian Inquisition arrested him, and in January 1593 he was sent to Rome. He was held for about seven years. The Roman trial volumes are lost; what survives is a summary of the case, the Sommario, found in the papers of the Holy Office on 15 November 1940 by Angelo Mercati, prefect of the Vatican Archives, after some fifteen years of searching, and published in 1942. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_linder_bruno]

The charges recorded against him were mostly theological. He was accused of denying the Trinity, the divinity of Christ and the Incarnation, of denying the virginity of Mary and transubstantiation, and of teaching the transmigration of souls, the existence of many worlds, and magic and divination. Cardinal Robert Bellarmine, the leading theologian on the case, demanded a full retraction; Bruno refused. His cosmology was part of the case: in a late interrogation, probably in April 1599, the judges questioned him on his books about the infinite universe, and he defended them. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_linder_bruno]

On 20 January 1600 Pope Clement VIII ruled that he was an obstinate heretic and should be handed to the secular authorities. When the sentence was read, Bruno is reported by a witness, Kaspar Schoppe, to have told his judges: "Perhaps you pronounce this sentence against me with greater fear than I receive it". On 17 February 1600 he was burned alive in the Campo de' Fiori in Rome, his tongue held in a gag, and denied the usual mercy of strangulation; his ashes were thrown into the Tiber. In 1603 all his works were put on the Index of Prohibited Books. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_ht_bruno]

Martyr of science, Hermetic magus, or heretic

Each later age has read Bruno as its own. On 9 June 1889 the city of Rome set up his statue on the spot where he was burned. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] In 1964 the historian Frances Yates argued in Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition that he was above all a Hermetic magus, who read Copernicus through the Hermetic texts and whose real cause was a magical religion. The thesis has been sharply contested: Hilary Gatti, among others, argued that Yates overstated the magic and understated Bruno's contribution to natural philosophy, and other scholars stress that cosmology was only one strand of a case built mainly on theology. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_ht_bruno]

The record allows a narrower statement than any of these. Bruno was condemned by a church tribunal, and executed by the city authorities to which it handed him, for a long list of theological heresies that he refused to retract, with his cosmology and his magic among the charges. Nothing in the Sommario describes the case as one about "inner spiritual work", and the Sophia claim that he was burned for alchemy has no basis in the charges. [cite:vt29_linder_bruno] [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] The Holy See has not withdrawn the condemnation. In 2000, the fourth centenary, Cardinal Angelo Sodano called his death a "sad episode" while defending the judges' intentions, and Pope John Paul II's general request for pardon that year included the use of violence "in the service of truth". [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno]

Galileo: 1616

By the 1610s Galileo was the most famous defender of the sun-centred system of Copernicus. In a letter to his pupil Benedetto Castelli, expanded in 1615 into a letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, he argued that passages of Scripture about nature should not be read as settling questions that observation could decide. Dominican critics sent a copy of the Castelli letter to the Inquisition in February 1615. [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo]

On 24 February 1616 the Holy Office's theological consultants declared unanimously that the proposition that the sun stands still at the centre of the world was "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical". On 26 February Cardinal Bellarmine instructed Galileo to abandon the opinion; whether he was also forbidden to teach or discuss it in any way is disputed, and that disputed wording later decided the trial. On 5 March 1616 the Congregation of the Index suspended Copernicus's book "until corrected" and banned a letter by Paolo Antonio Foscarini defending Copernicus. [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo]

1633: the trial

In 1632 Galileo published his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems, which cost him the patronage of Pope Urban VIII. He was summoned to Rome. On 22 June 1633 the tribunal found him "vehemently suspect of heresy", required him to abjure, banned the Dialogue and sentenced him to formal imprisonment, commuted the next day to house arrest. He lived the rest of his life confined, first in Siena and then at his villa at Arcetri near Florence. [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo] The Sophia module's phrase "imprisoned 1633–1642" should read house arrest. The trial papers are among the holdings of the Vatican Apostolic Archive, described in [scene:vt14_apostolic_archive|Part Fourteen].

The slow reversal, 1758–1992

The Church dropped its general ban on heliocentric books from the Index in 1758 but did not touch the 1633 sentence. In 1820 a papal censor refused permission for a textbook by Canon Giuseppe Settele that taught the earth's motion as fact; the refusal was overturned on appeal. In the 1835 Index, Copernicus's book and Galileo's Dialogue were left out. [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo] [cite:vt29_coyne_galileo] The Index itself, from 1559 to its suppression in 1966, is treated in [scene:vt30_forbidden_arts|Part Thirty].

On 10 November 1979 John Paul II asked scholars to study the case afresh, and on 3 July 1981 a commission was set up. On 31 October 1992 Cardinal Paul Poupard presented its conclusions to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences, and the pope spoke. He called the affair a "tragic mutual incomprehension" and said the theologians of the time had erred in treating the literal sense of Scripture as an account of the physical world. [cite:vt29_coyne_galileo] [cite:vt29_ap_galileo1992] News reports called it an admission that the Church had erred, and the physicist Giorgio Salvini, president of the Lincei Academy, welcomed it as "a recognition of errors and of guilt". [cite:vt29_ap_galileo1992]

The Jesuit astronomer George Coyne, writing for the Vatican Observatory, read the same texts more critically. He noted that the 1992 address attributed the "hasty and unhappy" decision to "certain persons" rather than to the popes or the congregations that issued it, that neither the 1616 decree nor the 1633 sentence was ever explicitly reformed, and that the conclusions were not submitted to the commission's own members for approval. [cite:vt29_coyne_galileo] The 1992 statement was a real change of tone; it was not a formal annulment.

Set side by side, the two trials show an institution that claimed the right to judge both doctrine and the reading of nature, and used the stake and the abjuration to enforce it. They do not show a campaign against inner religion: Bruno's case was about his theology, and Galileo's was about astronomy and the authority to interpret Scripture. Where they connect to the thread of this book is in the instrument: the same Inquisition procedure, born against the Cathars in [scene:vt28_inquisition|Part Twenty-Eight], was by 1600 the means by which Rome decided which knowledge could be held.
