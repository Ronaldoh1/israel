The Final Score, Tested

This section adds up the scores, tests the totals against the choices that produced them, answers directly the questions readers most often ask about the two systems, and gives this volume's final assessment. Every total below is this book's synthesis of the published measures in the previous section, made by the rules stated in the first; none is a fact in the way a census count is, and a reader who weighs the areas differently can recompute from the same rows. [scene:us22_scores|The scores, area by area] [scene:us22_method|How this scorecard grades]

The totals

On what households feel, Israel's system scores 6.4 and America's 4.6, on a method that publishes every row, scores results rather than spending and leaves the contested bankruptcy estimate unscored. Israel wins seven of the twelve areas clearly: health, household debt and prices, housing, family and the safety net, public safety, wellbeing, and the family budget. The United States wins education clearly, and work and wages, the economy and equity narrowly, each lead resting on a single row: average pay, output per person and the gender-gap index. Liberty and coercion goes to whichever side the reader counts.

The tests

The overall order holds in every test: under every weighting, every goalpost setting, every single area removed and with no goalposts at all, Israel's system scores higher, by between about 0.2 and 1.8 points of 10. The narrowest margin, 5.83 against 5.63, comes when only three broad pillars are averaged in the everyone-governed view, because liberty then carries a third of the weight; on that weighting the two systems are close to level. The household result holds the same way: Israel is ahead on 20 of the 28 household rows scored for both countries, and the household score holds whichever single area is removed. The liberty result does not hold. Counting citizens only, Israel's narrow lead depends on the prisons and executions rows and on where the executions floor is set; that is reported here as a tie. Counting everyone each state governs, the United States leads by 6.3 to 5.5, and the order survives the removal of any single row except the vote row, the largest single difference between the two states, without which they are level.

The household score and the lived difference

Readers who know Israelis who have lived in both countries often hear a sharper contrast than an overall score of 6.1 against 4.7 suggests: no hesitation before seeing a doctor, children without financial dread, money left for a holiday. The scorecard bears that out where it measures what a household feels. On the nine household areas Israel scores 6.4 and the United States 4.6, and the gap is widest exactly where those accounts put it: health, 8.4 against 4.0; housing, measured by homelessness; household debt; and family support, where Israeli law guarantees paid leave after a birth, a child allowance, paid sick days, a pension and, when a parent stops paying, child support paid by the state itself. [scene:us22_scores|The scores, area by area]

The overall score is closer because it also weighs things a family budget does not register: output per person and public debt, where America leads; PISA results, where America leads; and liberty, where counting the Palestinians Israel governs puts America ahead. Two further limits explain the rest. Some of what Israelis pay more for is real: food took 17.9 per cent of Israeli household spending against 12.9 per cent of American, and housing a larger share too, which is why hundreds of thousands of Israelis marched over the cost of living in 2011. [scene:us14_protests|Part Fourteen: the 2011 protests] And a national average includes everyone: the experience of a two-salary family in central Israel is not the experience of the Arab and Haredi households whose poverty rates in 2024, 37.6 and 32.8 per cent of families, ran well above the national 20.0 per cent [cite:us8_nii_2024], just as an American family with good employer coverage lives a different budget from one without it. [scene:us8_israel|Part Eight: equality in Israel] What no national survey here can score is the quality of food or the felt ease of daily life; those belong to testimony, and the book records what the measures can support.

One life in each system

The household score can also be read the way a person lives it, stage by stage. The table follows one life through both systems on the figures scored or reported above; green marks the system that comes out ahead at that stage where the figures point one way, and stages where they split are left unmarked.

Read stage by stage, the American system pays better and schools better, and asks its people to buy for themselves, through employers or out of pocket, much of what Israel's laws guarantee: care when sick, income when sick or laid off, leave after a birth, support when a parent stops paying, and saving for old age. Israel's system charges more for food and housing and pays less, and returns more of it as guarantees. The second is the life that people who have lived in both most often describe as less anxious, and the scorecard's household measures point the same way; for the Arab and Haredi citizens whose poverty rates are far above the average, and for the Palestinians Israel governs without citizenship, the table describes a life they do not fully share.

The questions readers ask

Which country carries more public debt? The United States, and its debt has risen for four decades while Israel's fell from the 1980s before rising again with the war. American federal debt held by the public was 99 per cent of GDP in 2025, up from 26 per cent in 1980, and the Congressional Budget Office projects 120 per cent by 2036. Israel's general government debt was about 68 per cent of GDP in 2025, up from 61.9 per cent in 2023 but far below the roughly 163 per cent of the mid-1980s. [cite:us11_omb_debt] [cite:us11_cbo_outlook2026] [cite:us11_imf_debt] [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] [scene:us11_fiscal|Part Eleven: taxes and debt]

Whose households carry more debt? American households: 69.2 per cent of GDP in 2024 against 42.3 per cent in Israel. On top of that, Americans owed $1.651 trillion in student loans in 2026, with 9.3 million borrowers in default; Israel keeps university tuition low by regulation and publishes no comparable national student-debt series. [cite:us14_globes_mortgagegdp] [cite:us14_nyfed_q2_2026] [cite:us17_ci_defaults] [cite:us14_huji_tuition]

Who spends more? It depends on who is spending, and on what. America spends far more per person on health, $13,473 in 2023 against $4,033, and almost twice as much per student across all levels of education, $19,973 against $10,279; on health it gets shorter lives for the money, on schools higher scores. Israel's governments take more in tax, 29.8 per cent of GDP in 2023 against America's 27.7 per cent in 2022, and spend about two and a half times America's share of GDP on defence, 7.8 per cent against 3.1 per cent in 2025. American aid equals about 0.6 per cent of Israel's GDP, and the Congressional Research Service put it at about 14 per cent of Israel's defence budget. [cite:us16_wb_chepc] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_usa] [cite:us17_oecd_eag2023_isr] [cite:us11_oecd_rev_il] [cite:us11_dbn_rev_us] [cite:gi16_boi_2025] [cite:us19_sipri_fs2025] [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us1_fred_ilgdp] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025]

Who works more? Israelis, by about 70 hours a year: 1,870 against 1,800 in 2025, down from 2,033 against 1,832 in 2000, so the gap has narrowed from about 200 hours. Israeli law guarantees 12 days of paid vacation and 15 weeks of paid leave after a birth; American federal law guarantees neither. Israelis also serve: most Jewish citizens are conscripted, and reservists had averaged 233 days of service since October 2023 by mid-2026. [cite:us13_wiki_hours] [cite:us13_toi_vacation] [cite:us13_dol_vacation] [cite:us18_nii_maternity2018] [cite:us19_taub_miluim]

Who is taxed more? It depends on where a person sits. A low-wage worker loses less of each paycheck to income tax and social contributions in Israel, 19.4 per cent of labour costs against 27.5 per cent on the OECD's 2025 figures, but pays an 18 per cent value added tax on most of what he buys, against American sales taxes averaging 7.53 per cent. [cite:us22_oecd_tw26_ch3] [cite:us22_pwc_il_vat] [cite:us22_tf_sales26] At the top, Israel's highest personal rate is 50 per cent including its surtax, with a further 2 per cent on capital income since 2025, against an American federal top rate of 37 per cent plus any state tax. [cite:us22_pwc_il_pit] [cite:us22_irs_ty26] Both systems tax their largest companies below the headline rate. The combined American corporate rate averages 25.57 per cent with state taxes, and a study by the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, an advocacy group, found that 342 large profitable companies paid an effective 14.1 per cent from 2018 to 2022; Israel's rate is 23 per cent, but its "preferred enterprise" regime taxes qualifying firms at 7.5 or 16 per cent, Intel's plant at 7.5 per cent after a $3.2 billion grant, and the State Comptroller counted NIS 67 billion of tax breaks in 2019. [cite:us22_tf_cit25] [cite:us22_itep_corp24] [cite:us22_pwc_il_cit] [cite:us22_pwc_il_incent] [cite:us22_ctech_intel] [cite:us22_mevaker_taxexp] On the whole economy, Israel's governments take a larger share in tax, 29.8 against 27.7 per cent of GDP. [cite:us11_oecd_rev_il] [cite:us11_dbn_rev_us]

Does Israel receive aid from other countries as well? Not on any scale this book found. The United States is the only state that gives Israel regular military aid. Germany has paid part of the cost of Israel's navy: it donated the first two Dolphin submarines, paid about half of the third and a third of the fourth and fifth, up to €135 million of the sixth, €115 million of the Sa'ar 6 corvettes, which cost about €430 million, and €540 million of a roughly €3 billion order for three new submarines agreed in 2022. [cite:us22_ynet_dolphin11] [cite:us22_solar_dolphin6] [cite:us22_gulf_saar6] [cite:us22_almon_dakar] No other government grant of comparable size was found. The larger outside flow is private: Israeli non-profits received $4.4 billion in 2015, $2.8 billion of it from abroad and $1.8 billion from American funders. [cite:us22_chron_giving] [scene:us20_flows|Part Twenty: the money flow both ways] The United States receives no foreign aid of this kind; it gives it, $73.5 billion in fiscal 2024 in 2025 dollars, about a quarter of it military and security aid. Japan's payments toward the American forces stationed there, ¥1,055 billion over five years, are cost-sharing for American bases, not aid. [cite:us22_pew_aid26] [cite:us22_ap_japanhns]

Could the aid explain the gap? The scorecard tests it directly. If Israel had to replace every dollar of American aid itself, about 0.6 per cent of its GDP, and took all of it from family benefits, cutting them from 1.89 to 1.29 per cent of GDP, its household score would fall from 6.43 to 6.39 and its overall score from 6.08 to 6.05; America's would stay at 4.57 and 4.74. The gap in how households live is paid for by how each country taxes and spends its own money, not by the aid. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:us18_socx_isr]

Who is happier? Israelis, on the one survey that asks both populations the same question: 7.187 against 6.816 out of 10, eighth against twenty-third in the 2026 World Happiness Report. [cite:us22_whr26_hli]

Which state is more oppressive? Measured by how each treats its own citizens, neither clearly: the scores tie. The United States imprisons people at two and a half times Israel's rate and still executes them; Israel restricts religion by law, has had a state of emergency in force since 1948, censors its press through a military censor and ranks 116th of 180 for press freedom, against America's 64th. Measured by how each treats everyone it governs, Israel is the more coercive state on most of the rows this volume could score, the vote, Freedom House's rating, press freedom, detention without charge, religion and corruption, though not on imprisonment or executions: about a third of the people under its control cannot vote for its government and, in the West Bank, are tried in military courts, and it holds about 4,500 people without charge. [scene:us4_prisons|Part Four: prisons] [scene:us2_emergency|Part Two: emergency powers] [scene:us3_speech|Part Three: speech and the press] [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] [cite:us4_hamoked_charts]

The final assessment

On this scorecard's published measures, Israel's system delivers more of what households need to the people it treats as citizens than America's does: in coverage, life expectancy, infant survival, household debt, homelessness, family support, safety from homicide and how people rate their lives, Israel scores higher, while spending less than a third as much per head on health. The United States delivers higher pay, higher output per person, better schools on the 2025 results and somewhat smaller gaps between groups on the measures this volume could compare. Taken together, on rules published in full and tested under every weighting above, Israel's system scores about 6.1 of 10 on this scorecard and America's about 4.7; on the household score alone, about 6.4 against 4.6.

That is a statement about systems, laws and governments, not peoples, and it has two limits a reader should keep in view. The first is coverage: Israel's citizens' figures leave out the 5.56 million Palestinians it governs without citizenship, and on liberty, the one area in which the people counted change the answer, counting them breaks the tie in America's favour. The second is cause. The scorecard measures what each system delivers; it does not show that American aid pays for any of Israel's advantages. The aid is military money, spent mostly in the United States, and weighs about 0.6 per cent of Israel's GDP; Israel's health law, wage floor and family benefits are paid for by Israeli taxes, though money is fungible, and aid that covers part of Israel's defence budget leaves an equal sum of Israel's own money free for other uses. [scene:us21_verdict|Part Twenty-One: the aid, the claims and what is open] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable]

What the record does and does not show. It shows each system's score on 39 to 41 shared measures, each from a named source, scored against published goalposts, and shows that the overall ranking survives every weighting tested, narrowly in one. It does not show the result on measures neither country publishes comparably, among them police killings, firearm deaths, medical debt and house prices against income, nor on the spending this scorecard reports but does not score; one row, the American child-poverty rate on the OECD basis, awaits confirmation and is bounded above. Scores are as of October 2026, days before Israel votes on 27 October and the United States on 3 November, and several rows, from health coverage under America's 2025 budget law to Israel's wartime spending, are moving.
