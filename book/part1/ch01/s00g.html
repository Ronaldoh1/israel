IS-RA-EL: A Symbolic Reading of the Name

The previous section set out the documented etymology of the name Israel: El, the Semitic word for God, joined to a root meaning to strive or prevail. This section offers something of a different kind. It presents one symbolic reading of the name, drawn from esoteric traditions. It is an interpretation applied to a documented text, not a documentary or linguistic finding, and it should be read as such. One objection holds that it is mere wordplay with no connection to any religious tradition. The objection is partly right and partly wrong, and the distinction is worth drawing carefully.

The reading divides the English transliteration of the name into three parts. IS is taken to evoke Isis, the Egyptian goddess associated in Hermetic and other inner traditions with a receptive, magnetic, lunar principle, what later traditions would call the divine feminine.[cite:wiki_isis_egyptian] RA is taken to evoke the Egyptian solar deity, a radiant, generative principle that later traditions would call the divine masculine.[cite:wiki_ra_egyptian] EL is the one component that is genuine etymology: the actual ancient Semitic word for God.[cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] In this reading, El stands for the unified field above both polarities. "Israel," so understood, names neither a place nor a people but a state of being, the integration of feminine and masculine under the divine. Its proponents connect this to the transformation the text of Genesis 32 describes in Jacob.

The limits of this reading must be stated plainly. It operates on the English spelling, not on the Hebrew original. It does not claim that ancient Hebrew scribes embedded Egyptian deity names in the word. It does not displace the documented derivation, which remains the scholarly linguistic record. What it proposes is a symbolic resonance: that transformation traditions across the ancient Near East and Egypt shared a common structural language of polarity, integration and ascent, and that the Jacob story reads coherently within that language, whether or not the etymology is literal.

Part of this reading, however, is not new. The idea that Jacob's story describes an inner transformation available to any person, rather than only a genealogical origin, has support in mainstream Jewish commentary. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks, a widely respected Orthodox authority, wrote of Jacob and Israel as two aspects of one person, asking readers whether they are Jacob, embarrassed by who they are, or Israel, with the courage to stand upright.[cite:sacks_jacob_israel] What this book adds, and identifies as its own interpretive layer, is the specifically Hermetic vocabulary of IS-RA-EL, which is not part of mainstream Jewish or Christian commentary.

Non-literal reading of scripture is also formally embedded in the traditions themselves. Judaism has PaRDeS, a named four-level method of interpretation: Peshat, the plain sense; Remez, the hinted or allegorical; Derash, the comparative and homiletical; and Sod, the secret or mystical, the level from which Kabbalah grows. One traditional framing has the text descending from above while the reader ascends through the four levels from below.[cite:wiki_pardes_exegesis] Islam developed a parallel distinction between tafsir, conventional exegesis, and ta'wil, esoteric interpretation, practiced across Shia, Sufi and Sunni scholarship since at least the eighth century CE; the Quran's account of Khidr itself refers to an inner meaning behind outward acts.[cite:wiki_esoteric_quran_interpretation] [cite:britannica_tawil] In Christianity, the third-century Church Father Origen helped establish allegorical interpretation, and scholarship on medieval Catholic exegesis records that High Medieval Catholic scripture commentary was thought perfect only if it contained both literal and allegorical interpretation.[cite:wiki_aggadah] [cite:hnet_tawil_medieval_catholic] In each case the plain or literal sense is named alongside the non-literal one.

The symbolic reading also suggests a narrower argument about the modern state's name. Herzl's 1896 pamphlet weighed Argentina alongside Palestine, and the Zionist Congress voted on British East Africa in 1903. Whichever territory had been chosen, a state named "Israel" would have carried the same scriptural weight, because that weight travels with the word rather than with a particular piece of land.[cite:genesis_15_18] On this view, a largely secular political movement attached its project to a name already freighted with religious meaning. This book has found no primary source showing that Herzl, Ben-Gurion or the 1948 founders consciously intended any such symbolism, and it does not claim they did. The name carries the weight regardless of intent.

The reading, then, is a key for considering the name's layered resonance. It is not a documented finding about origin or intention, and it should be weighed accordingly.
