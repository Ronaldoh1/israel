Four Centuries of the Index, 1564–1966

After Carafa, the Index became an institution. The Council of Trent's Index of 1564, printed at Rome by Paulus Manutius, was the most authoritative guide the church had yet published, and its rules were accepted by later censors. [cite:cn_fordham_index] In 1571 a permanent body, the Congregation of the Index, took over the work; it lasted until 1917, when its duties passed to the Holy Office, the Roman Inquisition. [cite:vt14_brit_index] Edition followed edition. The last, the thirty-second, appeared in 1948 and contained about four thousand titles. [cite:cn_fordham_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index]

The list is the best single record of what the institution feared, and its contents fall into three broad groups. The first was rival religion and rival readings of scripture. Protestant works were banned wholesale, and the Index also contained, the Fordham sourcebook notes, "many theologians and translators of the Bible", among them Richard Simon's Critical History of the Old Testament, one of the founding works of modern biblical scholarship. [cite:cn_fordham_index] The guardians of the canon banned the scholars who studied how it was made.

The second group was knowledge about the world. Copernicus's On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres, published in 1543, was placed on the Index in 1616, suspended until corrected, and removed only in 1835; three works of Kepler stayed on the list until the same year. [cite:cn_wiki_indexlist] [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo] Galileo's Dialogue was banned when he was condemned on 22 June 1633 as "vehemently suspect of heresy". [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo] Descartes was banned in 1663 and Kant's Critique of Pure Reason in 1827. [cite:cn_wiki_indexlist] Giordano Bruno, a former Dominican who taught an infinite universe of suns and planets and took a "dangerous interest" in Hermetic texts and high magic, was burned in Rome in 1600 as an obstinate heretic, and all his works were banned the same year. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:cn_wiki_indexlist]

The third group was the modern mind: philosophy, fiction and politics. Victor Hugo's Notre-Dame de Paris and Les Misérables remained on the Index until 1959. All of Jean-Paul Sartre's works were banned in 1948, and Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex and The Mandarins in 1956. [cite:cn_wiki_indexlist] Hume, Hobbes and Zola were on it too. [cite:vt14_brit_index]

The end came quietly. On 14 June 1966, after the Second Vatican Council, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, the renamed Holy Office, issued a notification signed by its pro-prefect, Cardinal Ottaviani. It said that the Index "remains morally binding, in light of the demands of natural law", but that it "no longer has the force of ecclesiastical law with the attached censure". [cite:cn_va_index1966] The list was not repudiated. It was demoted from law to moral guidance, and no new edition has appeared since. [cite:cn_va_index1966] [cite:vt14_brit_index] In 1992 John Paul II acknowledged that the theologians who condemned Galileo had erred in treating the literal sense of scripture as an explanation of the physical world, and spoke of a "tragic mutual incomprehension". [cite:vt29_ap_galileo1992]

Seen from the length of this book, the Index was the last and most systematic form of a power that began with Irenaeus's list of four gospels. The canon said which books were scripture. The councils of the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries said who could read them and in what language. The Index said which other books a Catholic could read at all, and it said so with the force of church law, under pain of excommunication, from 1559 until 1966: four hundred and seven years. For all that time, the authority to define the list of forbidden books sat in the same institution that had fixed the list of sacred ones.
