The Warnings of 1947: State, the Pentagon and Oil

On 22 September 1947 the State Department's senior official for the Middle East put a blunt judgment on paper: the partition of Palestine was "not workable." Eighteen days later the Joint Chiefs of Staff said much the same in a memorandum of their own, and the Secretary of Defense made the argument in public, before a committee of Congress. The oil company that held America's concession in Saudi Arabia, meanwhile, was lobbying the White House directly. A familiar explanation of American policy holds that the strategic costs of backing partition were not understood until much later, and that the gap was one of limited information or the slow communications of the era. The documents do not support that explanation. Each warning was written down, each was dated, and the most important of them was read aloud to the President. The question the record raises is not whether Washington knew, but why what it knew did not decide the outcome.

The answer is not a hidden hand. It is a government divided against itself: career diplomats, the military command and the Defense Secretary on one side, the President's political advisers on the other, with an oil company pressing from outside and a President who had to choose. This section follows the warnings in the order they were made, then the decision that set them aside.

The State Department's regional expert. Loy Henderson directed the State Department's Office of Near Eastern and African Affairs, the office whose job was to understand the practical consequences of American policy in the region. He was not a desk theorist. He had served as American ambassador to Iraq in the mid-1940s and had watched at first hand how Arab governments were reacting as the Palestine question took shape. [cite:wrmea_henderson_warning_1994]

On 22 September 1947 Henderson sent Secretary of State George Marshall a memorandum warning that partition was "not workable" and would lead to "untold troubles" in the future. He did not present this as a private opinion. His views, he wrote, were shared by "nearly every member of the Foreign Service or of the department who has worked to any appreciable extent on Near Eastern problems." [cite:wrmea_henderson_warning_1994] That sentence turns the memo from one official's dissent into a statement of the settled view of the government's own regional specialists. Later historians have described the memo as one of the most perceptive warnings in the documentary record of the period, and it came from inside the government rather than from a critic outside it.

It was not filed and forgotten. Under Secretary of State Robert Lovett was impressed enough by its substance to read it aloud to President Truman himself. [cite:wrmea_henderson_warning_1994] That is a documented escalation: the warning reached the President in the Under Secretary's own voice. What the record does not show is that every one of Henderson's predictions proved right in every detail. Historians continue to debate how much of the violence that followed was inevitable and how much depended on other factors. What is not in dispute is that the warning existed, in writing, before the vote, and that it came from the department's own experts.

The Joint Chiefs of Staff. The military reached the same conclusion through its own channel. On 10 October 1947 the Joint Chiefs of Staff filed a memorandum titled "The Problem of Palestine." It warned that dividing Palestine into Jewish and Arab states would open the way for the Soviet Union to displace American and British influence in the region, and that it would endanger American access to Middle East oil. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] These were the two concerns, regional alignment and access to resources, that drove American policy in the Middle East generally in these years. The memo did not come from diplomats worried about relationships or from oil executives worried about profits. It came from the country's senior military command, whose task was to assess strategic and resource risk.

The timing is what makes the document stand out. On 11 October 1947, one day after the Joint Chiefs filed it, the United States announced its support for the UN partition plan. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] There are few cases in the period with so narrow a gap between a formal military warning and a policy decision that ran against it. The surviving record documents both the warning and the announcement. It does not contain a stated internal explanation of why this warning, arriving when it did, did not change the timetable. The connection between the two has to be inferred; it is not written down.

The Secretary of Defense, in public. James Forrestal had been Secretary of the Navy and became the first Secretary of Defense, overseeing the consolidation of the armed forces under the new Department of Defense created by the National Security Act of 1947, the same year as his warning. He was the Cabinet official most directly responsible for whether the American military could function. Testifying before a House committee, he said that oil was "the life blood of a war machine," and warned that the Arab world's response to partition would threaten the pipelines carrying oil out of the Persian Gulf, the physical infrastructure on which American forces and the American economy relied. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] The sources available here do not give the date of the testimony or the name of the committee.

Forrestal's testimony and the Joint Chiefs' memorandum made the same argument through two different routes, one public and political, the other internal and military. The record does not show that his warning decided any particular administration decision. It shows it as one voice in a chorus of institutional warning, alongside Henderson and the Joint Chiefs, that was set aside together. Forrestal's diary entries decrying the "bullying" that preceded the final vote belong to the story of the vote itself, told in [scene:s09|the partition resolution].

The oil company. The oil industry did not wait on the government. A 1947 memorandum submitted to President Truman was published under the title "Oil and the State Department policy on Palestine: the documentary evidence of how ARAMCO attempts to destroy the partition resolution of the United Nations." [cite:loc_aramco_oil_pamphlet] Its title states its thesis: that it presents documentary evidence of an active lobbying campaign by the Arabian American Oil Company against partition. ARAMCO held the core American oil concession in Saudi Arabia, the same relationship that lay behind Franklin Roosevelt's meeting with King Ibn Saud two years earlier. A Middle East turned against the United States by partition threatened that concession's value and its security.

Here the money and the policy can be seen side by side. ARAMCO's interest was commercial; the Joint Chiefs' was strategic; Henderson's was diplomatic. All three pointed in the same direction at the same time. The sources treat them as separate and independent warnings, not as one coordinated effort, and the convergence is better explained by a shared dependence on Gulf oil than by any common direction. Nor does the published memorandum reproduce ARAMCO's internal communications or give a full account of its methods. What the record establishes is that a documented lobbying campaign existed and that it failed: the company with the most money at stake in Arab goodwill did not move the decision.

The decision. None of this changed the outcome. Truman had heard Henderson's memo directly. According to several historical accounts, he was worried about his prospects in the 1948 election, and his adviser Clark Clifford urged him to endorse partition in order to secure Jewish-American votes in key swing states. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] Clifford set out that reasoning in a memorandum to Truman in November 1947, naming New York, Pennsylvania and Illinois, and his later memoirs acknowledged both the political framework and Marshall's opposition to it. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The President's own account of his motives, and the case that conviction rather than calculation moved him, are examined in [scene:s08|Truman's succession and his advisers]; the sources used here present the decision as a political calculation weighed against strategic warnings, not as a failure of information.

The chronology needs one correction. One of the sources says the United States voted for partition at the General Assembly "five days" after Henderson's memo. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] The dates in the record do not fit that interval. The memo is dated 22 September; the American announcement of support came on 11 October; the General Assembly vote came on 29 November 1947. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] [cite:unscop_wiki] Whatever the "five days" originally referred to, it cannot be the interval between the memo and the vote, and this book follows the dated documents.

What followed was what the warnings had described. In the months after the vote, Palestine descended into serious violence. By 24 February 1948 George Kennan, the State Department's Director of Policy Planning, was warning that only the introduction of foreign troops could now stop it, an escalation of exactly the "untold troubles" Henderson had forecast five months earlier. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman] A month later Washington itself would retreat from partition at the United Nations, a reversal told in [scene:rd5_trusteeship|the trusteeship proposal].

Its first node is the Anglo-American Committee, whose unanimous report of April 1946 recommended that Palestine be neither a Jewish nor an Arab state; Washington kept only one of its recommendations, the immediate admission of 100,000 Jewish refugees. [cite:avalon_aac] [cite:w_aac] The next three nodes are the warnings of September and October 1947: Henderson at State, the Joint Chiefs, and Forrestal at Defense. A fifth, the Central Intelligence Agency's estimate ORE-55 of 28 November 1947, came the day before the vote and is the subject of the next section. All five lines run into Truman, and all five are marked as overruled. On the other side of the scale sit Clifford and David Niles, who made the domestic political case, and Eddie Jacobson, Truman's former business partner, whose personal channel brought Chaim Weizmann to the President on 18 March 1948. [cite:forward_state_dept_truman_israel] The final node is the outcome: American support for Resolution 181 in November 1947, and recognition of Israel in May 1948.

Read as a whole, the map shows a structure rather than a plot. Every warning came through an established institution doing the work it existed to do: the regional desk assessing the region, the military assessing strategic risk, the Defense Secretary speaking for readiness, the oil company protecting its concession. The counterweight came through the White House's own political staff and through a personal friendship. The President stood where those lines met, and he had the authority to decide. Neither the warnings nor the political advice were secret. Both were on paper.

What the record does not settle. Two limits apply. First, the record cannot show that a different decision would have produced a better outcome by every measure; that counterfactual cannot be tested. Second, the surviving documents show the warnings and the decision but do not contain a written internal rationale connecting the two, so the weight Truman gave to the 1948 election, against his own stated convictions, rests on the accounts of his advisers and on historians' readings rather than on a single decisive document. What the record does settle is the question the familiar account gets wrong. This was not a blind spot or a failure of communication. The State Department's regional director, the Joint Chiefs of Staff and the Secretary of Defense reached the same conclusion independently, in writing, within a few weeks of each other, and the President heard it. The decision to back partition was taken in full knowledge of the strategic risk, by a government in which those who warned and those who prevailed both had a documented voice.
