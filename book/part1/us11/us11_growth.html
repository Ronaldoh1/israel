Growth: Two Economies, 1980–2026

The comparison of the two economies starts with a measurement choice. Figures quoted in arguments about Israel and the United States often set a 2026 projection for one country against a past year for the other, or mix exchange-rate dollars with prices adjusted for what money buys. This section uses one source for both countries throughout: the World Bank's World Development Indicators, updated on 8 October 2026, which give gross domestic product per person in constant 2015 dollars (for real growth over time) and in purchasing-power-parity dollars (for living-standard levels across countries). The most recent full year is 2025. [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_const] [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_ppp]

Output per person over time

In constant 2015 dollars, Israel's GDP per person was $18,597 in 1980 and $42,596 in 2025; the American figure was $31,082 and $67,946. Over the forty-five years Israel's output per person grew by an average of 1.86 per cent a year and America's by 1.75 per cent. Israel's level as a share of America's, by this book's arithmetic from the same series, was 59.8 per cent in 1980, 57.1 per cent in 1990, 60.5 per cent in 2000, 63.1 per cent in 2010 and 64.4 per cent in 2019. It reached 66.1 per cent in 2022 and then fell back to 62.7 per cent in 2025. [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_const] Measured at market exchange rates in current dollars the gap narrowed further, from 37 per cent of the American level in 1985 to 67 per cent in 2025; the difference from the constant-dollar series reflects prices and exchange rates rather than output. [cite:us1_fred_uspc] [cite:ir13_fred_ilpc]

The decades differ. In the 1980s, which for Israel included the inflation crisis described in the next section, its output per person grew 1.88 per cent a year against America's 2.35 per cent. In the 1990s Israel grew faster (2.76 against 2.18), and again in the 2000s (1.25 against 0.83), a decade that held Israel's downturn of 2001–03, when unemployment reached about 10 per cent, and America's financial crisis of 2008–09. [cite:us11_jvl_melnick] From 2010 to 2019 Israel still led (1.86 against 1.62). From 2019 to 2025, a span that holds the pandemic and, for Israel, the wars that began on 7 October 2023, the order reversed: 1.34 per cent a year in Israel and 1.80 in the United States. [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_const] Israel's GDP per person fell in both 2023 and 2024 and in 2025 stood 0.1 per cent above its 2022 level; American output per person rose in each of those years. [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_const] The Bank of Israel puts the gross budgetary cost of the war at about NIS 352 billion for 2023–2026, and defence spending at 8.4 per cent of GDP in 2024 and 7.8 per cent in 2025, against 4.2 per cent in 2022. [cite:gi16_boi_2025]

The level in purchasing power

Constant-dollar figures measure growth; they do not say how much a person's income buys in each country. For that the World Bank publishes GDP per person at purchasing-power parity, which adjusts for price levels. On that measure Israel stood at $15,712 against America's $23,889 in 1990 (65.8 per cent), $25,747 against $36,330 in 2000 (70.9 per cent), $29,418 against $48,643 in 2010 (60.5 per cent), and $59,717 against $90,027 in 2025 (66.3 per cent). [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_ppp] These are current international dollars, so the levels rise with prices; only the ratio is comparable across years. The PPP gap is about the same as the constant-dollar gap, at roughly two-thirds. One reason Israel's purchasing-power figure does not rise further is that its price level is high: the OECD ranked Israel fourth-highest among its members in comparable price levels in 2025, a finding Part Fourteen takes up. [cite:us14_oecd_survey2025] [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the basket]

Total growth and growth per person

Total output tells a different story because the populations grew at different speeds. Israel's population was 3.88 million in 1980, 6.29 million in 2000 and 10.12 million in 2025; America's was 227.2 million, 282.2 million and 341.8 million. [cite:us11_wb_pop] That is average growth of about 2.2 per cent a year for Israel and 0.9 per cent for the United States. Combining the two series, Israel's real GDP grew about six-fold over the period and America's about 3.3-fold, by this book's arithmetic. [cite:us11_wb_pop] [cite:us11_wb_gdppc_const] A claim that Israel's economy grew far faster than America's is therefore true of the total and not of output per person, where the two countries' long-run rates are close. Immigration law and family policy, the two drivers of that difference, are compared in Parts Six and Eighteen. [scene:us6_citizenship|Part Six: citizenship] [scene:us18_family|Part Eighteen: family policy]

Openness

The two economies also differ in how much they trade. Israel's exports and imports together equalled 80.0 per cent of its GDP in 1980, 60.9 per cent in 1990, 68.4 per cent in 2000 and 65.7 per cent in 2010, then fell to 51.1 per cent in 2020 and 53.0 per cent in 2025. The American figure was 20.1 per cent in 1980, 16.6 per cent in 1985, 25.1 per cent in 2000, 28.2 per cent in 2010 and 25.0 per cent in 2024. [cite:us11_wb_trade] A small economy trades more of what it makes, so the levels are not a verdict on either policy; the trends are the point. Israel's economy has become less dependent on trade as a share of output since 2000, while America's share has been roughly flat since then. The last section of this part sets out the trade between the two countries and the tariffs of 2025–26. [scene:us11_concentration|Concentration and trade]

What the averages do not show

GDP per person is an average. It does not say who receives the income, and on that the record for the United States is clear: the Congressional Budget Office finds that the top 1 per cent's share of household income before taxes and transfers doubled from 9 per cent in 1979 to 18 per cent in 2022, and after taxes and transfers rose from 7 to 14 per cent. [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] Wages, the cost of living and poverty, the measures closer to what households experience, are compared in Parts Thirteen, Fourteen and Eighteen, with Israel's own distributional figures. [scene:us13_wages|Part Thirteen: wages] [scene:us14_wages|Part Fourteen: wages and prices] [scene:us18_poverty|Part Eighteen: poverty] Two further limits apply. Israel's figures do not include the Palestinian economies of the West Bank and Gaza, which Part Nine treats. [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] And American aid, by Part One's arithmetic 12.7 per cent of Israel's GDP in 1985 and 0.6 per cent in 2025, weighed far more on Israel's economy at the start of this period than at the end.
