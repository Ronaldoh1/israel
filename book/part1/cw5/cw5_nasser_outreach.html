Kennedy and Nasser, 1961–1962: The Wager on Cairo

The confrontations of 1962 and 1963 between Washington and Israel, over the reactor at Dimona and over an American lobby's money, took place inside a larger American policy that had nothing to do with either. From the spring of 1961 John F. Kennedy tried to draw Gamal Abdel Nasser, president of the United Arab Republic, away from dependence on the Soviet bloc, with letters, with a visit that was weighed in Washington, and above all with American wheat. Everything else in this stage, the war in Yemen, the Arab unity talks of 1963, the American reading of Israel's fears, happened against that wager. This section tells how Washington made it and argued for it, from the documents the State Department has since published.

Six letters, 11 May 1961. On 11 May 1961 Kennedy sent letters to six Arab heads of state: Nasser; Chehab of Lebanon; Hussein of Jordan; Qassim of Iraq; Saud of Saudi Arabia; and Imam Ahmed of Yemen. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_jfk_arab_letter] The text, a State Department telegram reproduced by the Jewish Virtual Library, named the "unresolved Arab-Israel controversy" as an underlying tension in the region and conceded at once that "No easy solution presents itself." [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_jfk_arab_letter] What it offered was specific. The United States, the letter said, was "willing to help resolve the tragic Palestine refugee problem on the basis of the principle of repatriation or compensation," a position it anchored in American "support for General Assembly recommendations concerning the refugees." It promised that Washington would use its influence to make the Palestine Conciliation Commission "intensify its efforts." [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_jfk_arab_letter]

A common picture of American policy in the region runs in a straight line toward Israel, as though the relationship deepened by a kind of gravity from one administration to the next. This initiative points another way. It went to six Arab rulers, not to Israel; it spoke of repatriation as well as compensation; and it tied the United States, in writing, to the General Assembly's refugee recommendations. The letter did not say how repatriation would be carried out, or how Israel's government would be brought to accept it. It was an opening, written to be received as one.

"Anxious to get closer to this fellow." The push inside the administration came within weeks. In June 1961 the UAR's ambassador suggested to Senator Fulbright that American food aid under PL-480 be put on a multi-year basis. [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d64] On 8 June Walt Rostow told Under Secretary Ball that "the President is anxious to get closer to this fellow." Bundy forwarded a proposal for a visit by Nasser. The State Department's historians, summarizing the file in an editorial note, record the condition attached to it: the visit made sense if it could proceed without "disruptive Zionist action." [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d64] The phrase is the officials' own, as the note gives it, and it shows that the domestic politics of an approach to Cairo were weighed from the start. It does not show who in the United States was expected to object, or how; the note says no more.

Nasser's answer, 22 August 1961. Nasser replied on 22 August 1961. A State Department memorandum, prepared by Veliotes for Battle and sent to Bundy in September, judged that the reply made "the usual remarks about Israel" but did "not close the door to further initiatives with respect to the Palestine question." [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d103] That was the reading Washington wanted, and it is a measured one: it registered the disagreement and set it aside. The Egyptian side of the letter is known in this book only through excerpts published in 2018 by the news site Egyptian Streets. According to those excerpts, Nasser wrote that "the problem is – no doubt – that of Palestine"; he attacked the Balfour Declaration as a promise made by Britain over land it did not own; and he urged that "the time has come when the United States should open her eyes." [cite:rs_cw5_egyptianstreets_letters] The same article cites Mohamed Hassanein Heikal for a count of 91 letters between the two men from 1961 to 1963. [cite:rs_cw5_egyptianstreets_letters] The full texts were not reached for this book, and the count is Heikal's.

Set side by side, the two readings of the same letter are instructive. Nasser, on the excerpts, placed Palestine at the centre and called on the United States to change its view. The State Department filed those passages under "the usual remarks" and fastened on what was not said: that the door was not closed. Both readings could be true at once, and the policy that followed rested on the second.

The offer, December 1961. In about early December 1961 Rusk instructed the embassy in Cairo to tell Nasser that the United States was "prepared in principle" to work out a multi-year PL-480 arrangement, possibly worth "several hundred million dollars," tied to the UAR's development plans. [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d148] The telegram noted, in the same breath, that a multi-year request from Israel was also pending. [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d148] Aid to Cairo was thus weighed alongside Israel's own claim on the same programme; the documents fetched here do not say how the Israeli request was answered.

How the wheat worked. The mechanism was laid out for the President in April 1962. A memorandum from Hamilton, the Administrator of the Agency for International Development, transmitted on 17 April 1962, gave the size of the programmes for Egypt by fiscal year. PL-480 programmes had been $81.4 million in fiscal 1960, $102.8 million in fiscal 1961 and $180.3 million in fiscal 1962, of which wheat accounted for $76.6 million. AID expected fiscal 1963 needs of about $140 million. [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d249] The argument for these sums was strategic. The aid freed Egypt's foreign exchange for other uses, and AID argued that, with it, the UAR would "become less dependent upon assistance from the Soviet Bloc." [cite:rs_cw5_frus17_d249]

The figures show a programme that more than doubled in two fiscal years, before any multi-year agreement had been signed. Who held the power in this chain is plain from the documents: the President decided, the State Department and AID proposed and carried out, and the UAR asked, first through its ambassador to a senator. What the documents fetched here do not show is the rest of the ledger: who in Egypt or the United States gained or bore the cost, or whether the foreign exchange saved was in fact spent away from Soviet suppliers. AID's rationale is an argument made to win approval, not a measured result.

Komer's case, October 1962. By the autumn of 1962 the region had shifted under the policy. In late September an army-led rebellion in Yemen overthrew the new imam and proclaimed a republic, which the UAR recognized at once; that war is the subject of [scene:cw5_yemen|the next section]. The multi-year agreement was ready for signature at the moment Egypt was committing itself in Yemen. Robert W. Komer urged Kennedy in early October 1962 to sign it anyway. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70] His memorandum set out his reasons. The agreement had been promised in May. The UAR had cooperated over "the Hawk offer," the American offer of Hawk missiles to Israel, which, Komer wrote, had "jolted UAR"; how that offer was decided belongs to [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision] in the previous stage. The UAR had also cooperated over the Johnson Plan, which Komer names but which the documents fetched for this book do not describe. And the Nasser policy as a whole, Komer argued, mattered more than the Yemen policy. He was candid about the cost: "nothing we do will be right." [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70]

Komer's memorandum is the clearest single statement in these papers of how Washington understood its own position. In the same months it was offering missiles to Israel and food to Egypt, and it expected each to resent what the other received. The Hawk offer had been made despite its effect in Cairo; the wheat agreement would be signed despite Egypt's role in Yemen. The policy did not choose between the two countries. It tried to keep both within reach.

Signed quietly, 8 October 1962. Kennedy approved on 5 October 1962, asking for "every effort to insure minimum publicity." [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70] On 8 October 1962 Kaissouni, for the UAR, and Talbot, for the United States, signed the agreement. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d70] The request for minimum publicity is documented; its reason is not stated in the record fetched here, and this book does not supply one.

The correspondence continued. The two men kept writing through the crises that followed. Kennedy's last letter to Nasser, in August 1963, as excerpted by Egyptian Streets, hoped that "mutual understanding will keep those differences within intransgressible bounds." [cite:rs_cw5_egyptianstreets_letters] The sentence is a fair summary of the policy: it did not expect agreement, only limits.

What the record leaves open. Several things are not settled by the documents reached for this book. The total value of the agreement signed on 8 October 1962 is not given in them; Rusk's "several hundred million dollars" was a possibility named ten months earlier, not the signed figure, and AID's projection of about $140 million was for a single year. The Nasser visit proposed in June 1961 is documented as a proposal, and the sources do not show it taking place. Nasser's letters are known here only in excerpt, through a news site, and Heikal's count of 91 letters has not been checked against an archive. The Johnson Plan that Komer cites is named but not described. And whether the wheat did what AID promised, loosening Cairo's dependence on the Soviet bloc, is a question the April 1962 memorandum could only predict. What the documents do settle is the direction of American policy across these months: toward Cairo, by the President's deliberate choice, argued in the language of the Cold War, and carried forward even as Egypt went to war in Yemen.
