The Gaza Raid and Its Aftermath, February–September 1955

On the night of 28 February 1955 Israeli forces crossed the armistice line into the Gaza Strip, then under Egyptian administration, and attacked an Egyptian army camp. Israel's Defense Ministry archive records the action as Operation Black Arrow. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_black_arrow] It set off three chains of consequence that this section follows in turn: a censure at the United Nations, a political uprising in Gaza against the Egyptian authorities themselves, and the creation by Egypt of Palestinian fida'i units that carried attacks into Israel. A fourth consequence, whether the raid drove Egypt to buy Soviet-bloc arms, is the subject of a dispute that the documents allow to be stated precisely but not closed.

Israel's case for the raid. The Defense Ministry archive places the operation at the end of a sequence. It followed a series of border incidents, the archive says, which ended with the infiltration of an Egyptian intelligence squad into Israel and the murder of an Israeli citizen. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_black_arrow] On this account the raid was an answer to attacks organized from Egyptian-held territory, aimed at the army that held it. The raid came one week after David Ben-Gurion returned to the government as defence minister, on 21 February 1955; [cite:rs_cw2_mod_lavon] the internal Israeli argument over this policy of reprisal is told in [scene:cw2_sharett_ben_gurion|the section on Sharett and Ben-Gurion].

The night of 28 February. Egypt's account went to the Security Council the next day. In a letter of 1 March 1955, Egypt's representative described a force of about two platoons that advanced some 3 kilometres into the Strip, blew up an army camp and a water-pump house, and ambushed a relief platoon of 35 men sent to help. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3365] The sources do not agree on the toll, and each count should be read with its source.

Israel's Defense Ministry archive records thirty-eight Egyptian soldiers and eight Israelis killed. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_black_arrow] Egypt's letter to the Council gave 37 members of the armed forces and 2 civilians killed, and 30 soldiers and 2 civilians injured, 32 in all. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3365] The historian Avi Shlaim gives 38 Egyptian soldiers killed and 31 wounded. [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_gaza] PalQuest, the Institute for Palestine Studies' reference work, gives 38 dead and 33 injured. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza] The sources converge on thirty-eight or thirty-nine Egyptian dead. Only Egypt's letter records civilians among them, and only the Israeli archive records the eight Israeli dead.

The armistice machinery and the Council. The Egyptian–Israeli Mixed Armistice Commission, the joint body that supervised the 1949 agreement, ruled on 6 March 1955. It found that a "planned attack ordered by Israel authorities" had been "committed by Israel regular army forces against the Egyptian regular army force." [cite:rs_cw2_un_res106] The finding mattered for its precision. It established that this was not an act by irregulars or settlers, but a state action by a regular army against another regular army.

The Security Council acted on 29 March 1955. Resolution 106, adopted unanimously at its 695th meeting, condemned the attack as a violation of the cease-fire and as "inconsistent with the obligations of the parties." [cite:rs_cw2_un_res106] No member voted against it or abstained. Its weight lay in the record it made: an armistice body and the Council itself had placed responsibility on the Israeli government.

The two readings of the raid can each be stated at full strength. Israel's case is that a state whose citizens were being killed by squads sent from across the line was entitled to strike the force that controlled that territory, and that Egypt, which administered Gaza, bore responsibility for what was launched from it. [cite:rs_cw2_mod_black_arrow] Egypt's case, which matches the Commission's finding and the Council's resolution, is that a planned attack by a regular army across an armistice line was a breach of the cease-fire, and that the toll fell on soldiers in their camp and on a relief platoon caught in an ambush. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3365] [cite:rs_cw2_un_res106] The resolution, as quoted in the sources here, does not address the incidents Israel cited; the archive does not address the Commission's finding.

Gaza's own response. One political consequence of the raid came not in Cairo but in Gaza, and it was directed against Egypt. On 1 March 1955, the day after the attack, a mass demonstration filled the streets of Gaza. According to PalQuest, the crowds chanted against relocation and against "US agents." Egyptian police opened fire and killed a textile worker, Hosni Belal. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza]

The protesters had a programme. They demanded the cancellation of the Sinai resettlement plan, a scheme agreed by Egypt in 1953 to settle 12,000 refugee families in Sinai; arms and military training for the residents of the camps; and civil freedoms. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza] The demands joined two grievances: refugees who refused permanent settlement away from Palestine, and a population that wanted the means to defend itself.

The organization behind the protests was itself unusual. PalQuest records that they were organized jointly by communists and the Muslim Brotherhood, through a Supreme National Committee. Mu'in Bseiso of the Palestinian Communist Party and Fathi al-Balaawi of the Brotherhood negotiated with Egyptian officials. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza] Gaza's military governor promised to end the relocation scheme. Then, on the night of 8–9 March 1955, he ordered mass arrests, and the detainees were held until July 1957. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza] The episode shows Palestinians in Gaza acting as a political force of their own, with leaders, organizations and stated aims, pressing an Arab government that administered them and paying for it in a death and more than two years of detention.

Late summer: the escalation of August 1955. Within six months the Gaza line had broken into open fighting. General Burns reported to the Security Council at its 700th meeting on 8 September 1955. On his account, Israeli forces occupied an Egyptian post on 22 August, killing an Egyptian officer and two soldiers. Organized attacks by marauders inside Israel followed, killing eleven military and civilian personnel and injuring nine. [cite:rs_cw2_io_1955]

Israel put its own record before the Council. Its letter of 31 August 1955 listed attacks of 30–31 August that it attributed to Egypt, among them the killing of four civilians in a vehicle south of Kfar Menahem, a woman gravely wounded by grenades at Nahala, and three soldiers wounded. [cite:rs_cw2_un_s3427]

On the night of 31 August–1 September 1955 Israeli forces attacked Khan Yunis, in the south of the Strip. Two counts survive in the Council's annual report to the General Assembly. The UNTSO Chief of Staff's account gives "the killing of thirty-six Egyptians, and the wounding of thirteen." Egypt's letter of 6 September 1955 gives "ten Egyptian soldiers and twenty-five refugees" killed, 35 in all, and nineteen injured. [cite:rs_cw2_un_a3157] The difference is not only in the total. The UN observer's figure does not distinguish soldiers from civilians; Egypt's figure says that most of the dead were refugees.

The fedayeen. One of the protesters' demands, arms for Gaza's Palestinians, was answered by Egypt in its own way and on its own terms. The following month, in September 1955, Egypt formed Palestinian fida'i units, known as Battalion 141, under Lieutenant Colonel Mustafa Hafez, based in Gaza. Their operations ran until Israeli intelligence assassinated Hafez in July 1956. [cite:rs_cw2_palquest_gaza] How the Egyptian government presented these units in public, and how they fit the longer history of crossings over the line since 1949, is told in Volume II's account of [scene:rd6_return_barred|the infiltration years].

The dispute: did the raid push Egypt to Moscow? The most consequential claim about the Gaza raid concerns its effect on Egypt's foreign policy, and here the evidence divides.

The case that it did is made most fully by Avi Shlaim. He writes that the raid "seriously destabilised" Nasser's regime, that Nasser sought arms in its wake, and that the search ended in the Czech arms deal. His conclusion: "by pressing its military advantage too far, Israel inadvertently helped to push Egypt into the arms of Moscow." [cite:rs_cw2_shlaim_gaza] An American official at the time read the raid in a similar way. On 11 October 1955 George Allen, the Assistant Secretary of State, met Israel's ambassador Abba Eban; both treated the raid as a turning point. Allen said that Nasser had been "deeply impressed" by reports of Israeli superiority. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_allen]

The case that it did not, or not by itself, rests on two pieces of evidence. The first is Eban's own argument at the same meeting: it was the Turkish–Iraqi pact, not the raid, that changed Nasser's attitude. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_eban_allen] The second is a Soviet document. A Soviet record of a conversation between Nasser and the Soviet ambassador, Solod, shows Nasser formally asking about Soviet arms on 15 June 1954, eight months before the raid. [cite:rs_cw2_solod_gaza]

The two cases are less opposed than they first appear. The Soviet record shows that Nasser was already exploring Soviet arms before the raid; it does not show that he would have bought them without it. Shlaim's argument concerns what turned exploration into a deal; Eban's points to a different cause for the same turn. The participants in October 1955 agreed that something changed in 1955, and disagreed about what. The October 1955 readings came from officials of interested governments, not from detached observers, and should be weighed as such. The arms deal itself, and Washington's handling of Israel's request for arms in response, are told in [scene:cw2_czech_arms|the section on the arms race].

What the record does not settle. The toll of the raid of 28 February varies by source between thirty-eight and thirty-nine Egyptian dead, with Israel's eight dead recorded only in its own archive and the number of wounded given as 31, 32 or 33. The Khan Yunis toll stands at thirty-six by the UN's count and thirty-five by Egypt's, with the two sources disagreeing on how many of the dead were civilians. A higher figure of 72 dead circulates in secondary works and has not been verified; it is not used here. The identity and the full record of the Egyptian squad whose infiltration preceded the raid are not given in the sources. Nor do the sources give the casualties caused by the fida'i units between September 1955 and July 1956, or establish who in Egypt decided to form them and why. Whether the raid caused the Czech deal remains, on this evidence, a question of weight rather than of fact: the documents establish the sequence, and historians and participants still disagree on its cause.
