The Cunning Folk

In every part of Europe there were people who, for a fee or a gift, healed sick people and animals, found stolen goods and missing persons, told fortunes, provided love charms and, above all, undid the harm that witches were believed to cause. In England they were called cunning folk, and they worked from the medieval period to the early twentieth century. Poorer people often went to them because they could not afford apothecaries or physicians. [cite:iq_wiki_cunningbritain] The historian Owen Davies has suggested that "as many as several thousand" may have practised in England at any one time until the mid-nineteenth century. [cite:iq_wiki_cunningbritain]

Churches of every confession condemned them. Some theologians and church authorities, Roman Catholic, Anglican and Protestant, saw cunning folk as in league with the Devil, and early Quakers were especially vocal against them. [cite:iq_wiki_cunningbritain] The Council of Trent's Index rules banned the books of their arts, and the Catholic Catechism today says that "all forms of divination are to be rejected" and calls magic "gravely contrary to the virtue of religion" even when used to restore health. [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] Most ordinary people, however, drew a sharp line between the cunning folk, whom they found useful, and witches, whom they feared. [cite:iq_wiki_cunningbritain]

The popular story says the witch trials were a war on such healers. The record does not support it. In Essex, about 400 people were tried for witchcraft, and only four of them can be identified as cunning folk. [cite:iq_wiki_cunningbritain] Studies of midwives reach the same conclusion: they were not prominent among the accused. [cite:vt30_harley_midwife] The cunning folk were condemned from pulpits and fined by courts, but they were more often the people villagers went to for protection against witches than the people villagers accused.

The strangest case comes from Friuli, in north-eastern Italy, and from the Roman Inquisition's own records. There, peasants called benandanti, "good walkers", said they had been born with the caul and that on the Ember Days their spirits left their bodies at night to fight witches who threatened the crops, armed with stalks of fennel against the witches' stalks of sorghum. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles] In 1575 a priest questioned two of them, Paolo Gasparutto and Battista Moduco; in 1580 the inquisitor Felice da Montefalco reopened the case, and both men eventually admitted that they had been deceived by the Devil. They were sentenced in 1581 to six months in prison, a sentence later remitted. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles]

Over the following decades, as Carlo Ginzburg showed in The Night Battles (1966), the Inquisition came to regard the benandanti as witches themselves, and their testimony was reshaped toward the diabolical sabbath the inquisitors expected. Few cases reached a conclusion; Ginzburg records that none against a benandante was concluded between 1575 and 1619. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles] His wider thesis, that the benandanti were the remnant of a pre-Christian fertility cult, is disputed: Norman Cohn found "nothing whatsoever" in the sources to support it, and Ronald Hutton judged its foundations imperfect. [cite:iq_wiki_nightbattles] The trial records themselves are not in dispute. They show a tribunal hearing a village belief it did not understand and translating it into the only category it had.
