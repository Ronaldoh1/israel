The United States: Separation, Exemptions and Public Money

The American arrangement starts from a text rather than an inherited system of religious courts. The First Amendment, ratified in December 1791, says that "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof". [cite:us7_wiki_estab] Marriage in the United States is a civil act licensed by the state; clergy may officiate, but no religious body decides who may marry. Since Obergefell v. Hodges, decided 5–4 on 26 June 2015, every state must license and recognise same-sex marriages, and the Respect for Marriage Act, signed on 13 December 2022, requires every state and the federal government to recognise same-sex and interracial marriages validly performed elsewhere. The Supreme Court later declined, without comment, a petition from the former Kentucky clerk Kim Davis asking it to overturn Obergefell. [cite:us7_wiki_obergefell] The live American questions are therefore not who may marry but two others: how far religious believers may be exempted from general laws, and how much public money may reach religious institutions. On the first, Congress and the Court have pulled in different directions since 1990; on the second, the Court has moved from permitting public money for religious schools to requiring that they not be excluded from it.

From the wall to history and tradition

The Establishment Clause was applied to the states in Everson v. Board of Education (1947), in which Justice Hugo Black invoked Thomas Jefferson's "wall of separation between church and State" while upholding New Jersey's payment of bus fares for pupils at parochial schools. In Lemon v. Kurtzman (1971) the Court struck down Pennsylvania laws paying for services and part of teachers' salaries at religious schools, and set a three-part test (secular purpose, primary effect, no excessive entanglement) that governed for decades. [cite:us7_wiki_estab] On 27 June 2022, in Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, the Court ruled 6–3 that a public-school football coach's personal prayer on the field was protected, and replaced the Lemon test with an approach based on history and tradition; in 2023 it described Lemon as "now abrogated". [cite:us7_wiki_kennedy]

Exemptions from general laws

The exemption question turned on a 1990 case. In Employment Division v. Smith the Court held that a neutral law that applies to everyone need not meet strict scrutiny merely because it burdens someone's religion. Congress responded with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), which requires the government to show a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means whenever a law substantially burdens religious exercise. [cite:us7_wiki_hobbylobby] On 30 June 2014, in Burwell v. Hobby Lobby, the Court ruled 5–4 that RFRA protected closely held for-profit companies whose owners objected on religious grounds to the federal requirement that employee health plans cover contraception. [cite:us7_wiki_hobbylobby] The comparison with Israel's draft exemption is limited but real: in both countries a religious claim can lift an obligation that applies to everyone else. The difference lies in who decides. In the United States the courts weigh each claim against the government's interest case by case; in Israel the exemption from military service was a political arrangement that the courts have repeatedly found unlawful and that the government has enforced only in small part since the 2024 ruling. [cite:us7_globes_draft2024] [scene:us7_haredi|The Haredi exemption]

Public money for religious schools

The funding line has moved furthest. Zelman v. Simmons-Harris (2002) upheld vouchers that parents could spend at religious schools. [cite:us7_wiki_estab] The Court then turned from permitting such funding to requiring equal access to it: in Trinity Lutheran (2017) a state could not exclude a church school from a playground-resurfacing grant, in Espinoza (2020) a state scholarship programme could not exclude religious schools, and in Carson v. Makin, decided 6–3 on 21 June 2022, Maine could not limit its tuition payments to nonsectarian schools. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that Maine's rule "promotes stricter separation of church and state than the Federal Constitution requires". [cite:us7_wiki_carson] The line held once: on 22 May 2025 the Court split 4–4, with Justice Amy Coney Barrett recused, leaving in place an Oklahoma Supreme Court ruling that barred public money for St. Isidore, which would have been the first religious charter school; an even split sets no national precedent. [cite:us7_wiki_stisidore]

States and Congress have widened the channels. EdChoice, an organisation that promotes school choice, counts about 10,000 students in private school choice programmes in 1996, about 540,545 in 2019, more than a million in 2024 and more than 1.5 million in 75 programmes across 34 states, the District of Columbia and Puerto Rico by December 2025; Arizona made its education savings accounts open to all students in 2022. [cite:us7_edchoice_1m] [cite:us7_edchoice_abcs2026] These counts include secular private schools, and no national series separates religious from secular recipients. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on 4 July 2025, created the first federal programme of this kind, a tax credit for donations to scholarship organisations that takes effect on 1 January 2027; by 28 July 2026, 31 states had opted in or announced they would, and about 51.7 million of 56.4 million schoolchildren were expected to be eligible. [cite:us7_k12dive_taxcredit]

Churches also benefit from tax exemption, with a condition. Since 1954 the Johnson Amendment in section 501(c)(3) of the tax code has barred churches and other tax-exempt charities from endorsing or opposing candidates. [cite:us7_kpmg_johnson] On 7 July 2025, in a case brought by the National Religious Broadcasters in the Eastern District of Texas, the Internal Revenue Service joined the plaintiffs in asking the court to enter a consent judgment stating that a church's good-faith speech to its congregation on electoral matters during religious services is not campaign intervention. The settlement applies to the plaintiff churches and does not remove the Johnson Amendment from law; it required the judge's approval. [cite:us7_kpmg_johnson] [cite:us7_yahoo_johnson]

The two systems side by side

The religious make-up the two systems govern has changed too. The Pew Research Center's Religious Landscape Study found 78 per cent of American adults identifying as Christian in 2007, 71 per cent in 2014 and 62 per cent in 2023–24, while those with no religious affiliation rose from 16 to 23 to 29 per cent. [cite:us7_pew_rls2025] On Pew's Government Restrictions Index the United States scored 1.6 in 2007 and 2.7 in 2022, against Israel's 3.9 and 6.7. Both rose; Israel's started higher and rose further. [cite:us7_pew_gri2022]

The American gaps are of a different kind from Israel's. No American is barred from marrying by a religious court, but the line between public money and religious schooling that the Court drew in 1947 and 1971 has been redrawn, by the Court and by state legislatures, toward funding; and RFRA lets a religious objection lift a general legal duty, as it did for employers in the contraception case.
