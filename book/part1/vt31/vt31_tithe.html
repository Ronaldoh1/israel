The Tithe: From Gift to Law to Tax

The thesis Book Two tests holds that institutional religion built a taxed system on top of teachings that asked for nothing. On money, the first half of that claim can be checked date by date, because the change from gift to obligation left a paper trail of council canons, royal capitularies, papal claims and modern statutes. Part Twenty showed that the earliest communities gave voluntarily and kept no tithe system ([scene:vt20_first_communities|The First Communities]). This section picks up where that record ends: how a tenth of the harvest became a church law in sixth-century Gaul, a state-enforced tax under Charlemagne, a revenue that kings and popes fought over for a thousand years, and, in Germany today, a surcharge on income tax whose non-payment carries church penalties.

The tithe before the church

The tenth was not a Christian invention. In the Hebrew Bible the tithe of the land's produce went to the Levites, the tribe set apart for the Temple service, who passed a share to the priests; a second tithe was spent on a sacred feast, and every third year a tithe went to the poor. [cite:vt31_ce_tithes] It belonged to a temple economy in which one tribe held no land and lived from the others' harvests. The first Christian writers knew this system and did not adopt it. Irenaeus, around 180, contrasted the Jewish tithe consecrated under the Law with Christians who, being free, were to set aside all they had for the Lord's purposes, and Justin in Rome described a Sunday collection in which the willing "give what each thinks fit". [cite:vt20_na_irenaeus_iv18] [cite:vt20_justin_67] The Catholic Church's own 1912 encyclopedia states that ministers were at first supported by "the spontaneous offerings of the faithful". [cite:vt31_ce_tithes] Modern scholarship agrees: Eric Shuler, writing in the journal Traditio in 2012, sums up five decades of research with the finding that "mandatory tithing to the church was not a custom of early Christianity". [cite:vt31_shuler2012]

Sixth-century Gaul: the tithe becomes a duty

The first clear theory of the Christian tithe as an obligation comes from Caesarius, bishop of Arles (c.469–542), who taught that anyone earning income from farming or trade owed a tenth, and tied the payment to almsgiving. [cite:vt31_shuler2012] The first church legislation followed within decades. A letter of the bishops gathered at Tours in 567 urged the faithful to pay tithes, and the canons of the Council of Mâcon in 585 made payment a church law. [cite:vt31_ce_tithes] Mâcon attached the institution's strongest spiritual sanction: those who refused to pay were to be excommunicated. [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] At this stage the obligation was enforced by the church alone. Its weight depended on whether a believer feared exclusion from the sacraments.

Charlemagne: the state collects

In March 779 Charlemagne's assembly at Herstal, meeting during a famine and a run of military and political crises, issued a capitulary that made the payment of tithes obligatory throughout the Frankish kingdom, with part reserved for relief of the poor. [cite:vt31_wiki_herstal] Shuler calls this "secular enforcement in 779" the decisive moment in the tithe's history. [cite:vt31_shuler2012] Under Charlemagne the tithe was divided into four parts: the upkeep of church buildings, the poor, the bishop and the parish clergy. [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] From then on a tenth of the harvest was owed to the church by law, and royal officials could compel it. The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the earliest civil enforcement of the church tithe to these capitularies. [cite:vt31_ce_tithes]

The tithe spread with Latin Christendom. English law recognised it under Athelstan, Edgar and Canute before the Norman Conquest. [cite:vt31_ce_tithes] By the high Middle Ages the revenue was large enough to be fought over. Lords and princes acquired tithe rights, sometimes as hereditary property, and the Third Lateran Council of 1179 ruled that no tithe could pass to a layman without the pope's consent. In the late 1180s, under Pope Gregory VIII, a "Saladin tithe" was levied on those who did not themselves go on crusade. [cite:vt31_ce_tithes] The Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 ordered that tithes be paid before any other tax or expense, and allowed church censure to compel payment. [cite:vt31_lateran4]

The other streams: indulgences, annates, Peter's Pence

The tithe supported local churches and bishops. The papacy built separate revenues of its own. Annates were payments owed by a cleric on receiving a benefice, eventually set at half or all of its first year's income. Clement V claimed the first-year revenues of all vacant benefices in England in 1305, and John XXII those of all Christendom falling vacant over two years in 1319; bishops and abbots paid a parallel fee, the servitia communia, on appointment. The Council of Basel tried to abolish these payments in the 1430s and failed. [cite:vt31_wiki_annates] Peter's Pence began in Anglo-Saxon England as a payment to Rome; Canute's letter of 1031 set it at a penny per hearth, with poorer households exempt. [cite:vt31_wiki_peterspence]

Indulgences, remissions of the punishment due for sin, began as rewards for crusading and became, by the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the object of constant complaints about their "sale" by pardoners; secular governments often took as much as two-thirds of the yield. [cite:vt31_brit_indulgence] The case that broke the system shows how the streams were joined. Albrecht of Brandenburg, already archbishop of Magdeburg, became archbishop of Mainz in 1514 in breach of the ban on holding several sees, and borrowed 20,000 guilders from the banker Jacob Fugger to pay the confirmation fee owed to the Roman Curia. He then proposed an indulgence whose proceeds would go half to the new St Peter's Basilica in Rome and half to his own treasury; Leo X's bull was issued on 31 March 1515. [cite:vt31_wiki_albrecht] In January 1517 Albrecht made the Dominican Johann Tetzel his indulgence commissioner. The Catholic historian Ludwig von Pastor judged that Tetzel's preaching on indulgences for the dead went beyond official teaching. [cite:vt31_wiki_tetzel] On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther sent his Ninety-five Theses to Albrecht. [cite:vt31_wiki_albrecht]

The institution reformed these practices, under pressure. The Council of Trent condemned "all base gain" from indulgences in 1563, and Pius V abolished their sale in 1567. [cite:vt31_brit_indulgence] Kings did the rest. Henry VIII stopped annates to Rome around 1532 and turned them over to the Crown in 1534, and an act of the same year ended Peter's Pence in England. [cite:vt31_wiki_annates] [cite:vt31_wiki_peterspence] In France the decrees of August 1789 abolished "any kind of tithes", on condition that another way be found to pay for worship. The historian D. M. G. Sutherland argues that peasants ended up paying twice, because landlords raised rents by the amount of the old tithe. [cite:vt31_wiki_august] The servitia paid to Rome ended by the same decree. [cite:vt31_wiki_annates] England commuted its tithes into rentcharges in 1836. The Tithe Act of 1936 turned these into annuities paid to the state, and the last payments ended in 1977. [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe]

What the institution asks today

Modern Catholic law contains no tenth. Canon 222 of the 1983 Code says the faithful "are obliged to assist with the needs of the Church" so that it has what it needs for worship, the apostolate, charity and "the decent support of ministers". [cite:vt31_cic222] Canon 1260 claims for the Church "an innate right to require" what it needs, while canon 1261 says the faithful "are free to give"; a bishop may impose a moderate tax on parishes and other church bodies, and an extraordinary levy on individuals only in grave necessity. [cite:vt31_cic1260] The Catechism lists the duty as part of the Church's precepts: the faithful must provide "for the material needs of the Church, each according to his abilities". [cite:vt31_ccc2043] The obligation remains, but the rate is left to the giver, much as Paul and the Didache left it.

How the obligation is met depends on each state's settlement with the Church. Peter's Pence, revived by Pius IX's encyclical Saepe venerabilis of 5 August 1871, is a voluntary collection taken around 29 June. [cite:vt31_wiki_peterspence] Its figures and uses are in [scene:vt9_peters_pence|Part Nine]. Italy's otto per mille lets taxpayers direct 0.8 per cent of income tax to a church or to the state, with undeclared shares divided in proportion to declared choices ([scene:vt3_otto_per_mille|Part Three]). [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] Germany's Kirchensteuer, rooted in the settlement of 1803 and grounded today in Article 140 of the Basic Law, which takes over Article 137 of the Weimar Constitution, adds roughly 8 to 9 per cent to the income tax of registered members. [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] German Catholic dioceses received €6.751 billion from it in 2025, up from €6.628 billion in 2024, even as about 307,000 people formally left the Church that year. [cite:vt31_pillar_tax2025] [cite:vt31_cwr_de2025] How that tax connects to church discipline, so that leaving the tax roll means losing the sacraments, belongs with the law of obligation in [scene:vt31_sunday_and_law|the next section]. For the overall scale of Church wealth, see [scene:vt9_true_vs_public_wealth|True Wealth and Public Wealth].

The money record therefore supports the thesis on one point and qualifies it on two. The change from voluntary gift to compulsory tithe is documented. It took about five centuries, began in sixth-century Gaul, and reached full force only when a king enforced it. The qualifications are these. The compulsion was often driven by kings as much as by popes, who quarrelled over the proceeds. And the institution itself later abolished the sale of indulgences and dropped the fixed tenth, leaving in law today a duty to support the Church with no rate attached.
