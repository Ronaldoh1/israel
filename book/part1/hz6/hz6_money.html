Who Pays for Hezbollah

The plainest statement of where Hezbollah's money comes from was made by Hezbollah. In a televised speech on 24 June 2016, marking forty days since the death of its commander Mustafa Badreddine, Hassan Nasrallah said the group was open about the fact that its budget, income and expenses, "everything it eats and drinks, its weapons and rockets, come from the Islamic Republic of Iran." He added: "As long as Iran has money, we have money." [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] He was answering a US law, the Hezbollah International Financing Prevention Act, signed by President Obama on 18 December 2015, which threatened sanctions on banks that knowingly handled significant transactions for the group. Nasrallah's reply was that the law could not touch Hezbollah because "we don't transfer our money through the Lebanese banking system" and kept no money in Lebanese banks. [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016] Four years earlier, in February 2012, he had acknowledged Iran's "moral, and political and material support in all possible forms" since 1982, while insisting that Hezbollah took support, not orders, from Tehran. [cite:hz6_wilson_record]

How much money that is has never been published by Hezbollah or Iran, so every figure in circulation is an outside estimate, almost all of them from the US government. In about 2010 US officials put Iran's support at $100 million to $200 million a year. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] [cite:hz6_wiki_hziran] In June 2018 Sigal Mandelker, the Treasury's under secretary for terrorism and financial intelligence, told a meeting of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies that "Iran provides upwards of $700 million a year to Hezbollah", far above the earlier figure of about $200 million. [cite:hz6_jp_mandelker] The State Department's Country Reports on Terrorism for 2018 repeated "an estimated $700 million per year" and said this was "the overwhelming majority of the group's annual budget". [cite:hz6_icg_crt2018] On 13 November 2018 Nathan Sales, the State Department's counterterrorism coordinator, put Iran's spending on armed allies across the region at close to $1 billion a year, about $700 million of it for Hezbollah and $100 million for Palestinian groups. Iranian officials answered that US sanctions would not stop Iran supporting its allies in Syria, Lebanon, Palestine and Iraq. [cite:hz6_rferl_sales]

The jump from about $200 million to $700 million should be read with care. The higher figure was announced in 2018, the year the United States left the nuclear agreement with Iran and began its "maximum pressure" campaign of sanctions, by officials of the administration running that campaign, and none of the statements cited here gives the method behind it. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] That timing does not show the figure is wrong; it is a reason to treat it as a government estimate rather than an audited number. What is not in doubt, because Hezbollah's own leader said it, is that Iran is the source of most or all of its budget and its weapons. The dispute is over the size of the sum, not its origin.

Sanctions and the crime accusation

Beyond the 2015 banking law, the US kept widening its financial measures. In October 2018 President Trump signed a law widening the range of people who could be sanctioned for dealing with Hezbollah, at an event marking the 35th anniversary of the 1983 Marine barracks bombing (see [scene:hz2_1983|the 1983 bombings]), and on 15 October Attorney General Jeff Sessions named Hezbollah one of five top transnational organised crime threats to the United States. [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] That designation reflects a long-running US accusation that Hezbollah raises money through money laundering and the drug trade as well as from Iran. Nasrallah rejected the accusation in February 2012, saying Iran met the group's needs. [cite:hz6_wilson_record] Rebeiz, the Dickinson College scholar, describes the financial network that grew around Hezbollah as involving money laundering and drug trafficking. [cite:hz6_conv_rebeiz] The accusation is the US government's; Hezbollah denies it, and the individual cases behind it are not adjudicated here.

The pressure campaign on Iran did reach Hezbollah's books. According to the AP in October 2018, the group was cutting costs while continuing to pay stipends to the families of its dead, medical care for its wounded and retirement payments, and could still draw on tens of thousands of donation boxes placed in Shia neighbourhoods of Lebanon. Nasrallah admitted that the sanctions "will have an effect for sure", and Kataib Hezbollah, an Iraqi militia, offered to help it financially if needed (see [scene:hz10_iraq|the Iraqi militias]). [cite:hz6_ap_sanctions2018] NOW reported in 2022 that the sanctions had sharply cut the money reaching Hezbollah and forced austerity. [cite:hz6_now_welfare]

Its own bank, and the state's money

Nasrallah's statement that Hezbollah keeps its money outside Lebanese banks points to the parallel system it built instead. Al-Qard al-Hassan, a lending association, is described by the researcher Yara Asmar as Hezbollah's own alternative to the Lebanese banking system. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] [cite:hz6_alarabiya_2016]

The state's money also reaches Hezbollah's institutions, legally. After the August 2020 port explosion the health ministry, then run by Hezbollah according to NOW, added fifteen Hezbollah-sponsored medical centres to the list of providers the state pays, and state payments to Hezbollah-run hospitals nearly doubled, from about 16 billion to 30 billion Lebanese pounds. In 2020 the US refused to send aid to the health ministry while Hezbollah held it. [cite:hz6_now_welfare] The two streams, Iran's money for weapons and fighters and the Lebanese state's money for services, show how Hezbollah sits both outside and inside the state at once (see [scene:hz6_state|A Party With an Army]).
