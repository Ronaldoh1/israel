Rome and the Index

On 21 July 1542 Pope Paul III's bull Licet ab initio set up a commission of six cardinals as the Sacred Roman and Universal Inquisition. The Holy See's own history of the office says it was "initially a tribunal exclusively for cases of heresy and schism". [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] The historian Andrea Del Col estimates about 1,250 death sentences among the 51,000 to 75,000 cases it judged in Italy. [cite:vt34_wiki_rominq] Unlike the Spanish and Portuguese courts, it was the pope's own, and it survives: renamed the Holy Office in 1908, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith in 1965, and since 2022 the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] [cite:vt34_wiki_rominq]

Its most famous victim was Giordano Bruno, a former Dominican who taught that the universe is infinite and the stars are suns with worlds of their own. Arrested in Venice in 1592, he was held in Rome for about seven years. The charges recorded against him were mostly theological: denying the Trinity, the divinity of Christ and transubstantiation, and teaching the transmigration of souls and many worlds. He refused to retract. On 17 February 1600 he was burned alive in the Campo de' Fiori, his tongue held in a gag. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt29_linder_bruno] In 1633 the same tribunal found Galileo Galilei "vehemently suspect of heresy" for defending the sun-centred system, required him to abjure and confined him for the rest of his life. [cite:vt29_wiki_galileo] The full account of both is in [scene:vt29_bruno_galileo|The Vatican Architecture: Bruno and Galileo].

Its most revealing victim was not famous at all. Domenico Scandella, called Menocchio, was a miller of Montereale in the Venetian territories, born in 1532, who had read books on religion and history and drawn his own conclusions. He was tried for heresy in 1583, abjured in 1584 and spent about twenty months in prison. He kept talking. Arrested again in 1598 as a relapsed heretic, he was declared a heresiarch in 1599 and burned. [cite:iq_wiki_menocchio] His trial records survived, and the historian Carlo Ginzburg built from them The Cheese and the Worms (1976), a reconstruction of a sixteenth-century miller's view of the cosmos in his own words. [cite:iq_wiki_menocchio]

The tribunal's long reach was the Index of Forbidden Books. Paul IV issued the first Roman Index in 1559; the Council of Trent's Index of 1564 set ten rules that governed every later edition, and extended the ban from named titles to whole classes of writing, with excommunication for owning them. [cite:vt14_nwe_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index] Rule IX "absolutely repudiated" books of divination, palm reading, necromancy, sorcery and the magic arts, while allowing astronomy written for navigation, farming and medicine. [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] Bruno's works were listed in 1603; the last regular edition, of 1948, held about 4,000 titles. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] [cite:vt14_brit_index] A notification of 14 June 1966 ended its force as church law while stating that it kept its "moral value". [cite:vt14_nwe_index]

The Holy See's later statements repent of the method in general terms. In March 2000 Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger confessed that "even men of the church, in the name of faith and morals, have sometimes used methods not in keeping with the Gospel"; in 2004 John Paul II repeated the Church's repentance for "intolerance and even the use of violence" in the service of truth. [cite:vt28_america_2000] [cite:vt28_va_jp2_2004] Bruno's condemnation has not been withdrawn. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno]
