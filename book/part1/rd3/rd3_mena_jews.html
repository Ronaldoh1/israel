The Jews of the Arab World, 1929–1936: Baghdad and Constantine

The story of these years in Palestine was not the only story of Jews in the Arab world. Two places show how differently the early 1930s could run for Jewish communities outside Palestine, and how much turned on the decisions of states and of named individuals. In Iraq, a Jewish community that had taken part in building the new state's institutions found itself, after independence, under a series of restrictions. In French Algeria, a city's Jews were killed in a riot whose causes a recent historian has traced to the politics of the colonial state. Neither case is evidence about Arabs or Muslims as a whole, and neither should be read that way. Each has its own dates, its own actors and its own documentation, and the documentation for the two is not of equal strength.

Iraq first. Under the British Mandate there, according to Sephardi Voices UK, Jews held government posts, Hebrew could be taught, and several Zionist organizations were permitted to operate until 1929 [cite:rs_rd3_sephardivoices_iraq]. Iraq became independent in 1932. In the years that followed, the same source reports, prominent Jews helped build the Iraqi civil service, and several sat in the first independent parliament [cite:rs_rd3_sephardivoices_iraq]. The Mandate-era history of Iraq, and how its independence came about, is told in [scene:irq_e02|Iraq under the Mandate].

From 1934 the picture changed. According to Sephardi Voices UK, Iraqi Jews from that year had to pay a deposit of £50 to travel abroad. In 1935 the teaching of Hebrew was barred, and state secondary schools set quotas on the number of Jewish pupils. In 1936 Jews were dismissed from the civil service [cite:rs_rd3_sephardivoices_iraq]. These measures, if accurately reported, amounted to discrimination by the state against its Jewish citizens in movement, education and employment, within four years of a period in which Jews had helped staff that same state. They rest, however, on a single source, and this book has not found a second, independent account of them. The 1936 dismissals in particular are stated in that source in sweeping terms, and the source does not say how many people were affected or whether every Jewish official was removed. They are given here as that source reports them.

The same source records an episode from 1933 that connects Iraq to the events in Germany described in [scene:hol_s01|Weimar's collapse]. During the first premiership of Rashid Ali al-Gaylani, in 1933, the German chargé d'affaires in Baghdad, Fritz Grobba, was allowed to publish Mein Kampf in Iraqi newspapers [cite:rs_rd3_sephardivoices_iraq]. That was antisemitic propaganda, placed in the Iraqi press by a German diplomat with the permission of an Iraqi government. It is again attested here by one source. What these years led to in Baghdad, the Farhud of 1941, belongs to a later chapter.

Algeria second. In Constantine, in French Algeria, in August 1934, over a few days of violence, 25 Jews and 3 Muslims were killed [cite:rs_rd3_cole_constantine]. Those figures come from the historian Joshua Cole, whose book Lethal Provocation: The Constantine Murders and the Politics of French Algeria (Cornell University Press, 2019) is the fullest study used here.

Cole's argument is about the state, not about the crowd alone. He argues that the French colonial state had an interest in "provoking and nurturing" tension between Jews and Muslims, because that tension helped it resist demands to extend citizenship rights to Algerian Muslims [cite:rs_rd3_cole_constantine]. Cole also identifies Mohamed El Maadi, a veteran with ties to the extreme right, as a key participant in the violence [cite:rs_rd3_cole_constantine]. His account therefore names both a structure, the colonial state's interest, and a specific person with extreme-right ties, rather than attributing the killings to Muslims in general.

That is a historian's argument and should be weighed as one. The source gathered here presents Cole's case through an interview about his book, and it does not set out a rival interpretation; this book has not gathered one. What can be said is that Cole's reading rests on the colonial context of citizenship and on a named participant, and that the killings themselves, 25 Jews and 3 Muslims, are not in question in the sources used here.

Set side by side, the two cases make one point plainly. Antisemitism in these years reached Jewish communities in the Arab world through more than one channel: in Iraq, through state measures and through Nazi propaganda placed by a German diplomat; in Algeria, according to Cole, through violence that a European colonial state had reasons to tolerate or encourage, with a veteran tied to the extreme right among its key participants. Neither source used here ties these events to Palestine.

The gaps are considerable. The Iraqi measures of 1934 to 1936 rest on a single source, and their scale is not recorded: how many travellers paid the deposit, how many pupils the quotas excluded, how many officials lost their posts. The record gathered here says nothing about the Jews of Syria, Lebanon, Egypt, Yemen or Morocco in these years. For Constantine it gives the toll and Cole's argument, but not the course of the riot day by day, or what the French authorities did afterwards.
