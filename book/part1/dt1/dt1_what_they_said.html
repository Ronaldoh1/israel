What They Actually Said

A claim can only be tested in the words of the people who made it. The case that democracy has failed is often summarised loosely, by admirers and critics alike, so this section sets it out as its three main authors put it, with dates. It has three voices: Peter Thiel, the investor and PayPal co-founder, writing in 2009; Curtis Yarvin, the software engineer who blogged as Mencius Moldbug from 2007 and has spoken under his own name since; and Nick Land, the British philosopher who gathered their ideas into a serial in 2012 and called it the Dark Enlightenment. Their arguments differ in tone and in detail. They share a single structure, and the rest of this book is built to test it. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de1_land_gwern]

Thiel, 2009: freedom against democracy

On 13 April 2009 Thiel published an essay called "The Education of a Libertarian" in Cato Unbound, the online journal of the Cato Institute. Its most quoted sentence was a statement of personal conclusion: "I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible." He gave reasons. Since 1920, he wrote, the growth of welfare beneficiaries and the extension of the vote to women, two groups he called difficult for libertarians, had turned the idea of "capitalist democracy" into a contradiction in terms. [cite:de1_cato_thiel] He did not propose to win the argument at the ballot box. He named three frontiers where a new kind of freedom might be built outside existing states, cyberspace, outer space and the open sea, and he described the task as finding "an escape from politics in all its forms." [cite:de3_cato_thiel]

The essay drew a large response, much of it about the sentence on women's suffrage. On 1 May 2009 Thiel published a short follow-up, later reprinted with the essay. It narrowed the claim without withdrawing it. "While I don't think any class of people should be disenfranchised," he wrote, "I have little hope that voting will make things better." He added that voting was "not under siege in America, but many other rights are." [cite:de1_audit_cato_followup] [cite:de3_cato_thiel] Read together, the two texts hold that the vote should stay but cannot be relied on to protect liberty, and that the remedy lies in building outside the reach of majorities.

Thiel's later conduct is part of the record and sits awkwardly with the essay. In the 2022 midterm elections he spent about $32 million, most of it through super PACs, the committees that may raise and spend unlimited sums on political advocacy so long as they do not coordinate with a candidate. He gave $15 million to a super PAC backing J.D. Vance in the Ohio Senate primary and $15 million to one backing Blake Masters in Arizona's, plus $2.5 million in October; Vance won his seat and Masters lost. [cite:de15_cnbc_results] [cite:de15_wiki_az2022] A man with "little hope that voting will make things better" invested heavily in who would be on the ballot. Read alongside the essay, the spending suggests that his doubt was about what mass voting produces, not about using elections, and money in them, to choose who governs.

Yarvin: the Cathedral and the CEO

Yarvin's version is more systematic. Writing as Moldbug on his blog Unqualified Reservations, which began in 2007 with a post called "A formalist manifesto", he argued that formal democracy was a façade. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] Real power, in his account, sat with an unelected establishment of universities, the press and the permanent civil service, which he called "the Cathedral". Explaining the term on public radio in 2025, he described it as "essentially the reification of the legitimate sources of information." [cite:de6_wbur] Others who have written about him summarise it the same way: the prestigious institutions behave "as if they were a single organizational structure," without any chain of command. [cite:de1_tablet] Elections, on this account, change the faces at the top while the Cathedral governs.

His cure was what he called neocameralism. In the passage Land quotes, a state is "a business which owns a country"; its shares carry votes, "and the shareholders elect a board, which hires and fires managers"; the residents are its customers. [cite:de35b_land_ebook] At a technology conference in 2012 he put the transition bluntly: "If Americans want to change their government, they're going to have to get over their dictator phobia." [cite:de6_tnr_duran] In January 2025 he told David Marchese of the New York Times that it was "not even that democracy is bad, it's just that it's very weak." [cite:de7_yarvin_nyt2025] Asked whether he voted, he said no, and added that he did not "believe in actually voting at all"; voting, he said, provided "this almost pornographic stimulus," a feeling of relevance like supporting a sports team. [cite:de1_audit_nyt_voting] [cite:de35b_nyt] In May 2025 he told CNN that his model would "concentrate that power in a single individual and then just hope somehow that this is the right individual," with the ruler answerable to a board rather than to citizens. [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin]

Land: no voice, free exit

Land's 2012 serial supplied the movement's name and its sharpest formulations. "For the hardcore neo-reactionaries," he wrote in the first instalment, "democracy is not merely doomed, it is doom itself." [cite:de1_land_gwern] He did not deny that money shapes government; he said that "winning elections is overwhelmingly a matter of vote buying," and proposed that the bribery be mapped and converted into tradeable shares in the state. [cite:de7_land_de] He also coined the slogan that sums up the alternative: "No voice, free exit." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] The terms come from the economist Albert Hirschman, who in 1970 described two ways members respond to a declining organisation: they can leave, which he called exit, or complain to those in charge, which he called voice. Hirschman warned that an easy exit can "atrophy the development of the art of voice," because the most demanding members leave first. [cite:de8_hirschman] Land's design takes voice away entirely and leaves only exit.

The structure of the argument

Stripped to its logic, the three versions make the same inference. The United States is a democracy. The United States is governed badly: by an establishment no one elected, by money, by a state that cannot deliver what it promises. Therefore democracy governs badly, and the answer is less of it: a chief executive, a board, a right to leave in place of a right to vote. Each step can be examined on its own. The second premise, that American government performs badly, is tested against the record in the next section, and much of it holds. The conclusion depends on the first premise, that the system being described is democracy, or at least democracy in a form fair to judge it by.

That premise is rarely argued in the texts, which take it as given. It is the one this book examines. The American constitution was not designed as a direct democracy. James Madison, defending it in The Federalist No. 10 in November 1787, wrote that pure democracies "have ever been spectacles of turbulence and contention," and praised a republic for its power "to refine and enlarge the public views, by passing them through the medium of a chosen body of citizens." [cite:dt1_fed10] The system that followed has been made more democratic in stages, by amendment and statute, and it retains features, such as the Electoral College and the role of money in campaigns, that by design or by later court rulings do not weigh each citizen's preference equally. If those features account for a large share of the symptoms the critics describe, then the critics have tested a partial democracy and drawn a conclusion about the whole.

Two cautions keep this test honest. First, Thiel, Yarvin and Land are not the only people who doubt democracy, and the movement's influence on any particular official is a separate question, examined in the companion volume, which finds convergence rather than any documented plan. [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|The Dark Enlightenment Architecture: who enforces the charter] Second, none of the texts quoted here engages the reforms that would make the American system more majoritarian: ranked-choice voting, a national popular vote for president or proportional representation. That absence is not proof that those reforms answer the critique. It means the critique has not yet been tested against them, which is the work of [scene:dt8_their_logic|Part Eight]. The fullest statement of how their model would choose and remove a ruler is set out in the companion volume's [scene:de35b_what_the_texts_say|account of who picks the CEO].
