Part Four set out to find Washington in the making of Israel, and found two Washingtons that could hardly be less alike.

The first is the District of Columbia: a city made by a debt bargain in 1790, governed since 1973 by an elected mayor and council that Congress can overrule, whose judges and chief prosecutor are chosen by the President and whose National Guard he commands. In 2025 a President used the Home Rule Act's emergency clause for the first time and sent troops into its streets; as of November 2026 they are still there, and a federal appeals court has yet to rule. [cite:dv4_wiki_homerule] [cite:dv4_crs_dc_courts] [cite:dv4_wiki_eo14333] [cite:dv4_wbal_guard_2026] It is the least sovereign of the three cities in this volume, an anomaly of too little self-government rather than too much, and it played no part of its own in Israel's story.

The second is the federal government the district houses. It recognized Israel within eleven minutes in 1948 while embargoing its weapons, and then, from the 1960s, became its principal supplier of arms, its largest source of aid, about $175 billion through 2025, and its shield at the Security Council, with more than fifty vetoes. [cite:truman_recognition] [cite:dv4_herf_embargo] [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025] [cite:dv4_jvl_vetoes] Its support was a policy, renewed by choice and bent when Presidents wanted something else, and in November 2026 its terms are being renegotiated. [cite:dv4_toi_rubio_nov2026] The verdict is that Washington was not Israel's parent at birth but became its guardian afterwards, and on the documented record that guardianship has no rival. Part Five turns to the child itself: how the State of Israel governs, and how a state so dependent on one guardian has so often defied it.
