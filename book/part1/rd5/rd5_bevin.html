Ernest Bevin and the Palestine Question, 1945–1946

In the same month that the network took shape in New York, the power that actually governed Palestine changed hands. Clement Attlee formed his Labour government on 26 July 1945 and chose Ernest Bevin as Foreign Secretary. Bevin had been born on 9 March 1881; he would die on 14 April 1951. [cite:rs_rd5_britannica_bevin] Over the next eighteen months his statements set the terms of British policy on Palestine, and his name became attached to it, by the Zionist leaders who fought him, by the Americans who pressed him, and by the historians who have tried to explain him.

His reputation in this story is unusually divided. To Chaim Weizmann and to Christopher Mayhew, who dealt with him, he was a man who disliked Jews. To the historian Alan Bullock, to Ben-Gurion and to Attlee, he was an opponent of a Jewish state, not an enemy of Jews. To a third school, his policy followed from what the Foreign Office took to be Britain's interests in the Middle East, and his personal feelings mattered little. All three readings rest on documents. Before weighing them, it helps to set out what Bevin actually said and did in his first eighteen months.

The general reference works are sparing. Britannica's entry on Bevin offers only one judgment on Palestine: that his plans of 1947–48 "for a federal Jewish-Arab nation or other partition of Palestine collapsed" in the face of the Arab-Israeli conflict. [cite:rs_rd5_britannica_bevin] The detail lies in his own statements, in American diplomatic reports, and in the scholarship that has tried to explain him.

The statement of 13 November 1945. Bevin's first major act on Palestine came on 13 November 1945. He announced a "joint Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry, under a rotating chairmanship." Until it reported, he said, there would be "no interruption of Jewish immigration at the present monthly rate." The text did not state what that rate was. Looking further ahead, he spoke of a trusteeship agreement that would "supersede the existing mandate." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_bevin_1945] The statement also set out the terms in which London saw the problem. Bevin referred to the Palestinian Arabs' "90,000,000 co-religionists in India," and he said that Palestine "does not by itself provide sufficient opportunity" for solving the Jewish problem. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_bevin_1945] Read together, the two phrases framed Palestine as one part of an answer for Europe's Jews, not the whole of it, and placed the Arabs of Palestine within a far larger population whose opinion London had to weigh.

The committee itself, its twelve members, its hearings and its report are the subject of [scene:aac_s01|the next section]. What matters here is that the inquiry was Bevin's chosen instrument. It brought Washington into the question, and in the interim it left immigration where it stood.

The same day, Bevin spoke privately to the American ambassador in London, John Winant. Winant's telegram, received in Washington on 14 November 1945, reported that Bevin said he would be willing to "stake his political future on finding solution for Palestine problem," and that he "made a plea against approaching this subject in spirit of racial antagonism." [cite:rs_rd5_frus_winant_1945] The telegram is a primary American record, not Bevin's own text, but it shows how he wished to be understood by the government he most needed to persuade: as a minister who had staked his career on a settlement and who wanted the question kept out of racial politics.

His public words that day did not always match that wish. According to the historian Raphael Langham, Bevin told the House of Commons on 13 November, "We cannot accept the view that the Jews should be driven out of Europe." At a press conference the same day, he warned that if Jews "want to get too much at the head of the queue" there was a danger of an antisemitic reaction. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] The first sentence stated a principle: the survivors had a future in Europe and should not be treated as people to be removed from it. The second, read plainly, made the survivors' claims the cause of the hostility they might meet. Langham calls the remark "unfortunate." [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012]

"Too many Jews in New York." The pressure Bevin resisted came above all from the United States, where President Truman was pressing for the admission of Jewish refugees to Palestine. At the Labour Party conference in June 1946, Bevin said of that pressure that "They did not want too many Jews in New York." Langham calls it "a gratuitous sneer." It angered Truman and provoked protests in the United States. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] The remark was an antisemitic jibe from a sitting Foreign Secretary. It suggested that American support for Jewish immigration to Palestine was a way of keeping Jews out of America, and it was made at a party conference, in public. Whatever conclusion one reaches about Bevin's policy, the sentence itself needs no softening.

The London conference and the American visit. In September 1946 Britain convened a conference at Lancaster House in London. According to Langham, Bevin told the Arab delegation there that no solution could ignore Jewish claims. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] This sits awkwardly beside the remarks above, and that is part of what makes him hard to read: the same minister who sneered at American motives told the Arab states that Jewish claims had to be part of any settlement. Two months later he traveled to the United States, in November and December 1946. During that visit, Langham writes, Bevin reportedly came to the view that Britain would have to give up the Mandate. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] The decision itself, taken in February 1947, belongs to [scene:rd5_britain_exits|Britain's exit], the subject of a later section.

The mechanism. In these months Bevin held the levers that mattered for Palestine: the rate of Jewish immigration, the choice of forum, and the timing of any settlement. The November 1945 statement used each one. It kept immigration at the existing rate rather than opening the gates or closing them, it moved the question into a joint inquiry with the United States, and it pointed toward trusteeship rather than either a Jewish or an Arab state. The parties most affected could not set any of these terms. The Jewish Agency and the Arab representatives could put their cases to the inquiry but could not decide; and Washington, though it pressed hard on immigration, did not govern Palestine. What Bevin could not control was the American response, and his June 1946 remark shows how much that pressure grated.

The dispute over his motives. Why did Bevin oppose a Jewish state? Historians have given three answers, and Langham sets them out side by side. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012]

The first answer is personal hostility. Two contemporaries who dealt with him said so plainly. Christopher Mayhew said that "Ernest detests Jews," and Chaim Weizmann said Bevin was "blinded by his hatred of the Jews." The historian Peter Weiler takes a more careful line: he finds that Bevin "held antisemitic views" but sees "no evidence that they shaped his policy." [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] The strength of this case is the remarks themselves, which are on the record and were made in public. Its weakness, as Weiler's own wording shows, is the step from a man's prejudices to the reasons for his decisions.

The second answer is that Bevin was an anti-Zionist, not an antisemite. The historian Alan Bullock wrote that he "was not moved by hatred of the Jews." David Ben-Gurion, who had every reason to think the worst of him, said, "I prefer to consider Bevin simply as an anti-Zionist." Attlee defended him in the same terms in the House of Commons in 1949. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] The strength of this case is that it comes in part from Bevin's opponents, Ben-Gurion above all, and that it fits his statement to the Arab delegation in September 1946. Its weakness is that the remarks of November 1945 and June 1946 are hard to describe as political opposition to Zionism alone.

The third answer is strategic interest. A Foreign Office briefing attributed to Harold Beeley stressed Middle East oil, communications and the Suez route as Britain's interests in the region, and Langham cites the historian W. Roger Louis on Britain's Middle East position. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] On this reading, Bevin's policy was the Foreign Office's policy: Britain needed the goodwill of the Arab states and of Muslim opinion, the "90,000,000 co-religionists in India" whom Bevin himself had invoked, and a Jewish state imposed against that opinion would put those interests at risk. The strength of this case is that it explains the policy without needing to explain the man, and that it rests on the Foreign Office's own statement of British interests. Its weakness is that it says little about why the remarks were made.

Langham's own conclusion leans toward the third answer. Bevin, he writes, was probably not motivated by antisemitism when he made policy, and "genuinely thought it was in the best interests of Britain." But his opposition hardened over time into anti-Zionism, and Langham leaves open whether, in that later stage, Bevin crossed into antisemitism. [cite:rs_rd5_langham_2012] That conclusion keeps two things apart that are often run together: what a man said about Jews, which in Bevin's case included antisemitic remarks stated in public, and why his government did what it did, which Langham ties to what Bevin judged to be Britain's interests.

What the record does not settle. The sources used here do not show what "the present monthly rate" of immigration was in November 1945, since Bevin's text gave no figure. They do not show what Bevin said to American officials during his visit in late 1946, only Langham's report that he came to think the Mandate would have to be given up. Nor can any document settle what Bevin felt, as against what he said and did. The remarks are a matter of record; the motive behind the policy remains a matter of judgment, and the historians who have studied him most closely still disagree.
