The New CIA's First Major Warning

One version of events holds that American intelligence either never assessed partition or supported it. The record shows that the newest intelligence agency in Washington assessed it formally, in writing, and warned against it on the eve of the vote.

Before 1947, American foreign intelligence ran first through the Office of Strategic Services, created in 1942 and led by William J. Donovan, whom Roosevelt appointed. [cite:w_oss] The OSS was dissolved in October 1945, and its analysts and operatives fed the agencies that followed, beginning with the interim Central Intelligence Group in 1946. [cite:w_oss] The National Security Act of 1947 then created the Central Intelligence Agency, effective 18 September 1947, together with the National Security Council and the structure that became the Department of Defense. [cite:w_nsa1947] The first Director of Central Intelligence, Rear Admiral Roscoe Hillenkoetter, was appointed by Truman. [cite:w_hillenkoetter]

The agency was barely ten weeks old when it delivered its estimate on Palestine. Its Office of Reports and Estimates issued ORE-55, "The Consequences of the Partition of Palestine," dated 28 November 1947, one day before the General Assembly vote. [cite:cia_ore55] The summary opened with a prediction that armed hostilities between Jews and Arabs would break out if the Assembly adopted partition. It stated that the United Nations would then be morally bound to enforce its decision. It assessed that Jewish forces would initially prevail because of superior organization and equipment. And it warned that the United States, by backing partition, had already lost much of its prestige in the Arab world, and that an imposed partition would put American commercial and strategic interests in serious danger. [cite:cia_ore55] The declassified estimate is held in the CIA's own reading room. [cite:cia_ore55]

The estimate did not stand alone. It echoed the warnings already on file from Loy Henderson at the State Department, from the Joint Chiefs of Staff and from Defense Secretary James Forrestal, described in [scene:s07j|the previous section]. Within roughly ten weeks, then, four separate parts of the American government, the State Department's Near East office, the Joint Chiefs, the Secretary of Defense and the new CIA, had warned in writing against American sponsorship of partition.

The case on the other side came from the President's domestic political advisers, led by Clark Clifford and David Niles, and their view prevailed, both in the partition vote and later in the recognition of Israel eleven minutes after its declaration of independence. The record shows that the warnings were made and read. It does not show them shaping the outcome.

The intelligence estimate is worth noticing for what it predicted and for how it framed the risks. It predicted war. It also predicted that the Jewish side would hold the initial advantage, because of organization and equipment. The concerns it set out were American interests: prestige in the Arab world, commerce and strategy. That was the language of the State Department and the Pentagon as well, and it was the language that lost.

The question often asked about this period is whether Washington understood what it was backing. On the evidence of ORE-55, the answer is that it did. American intelligence assessed partition, did so in writing, and predicted war and damage to American interests. The decision was taken anyway.
