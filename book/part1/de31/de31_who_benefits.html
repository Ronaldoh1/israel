Who Gains, Who Pays

Every brake that comes off moves power from one place to another, and money often moves with it. This section follows each of the five patterns of [scene:de31_five_patterns|the previous section] along one chain: the input (the order, ruling or notice), the mechanism (what it changes in practice), who gains, and who pays. Two rules govern it. A donor, lobby or company appears only where a document shows its role, and where none does the section says so. And timing is not causation: an interest that gained after a change did not thereby cause it. The chain shows one thing plainly. For most of these patterns the documented winner is not a company or a donor but an office, the presidency, and that office passes to whoever wins the next election.

Removal: the office gains

The input is Trump v. Slaughter, Executive Order 14215 and the new civil-service schedule. The mechanism is removal at will: a commissioner of the Federal Trade Commission, or one of about 8,000 senior career officials, now holds the post at the president's pleasure, and every independent agency's major rules pass through the White House first. [cite:de0_slaughter] [cite:de31_fr_14215] [cite:de30_npr_8000] The gain goes to the sitting president, of either party; the removal theory that won in 2026 is the one a Biden Justice Department opinion used in 2021 to remove the Social Security commissioner. [cite:de31_govexec_saul] The cost falls on the officials who lost their protection and on the design Congress chose for such commissions, whose members could be removed only for cause. The office's own director stated the purpose as accountability, "a restoration, in our mind, of the democratic process"; a scholar warned that it "creates bubbles around policymakers." [cite:de30_npr_8000] No donor or company is documented as seeking these changes; the case that ended Humphrey's Executor began with the removal of a single commissioner. [cite:de0_slaughter]

Watchdogs: the audited gain room

The input is the removal of 17 inspectors general on 24 January 2025. The mechanism is that the offices lost their confirmed heads overnight, without the notice and reasons the law requires. [cite:de31_npr_igs] [cite:de31_crs_in12363] The gain is structural: the agencies they audited lost, for a time, the auditor Congress had placed inside them. The cost is measurable in what the offices had been returning. Across government, inspectors general reported about $71 billion in monetary impact in fiscal 2024 against budgets of about $3.9 billion, a return of about 18 to 1; a report by the senior Democrat on the Senate's oversight committee counted $175 billion in potential savings on open recommendations from the offices of the 19 inspectors general removed in 2025. [cite:de31_lawfare_igs] Those are claims about potential, not money recovered, and the report's comparison with DOGE's savings depended on which DOGE figure was used. No specific audit is on record as stopped because of the removals, and no company or donor is documented as having sought them.

The purse: Congress pays

The input is the withholding of appropriated money and the pocket rescission. The mechanism is time: money held until its legal life ends is cancelled without a vote. [cite:de31_crs_pocket] The gain goes to the executive's spending priorities over Congress's; the budget director called the Supreme Court's order "a major victory." [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid] The cost is concrete and named in the auditor's own findings: Head Start programmes, medical research at the National Institutes of Health, museums and libraries, school energy upgrades, and FEMA's emergency food and shelter and disaster-warning grants. [cite:de31_sac_fema] About $4 billion in foreign aid lapsed in September 2025, foreign aid Congress had voted and the president had asked it to cancel. [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid] The deepest cost is to Congress's power of the purse itself, in Justice Kagan's words the "balance of power between Congress and the president." [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid] No private beneficiary is documented; the money was not redirected to a contractor but left unspent.

Emergencies: importers paid, then were repaid

The input is a set of emergency declarations under IEEPA. The mechanism was a tax set by the president alone: tariffs, which by law are paid by the importer of record when goods cross the border. The government collected about $166 billion in these duties before the Supreme Court struck them down on 20 February 2026, from more than 330,000 importers. [cite:de31_gt_s122] [cite:us2_wiki_learning] The gain went to the Treasury while the duties lasted; the cost fell first on importers, among them the educational-toy company whose name the case bears. After the ruling the money began to flow back through a customs refund system opened in late April 2026, which only the importer of record can use; whether customers who paid higher prices see any of it is a question the refunds do not reach. [cite:de31_gt_s122] No donor is documented as having sought the emergency tariffs. The replacements came under other laws, and those are traced in [scene:de31_what_was_blocked|the next section].

Data walls: enforcement gains, the record-holders pay

The input is the tax and Medicaid agreements, the TANF notice and the withdrawn consumer-data rules. The mechanism is reuse: records people gave to get health cover, cash help or a tax refund are matched against immigration files. [cite:de30_ap_krause] [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] The documented gain is to immigration enforcement, which receives addresses it did not have. A private company also appears in the documented record. In January 2026 a Medicaid dataset covering millions of people went from the health agency to ICE and was shared with Palantir, the data-integration company Peter Thiel co-founded and chairs, over a Teams chat, according to a sworn declaration in the California case; Palantir said the data was "purged pursuant to government instruction." [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] That is a documented use of Palantir's platform by the government, not a document showing that Palantir sought the data rules; none does. On the consumer side, the data-broker industry welcomed the withdrawal of the proposed rule: the head of its trade association thanked the bureau's acting director for "recognizing the concerns we flagged." [cite:de31_cdia] The cost falls on the people in the records, not all of them the intended targets: one dataset sent in error included a Minnesota file of refugees that contained citizens, and a judge found the tax agency had disclosed information unlawfully about 42,695 times. [cite:de24b_npr_medicaid] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [scene:de24b_benefits|Part Twenty-Five] follows what those records hold.

One brake removed by Congress itself

Not every brake was taken off by the executive. On 9 May 2025 the president signed a resolution of Congress, passed under the Congressional Review Act, cancelling a consumer bureau rule that would have capped overdraft fees at large banks at $5 or at cost; the bureau had estimated savings of up to $5 billion a year, about $225 for each household that paid overdraft fees. [cite:de21_hk_cra] [cite:ad9_cfpb_od_final] A consumer group later counted about $4 billion in overdraft revenue at the twenty largest banks in 2025, up 6.2 per cent on 2023. [cite:us22_nclc_od26] The beneficiaries are documented as a class, banks with more than $10 billion in assets; the payers are the households charged the fees. This was Congress using its own check by majority vote, the opposite of the patterns above, and it belongs in the ledger for that reason.

What the chains show, and what they do not

The chains show a transfer of discretion toward the presidency, documented in each pattern, and costs that fall on named programmes, officials and record-holders. They show one company from the network the book maps, Palantir, inside a documented data transfer, and one industry, the data brokers, welcoming a rule's withdrawal in its own words. They do not show Peter Thiel, Elon Musk or any other figure of the earlier parts drafting or lobbying for any of the five patterns; the money ledgers of [scene:de14_network_map|Part Fourteen] and [scene:de17_money_to_office|Part Seventeen] record campaign money and contracts, not authorship of these orders. The gainer that recurs is the office, and the office has been used the same way by presidents of both parties, as Biden's removal of a protected commissioner shows. [cite:de31_govexec_saul] Whether the brakes held is the next section's subject.
