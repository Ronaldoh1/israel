The Tools Were There

Two duties are often confused, and the difference matters for what this chapter can fairly say. The law does not require prosecutors to charge anyone. Whether to bring a case is left to their judgment, under the department's own principles of prosecution, and no court can order them to file one. [cite:ep15_jm_927] What the law does require is protection for the people already harmed. The Transparency Act let the department withhold victims' names and personal details, and forbade withholding anything to spare officials or public figures "embarrassment, reputational harm, or political sensitivity". [cite:ep8_govinfo_act] The federal victims' rights law speaks of treating victims "with fairness and with respect for the victim's dignity and privacy". [cite:ep15_lii_3771] Prosecution was a choice. Protecting the survivors was the instruction.

What happened when the instruction was broken

The 2026 release left 43 of the 47 victims' names checked by the Wall Street Journal readable, some beside home addresses. [cite:ep9_wsj_43] Lawyers told a federal judge that nearly a hundred survivors' lives had been "turned upside down"; one woman said her banking details appeared in 51 entries and she received death threats, and another called the release "life threatening". The department blamed "technical or human error", withdrew thousands of files and changed its process. [cite:ep15_fox_threats2026] In March 2026 a group of survivors filed a class action against the department and Google, alleging the department "made a deliberate policy choice to prioritize rapid, large-volume disclosure over protection of Epstein survivors' privacy"; the department said 0.1 percent of released pages had contained identifying information. [cite:ep15_scripps_classaction2026] The Inspector General opened a review in April. [cite:ep9_aj_ig2026] The survivors were exposed in one release. The men around Epstein had their names behind black bars until members of Congress read some of them aloud. [cite:ep15_cbs_sixnames2026]

What the government can do when it wants to

The government's capacity to watch people is not in doubt; Edward Snowden's disclosures in 2013 made it public. One program he exposed, the bulk collection of Americans' phone records, was ruled unlawful by a federal appeals court in 2020, which also found that officials' public claims about its value were "inconsistent with the contents of the classified record". [cite:ep15_tc_moalin2020] A surveillance court opinion released in 2023 found the FBI had improperly searched a foreign-intelligence database 278,000 times, including searches on people arrested at racial-justice protests, on suspects in the January 6 riot and, in one batch, on 19,000 donors to a congressional campaign, of whom eight had any sufficient tie to foreign influence. [cite:ep15_lawfare_702_2023] [cite:ep15_wowt_702_2023]

Those intelligence tools are, by law, for foreign intelligence, and pointing them at domestic crime suspects would itself be an abuse. That is not what the Epstein case lacked. It lacked the ordinary tools of a criminal investigation, aimed early and at the right people. The release shows those tools working once a decision was made: after the 2019 arrest, prosecutors subpoenaed Epstein's banks, his accountant and lawyer, Google, Facebook, Apple and the phone companies, and the airlines. [cite:ep15_doj_gjjpm] [cite:ep15_doj_gjkahn] Before that decision there was a written complaint to the FBI in 1996 that went nowhere, [cite:guardian_farmer] a federal case closed in 2008 by an agreement that immunised his associates, [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] and, as the previous chapter showed, no compulsory request on the record to the Swiss bank or to Black's companies, which paid him tens of millions.

What the record allows the book to say

It does not show that anyone decided to protect Epstein's circle by withholding surveillance, and the book does not claim it. It shows something that needs no hidden motive to be indefensible. The same government that searched the communications of tens of thousands of ordinary people on thin grounds published the names of the girls and women Epstein abused, while it kept the names of men around him hidden and left the people who paid him unasked. Whatever the intentions, the result was that the people with least power carried the most exposure, and the people with most power carried the least. That is not a technical error. It is a measure of whose privacy the system treats as worth protecting, and by its own law it chose wrongly. The survivors said so first, and they were right. [cite:ep9_ms_survivors2026]
