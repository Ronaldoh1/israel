What the Emperor Paid For

Constantine's conversion is usually told as a story of faith: a vision before the battle at the Milvian Bridge in 312, a victory, a new religion for the empire. What followed it can also be told as a story of money, and the documents for that version are better than those for the vision. Within months of his victory the emperor began to pay the church, protect it and give its bishops powers that no Christian teacher had ever held. The bishops in the line Irenaeus had described were the ones he paid. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] [scene:vt21_conversion|Constantine's conversion]

The first payment was an exemption. In a letter of 313 to Anullinus, governor of Africa, Constantine ordered that the clergy of the catholic church be "entirely exempted from all public duties", the expensive civic offices that wealthy Roman citizens were obliged to carry. [cite:vt21_hist_x7] The letter named the catholic church: the communion in agreement with the emperor's bishops, not every group calling itself Christian. In Africa, where a rival Christian body, the Donatists, claimed to be the true church, the exemption made the question of who was "catholic" a question of who paid tax and who did not. [cite:vt21_hist_x7] [cite:vt21_bateman]

The second was a court. By enactments of 318 and 333 litigants could take civil cases to a bishop, whose judgment the state would enforce. [cite:vt21_bateman] A bishop was now a public magistrate as well as a pastor, and his court was a source of influence over every family that used it. The third was property. Constantine built the Lateran basilica in Rome on the site of a demolished guards' barracks, then Old St Peter's over the shrine on the Vatican hill, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem; after 320 he endowed churches with land and other wealth. [cite:vt21_wiki_constantine] [cite:vt21_wiki_relpol] These endowments are the first documented foundation of the landed wealth that the Roman see would accumulate over the following centuries.

The emperor also paid for books. In a letter preserved by Eusebius he ordered fifty copies of the sacred scriptures, written on prepared parchment by skilled scribes, for the churches of his new capital at Constantinople, and authorised the use of public transport to deliver them. [cite:vt21_vc4] The letter does not list the books the copies contained, and the claim that surviving fourth-century manuscripts are among the fifty is speculation. [cite:vt21_vc4] What the order shows is that by the 330s an imperial treasury was paying for the production of the Bible, and the bishop who received the commission, Eusebius of Caesarea, was the same scholar who had sorted the books of his day into accepted, disputed and rejected. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3]

The last step in that chain is where patronage became a weapon. Eusebius preserves an undated edict of Constantine addressed to "Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites, Paulians, ye who are called Cataphrygians". It forbade them to meet, in public or "in any private house or place whatsoever", and ordered that their houses of prayer "be made over without delay to the catholic Church", with other property confiscated to the state. [cite:vt21_vc3_64] [cite:vt21_vc3_65] Eusebius adds that search was made for their books, and that many members, deprived of their meeting places, returned to the church. [cite:vt21_vc3_66] The two groups that had appeared in Part Two, the followers of Marcion and of Valentinus, now lost their buildings by law, and the buildings passed to the bishops who had excluded them.

The edict is the hinge of this book's story. Before it, the line between the canon and the excluded books was enforced by persuasion and by expulsion from a voluntary community. After it, belonging to the right communion carried tax relief, legal power and property, and belonging to the wrong one could cost a congregation its meeting house. Eusebius does not say whether the books that were searched out were burned, and how strictly the edict was enforced is not recorded. [cite:vt21_vc3_66] But the financial direction of the law is plain. It moved assets from the excluded to the included. Every later chapter of this story, through to the Index, rests on that arrangement: the guardians of the list were also the beneficiaries of the state that enforced it.
