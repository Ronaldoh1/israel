Palantir and Israel: The Partnership, the Denials and the Investors

Palantir's tie to Israel is a contract between an American company and a foreign ministry of defence. It is documented in outline, its contents are not public, and the most serious claims made about it are denied by the company and contradicted by Israeli reporting. Each of those layers needs to be kept separate.

The partnership, January 2024. Three months into the Gaza war, Palantir held the first board meeting of 2024 in Tel Aviv, with Peter Thiel and Alex Karp among the executives who travelled, and announced a strategic partnership with Israel's Ministry of Defense to "supply Palantir technology to help the country's war effort." [cite:ow9_calcalist_partner] Globes, the Israeli business daily, reported on 29 January 2024 that after speaking at Tel Aviv University, Karp went to the military headquarters in Tel Aviv and signed an upgraded agreement with the ministry, and was photographed with Danny Gold, head of its Directorate of Defense Research and Development. Bloomberg reported that Israel was set to buy Palantir's AI platform, with revenue expected in the tens of millions of dollars. "None of the parties would elaborate" on what Palantir actually does. [cite:ow9_globes_palantir]

What Israeli reporting says it does, and does not do. The same Globes report gave the most specific account in the public record. Palantir, it said, was understood to work on some logistics and manpower management for the Israel Defense Forces but "does not work with military intelligence"; Unit 8200, the army's signals-intelligence unit, and the Shin Bet security service had examined its system and "decided not to hire its services." [cite:ow9_globes_palantir] This report rests on unnamed sources, and Globes did not say how it knew; it is nonetheless the only account in the sources used here that describes the work unit by unit, and it runs against the widespread claim that Palantir supplies Israel's targeting systems.

What Palantir says. The company has answered that claim in writing. In a letter of 22 May 2025 to the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territory, it said it "did not provide the technology for, nor is it involved with, Gospel or Lavender," two AI targeting systems named in reporting on the Gaza war; that its work for the Ministry of Defense "only began in the wake of the attacks of 7 October 2023," its earlier Israeli customers having been civilian agencies; that it "does not and has not supported predictive policing workflows in Israel"; and that its defence work is done "in accordance with domestic, international, and humanitarian law." [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] Karp has defended the work publicly and on political grounds. Asked in March 2024 about staff who left over it, he said: "We've lost employees, I'm sure we'll lose employees. If you have a position that does not cost you to ever lose an employee, it's not a position." [cite:ow9_fortune_karp]

A separate company: Microsoft and Unit 8200. The one documented case of an American technology company's infrastructure holding Israeli intelligence data on Palestinians does not involve Palantir. On 6 August 2025 The Guardian reported, citing eleven sources, that Unit 8200 had stored recordings of Palestinians' phone calls in a segregated area of Microsoft's Azure cloud. Microsoft ordered a review, and on 25 September 2025 its president, Brad Smith, said the review had found evidence that "supports elements of the Guardian's reporting," including the use of Azure storage in the Netherlands and of AI services, and that Microsoft had disabled the unit's use of those services. "We do not provide technology to facilitate mass surveillance of civilians," he said. Israel's Defense Ministry declined to comment. [cite:ow9_nbc_msft] The case is examined with Unit 8200 in [scene:ow4_gaza|Part Four]. Accounts that attach this story to Palantir conflate two companies; the material reviewed for this volume contains no source that does so.

Investors. Palantir's Israeli business has had a cost in its share register. In October 2024 Storebrand Asset Management, a large Norwegian investment firm, sold a holding worth about $24 million, saying it had excluded Palantir because of "sales of products and services to Israel" for use in the occupied Palestinian territories, which it believed included "AI-based predictive policing systems." [cite:ow9_globes_storebrand] [cite:ow9_silicon_storebrand] Palantir's later letter to the UN denies supporting predictive policing in Israel. [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] Neither report records a response from Palantir to Storebrand itself. Storebrand's holding was small beside the company's market value, then about $96 billion. [cite:ow9_globes_storebrand]

Testing the one-way claim. On the documents, the flow in this relationship runs from an American company to an Israeli customer, for payment, in a market Karp says is commercially minor. [cite:ow9_fortune_karp] What the United States receives is what any seller receives; no source used here records a reciprocal arrangement, Israeli access to American data through Palantir, or American access to what Israel does with Palantir's software. That last gap is the real asymmetry. The contract's terms are not public, the Israeli Ministry of Defense will not describe the work, and the only detailed account, from Globes, comes from unnamed Israeli sources. [cite:ow9_globes_palantir] The Pentagon's contracts with the same company are public in outline and its spending passes through Congress; the Israeli use of the company's tools passes through no process that Americans can see. That pattern, a documented American export and an undocumented use, recurs through the next part, which turns to the Israeli firms that sell their surveillance tools to American agencies.
