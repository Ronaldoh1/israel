After the Vote: Jews of the Arab World, 1947–1948

The war of 1948 is usually told as a war inside Palestine, and its refugees as one people's loss. The record of the same months shows a second set of events running in parallel, outside Palestine. From the day after the partition vote, Jewish communities in Syria, Egypt, Lebanon, Aden, Bahrain, Iraq, Libya and Yemen came under attack from mobs, under arrest and sequestration by governments, and in Iraq under a law that made Zionism a crime. These were not acts of "the Arabs" in general. Each was carried out by a named government, a mob in a named city, or a named organization, and this section ties each one to its author. Together they began the decline of these communities, a story that runs well past 1948 and is set beside the Palestinian displacement in [scene:s00e3|two displacements].

The riots of 30 November to 5 December 1947. On 30 November 1947, the day after the General Assembly voted for partition, anti-Jewish riots broke out in Damascus, Aleppo, Cairo, Beirut and Aden, according to the Center for Israel Education. In Cairo, the same account reports, leaders of al-Azhar declared a holy war against the Zionists. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_arabcities] The worst damage that the sources record in Syria was in Aleppo. A mob broke into the Great Synagogue, destroyed Torah scrolls and damaged the Aleppo Codex; most of the Codex was saved and smuggled to Jerusalem in 1958. Across the city, according to the same account, 150 Jewish homes, 50 shops, five schools and 18 synagogues were destroyed. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_arabcities] On 5 December 1947, in Bahrain, Jewish homes and shops were looted, the synagogue was destroyed and one Jewish woman was killed. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_arabcities]

The deadliest of these riots took place in Aden, then a British colony. The Center for Israel Education lists Aden among the cities where violence broke out after the vote; the accounts of Aden itself date the riots there from 2 December 1947. The toll is disputed. The Center for Israel Education gives 82 Jews killed and 76 wounded. [cite:rs_rd5_cie_arabcities] The Jewish Virtual Library, which credits Wikipedia for its entry, gives between 76 and 82 Jews killed, and also 38 others killed: 33 Arabs, four Indian Muslims and one Somali. It records that 106 of the 170 Jewish-owned shops in Crater were looted. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_aden] A study of 1951 gives 76 killed and 78 wounded. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] The sources agree on the order of magnitude, between 76 and 82 Jewish dead, and differ on the exact count. The sources here do not say how the 38 others died.

What the 1951 study is. Much of what is known about the official measures of 1948 comes from one document, and the reader should know what it is. It is "The Situation of Jews in Moslem Countries," by H. Dewey Anderson, chapter XIV of The Arab Refugee Problem, a set of proposals submitted to the United Nations General Assembly in December 1951. It is available today through the Center for Israel Education, which presents it as a UN report. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] It is a contemporary compilation by a named author, written three years after the events and placed inside a work about the Arab refugees. It is not a resolution or a finding of the General Assembly. Its dates and figures are specific, and they are used below as Anderson's, attributed to him, because the sources gathered for this book do not test them against the governments' own records.

Egypt: arrests, sequestration and bombings. When the Arab armies entered Palestine in May 1948, the Egyptian government turned against the Jews of its own cities. According to Anderson, more than 1,000 Jews were arrested in Cairo on 15 May 1948. Proclamation No. 26, issued on 29 May, sequestered the property of internees; of the 68 sequestration orders issued up to 30 September, 67 involved Jews. Proclamation No. 30, of 6 June, allowed Jews to be searched without a warrant. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] These were acts of state, published as proclamations, and the ratio of 67 to 68 shows on whom they fell.

Violence followed in Cairo's Jewish quarters. On 20 June 1948 a bombing struck the Karaite quarter. Anderson records 12 houses destroyed and 20 people killed; Wikipedia, citing the historian Beinin, gives 22 killed and 41 wounded. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_cairo1948] The Cicurel department store was bombed on 19 July, and a bombing on 22 September killed 19 people. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_cairo1948] In November 1948 the government arrested 32 leaders of the Muslim Brotherhood and banned the organization, and in a trial in 1950 members of the Brotherhood were charged with the bombings of June to November. [cite:rs_rd5_wiki_cairo1948] The sources gathered here record the charge; they do not record the verdict.

Iraq: a law, a hanging and an armistice never signed. Iraq took part in the war from the start. It joined the invasion on 15 May 1948, the day after Israel declared independence, and when the fighting ended it was the only Arab state that had fought which refused to sign an armistice with Israel, leaving a formal state of war that, in the Israeli government's account, has never ended. [cite:govil_aliyah] The invasion itself is told in the section on the Arab states' war. At home, according to accounts of the Iraqi Jewish community, the measures escalated after the partition vote from mob violence to the confiscation of property and prosecutions those accounts call trumped-up. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia]

The central instrument was a law of July 1948, and the sources describe its penalty differently. Anderson writes that it made Zionist activity punishable by death or by at least seven years' imprisonment. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] An account in Yated Ne'eman dates the law to 19 July 1948 and gives the penalty as up to seven years in prison. It adds that every Jew was presumed to be a Zionist under the law, so that it worked as a blanket criminalization of the community, and that the testimony of two Muslim witnesses was enough to convict, with little means of appeal. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] The two accounts agree on the month and on the law's target; they disagree on the maximum and minimum sentence, and this book cannot settle which is right from the sources it holds.

Further orders followed. On 28 August 1948 Jews were barred from banking and from dealing in foreign currency, and on 19 October they were ordered discharged from government departments, according to Anderson. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] Between those two dates came the case that the community's own accounts treat as the turning point. On 23 September 1948 Shafiq Ades, a prominent and well-connected Jewish businessman, was publicly hanged in Basra on charges of selling weapons to Israel and to the Iraqi Communist Party. His body was left hanging for hours. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] [cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] The case is widely cited as the event that convinced much of the community that no one, however powerful, was safe. [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] That is a judgement about how Iraqi Jews read the hanging, recorded by those who wrote their history; the charges themselves were never tested in any proceeding the sources describe.

Syria, Libya and Yemen. Anderson records that on 15 May 1948, the same day as the Cairo arrests, 250 Jews were imprisoned in Aleppo; that a "second wave of violence" struck the Jews of Tripoli, in Libya, on 14 June 1948; and that in December 1948 the ruler of Yemen permitted the country's Jews to leave. In what became known as "Magic Carpet," 45,009 were evacuated. [cite:rs_rd5_anderson_1951] The airlift itself belongs to the years after this chapter and is told in [scene:ye_s01|the Yemeni airlift].

What 1948 began. The events of 1947–48 were a beginning, not the whole of the story. Over the following decades the large Jewish communities of Iraq, Yemen, Libya, Egypt, Syria and North Africa shrank dramatically. Estimates of the total who left the Arab countries and Iran between 1948 and the 1970s commonly fall between 800,000 and 900,000 people, and the causes varied by country: emigration, pressure, expulsion and state persecution in different proportions. [cite:w_jewish_exodus_arab] Morocco shows the variety. Its Jewish community, rooted there for some two thousand years, numbered in the hundreds of thousands in the 1940s and largely emigrated after 1948, mostly to Israel; between 1961 and 1964 about 97,000 left in Operation Yachin, with payments to the Moroccan government that historians record. [cite:w_yachin] The later exodus from Iraq is told in [scene:irq_e05|the Iraqi exodus].

How to read this record. The war of 1948 is often described simply as a war between Israel and its neighbours, and Iraq's part in it as one army among several. The record of these months adds two things that description leaves out. Iraq's war had a domestic side, a law and a public hanging aimed at its own Jewish citizens, and it ended without the armistice every other Arab combatant signed. [cite:govil_aliyah] [cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] And the same pattern, in different degrees, appears in Egypt, Syria, Aden, Bahrain and Libya: riots on the day after the vote, the destruction of synagogues, killings, and proclamations and laws aimed at Jews by the governments of Egypt and Iraq. That was antisemitic violence and discrimination, and it should be called so. The record does not support treating it as the conduct of an entire people or faith. It names mobs in particular cities, particular governments issuing particular orders, the leaders of a particular institution, al-Azhar, declaring holy war, and, in Cairo, a particular organization whose members were charged in court.

What the record does not settle. The exact death tolls in Aden and in the Cairo bombings remain in dispute, as the figures above show. The penalty in Iraq's July 1948 law is given differently by the two sources. Much of the official record of 1948 reaches this book through a single compilation, Anderson's, made in 1951 for a debate about refugees, and the governments' own archives are not among the sources used here. The outcome of the 1950 trial in Cairo is not recorded in them. Nor do they give the number of Jews who left the Arab countries in 1948 itself, as distinct from the totals for the decades that followed. What is established is the pattern of the first thirteen months: violence by mobs, arrests and sequestration by governments, and a law in Iraq that put a whole community under suspicion.
