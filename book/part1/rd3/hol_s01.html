How Weimar Fell, 1929–1933

The German emigration that reached Palestine from 1933, and the Transfer Agreement that moved part of its capital there, began with the collapse of a democracy in Berlin. That collapse is often told as the work of an unstoppable wave of popular fanaticism. The documented record supports a narrower and more specific account: an economic catastrophe, a real but already declining surge of support for the Nazi Party, a decision taken by a small number of conservative politicians who believed they could control Hitler, and then a fire, an emergency decree, and a legislature that voted away its own authority. Each step can be dated, and at several of them people with power chose.

The first step was financial. The Weimar Republic's recovery in the mid-1920s had been built in large part on a steady flow of American loans. The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 led to the recall of those loans and plunged Germany back into economic catastrophe and mass unemployment [cite:ushmm_nazi_rise_to_power] [cite:history_weimar_republic]. The money ran in one direction and then reversed: American lenders supplied the capital that carried the stabilization, and when they withdrew it, the cost came due in Germany as mass unemployment, borne by a republic that had depended on that capital. The timing of Nazi support followed the money. According to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum's Holocaust Encyclopedia, genuine popular support for Hitler and his party began to accumulate from late 1929 onward, not before, while the economy still functioned [cite:ushmm_nazi_rise_to_power].

That connection has limits, and the record marks them. Economic hardship does not by itself explain Nazi support. Millions of Germans lived through the same conditions and did not become Nazis, and the more immediate causes of what followed were political decisions, not economic forces acting alone.

The second step is the one most often left out. The Nazi Party's electoral high point came in July 1932. By November 1932, three months before Hitler became Chancellor, its vote share had fallen to roughly 33 percent [cite:historycollection_weimar_collapse]. Hitler did not come to power at the crest of a rising wave. He came to power after the wave had begun to fall. That decline is the strongest evidence for the view that the Republic might have survived this particular crisis given more time. It is evidence, not proof. Historians treat survival as a real possibility; no one can show it would have happened.

The third step was a decision. Franz von Papen, an aristocrat and former chancellor, brokered the arrangement that made Hitler Chancellor. According to the accounts cited here, Papen assured his associates that he had Hitler's measure and could control him from within the cabinet, using him, in the phrase historians use, as a "battering ram" against the political left [cite:historycollection_weimar_collapse] [cite:britannica_third_reich]. Hitler was appointed Chancellor on 30 January 1933 [cite:history_weimar_republic]. This was not a popular revolution. A small circle of men who held real institutional power chose to hand him the office, on the calculation that they would remain in control. The record shows a catastrophic misjudgment about whether he could be controlled. It does not show that Papen or his circle intended, or foresaw, what came after.

The fourth step supplied the emergency. In February 1933 the Reichstag building burned, and the regime used the fire to push through the Reichstag Fire Decree, which suspended civil liberties and made possible the mass arrest of political opponents [cite:history_weimar_republic]. Who set the fire remains a question historians dispute. Its political use does not depend on the answer: whatever its origin, the fire provided the pretext for the decree, and the decree cleared the way for the next step weeks later.

The fifth step ended the Republic in law. In March 1933 Hitler introduced the Enabling Act, which allowed him to pass laws without the approval of Parliament or the President. To secure its passage he forcibly prevented the Communist members of Parliament from voting [cite:history_weimar_republic] [cite:britannica_third_reich]. With its enactment the Weimar Republic's twelve-year democratic experiment ended, not by a street coup but by a legislature voting away its own constitutional authority [cite:history_weimar_republic]. The mechanism is precise. The instrument was a statute passed through the legislature's own procedure; the members who could have opposed it were, in the Communists' case, physically kept from the vote, and the remaining members voted under the climate of intimidation that followed the February decree. The record does not show that every member who voted for the act did so freely.

Two readings of these years meet in the evidence. One emphasizes the depth of the economic collapse and the real popular support the Nazi Party won from late 1929; on that reading the Republic was gravely weakened from below, and the support was genuine. The other emphasizes the decline of November 1932 and the deal that followed; on that reading the decisive act was taken from above, by men who were not swept along by a mass movement but believed they could use one. The record supports both parts, in sequence. Popular support was real and was falling; the appointment was an elite choice; the dismantling of the constitution was carried out through law, under coercion. What it does not support is the familiar picture of a democracy overrun by a fanaticism no one could have stopped.

The record leaves several things open. It does not settle who set the Reichstag fire. It cannot show that the Republic would have survived had Hitler not been appointed when he was; the decline of late 1932 makes that possible, not certain. And it does not show what Papen and his allies expected beyond their belief that they could control the man they empowered. The regime installed by these steps is the one that appears, within months, as a party to [scene:hv_s01|the Transfer Agreement of 1933], and two years later as the author of [scene:hol_s02|the Nuremberg Laws of 1935].
