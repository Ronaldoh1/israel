Israel: Religious Courts and Personal Status

Israel has no civil marriage. That fact, more than any other, defines how religion and the state meet in the country's law. Its roots are older than the state. Under Ottoman rule, marriage, divorce and other matters of personal status belonged to the courts of each recognised religious community, the millet system. The British Mandate kept a modified version, recognising eleven communities (Sunni Islam, Orthodox Judaism and nine Christian denominations) and setting up the Orthodox Chief Rabbinate; marriages outside those communities were not recognised. In 1947 David Ben-Gurion and the religious parties agreed that personal status would stay with the religious authorities, the arrangement known as the "status quo". In 1953 the new state gave the rabbinical courts, under the Chief Rabbinate, jurisdiction over the marriage and divorce of all Jews in Israel. Muslims marry and divorce in sharia courts, members of ten recognised churches in church courts, and Druze, recognised as a separate community in 1957, in their own courts since 1962. [cite:us7_wiki_marriage] The deadlock over religion's place was also one reason the first Knesset chose to legislate Basic Laws one by one rather than adopt a constitution, as Part Two describes. [scene:us2_founding|Part Two: how the state is built]

Who cannot marry in Israel

Because each community's court applies its own religious law, a couple can marry in Israel only if both partners belong to the same recognised community and that community's law permits the match. Religious authorities do not marry couples of different religions or couples of the same sex. Jewish law as the Rabbinate applies it also bars some Jewish couples: a man of priestly descent (a kohen) may not marry a convert, and children born of adultery or incest face limits on whom they may marry. People whose Jewish status the Rabbinate doubts, often immigrants from the former Soviet Union or Ethiopia, must undergo Orthodox conversion first, and the Rabbinate does not accept non-Orthodox conversions, or some Orthodox ones, for marriage. [cite:us7_wiki_marriage] The Law of Return draws the line differently: since its 1970 amendment it grants immigration rights to the child and grandchild of a Jew and their spouses, so the state admits as citizens many people its marriage law treats as not Jewish. [cite:rs_ch01_lor_text] [cite:rs_ch01_joppke_rosenhek]

How many people this affects is an estimate, not an official count. Hiddush, an Israeli organisation that campaigns for religious freedom, put the number of citizens registered as "without religion" and unable to marry in Israel at 300,000 in 2013. In January 2016 it told a Knesset conference that about 660,000 Jewish Israelis could not legally marry in the country, including 364,000 immigrants from the former Soviet Union, 284,000 gay and lesbian Israelis and 13,000 non-Orthodox converts, using polls and Central Bureau of Statistics data. [cite:us7_toi_hiddushmap] [cite:us7_jta_660k] On its own world map of marriage freedom, Hiddush placed Israel among the 45 of 194 countries with "severe restrictions", the only democracy in that group. [cite:us7_toi_hiddushmap]

The routes around the Rabbinate

The state built exits rather than an alternative. In 1962 the Supreme Court ordered the Interior Ministry to register civil marriages performed abroad, even between Israeli citizens, at first for statistical purposes; a 2006 ruling extended recognition to the marriage's validity, and the same year the court voted 6–1 to register same-sex marriages performed abroad. [cite:us7_wiki_civmar] Cyprus became the usual destination. About 9,000 couples registered overseas marriages with the Central Bureau of Statistics in 2011, and Hiddush reported in 2016 that one in five weddings registered in Israel had taken place abroad. [cite:us7_wiki_civmar] [cite:us7_jta_660k] Couples also married over video link before officiants in Utah. A court in Lod recognised those marriages in September 2022, and the Supreme Court upheld that ruling against the Interior Ministry on 7 March 2023; Hiddush estimated that more than 3,000 couples with an Israeli partner had married this way by 2024. The Central Bureau of Statistics identified 9,200 overseas weddings registered in 2022. [cite:us7_wiki_civmar] Same-sex couples registered 1,151 foreign weddings between 2006 and 2019. [cite:us7_wiki_civmar] Inside Israel the only civil option is the 2010 Civil Union Law, which applies only when both partners are registered as having no religion. [cite:us7_wiki_marriage]

Divorce stays with the same courts. A Jewish divorce requires the husband to give a writ of divorce, the get, and a wife whose husband refuses cannot remarry under religious law. Rules adopted in 2016 allow criminal prosecution for refusal, but only after a rabbinical court has issued an order of a kind it rarely issues. There were 11,145 Jewish divorces in 2018. [cite:us7_wiki_marriage]

Conversion and burial

Who counts as Jewish for the state's purposes has been fought out in the courts. On 1 March 2021 a nine-justice panel ruled 8–1 that conversions performed in Israel by the Reform and Conservative movements must be recognised for the Law of Return, after the court had waited about fifteen years for the Knesset to legislate; the ultra-Orthodox parties Shas and United Torah Judaism condemned the ruling. [cite:us7_globes_conversion2021] The ruling concerned immigration and citizenship. It did not give those converts access to marriage through the Rabbinate. [cite:us7_wiki_marriage] Burial follows the same pattern of a religious default with a narrow exit. Local burial societies, 521 of them in 2016, operate under the Religious Services Ministry and bury Jewish Israelis at the National Insurance Institute's expense. A 1996 law gives every citizen the right to a secular burial on the same public funding, but by 2016 the state had established only a handful of civil cemeteries. [cite:us7_jn_burial]

One cross-country measure tracks the trend. The Pew Research Center's Government Restrictions Index scores each country from 0 to 10 on how far its government limits or controls religious activity, across many kinds of restriction, of which marriage law is only one. Israel scored 3.9 in the year to June 2007 and 6.7 in 2022, when a one-point rise moved it into Pew's "very high" band (6.6 and above).
