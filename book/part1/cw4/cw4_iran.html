Iran and Israel, 1957–1960: Oil, Intelligence and a Recognition Never Announced

Iran's relations with Israel before 1953, and the coup of August 1953 that restored the Shah's power, are told earlier in this volume, in [scene:s11|the coup of 1953]. By the late 1950s the relationship had become something neither government described in public. Iran sold Israel oil; its new intelligence service asked Israel's for training; Israeli agricultural experts worked in Iranian ministries; and Israel's mission in Tehran called itself a trade delegation. In July 1960 the Shah said in public, briefly, what all this implied, and Egypt broke off relations with Iran. This section follows that relationship from its beginnings in 1956 to the break, and sets out the two questions the sources still answer differently: when Iran recognized Israel, and why Egypt broke with Iran.

The record of these years shows two governments that dealt with each other closely and deliberately, for interests each could name, while keeping the extent of their dealings out of view. What divided them in public was not hostility but the cost of being seen together.

Oil first, 1956. The first thread was commercial. According to the historian Noa Schonmann, oil trade between Iran and Israel began quietly in late 1956, at Iran's initiative. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] It quickly became important to Israel. A US embassy telegram printed in the State Department's documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States, records that Israel depended on Iran for the oil for its Eilat pipeline, under a contract recently signed with the Rothschild group. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] Iran was the supplier on which the pipeline depended.

The intelligence services, 1957–1958. The second thread ran between the intelligence services. Iran's internal security service, SAVAK, was founded in 1957, with American and Israeli help, as told in [scene:ira_s00b|the 1954 consortium and SAVAK's founding]. Schonmann writes that in 1957 SAVAK's director, General Teymur Bakhtiar, met an Israeli representative in Paris, and that the Shah wanted the Mossad to train Iranian agents in surveillance and listening techniques. By November 1957 high-level visits and exchanges of liaison officers had followed. In late February 1958 the head of the Mossad, Isser Harel, visited Tehran. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2024] The initiative, in Schonmann's account, again came from the Iranian side: it was the Shah who wanted the training, and Israel that supplied it.

One further step is reported but less firmly documented. According to Paul Iddon, writing in The New Arab, the Shah in 1958 secretly arranged with Israel to set up an anti-Nasser broadcasting station at Ahvaz, in the province of Khuzestan. [cite:rs_cw4_newarab_iddon] That claim rests on a single later account and has not been corroborated in the other sources used here. If it is accurate, it shows the two governments acting together against a common rival, Nasser, and not only exchanging oil and training. These ties ran alongside, and fed into, the wider Israeli effort of 1957–1958 to build alliances on the region's edges, told in [scene:cw4_periphery|the periphery doctrine].

A trade delegation. The form of Israel's presence in Iran mattered as much as its substance. Yossi Alpher, a former Israeli intelligence officer, has said that Israel's presence in Tehran was formally a trade delegation, and that it was kept at a low level so as not to offend Iran's Arab partners. [cite:rs_cw4_alpher_brookings] That is a participant's later account rather than a document of the time, but it fits the rest of the record: the relationship was real, and both governments kept its official face small.

Bakhtiar's complaint. What Iran wanted from these ties is visible in a conversation Ben-Gurion reported to the American embassy. The telegram printed in Foreign Relations records his account of a secret talk, around 27 July, with Bakhtiar, whom Ben-Gurion called the "No. 2 man Iran who had ear of Shah." Bakhtiar told him that Iran felt "let down" by the United States, because Turkey and Pakistan received far more American arms, and asked whether Iran "should … turn to Russia." Ben-Gurion replied that Iran had no alternative to close alliance with the West. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] The exchange shows an Israeli prime minister arguing the West's case to the head of an Iranian service, and doing so in a conversation he then reported to Washington.

The same telegram notes that several Israeli cultural and agricultural experts had visited Iran at Iran's request, and that one Israeli was serving as a consultant to Iran's Ministry of Agriculture. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] The relationship, in other words, was not confined to oil and intelligence. It extended into Iran's civil administration, again at Iran's invitation.

The date of this telegram is uncertain, and the uncertainty matters. Its file number reads as August 1959. But the editors' note attached to it describes the Shah's statement of July 1960, discussed below. The talk with Bakhtiar "around 27 July" may therefore belong to 1959 or to 1960, and the sources researched for this book do not settle which. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] If it was 1960, Bakhtiar's complaint came in the same week as the Shah's public statement on Israel; if 1959, a year earlier.

The Shah's statement, July 1960. In July 1960 the Shah was asked at a press conference whether Iran would recognize Israel. According to Shahrough Akhavi, writing in the Encyclopaedia Iranica, he said that Iran "had recognized Israel years ago." [cite:rs_cw4_iranica_akhavi] The FRUS editors give the fuller version: on 24 July the Shah announced that Iran's de facto recognition of Israel had existed since 1949, although Iran's envoy had been recalled from Tel Aviv in 1951, and that de jure recognition had never been in question. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] The distinction between the two terms carried the weight of the statement. De facto recognition acknowledges that a government exists and deals with it; de jure recognition is the formal, legal act. The editors' phrase that de jure recognition "had never been in question" can be read as meaning that formal recognition was not on offer; their summary does not say more, and the Shah's own words beyond it are not in the sources used here.

The sources disagree on when that de facto recognition began. The Shah, as the FRUS editors summarize his statement, put it in 1949. The New Arab gives 1950, and Akhavi records that the Shah denied any change in Iran's dealings with Israel "since 1950." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] [cite:rs_cw4_newarab_iddon] [cite:rs_cw4_iranica_akhavi] The difference may lie in what each source counts as the act of recognition, but none of them explains it, and the year cannot be fixed from these sources alone.

The break with Egypt. The reaction from Cairo was immediate. According to Akhavi, Nasser attacked the Shah's "pro-Zionist" policies and broke diplomatic relations with Iran. [cite:rs_cw4_iranica_akhavi] The New Arab reports that Nasser denounced the Shah for "selling himself cheaply to imperialism and Zionism," and that Iran's Foreign Minister, Abbas Aram, answered by calling Nasser a "feeble-minded pharaoh of Egypt." [cite:rs_cw4_newarab_iddon] A religious voice was added to the political one: Akhavi records a telegram to the Shah from Shaltut, saying that the recognition had hurt Egyptian sentiment and the feelings of the 'ulama of al-Azhar. [cite:rs_cw4_iranica_akhavi] The FRUS editors describe the rupture from the other end: after Arab and especially Nasser's criticism, Iran declared the representative of the United Arab Republic in Tehran persona non grata. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] The two accounts are not incompatible, since each government took a step against the other, but the sources do not establish which came first.

Why Egypt broke with Iran. The obvious answer is the one Nasser gave: the Shah's statement on Israel. The FRUS editors and The New Arab both connect the rupture to it directly, and the sequence of statement, denunciation and break supports them. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58v12_d273] [cite:rs_cw4_newarab_iddon] Akhavi offers a different reading. He suggests that the break may have owed more to Iran's growing ties with the Arab states of the Gulf than to Israel. [cite:rs_cw4_iranica_akhavi] On that view, Israel was the occasion rather than the cause: the Shah's remark gave Nasser a ground for a rupture whose stakes lay in Iran's relations with the Gulf states. Akhavi presents it as a suggestion, not a demonstration, and the sources used here do not contain the evidence that would decide between the two readings. What both accept is that the Shah's statement told the public nothing that the Iranian and Israeli governments had not already been acting on for some years.

The interests on each side can be stated plainly from these sources. Iran sold oil to a buyer that needed it, obtained intelligence training its ruler wanted, brought in agricultural expertise, and, if the Ahvaz report is accurate, gained a partner against Nasser, while keeping relations formally at a level its Arab partners could accept. Israel gained the crude that made the Eilat pipeline work, a working relationship with Iran's government and its security service, and a listener in Tehran whom Ben-Gurion could urge to stay with the West. Each government was acting for its own reasons, and the record shows neither acting as the other's instrument.

The record leaves several points open. The year of the Bakhtiar conversation, 1959 or 1960, cannot be fixed from the telegram as printed. The year of Iran's de facto recognition is given as 1949 and as 1950. The Ahvaz broadcasting station rests on one report. Whether Israel or the Gulf weighed more in Nasser's decision is a matter of interpretation on which the sources divide. And the content of the oil contract with the Rothschild group, its volumes and its price, is not given in the material researched here. Iran's Jewish community, which forms part of this relationship's background, is discussed elsewhere in this library.
