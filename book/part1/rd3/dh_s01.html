Jacob de Haan and the First Political Killing

A common account of the years before 1948 holds that Jewish opposition to Zionism was marginal and peaceful, and that it simply faded away. The case of Jacob Israël de Haan does not fit that account. His life in Jerusalem shows that organized Jewish dissent from Zionism existed inside Palestine, that it was carried to the British and to Arab leaders, and that on at least one occasion it was answered with gunfire.

De Haan was born in the Netherlands in 1881. A poet and a lawyer, he came to Palestine in 1919 as a newspaper correspondent and taught at the government law school in Jerusalem, the Mandate's law classes [cite:w_dehaan]. He arrived sympathetic to Zionism. He later moved to the other side of the argument and became the political spokesman of the Haredi community, and he became its most effective political voice in English, writing for British newspapers and lobbying Mandate officials [cite:w_dehaan].

The community he spoke for was the "old Yishuv," the Orthodox Jewish population of Jerusalem, Safed, Hebron and Tiberias, which had lived in the country for generations before political Zionism appeared. Its leadership, including Rabbi Yosef Chaim Sonnenfeld, rejected a secular Jewish state on religious grounds and objected to the Zionist Organization speaking in its name. Agudat Israel, founded in 1912, gave that objection an organized form [cite:w_dehaan]. De Haan's case to the British was precise rather than sweeping. The Zionist Organization, he argued, was one political movement among Jews, not the representative of all Jews in Palestine. For a community whose presence in Jerusalem long predated the Zionist movement, that distinction was the whole point [cite:w_dehaan].

His boldest step was diplomatic. De Haan sought a separate understanding between Orthodox Jews and Arab leaders, on the premise that Jews who did not seek a state need not be in conflict with the Arab majority. In 1924 he met the Hashemite leadership in Transjordan. He was preparing to travel to London with an anti-Zionist Orthodox delegation when he was killed [cite:w_dehaan].

On 30 June 1924, as he left evening prayers at a synagogue on Jaffa Road in Jerusalem, de Haan was shot three times. He died soon afterward. No one was charged. The Zionist leadership publicly condemned the killing; Orthodox leaders blamed Zionist circles. For sixty years the question of who had ordered it remained formally open [cite:w_dehaan].

The answer, or part of it, came in 1985. Avraham Tehomi, by then elderly, a former member of the Haganah who had later co-founded the Irgun, said on Israeli television that he had shot de Haan on the Haganah's orders. He named the source of the order as Yitzhak Ben-Zvi, who later became Israel's second president and who had died in 1963 [cite:w_dehaan]. That last claim must be handled with care. Ben-Zvi's role rests on Tehomi's account and is disputed by some historians. What stands on firmer ground is the confession itself: the man who fired the shots said the killing was a Haganah operation [cite:w_dehaan]. Historians widely describe it as the first political assassination in the Jewish community of Palestine.

The episode establishes three things. Organized Jewish opposition to Zionism existed inside the country. It was argued openly, to British officials and to Arab leaders. And, by its gunman's own admission, the movement's armed wing killed its most effective spokesman. What the record does not establish is a single, settled chain of command above the gunman. The question of who gave the order, beyond Tehomi's word, remains open.

The killing also marks a point in the wider story this book follows. Tehomi himself would help found the Irgun, one of the rival armed organizations traced later in this stage in [scene:s07e2|the paramilitary line]. Before that, the first political assassination in the Jewish community of Palestine had already taken place, and its target was a Jew who argued that Zionism did not speak for all Jews in the country.
