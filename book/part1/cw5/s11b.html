The Battle of the Letters, March–June 1963

Between the end of March and the middle of June 1963, the President of the United States and the Prime Minister of Israel conducted, in writing, the sharpest argument the two governments had yet had. Its subject was whether American scientists would be allowed to visit the reactor at Dimona on a regular schedule. The letters, telegrams and memoranda of that argument are now declassified and published, most of them by the National Security Archive. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963] [cite:avner_cohen_wsj_2009] They answer a common assumption directly. It is often said or supposed that American support for Israel's nuclear programme was unconditional and uncontested from the start. The documents show a sitting president spending much of 1963 in an escalating confrontation with Israel's government over exactly that programme, and writing, in his own letters, that American support could be put at risk by it.

The exchange also shows something the assumption misses on the other side. Ben-Gurion did not answer Kennedy's question. He answered a different one, about Egypt and the survival of Israel, and he did so at length and in the strongest terms. The argument was therefore never only about inspections. It was about what each government thought the other owed it.

The background. The reactor had been built with substantial French assistance after the Suez war of 1956, and Ben-Gurion had acknowledged its existence to the Knesset in December 1960 in terms that presented it as serving peaceful research and played down its weapons-relevant capacity. [cite:wilsoncenter_kennedy_dimona_61_62] How and when Washington first learned of the site, and Ben-Gurion's statement itself, are told with their disputed dates in [scene:cw4_dimona_discovery|how Washington found Dimona]; that section's dating governs here where the shorter accounts differ from it. The spring of 1963 opened with a new American estimate, NIE 30-63 of 23 January, which judged that the reactor at full power could produce plutonium for one or two weapons a year, and with a presidential directive, NSAM 231 of 26 March, ordering that "the next informal inspection" be arranged promptly. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Those documents are the subject of the previous section. The letters began the next day.

Telegram 658 and the meeting of 2 April. On 27 March 1963 the State Department's telegram 658 instructed the ambassador in Tel Aviv, Barbour, to seek Israel's assent to "semi-annual" visits to Dimona. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Barbour raised the matter with Ben-Gurion on 2 April 1963, at the end of a meeting that had run two hours. Ben-Gurion asked to put the subject off until after Passover. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That request for delay was the first of several. The American demand, from this point, was specific: not a single visit but a standing schedule, twice a year.

Peres at the White House, the same day. On the same 2 April, Shimon Peres was in Washington, where Myer Feldman had arranged for him to see Kennedy. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] What Peres promised exists in two records, and they differ in a way that matters.

The Israeli record has Peres saying: "we will not introduce nuclear weapons to the region, and certainly we will not be the first." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Feldman's American record of the same meeting has Peres giving "an unequivocal assurance" that Israel "would not do anything in this field" unless others in the area did. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The editors of the National Security Archive's collection draw the distinction plainly: "'Not do anything' has a different meaning than 'not introduce.'" [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] A promise not to "introduce" weapons leaves room to develop the capacity to make them; a promise not to "do anything in this field" does not. Which words Peres used, or whether each side heard what it wanted, the two records cannot settle. Each is a record made by one party to the meeting. Feldman's broader version is the one Kennedy's staff had in front of them in April 1963.

The démarche, 4 April. Two days later the pressure moved to Washington. On 4 April 1963 Assistant Secretary of State Phillips Talbot delivered a démarche to Ambassador Harman of Israel. On 19 April telegram 720 asked Barbour to press Ben-Gurion for "an affirmative reply." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] By then the region had changed under both governments' feet.

The Arab Federation, 17 April. On 17 April 1963 Egypt, Syria and Iraq, the latter two newly under Ba'athist rule after the coups of February and March told earlier in this stage, announced in Cairo an agreement for a federal union. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] The Arab Federation Proclamation of that day called for a military union to bring about "the liberation of Palestine." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] For Ben-Gurion the proclamation was the issue. Nine days later he wrote to Kennedy.

Ben-Gurion's letter of 26 April. The letter of 26 April 1963 did not answer the request about Dimona. It took up the Arab Federation, and it proposed that the United States and the Soviet Union jointly guarantee the territorial integrity of the states of the region. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The proposal reframed the problem. Washington had asked about a reactor; Ben-Gurion replied that the danger to peace in the region was a declared intention, by three Arab governments, to unite their armies against Israel, and that the remedy was a great-power guarantee of borders.

Kennedy's reply, 4 May. Kennedy answered on 4 May 1963, in telegram 780; Ben-Gurion afterwards referred to it as the letter of 5 May. Kennedy said he was "much less worried about an 'early Arab attack'" than about the spread of advanced offensive weapons systems in the region. He was puzzled by the idea of a joint declaration with Moscow, because it would add to Soviet prestige. And he proposed that the two governments "consider carefully together how such a trend can be forestalled." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The reply turned Ben-Gurion's argument back toward the question Washington had asked. If the danger was an arms race, then Dimona was part of the arms race.

Ben-Gurion's letter of 12 May. Ben-Gurion's answer, dated 12 May 1963, ran to nine pages. It is the fullest statement in the exchange of Israel's case, and it should be read in its own terms. Ben-Gurion invoked "another Holocaust": "I have no doubt," he wrote, "that a similar thing might happen to Jews in Israel if Nasser succeeded in defeating our army." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] He asked for four things: a security agreement between the United States and Israel; parity in arms with Israel's neighbours; a demilitarized West Bank; and disarmament with inspection. The letter did not address Dimona. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The case behind the letter was a coherent one, and the record supports its premises. Three Arab governments had, the month before, proclaimed a military union for "the liberation of Palestine." Ben-Gurion's letter drew the line from the destruction of Europe's Jews to Israel's position in 1963; he read the proclamation as a statement of intent and asked the one power that might deter it to commit itself in writing. His proposals were not only requests for American protection; they included a demilitarized West Bank and disarmament under inspection, terms that would have bound Israel's neighbours and, in principle, Israel. In this reading, the reactor was not a separate subject from the Arab Federation; it was a reason for Israel to want a guarantee, and a guarantee was the price at which the reactor might be discussed.

The American reading of the same letter was different. On this view Ben-Gurion was redirecting the exchange toward conventional threats, which both he and later Eshkol described as their people's major anxiety, rather than refusing inspections outright. [cite:timesofisrael_kennedy_eshkol] [cite:jvl_dimona_inspections_memo_aug63] The historian Avner Cohen and the journalist Seymour Hersh, in The Samson Option (1991), both document this pattern of delay in detail. [cite:timesofisrael_kennedy_eshkol] [cite:jvl_dimona_inspections_memo_aug63] The two readings describe the same letter. What one side saw as a statement of Israel's real priorities, the other saw as a way of not answering.

The guarantee Washington would not give. Inside the administration, Ben-Gurion's requests were weighed seriously. On 16 May 1963 Robert Komer of the National Security Council staff called a State Department proposal a "good opening bid": security assurances to Israel tied to a standstill on nuclear weapons and missiles. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The idea was a trade, assurances for restraint. It did not become policy. Kennedy had already refused Ben-Gurion's April idea of a joint American–Soviet guarantee, an American official named Grant advised that a unilateral American guarantee "should not be considered," and through the summer of 1963 Kennedy remained "unwilling to make 'explicit' guarantees" of Israel's security. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The commitment he had given Golda Meir at Palm Beach in December 1962, to support Israel "in case of an invasion," stayed a spoken assurance. It was not turned into a document. Kennedy's own condition at Palm Beach, that the relationship could not damage American ties with the other states of the region, points to one reason. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

Kennedy's letter of 17 May. Kennedy's next letter carries two dates in the record. Telegram 835 dates it 17 May 1963; another document gives 19 May; some published summaries give 18 May. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963] Whatever its day, it changed the terms of the argument. It pressed for the visits "first put to you by Ambassador Barbour on April 2." [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963] It separated Israel's existential security, which Kennedy was prepared to discuss, from Dimona, which he was not prepared to set aside. And it warned, for the first time, that American "commitment to and support of Israel could be seriously jeopardized" without "reliable information" on Dimona. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The editors note that this language "remained in all subsequent letters." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

That sentence is the core of the exchange. Kennedy did not say that aid would be cut, or name any sanction. He said that the American commitment itself "could be seriously jeopardized," and he tied that risk to the one thing he wanted: reliable information. The letter thus did what Ben-Gurion's letters had tried to prevent. It refused to treat a guarantee and an inspection as items in one bargain, and it made the inspection a condition of the relationship rather than a concession to be traded for one.

Ben-Gurion's reply of 27 May. Ben-Gurion answered on 27 May 1963. He asked for delay. There would be nothing to see at Dimona, he wrote, until the reactor started up in early 1964, and he did not take up the request for visits twice a year. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] One secondary account describes the same reply as proposing alternative, more limited inspection arrangements than Kennedy had asked for, and quotes the historian Sandler, as cited in several secondary sources, to the effect that Kennedy was "far from satisfied," "particularly his attempt to stall any inspection in Dimona." [cite:timesofisrael_kennedy_eshkol] The declassified text, as the National Security Archive's editors summarize it, is the firmer guide: a request for delay and silence on the schedule. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The schedule, and the letter that was never delivered. Washington answered with a timetable. A State Department draft of 11 June 1963, prepared by Brubeck, proposed a visit in mid-summer 1963, another in June 1964, and visits at six-month intervals after that. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Kennedy's letter of 15 June 1963, sent as telegram 938, set out that schedule. It was never delivered to Ben-Gurion. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Before it could be handed over, Ben-Gurion announced his resignation, the subject of the next section, which also weighs the claim that the letters helped bring it about.

The exchange did not end with Ben-Gurion. On 4 July 1963 Kennedy sent the new Prime Minister, Levi Eshkol, a telegram repeating the undelivered text, including the sentence that had first appeared in May: "this government's commitment to and support of Israel could be seriously jeopardized if it should be thought that we were unable to obtain reliable information on a subject as vital to peace as Israel's effort in the nuclear field." [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963] Eshkol's own reply of 19 August 1963 has been read as continuing his predecessor's approach, which would suggest an institutional policy rather than one leader's style. [cite:timesofisrael_kennedy_eshkol] [cite:jvl_dimona_inspections_memo_aug63] What Eshkol actually agreed, and whether it met Kennedy's demand, is told in [scene:cw5_eshkol_agreement|Eshkol's answer].

An ultimatum, or not? Historians and participants have disagreed over how to describe Kennedy's letters, and both sides have a case. The editors of the National Security Archive's collection call the exchange "the toughest American-Israeli confrontation over the Israeli nuclear program," with Kennedy writing "in a near ultimatum tone," "akin to an ultimatum." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Some Israelis at the time went further. Yuval Ne'eman (1925–2006) called the demands an "ultimatum," and some of Eshkol's advisers read the July letter the same way. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] On this view, a letter from the President telling a small state that his support "could be seriously jeopardized" unless it accepted inspections was an ultimatum in everything but name, and was understood as serious pressure by both governments. [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963]

The case on the other side rests on the text. The letters named no sanction. They did not threaten to cut aid or to break off relations. They said that support "could be" jeopardized, not that it would be. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:nsarchive_battle_letters_1963] By that standard "near ultimatum," the editors' own phrase, is accurate, and "ultimatum" overstates the documented words. Kennedy was, in the same months, arming Israel and refusing it a guarantee; the letters linked support to inspection without spelling out what would happen if inspection were refused.

Did Kennedy threaten to cut aid? On the documents, no. He threatened the relationship in general terms and left the consequences unstated. What did Israel promise? In April, through Peres, either not to "introduce" nuclear weapons or not to "do anything in this field," depending on whose record one reads; in May, through Ben-Gurion, nothing on Dimona beyond delay. Why did Ben-Gurion answer about Nasser instead of Dimona? Because in his reading, stated at length in the letters, the Arab Federation was the threat to peace and a guarantee was the prior question. The American reading was that this was a way of not answering. Both readings are on the record.

What came after. The inspections that the letters were fought over began only after Kennedy's death. The Johnson administration kept insisting on the visits begun under Kennedy. [cite:rs_cw5_frus6468_summary] What those visits actually found, including a 2020 release of declassified records from 1964–65 that its editors titled "Duplicity and Self-Deception," is told in [scene:s11d|Johnson's inspections]. [cite:nsarchive_duplicity_1964_65]

The record leaves some questions open. The exact date of Kennedy's May letter is given three ways in the sources, as 17, 18 and 19 May. The two records of Peres's assurance cannot be reconciled from the documents, and nothing here shows which formula Kennedy himself took away from the meeting. The letters establish what each leader asked and refused; they do not establish what Israel's government had decided about the reactor's purpose in the spring of 1963, and none of Ben-Gurion's replies confirms or denies a weapons intention. Nor do they show what Kennedy would have done had Ben-Gurion refused outright, because he never did. He delayed, and then he resigned.
