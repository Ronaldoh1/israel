From Bonn to Washington: The First Direct Tank Sale, February–July 1965

In the first half of 1965 the United States did what it had refused to do a year earlier: it agreed to sell tanks to Israel directly. The decision came in two steps, a secret memorandum of understanding transmitted from the American embassy in Israel on 10 March 1965 and a closing deal reported to President Johnson on 29 July. Neither step began with Israel. It began with Jordan, and with the collapse of the German route described in [scene:cw6_germany|the previous section].

What Israel had asked for. Israel's request had been on the table since late 1963. In a meeting recorded by Komer, which refers back to Prime Minister Eshkol's letter of 4 November 1963 and a meeting of 14 November, Ambassador Harman, Minister Gazit and the military attaché Col. Ram Ron asked Bundy and Komer for surface-to-surface missiles, modern tanks and an answer to the growing naval strength of the United Arab Republic, with tanks the immediate priority. The tank request was specific: replace about 300 Shermans with 200 M48A3s and 100 M60s, then obtain about 200 more M60s over two to three years. The memo records Israel's defense spending at about 40 percent of its regular budget and 11 percent of its GNP. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d7] After an interagency review in early 1964 Washington declined to sell tanks directly, because of its policy of not becoming a major arms supplier to the region, and agreed instead to help Israel get them in Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] Johnson repeated the refusal to Eshkol in June 1964, saying the United States "could not provide tanks directly"; Eshkol asked him, "please do not push us into the Centurions." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d65] Those exchanges belong to [scene:cw6_johnson_eshkol|Eshkol's visit]; the point here is the baseline. In mid-1964 American tanks for Israel were to come, if at all, by way of Germany, refitted in Italy with American kits and kept secret.

That route failed in stages. After the deliveries became public in the autumn of 1964 and Nasser invited East Germany's head of state to Cairo, Bonn halted its arms deliveries to Israel; only 40 of the German tanks had arrived. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965]

The Jordan problem. At the same moment Washington faced a request from Amman. The State Department's historians record that King Hussein's 1964 request for American arms came under pressure from the Unified Arab Command, which an Arab summit had created. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_plo] In February 1965 the administration concluded that a tank sale to Jordan was necessary, because Hussein warned that he would otherwise be pressed to accept Soviet aircraft; at the same time it tried to talk him out of supersonic jets. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] The reasoning was the Cold War's: if Jordan did not get Western tanks it might get Soviet planes. But a sale to an Arab neighbour with no matching sale to Israel was what Eshkol objected to. According to the editors, his strongly negative reaction convinced policymakers that a direct sale to Israel would be needed. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] The decision to arm Jordan made the decision to arm Israel directly.

The Harriman–Komer mission and the conditions Israel refused. During the mission of Harriman and Komer, Washington tried to attach conditions to future arms: nuclear inspections, a pledge on the waters of the Jordan River, and referral to the United Nations. According to the editors, Israel resisted them, and Johnson then offered a package. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] The three conditions went to the three things Washington most wanted from Israel in these years: access to Dimona, restraint in the water conflict with its neighbours (told in [scene:cw6_water|the water war]), and the use of the UN rather than force. None of them appears among the clauses of the text that followed.

The memorandum of 10 March 1965. The agreement that resulted was transmitted to Washington by the American ambassador, Barbour, on 10 March 1965. Read clause by clause, it did six things. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185]

First, the United States reaffirmed its concern for Israel's security and integrity. Second, Israel reaffirmed that it "will not be the first to introduce nuclear weapons into the Arab-Israel area." Third, the United States undertook to sell Israel "at least the same number and quality of tanks that it sells to Jordan." Fourth, it undertook to complete the deliveries of the German M48s. Fifth, it undertook to help Israel obtain "a certain number of combat aircraft, if not from Western sources, then from the United States." Sixth, both governments agreed to full secrecy. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185]

The State Department editors summarize the aircraft clause as covering up to 24 combat aircraft, and they date Israel's nuclear reaffirmation to 11 March 1965, a day after the telegram. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t] The Jewish Virtual Library, citing the Israel State Archives, presents the memorandum as the first American agreement to sell tanks to Israel directly, and says it implied that Israel would not oppose American arms sales to Jordan. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_mou_1965] That last reading is an inference from the text, not a clause in it, and it is reported here as the Library gives it.

The structure of the memorandum is its meaning. The tank clause tied Israel's supply to Jordan's: whatever Washington sold Amman, it would match for Israel in number and quality. The aircraft clause kept Europe as the first supplier and the United States as the fallback. The nuclear clause gave Washington a written Israeli pledge, but not the inspections it had asked for. And the secrecy clause continued the logic of the German deal. The United States had not wanted to be seen as Israel's arms supplier; now it would be one, but not yet in public.

Komer closes the deal, 29 July 1965. On 29 July 1965 Komer reported to Johnson under the heading "U.S. Closes Deal on Tanks for Israel, Moves On to Planes." The United States would sell Israel 110 M48A2C tanks to replace the German ones that had not been delivered, and 100 M48A1 tanks to match those sold to Jordan, for around $42 million spread over two years or more, together with conversion kits to fit 105 mm guns on the 40 tanks Bonn had already delivered. [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] Both halves of the March memorandum's tank clauses were now filled: the German shortfall and the Jordanian match.

The planes were another matter. Komer added that Rusk was "strong against any US sale" of the 24 aircraft, "so we'll stall for a while and try to shift them to Europe." [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] The Secretary of State and the White House staff were not of one mind. The aircraft question was settled only in February 1966, with the Skyhawk sale told in the next part of this stage.

Where 1965 sits in the longer record. The aid dashboard's timeline sets this deal against the longer arc of American support.

From 1948 to the early 1960s, American assistance to Israel was economic aid and loans only; Washington avoided selling arms for fear of alienating Arab oil-producing states. The first American military aid came in 1962, and it came as loans rather than grants. [cite:merip_us_aid_israel_1990] The same year saw the sale of Hawk anti-aircraft missiles, told in Stage Four and Stage Five. [cite:wiki_israel_us_military_relations] The financing terms of that era recur in this section: the German tank kits of 1964 were sold on credit of ten years at 3.5 percent with 10 percent down, and in February 1966 McNamara would describe the same terms, 10 percent down, 3.5 percent interest and ten years, as the Hawk terms. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_t]

What 1965 changed was the kind of weapon and the channel. A defensive missile system had been sold in 1962; in 1965 the United States agreed to sell tanks, and to do so directly rather than through a European intermediary. It also wrote into the agreement a rule linking Israel's supply to an Arab state's: "at least the same number and quality of tanks that it sells to Jordan." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185] The money involved was modest beside later decades, around $42 million for 210 tanks over two years or more, and Komer's report does not say on what credit terms it was to be paid. [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] The familiar idea that American arms for Israel began with the war of 1967 does not survive these documents; nor does the opposite idea, that Washington simply supplied what Israel asked. The tanks came because Jordan was being armed, on terms Israel negotiated against conditions it refused, and the planes Israel wanted were held back for another half year by the Secretary of State.

The nuclear words. The memorandum's nuclear clause is the most quoted line in it, and the American record of 1965–66 does not use it consistently. In March 1965 the text said Israel "will not be the first to introduce nuclear weapons into the Arab-Israel area." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d185] In February 1966, when McNamara set out conditions for the aircraft sale, the pledge he asked Eban to reaffirm was "not to be the first power in the Middle East to manufacture nuclear weapons." [cite:rs_cw6_hoopes_t] Later the same month Komer reported Israel's undertaking as a pledge not to go nuclear "unless others do." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d273_t] On their face the three formulas differ: the first speaks of introducing weapons into an area, the second of manufacturing them, the third makes the pledge conditional on what others do. The documents do not show whether the two governments understood these as one commitment phrased three ways or as three different commitments. What the record does show is that the first of these written assurances was obtained in the same weeks in which Israel refused Washington's condition of nuclear inspections during the Harriman–Komer talks. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_t]

What the record leaves open. Several things are not settled by the documents used here. The delivery dates of the tanks sold in July 1965 are not in them; Komer's report gives the deal, not the schedule. [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965_t] Nor do they say how many tanks Jordan actually received or what Jordan paid, beyond the fact that Israel's 100 M48A1s were meant to match them. The about 250 British Centurions mentioned in Rusk's October 1964 memorandum do not reappear in the 1965 record fetched for this book. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] And the reading that the memorandum bound Israel not to oppose arms for Jordan rests on one account citing the Israel State Archives, not on a clause in the American text. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_mou_1965] What is settled is that by the summer of 1965 Washington had agreed, in writing and in secret, to be Israel's direct supplier of tanks, two years before the war that is often said to have begun the relationship.
