The Computers That Left the House

Palm Beach police searched Epstein's house on 20 October 2005. About twelve days earlier, according to a 2005 memo from one private investigator to Epstein's lawyer Roy Black, another investigator, Paul Lavery, removed more than a hundred items at Black's direction. [cite:ep15_jkb_computers2026] [cite:ep15_abc_evidence2026] The memo lists three computers, 29 bound telephone directories, a three-page list of local masseuses, photographs of nude or partially nude women, more than forty videotapes and books, cash, and Epstein's concealed-carry permit. [cite:ep15_abc_evidence2026]

Lavery told federal agents he handed everything to the other investigator, William Riley, who managed storage units for Epstein, and never saw it again. Riley was subpoenaed and did not produce the material; Epstein's lawyers argued it was irrelevant and privileged, and later cited the non-prosecution agreement when victims' lawyers asked for it. Emails show Epstein still paying for a storage unit in 2010. The Justice Department's own professional-responsibility review in 2020 said the computers likely held "potentially critical" evidence. At Maxwell's trial an FBI analyst suggested a copy of at least one computer had been recovered; the originals and the papers have never been found. [cite:ep15_abc_evidence2026]

The federal prosecutor on the case, Marie Villafaña, has said her supervisors prevented her from obtaining the evidence. [cite:ep15_jkb_computers2026] Black died in 2025; Lavery and Riley did not respond to ABC News. [cite:ep15_abc_evidence2026] This is the clearest documented instance in the case of evidence leaving the reach of the police before a search, and it has drawn a fraction of the attention given to the flight logs. The document that would close it is the chain of custody: where the storage units were, who paid for them after 2010, and what happened to their contents.
