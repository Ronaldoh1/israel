Both Sides Were Heard: The Anglo-American Committee of 1946

In the winter of 1946, a year before the partition vote, a committee of twelve Britons and Americans set out to hear everyone with a stake in Palestine. They listened to Zionist leaders, Arab spokesmen and Jewish advocates of a single shared state. Then they issued a report that rejected both a Jewish and an Arab state. The familiar claim that no international body heard the Arab case, or the case for a shared state, does not survive this record. What happened to the report is as revealing as what it said.

The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry was announced in November 1945. It had twelve members, six Americans and six Britons. It was created as Washington pressed London to admit Jewish displaced persons, the uprooted survivors of the war in Europe, into Palestine. Its American members included Bartley Crum and James McDonald; its British members included Richard Crossman. [cite:w_aac] Between January and March 1946 the committee held hearings in Washington, London, the displaced-persons camps of Europe, Cairo and Jerusalem. [cite:w_aac] It took evidence from governments, from the Jewish Agency, from Arab states and the Arab Office, from Christian and Jewish organizations, and from refugees in the camps. No fuller hearing of the competing cases took place before 1947. [cite:w_aac]

The range of witnesses mattered. The committee heard the leaders of the Jewish Agency, the body that spoke for the Jewish community in Palestine. It heard Arab representatives, including the Arab Office, where the historian Albert Hourani gave notable testimony. And it heard Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, and the Ihud association. They argued that Arabs and Jews should share one state with political parity. [cite:w_aac] Their testimony is documented proof that a Jewish alternative to partition was put before an official body, in person.

In April 1946 the committee reported, unanimously. It recommended the immediate issue of 100,000 immigration certificates for Jewish refugees and the repeal of the 1940 land-transfer regulations. It also recommended continuing the Mandate, Britain's authority to govern Palestine, toward a United Nations trusteeship. And it stated that Palestine should be neither a Jewish state nor an Arab state, with neither people dominating the other. [cite:avalon_aac] The recommendations were a package. On one side stood relief for the refugees; on the other, a constitutional settlement that denied either community a state of its own.

Washington did not take the package. President Truman publicly endorsed the figure of 100,000, which was popular with American voters. He said nothing about the recommendation against a Jewish state. Prime Minister Attlee made any admissions conditional on the disarmament of the Jewish and Arab armed groups. [cite:w_aac] Neither government adopted the report as a whole.

A follow-up attempt fared no better. In July 1946 an Anglo-American plan for provincial autonomy, known as the Morrison–Grady plan, was rejected by Zionist and Arab leaders alike, and Truman declined to back it. [cite:w_morrisongrady] The question went to the United Nations the following year. [cite:w_morrisongrady]

So the Arab case and the binational case were not ignored. Both, and a third option besides, were heard in detail and on the record. What the episode shows instead is selective use. The part of the report with political value in the United States survived into American policy. The part that would have ruled out a Jewish state did not. Three months after the Morrison–Grady plan failed, Truman's Yom Kippur statement of October 1946 said the United States could support a viable Jewish state "in an adequate area of Palestine," as described in [scene:wh_s01|the White House chapter].
