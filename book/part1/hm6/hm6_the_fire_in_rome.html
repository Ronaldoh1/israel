The Fire in the Campo de' Fiori

The philosopher Giordano Bruno was denounced to the Venetian Inquisition in May 1592 by his host, Giovanni Mocenigo, who was disappointed by Bruno's private lessons in the art of memory and resented his plan to publish in Frankfurt. [cite:hm_brit_bruno] On 27 January 1593 Bruno entered the jail of the Holy Office in Rome. For seven years he argued that his views were philosophical rather than theological; the inquisitors demanded an unconditional retraction. Bruno said he had nothing to retract and did not know what he was expected to retract. [cite:hm_brit_bruno] Pope Clement VIII ordered that he be sentenced as an "impenitent and pertinacious heretic". When the sentence was read, on 8 February 1600, Bruno told his judges: "Perhaps your fear in passing judgment on me is greater than mine in receiving it." He was taken to the Campo de' Fiori, gagged and burned alive. [cite:hm_brit_bruno]

Why he was condemned is less clear than the legend. Britannica's account records that he admitted "minor theological errors" in Venice and was condemned in general for heretical theories, and that his "emphasis on the magical and the occult" has been a source of criticism of his work. [cite:hm_brit_bruno] For a long time Bruno was remembered chiefly as a martyr for science and for the idea of a sun-centred universe. In 1964 the historian Frances Yates argued instead, in Giordano Bruno and the Hermetic Tradition, that he was burned for espousing the Hermetic tradition, not for affirming heliocentrism; her book "transformed" the study of the Renaissance. [cite:hm_wiki_yates]

The record supports a narrow conclusion. Bruno was burned by the Roman Inquisition, on the pope's order, because he refused to retract what the Church called heresy. [cite:hm_brit_bruno] Which of his ideas mattered most to his judges, his theology, his cosmology or his Hermetic magic, is a question historians still argue about; this book does not settle it. What his case shows is the same line this shelf has traced since The Canon: a thinker could study the ancient wisdom with a patron and an orthodox defence, as Ficino did, but one who would not submit to the office that judged orthodoxy could be killed for it.
