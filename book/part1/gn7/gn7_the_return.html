The Return: A Gnostic Church, a Psychologist and a Bestseller

For centuries the Gnostics were known only as heretics in their opponents' books. The name was chosen again as a self-description in the nineteenth century, and the modern revival was shaped by three figures: a Frenchman who founded a church, a Swiss psychologist who bought a codex, and an American historian who wrote a bestseller.

Jules Doinel reported visionary experiences in 1888 and 1889 and in the autumn of 1890 founded the Église Gnostique, the Gnostic Church, in Paris. Its later history shows how unstable the revival was: Doinel resigned in 1895, returned to Catholicism and worked for a time with Léo Taxil before being readmitted to his own church in 1900. [cite:vt24_wiki_doinel]

Carl Gustav Jung gave the Gnostics a different kind of afterlife. In 1916 he wrote and privately published the Seven Sermons to the Dead, a visionary text that he attributed to Basilides of Alexandria. [cite:vt24_wiki_septem] In 1952 his institute in Zurich bought the first Nag Hammadi codex, and on 15 November 1953 it was presented as the Jung Codex. [cite:gn_pearse_jung] The psychologist who had written in the voice of a second-century Gnostic now owned one of their books.

The bestseller came two years after the English translation. Elaine Pagels's The Gnostic Gospels of 1979 won the National Book Award in 1980 and introduced the Nag Hammadi texts to a mass readership, with the argument, set out in Part Three, that orthodoxy's doctrines served the bishops' authority and gnosis justified refusing to obey them. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] In this book's view, the appetite for "lost gospels" that followed is part of the context in which the Gospel of Judas was marketed and the "Gospel of Jesus' Wife" was forged. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] [cite:gn_wiki_gjw]

The scholarly work of Michael Williams and Karen King cuts against admirers as well as enemies: a single, monolithic "Gnosticism" is no more accurate when it is admired than when it is condemned. [cite:gn_pup_williams] [cite:vt24_wiki_gnosticism] Both pictures say as much about the people who drew them as about the ancient teachers. The texts themselves are more varied than either: some world-denying, some serene, some polemical, some, like the Thunder, hardly Gnostic at all. Reading them in their own words, rather than through their enemies or their admirers, is the point of the recovery that began in 1945.
