The Jewish Agency's Campaign at the United Nations

In August 1946, more than a year before any United Nations committee reported on Palestine, the Jewish Agency, the body that represented the Jewish community there, put a detailed partition map on paper. American recognition was one track toward statehood. Admission to the community of nations through the United Nations was another, and the two were separate. The Agency worked both, and its work on the second began before the United Nations had any committee on Palestine at all.

The usual account holds that the United Nations arrived at partition on its own, through the neutral fact-finding of its eleven-member committee. The documents complicate that picture. The Agency's August 1946 proposal drew boundaries for a Palestinian rump state that bear a notable resemblance to the territory that later became the West Bank. [cite:jewishagency_1946plan] This was formal, prepared diplomatic work, set down well ahead of any international process that might adopt something like it.

Nor was the proposal a one-off tactical move. Historical accounts of the United Nations process note that by 1946 partition had become the Jewish Agency's openly avowed policy. [cite:unscop_testimony] The Agency did not adopt partition in reaction to an initiative from New York. Its position was public and settled before the formal process began. It entered that process years into a coordinated, prepared diplomatic campaign, not responding to a fresh proposal from outside. [cite:unscop_testimony]

Two limits on this argument should be stated plainly. First, resemblance is not adoption. The sources show that the 1946 boundaries resemble a later territorial outcome. They do not show that the United Nations committee copied its lines from the Agency's map. Second, a settled commitment to partition did not mean that every detail of the Agency's later presentation at the United Nations matched the 1946 document. What was fixed by 1946 was the principle, not every line on the map.

Even within those limits, the timing matters. When the United Nations committee began its work in 1947, one party to the dispute arrived with a worked-out plan and a stated policy. Who else would present a case, and how, was still an open question. That imbalance shaped the hearings that followed, taken up in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair]. It also placed the diplomatic groundwork for what became Resolution 181 inside the Jewish Agency's own planning, alongside the procurement network in the Americas and the political channels in Washington described in earlier chapters.

In the same summer that the Agency set out its proposal, however, its relationship with Britain, the governing power in Palestine, reached a breaking point. Weeks before the August 1946 submission, Britain had raided the Agency's own offices and arrested its senior officials. The consequences of that raid would soon dominate events on the ground.
