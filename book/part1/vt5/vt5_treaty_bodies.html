The UN Treaty Bodies: the 2014 Reviews

A sovereign that signs human rights treaties accepts that committees of independent experts will examine it. The Holy See has signed fewer of them than most states. It ratified the Convention on the Rights of the Child on 20 April 1990 and the Convention against Torture on 26 June 2002, and it is party to the convention against racial discrimination, the 1951 Refugee Convention and the Geneva Conventions; it has not signed the two International Covenants of 1966 or the convention on discrimination against women. [cite:vt5_umn_ratif] The two treaties it did accept produced, in 2014, the most searching outside examination of its record on clerical sexual abuse that any international body has made. This section sets out what the two committees found, what the Holy See replied, and the point on which the two sides never agreed: for whom the Holy See answers.

The Committee on the Rights of the Child, January 2014

The Holy See's first report to the Committee on the Rights of the Child was filed in 1994 and its second in 2011, years after it was due. [cite:vt5_tapsell2025] The committee examined the second report in a public session in Geneva on 16 January 2014. The Holy See's representative, Archbishop Silvano Tomasi, set out the distinction on which its whole defence rested: the Holy See as a subject of international law, Vatican City State as the territory with its own small population and its own criminal law, and the local Churches, which adopt guidelines within their own countries' laws. [cite:vt5_va_tomasi_crc] Serious cases of abuse, he said, "have rightly been adjudicated and punished by the competent civil authorities in the respective countries." [cite:vt5_va_tomasi_crc] He also pointed to Vatican City laws of 11 July 2013 that gave its courts jurisdiction over crimes committed abroad by its public officials, curial staff and diplomats among them. [cite:vt5_va_tomasi_crc]

The committee adopted its concluding observations on 31 January 2014 and issued them as document CRC/C/VAT/CO/2 on 25 February. [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] It rejected the distinction at the outset: by ratifying, it wrote, the Holy See had undertaken to implement the Convention "not only within the territory of Vatican City State, but also, as the supreme power of the Catholic Church, worldwide." [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] On abuse it found that known abusers had been "transferred from parish to parish or to other countries," that a "code of silence" had been imposed on clergy under penalty of excommunication, and that reporting to the police "has never been made compulsory." [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] It recommended that known and suspected abusers be removed and referred to law enforcement, that reporting be made mandatory, that archives be opened, and that canon law treat child abuse as a crime rather than a "delict against the moral." [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] It noted that a Commission for the Protection of Minors had been announced on 5 December 2013, and urged the Holy See to withdraw its reservations, which made the Convention's application depend on its compatibility with the law of Vatican City State. [cite:vt5_crc_co2014]

The observations went beyond abuse. The committee asked the Holy See to oppose all corporal punishment, to investigate the forced labour of girls in the Irish Magdalene laundries and compensate them, to "review its position on abortion" and amend canon 1398, to allow adolescents access to contraception, and to remove gender stereotyping from Catholic school textbooks. [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] Those recommendations went to the content of Catholic teaching, and they shaped the reply.

The Holy See's statement of 5 February 2014 said it "takes note" of the observations, which would "be submitted to a thorough study and examination," and it objected to "an attempt to interfere with Catholic Church teaching." [cite:vt5_ba_hs_crc_reply] [cite:vt5_am_crc2014] Tomasi said his first reaction was surprise, since the document's negative tone made it seem it was "prepared before" the January hearing, and called the abortion recommendation "a real contradiction" in a treaty on children; he stressed "a policy of transparency and zero tolerance for abuse." [cite:vt5_am_crc2014] The reply thus answered two different things at once: the abuse findings, which it did not contest in terms, and the recommendations on doctrine, which it rejected as outside the committee's remit.

The Committee against Torture, May 2014

Three months later the Holy See presented its initial report under the Convention against Torture. Its delegation told the committee that between 2004 and 2013 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith had confirmed 3,420 credible allegations of abuse of minors by priests, 848 priests had been dismissed from the priesthood and 2,572 had received lesser penalties. [cite:vt5_cat_co2014] These are counts of cases decided by one Vatican office over ten years, not an estimate of victims, and they are not to be added to any national figure. [scene:vt12_the_numbers|Part Twelve: the numbers]

The committee's observations, adopted on 21 and 22 May and issued as CAT/C/VAT/CO/1 on 17 June 2014, again took up jurisdiction. It noted the Holy See's interpretative declaration that the Convention applied only to Vatican City State, found that position inconsistent with the Convention and with the Holy See's own law, and said a state's obligations extend to its officials "wherever they exercise effective control over persons or territory." [cite:vt5_cat_co2014] It was concerned that church officials "resist the principle of mandatory reporting" to civil authorities, asked the Holy See to suspend accused clergy at once and stop transfers that frustrated investigation, asked for information on the Vatican investigation of Archbishop Józef Wesołowski, cited a 2013 refusal to share information with an Australian inquiry, and asked for follow-up information by 23 May 2015. [cite:vt5_cat_co2014]

The Holy See's communiqué of 23 May 2014 was warmer than its February statement. It said the observations "include many positive outcomes" and a "few points that require further clarification." [cite:vt5_rv_cat_reply] It repeated that the Convention "applies to Vatican City State," objected to the impression that priests worldwide were "indirectly legally dependent" on the Vatican, called an "implicit fundamental assumption" that sexual abuse amounts to torture "fundamentally flawed," and reminded the committee that its role was "to monitor and report, and not to adjudicate." [cite:vt5_rv_cat_reply]

What the reviews checked, and what they could not

The two reviews are the clearest answer to the claim, repeated in earlier Observe The System material, that the Holy See faces "zero external accountability." On abuse, it faced two committees in four months, gave figures in public, and was told in writing that its reading of its own obligations was wrong. The reviews also show the limits of that kind of check. A treaty committee has no power to enforce its observations; it can ask, record and publish. The Holy See accepted the jurisdiction of the committees under the two treaties it chose to ratify, and it held throughout that its treaty obligations stopped at the borders of Vatican City, so that the conduct of bishops and priests abroad fell under the laws of the states where they served and under canon law. [cite:vt5_rv_cat_reply] [cite:vt5_va_tomasi_crc] The committees held the opposite. Neither side moved.

The reporting cycle has also slowed. In December 2025 the Australian lawyer and writer Kieran Tapsell, a critic of the Holy See's handling of abuse, wrote that it had filed no report to the Committee on the Rights of the Child since 2011, although reports are due every five years, and that after the 2014 review it had described its obligation as running only to the children who live in Vatican City; he urged Pope Leo XIV to submit one. [cite:vt5_tapsell2025] The UN's own treaty-body database could not be consulted for this volume, so the reporting status as of October 2026 rests on that December 2025 account. The legal changes that followed the reviews inside the Church, among them the 2019 abolition of the pontifical secret for abuse cases and the reporting duties of Vos estis lux mundi, belong to the history of the Holy See's own rules and are set out in Part Twelve. [scene:vt12_the_rules|Part Twelve: the rules] [scene:vt12_accountability|Part Twelve: accountability] The other outside check the Holy See accepted in the same years was financial, and it was built very differently: by agreement with the European Union and by membership of a Council of Europe evaluation body.
