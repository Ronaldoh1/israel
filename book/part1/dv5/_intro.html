Parts Two to Four examined three cities said to have raised a state. This part turns to the state itself, the fourth sovereign of the volume's title, and asks the question the volume has asked of every city: how does it actually govern, and what makes it unusual? Israel is not an anomaly of the same kind as the three cities. It is a full member of the United Nations, with an army, a currency and an elected parliament. Its unusual features lie inside its constitutional order: a state promised a constitution in 1948 that never adopted one; that defines who may become a citizen by descent from a people living mostly elsewhere; whose governments are always coalitions, in which small parties can hold the balance; and whose decisions about millions of people in the territories it has occupied since 1967 are taken by cabinet committees those people do not vote for.

The part sets out that machinery as of November 2026, when Israel is between governments: an election on 27 October left neither bloc with a majority, the President has asked the opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot to form a coalition, and Benjamin Netanyahu governs as caretaker while his criminal trial continues. It then sets the state against the rules other states follow, at the nuclear treaty, the two courts in The Hague and the United Nations, and ends with the relationship that runs through this whole volume: a state heavily dependent on one guardian that has repeatedly defied it.
