Who Can Stop You

This section asks who in the United States can already stop a person from boarding a plane or leaving the country, on what trigger, with what notice, and before which judge. "Exit", the Dark Enlightenment's word for the resident's one remedy against a bad government, assumes the resident can leave ([scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two], [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight]). Whether that holds depends less on airport cameras than on the passport.

The passport is the exit permit

The United States has no exit visa. It has something close to one in law: federal statute makes it unlawful "for any citizen of the United States to depart from or enter" the country "unless he bears a valid United States passport". [cite:de25b_usc_1185] Whoever controls the passport controls departure, and that control has been used for politics before. In the early Cold War the State Department refused passports to people it suspected of Communist ties. In Kent v. Dulles, decided on 16 June 1958, the Supreme Court held that the Secretary of State had no authority to deny passports for beliefs and associations, and declared that "the right to travel is a part of the 'liberty'" the Fifth Amendment protects. [cite:de25b_kent] In 1964, in Aptheker v. Secretary of State, it struck down a statute barring Communist Party members from holding passports. [cite:de25b_aptheker] The limit cuts both ways. In Haig v. Agee (1981), the Court upheld, 7 to 2, the revocation of the passport of Philip Agee, a former Central Intelligence Agency officer who was publicly exposing agents, and told courts to defer to the executive on national security. [cite:de25b_mtsu_agee] Belief alone cannot cost a citizen a passport; conduct the executive calls dangerous can.

The no-fly list

The government's terrorist screening database held about two million identities at the end of 2023, up from 1.16 million in 2017; most are not US citizens or permanent residents, though "thousands of Americans" are included. The No Fly list is one part of it, and its size is not public. [cite:de25b_cbs_watchlist] For years a listed person could not learn whether they were on it or why. In June 2014, in Latif v. Holder, a federal judge in Oregon found the redress process "wholly ineffective", recognised "a constitutionally protected, private interest in international travel by air", and ordered the government to tell plaintiffs their status and its reasons. [cite:de25b_lf_latif] The government revised the procedure in 2015. In Kashem v. Barr, on 21 October 2019, the Ninth Circuit upheld the revised process and the list's criteria as applied to the men before it, and confirmed that the final order comes from the TSA administrator and can be challenged only in a federal appeals court. [cite:de25b_kashem] On 19 March 2024 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in FBI v. Fikre that the government could not end an Oregon man's challenge simply by taking him off the list with a pledge not to relist him "based on the currently available information"; Yonas Fikre had learned of his listing while in Sudan in 2010. The Court did not decide whether the listing was lawful. [cite:de25b_scb_fikre]

Passports and debt

Two statutes already let one agency's certificate stop a citizen leaving. Under section 7345 of the Internal Revenue Code, the Internal Revenue Service certifies "seriously delinquent tax debt" to the State Department, which then refuses a new passport and may revoke a current one. The threshold for 2026 is $66,000, indexed each year. The debt must carry a filed tax lien with appeals exhausted, or a levy; it does not count if the taxpayer is on a payment plan, has asked for a collection hearing, is bankrupt or cannot pay because of hardship. The taxpayer gets a notice and the right to sue in the Tax Court or a district court over a wrong certification. [cite:de25b_irs_passport] The second is child support. Since 1996 states have certified parents with arrears to the federal Office of Child Support Enforcement, which passes them to the State Department's lookout system; the threshold, first $5,000, has been $2,500 since the Deficit Reduction Act of 2005. It has collected nearly $621 million since its start. Existing passports can be revoked only when the State Department physically holds them, for a renewal or a name change. On 27 April 2026 the House passed by voice vote a bill, H.R. 6903, that would make revocation mandatory; the Senate referred it to its Finance Committee the next day. [cite:de25b_crs_childsupport] [cite:de25b_fd_hr6903] Criminal law adds a narrow third bar: a person convicted of a felony drug offence that involved crossing a border gets no passport while in prison or on parole. [cite:de25b_usc_2714]

A broader power was proposed and withdrawn. In September 2025 the House Foreign Affairs Committee's State Department bill included a provision letting the Secretary of State deny or revoke the passports of citizens he deemed to have "materially supported terrorists". An American Civil Liberties Union lawyer called it "a huge risk of abuse, of politicized enforcement", and activists said it could be aimed at critics of Israel. The committee's chairman, Brian Mast, filed an amendment striking it within days. [cite:de25b_int_mast] [cite:de25b_gu_mast]

The convenience tier as leverage

Global Entry and TSA PreCheck are memberships that buy a faster line; their loss keeps no one at home. They are also the one travel benefit the government has demonstrably withdrawn as leverage. In February 2020 the Department of Homeland Security barred every New York resident from its trusted-traveller programmes because the state's Green Light Law limited federal access to driving-licence records. It lifted the bar on 23 July 2020, after the government admitted in court that statements DHS had made to justify it were "inaccurate and misleading"; New York's attorney general called the policy "political retribution, plain and simple". [cite:de25b_abc_ny]

In 2026 the question came back in individual form. Nicole Cleland, a Minnesota resident, said in a sworn court declaration that on 10 January 2026, while she was watching vehicles she believed carried immigration agents, a Border Patrol agent called her by name, told her he had facial recognition and accused her of impeding agents; three days later her Global Entry and PreCheck were revoked without explanation. [cite:de25b_kstp_cleland] In late July 2026 she and two other citizens, represented by the Electronic Privacy Information Center, sued in federal court in San Diego under the Administrative Procedure Act, the Privacy Act and the First Amendment. A South Carolina woman says her Global Entry was revoked ten days after she observed an enforcement operation in Charlotte and later reinstated without explanation; a San Diego woman says hers was restored after a reporter asked about it. CBP denies revoking memberships in retaliation for protected speech and says they can be withdrawn for crimes, warrants, customs or immigration violations or failure to remain "low-risk". The allegations have not been tested in court. [cite:de25b_bu_cleland]

Where leaving needs permission

Other states have made leaving itself require permission. Soviet citizens needed an exit visa. Those refused, the "refuseniks", were often turned down for "months, years, and even decades" and lost their jobs; Mikhail Gorbachev let most of them go from 1987. [cite:de25b_enc_refuseniks] From August 1972 Moscow also charged emigrants "education reimbursement fees" that could reach tens of thousands of dollars; the US answer, the Jackson–Vanik amendment to the Trade Act of 1974, tied trade benefits to the freedom to emigrate and to the absence of "a more than nominal tax on emigration". [cite:de25b_crs_jv] China's exit bans today run on at least 15 laws; the rights group Safeguard Defenders counted court-record mentions rising from under 5,000 in 2016 to 39,000 in 2020, estimated bans "in the millions" counting those imposed by ethnicity, and documented their use against critics' families, journalists and foreign businessmen. [cite:de25b_aj_china] Russia's law of 14 April 2023 made a draft summons count as delivered seven days after it is posted to the state services portal, and barred its recipient from leaving the country; after 20 days without reporting, he loses access to loans and property registration. Since July 2025 border guards have had direct access to the draft register. [cite:de25b_ng_russia] [cite:de25b_mt_russia]

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, reads immigration enforcement as the occasion for building a machine that a sovereign executive without a veto could turn on anyone. Documented: the state already holds a fifteen-year archive of international travel, routinely photographs non-citizens at the border and many travellers at checkpoints, and since 2025 has passed domestic passenger lists to ICE under an agreement made public only through a records lawsuit; a counter-terrorism system was put to an immigration use its own rule disclaimed. [cite:de25b_pia_ats] [cite:de25b_ao_moa] [cite:de25b_reuters_tsaice] Documented: the government has twice used trusted-traveller status as leverage, against a whole state in 2020 by an admitted false justification, and, on sworn allegations not yet tried, against observers in 2026. [cite:de25b_abc_ny] [cite:de25b_bu_cleland] Probable: that travel records, being complete and archived, will keep finding enforcement uses beyond their founding purpose; that is a reading of the documented drift, not of any stated plan. Not supported: any US exit permit, or any power to deny a passport for speech. The record runs against the worst case here more than in most parts: the courts closed political passport denial in 1958 and 1964, forced the no-fly process open in 2014 and kept Fikre's suit alive in 2024; a House chairman dropped the "material support" clause within days; DHS reversed its New York ban; and a Republican administration ended a programme it said a Democratic one had abused. [cite:de25b_kent] [cite:de25b_lf_latif] [cite:de25b_scb_fikre] [cite:de25b_gu_mast] [cite:de25b_cnn_quietskies] The same standard applies in both directions: the watch on unaccused travellers and the no-fly secrecy grew under both parties, and so did the brakes. The exit still opens, for those whose passport is good and whose debts are below the line.
