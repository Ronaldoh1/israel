What They Taught: Gnosis, the Hidden God and the Way Back

The question about these teachers has a teaching half and a practice half. The teaching half asks whether they taught the ascension of consciousness, alchemy and the kingdom within. The answer differs for each: the kingdom within and the ascent of the soul are documented in their own texts, while alchemy is not, except through a later bridge figure. This section sets out the core teachings first, using the groups' own writings where they survive and the heresy hunters' summaries where they do not, and then answers the three questions one by one. The practice half (tithe, commandments, Sunday duty) is answered in [scene:vt24_how_they_lived|the next section].

Salvation as knowing, not as believing

The common thread is that salvation comes from knowledge of one's own origin in God, not from faith in a past event or obedience to a rule. The Gospel of Thomas, a collection of 114 sayings found at Nag Hammadi, opens by promising that whoever finds the interpretation of these sayings "will not experience death", and its saying 70 puts the mechanism plainly: what you have will save you "if you bring it forth from yourselves". [cite:vt24_gthom] Irenaeus records that some Valentinians went further and rejected outward rites altogether, holding that "the knowledge of the unspeakable Greatness is itself perfect redemption", since the human problem was ignorance and knowledge undid it. [cite:vt24_ah1_21] This is a different account of what saves a person from the one the bishops defended, in which faith in Christ's death and resurrection and the church's sacraments did the saving. Both accounts were live in the second century.

The hidden God and the ignorant maker

The most distinctive teaching, and the one that most offended opponents, concerns who made the world. In the Apocryphon of John, the central Sethian text, the highest God is beyond all names. From this God come a first thought, Barbelo, and a series of eternal beings called aeons, which together make up the Pleroma, the fullness. The last aeon, Sophia (Wisdom), acts without the consent of the Spirit or her partner, and "something came out of her which was imperfect"; she casts it away and names it Yaltabaoth (Yaldabaoth). This being makes the rulers and the visible world and announces, "I am a jealous God, and there is no other God beside me", a claim the text says "indicated to the angels who attended him that there exists another God". [cite:vt24_apjohn] The line borrows the words of the God of Israel from Exodus and Isaiah, and the myth is in effect a second-century Christian rereading of Genesis. It is a claim about a figure inside a myth. It is not a statement about Judaism or about the God Jewish communities worship, and later attempts, ancient and modern, to turn it into hostility toward Jews misuse it. [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism]

Valentinian teachers told a gentler version in which Sophia's lower aspect, not Sophia herself, produces the maker, who is ignorant rather than wicked. Ptolemy's Letter to Flora says the giver of the Law was neither the perfect God nor the devil but the maker of this universe, "neither good nor evil or unjust, but can properly be called just". [cite:vt24_ect_flora] In both the Sethian and Valentinian versions, humans carry within them light or seed from the Pleroma; awakening it is the point of the whole story. [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism] [cite:vt24_apjohn] The Valentinians, according to Irenaeus, divided people into three kinds, material, "animal" (psychic) and spiritual, and said the spiritual are saved "because they are spiritual by nature" while ordinary believers, whom they called "animal", needed faith and good works. [cite:vt24_ah1_06] Critics then and now have pointed to this as spiritual elitism, and it is part of the record alongside the more attractive teachings.

The resurrection as something you do now

The Valentinians reread the resurrection. The Gospel of Philip says "Those who say they will die first and then rise are in error", and warns that whoever does not receive the resurrection while alive will receive nothing after death. [cite:vt24_gphil] The Treatise on the Resurrection, a letter to a student called Rheginos, insists that resurrection "is no illusion, but it is truth", tells him "already you have the resurrection", and asks "why not consider yourself as risen". [cite:vt24_treatres] Philip uses the language of transformation: "You saw the Spirit, you became spirit. You saw Christ, you became Christ." It is also a sacramental text, naming five mysteries, "a baptism and a chrism and a eucharist and a redemption and a bridal chamber". [cite:vt24_gphil] The claim, repeated in some popular writing, that the Gnostics rejected sacraments as empty symbols is wrong for Philip; it treats them as real participation in the divine.

On the Law

Ptolemy's letter is the clearest surviving Gnostic statement on commandments, and it does not reject them wholesale. He divides the Law of Moses into what came from God, what Moses added, and what the elders added. God's part is itself threefold: the pure law of the Ten Commandments, "which the Savior came not to destroy but to complete"; laws mixed with injustice, such as an eye for an eye, which the Saviour set aside; and symbolic law (offerings, circumcision, the sabbath, fasting, Passover), whose "names remained the same but the content was changed" into a spiritual meaning. True fasting, he writes, is "abstinence from everything evil". He closes by promising Flora more teaching "when you are judged worthy of the apostolic tradition which we too have received by succession". [cite:vt24_ect_flora] A Valentinian teacher, then, claimed his own apostolic succession and kept the moral core of the Decalogue.

Three questions about teaching

The kingdom within: yes, and plainly. Thomas saying 3 has Jesus say "the kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you", and saying 113 that the kingdom "is spread out upon the earth, and men do not see it". [cite:vt24_gthom] In the Gospel of Mary, preserved in the Berlin Codex, the risen Saviour tells the disciples that "the Son of Humanity exists within you" (older translations: "Son of Man"). [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex] The idea was not confined to them, however. Luke 17:21, in the canonical Bible, has Jesus say the kingdom of God is entos hymōn. Most modern translations render it "among you"; the classicist Ilaria Ramelli has argued that "among" is practically unattested for entos, that the Latin Vulgate and later Syriac versions say "inside", and that orthodox writers including Origen, Athanasius, Basil and Gregory of Nyssa read it as "within". [cite:vt24_ramelli] The inner kingdom stayed inside the canon and inside orthodox commentary; what divided the parties was whether that inner knowing made bishops and sacraments unnecessary.

The ascent of the soul: yes, extensively, though in the language of the period rather than of "consciousness". The Gospel of Mary describes the soul rising past a series of powers, the last with seven forms of wrath. [cite:vt24_mary_pbs] Irenaeus reports Valentinian groups that anointed the dying so they would be "incapable of being seized or seen by the principalities and powers" and gave them words to speak on the way up, beginning "I am a son from the Father". [cite:vt24_ah1_21] Origen, writing against the pagan critic Celsus about 248, describes an Ophite diagram of ten circles that he says he had seen himself, and the formulas the soul addressed to the rulers Ialdabaoth, Iao, Sabaoth and Astaphaeus as it passed their gates. [cite:vt24_origen_cc6] The Sethian treatises Zostrianos and Allogenes are visionary ascents, and were read in the circle of the philosopher Plotinus. [cite:vt24_bmcr_plotinus] The Pistis Sophia carries the same passwords-and-seals scheme further; see [scene:vt26_pistis_sophia|Part Twenty-Six].

Alchemy: not in their own texts. No alchemical treatise survives among the Nag Hammadi codices or in any Valentinian or Sethian writing; Philip's line "God is a dyer" uses the image of a dye-works, not of transmutation. [cite:vt24_gphil] The documented bridge is Zosimos of Panopolis, an Egyptian alchemist active about 300 whose books are the oldest known on alchemy and who treated the transformation of metals as a mirror of inner purification. [cite:vt24_wiki_zosimos] The historian Kyle Fraser argues that Zosimos was a practising Hermetist who drew on Sethian works, including the ascent literature, to describe the alchemical work as a baptism bringing gnosis. [cite:vt24_fraser2007] That makes Zosimos a reader of Gnostic texts who practised alchemy, not evidence that Valentinians or Sethians practised it. How alchemy was later treated by Rome is in [scene:vt30_forbidden_arts|Part Thirty].

Interpretation, not finding: the myth as a map of inner ascent

Read through an esoteric lens, the fall and return of Sophia is not cosmology but a map of the mind: a descent of awareness into matter and its climb back through layers of conditioning, the archons, to its source. The best-known modern reader in this vein is Carl Jung, who in 1916 privately published his own Seven Sermons to the Dead under the name of Basilides. [cite:vt24_wiki_septem] Later spiritual-alchemy writers, and the Sophia platform's own Reignite material, read solve et coagula and the ascent through the powers as the same process told in two vocabularies. In this reading the Gnostic texts and the alchemists describe one inner work. It is a modern reading, held by those writers; the ancient texts describe a cosmos and a soul's journey through it, and the link to alchemy runs through Zosimos, not through the second-century teachers themselves.
