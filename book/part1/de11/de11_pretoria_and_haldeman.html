Pretoria, a Grandfather, and Three Passports

Elon Reeve Musk was born on 28 June 1971 in Pretoria, then the administrative capital of apartheid South Africa. His father, Errol Musk, was a South African electromechanical engineer; his mother, Maye Musk, née Haldeman, a model and dietitian, had been born in Saskatchewan, Canada. He has a brother, Kimbal, and a sister, Tosca. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] Much of what is written about his childhood comes from two kinds of source that this section keeps apart: his own accounts, given to biographers and in interviews, and documents or independent reporting. Where a detail rests on his word alone, the text says so. Where his father's account differs, both are given. A father's or a grandfather's record is never treated here as the son's.

Musk's place in this book does not rest on any documented reading of Curtis Yarvin or Nick Land. It rests on money, office and platforms: the largest political spending of any individual in the 2024 American election, a role as the public face of the government cuts of 2025, and ownership of one of the world's main public forums. Those are the subjects of the sections that follow. This one sets out where he came from, and tests the family line that is often drawn from his grandfather to his politics.

The Haldemans and the move to Pretoria

Musk was born in Pretoria because of a decision his grandfather made two decades earlier. Joshua N. Haldeman, his mother's father, was a Canadian chiropractor who led the Technocracy movement in Canada in the late 1930s, was charged under Canada's wartime regulations in 1940 for belonging to it, and later led the Social Credit party in Saskatchewan. In 1950 he moved his family, including his daughter Maye, to Pretoria. [cite:de3_cbc_leo] [cite:de3_wiki_haldeman] His documented politics there, set out with sources in [scene:de3_technocracy|Part Three], included praise for the South African government's handling of what he called "the native question" in a 1951 newspaper letter, and an earlier defence of the publication of the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, the antisemitic forgery, in which he said its authenticity "is not the point." [cite:de3_cbc_leo] He died in a plane crash on 13 January 1974, when his grandson was two. [cite:de3_cbc_leo]

Only one thing in that record bears on Musk himself, and it is his own words. The documented references Musk has made to his grandfather concern adventure, not politics: "My grandfather had this desire for adventure, exploration — doing crazy things," he told his biographer Ashlee Vance. [cite:de3_cbc_leo] His single documented use of his grandfather's movement's name was a 2019 post about "the Martian Technocracy," which he did not explain. [cite:de3_phys_tweet] No statement by Musk adopting his grandfather's views on race, on Jews or on apartheid is on record. A widely repeated line treats the grandfather's views as an inheritance; the record carries a family tie, not a transmitted creed.

A childhood in his own account and his father's

His parents divorced in 1979. At about nine, Musk chose to live with his father, he has said, "because he had an Encyclopædia Britannica set and a computer"; he later regretted the choice and became estranged from Errol. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] He attended Waterkloof House Preparatory School, Bryanston High School and Pretoria Boys High School, from which he graduated. He has described a wilderness camp as a "paramilitary Lord of the Flies" where "bullying was a virtue," and has said that after a fight at school he was thrown down concrete steps, beaten and hospitalised, and that his father berated him afterwards. Errol Musk has denied that part of the account. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] These are the son's memories and the father's denial; neither has been tested in any document this book has seen, and both are recorded as what they are.

The family's money has been the subject of a durable story. A widely repeated line holds that Musk's career was paid for by his father's emerald mine. The record is more tangled than either the story or the denial. In 2014 Musk said his father "had a share in an emerald mine in Zambia"; in 2019 he wrote that "He didn't own an emerald mine & I worked my way through college," with about $100,000 in student debt; in 2023 he called it "the fake emerald mine thing." [cite:de11_bi_emerald] Errol Musk told the biographer Walter Isaacson that he had traded a light plane in 1986 for emeralds from an unregistered Zambian mine owned by the buyer, had them cut in Johannesburg and sold them abroad, adding that "none of it was legal," and that the business later collapsed. Isaacson concluded that Errol never owned a mine. [cite:de11_bi_emerald] The documented core is a comfortable household with some income from emeralds. A mine that funded the son's companies is not documented, and the son's student-debt account has not been independently checked.

Leaving South Africa

Musk left South Africa at seventeen. He obtained a Canadian passport through his Canadian-born mother and arrived in Canada in June 1989, where he connected with a second cousin in Saskatchewan and worked odd jobs. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] His own stated reason was in part to avoid compulsory military service in the apartheid-era army. In a 2013 interview he said he left "in part, in order to avoid conscription in the army," and, pressed on the point, that "spending two years suppressing Black people doesn't seem to be a great use of time." [cite:de11_fc_conscription] That is his own account of his motive, and no document contradicts it.

He entered Queen's University in Kingston, Ontario, in 1990, and two years later transferred to the University of Pennsylvania, where he studied until 1995. He has said he earned his degrees that year; Penn did not award them, a BA in physics and a BS in economics from the Wharton School, until 1997. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] In 1995 he was accepted into a materials-science graduate programme at Stanford. He did not enrol. In a 2009 deposition he said he phoned the department chair two days after the semester began to say he would not attend, which is the root of his long-repeated line about leaving Stanford after two days. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] [cite:de11_wapo_visa]

The work-visa questions

On 26 October 2024, ten days before an election in which Musk was spending heavily on a candidate campaigning against illegal immigration, the Washington Post reported that he had himself worked in the United States without authorization in the mid-1990s. The report rested on company documents, court records and former associates. Six former associates and shareholders said he had told co-workers he was in the country on a student visa; he never enrolled at Stanford; and, by his own 2009 deposition, he began working at Zip2 in August or September 1995. [cite:de11_wapo_visa] When the venture firm Mohr Davidow invested $3 million in Zip2 in 1996, the funding agreement gave the Musk brothers and an associate 45 days to obtain legal work status or the firm could reclaim its money. A Zip2 board member recalled the authorizations coming through around 1997; a former staffer recalled processing work visas that year under the NAFTA category for Canadians. [cite:de11_wapo_visa]

The most direct document is Musk's own. In a 2005 email to his Tesla co-founders Martin Eberhard and JB Straubel, later filed in a California defamation suit, he wrote that he had applied to Stanford because "I had no money for a lab and no legal right to stay in the country, so that seemed like a good way to solve both issues." [cite:de11_wapo_visa] His earlier public accounts had been looser: in 2013 he called his status "a gray area," and in 2020 he said he had a "student-work visa" and "was legally there, but I was meant to be doing student work." [cite:de11_wapo_visa] He did not respond to the newspaper. After President Biden repeated the report, Musk wrote on X: "I was in fact allowed to work in the US," and "The Biden puppet is lying." [cite:de11_pbs_visa] His brother has given his own, different accounts of his own status; those are his brother's record, not his. The claim and the email are tested side by side in [scene:de11_claims_vs_record|the last section of this part].

Citizenship, and the country he left

Musk became an American citizen in 2002. [cite:de11_wiki_musk] He also holds Canadian citizenship through his mother. Whether he remains a South African citizen is not on the public record. In March 2025, asked in parliament whether Musk, Peter Thiel and David Sacks were citizens, South Africa's home affairs minister, Leon Schreiber, declined to disclose any of their details, citing the country's access-to-information law. [cite:de11_bt_citizenship] Musk's own description, in 2020, was "American, but born in South Africa. Left by myself when I was 17." [cite:de11_wiki_musk_sa]

He has since spoken often about the country of his birth, and his statements are his own record. In July 2023 he wrote to President Cyril Ramaphosa that South African politicians "are openly pushing for genocide of white people," after a rally at which the opposition leader Julius Malema sang the "Kill the Boer" chant; in May 2025 he called the country's Black Economic Empowerment rules "Apartheid version 2," and he has said his Starlink service could not operate there "because I'm not black." [cite:de11_wiki_musk_sa] South African courts have treated the chant differently in different contexts, and South Africa's government has rejected the genocide claim; Ramaphosa told President Trump in the White House in May 2025, referring to members of his own delegation: "If there was Afrikaner farmer genocide, I can bet you these three gentlemen would not be here." [cite:de11_wiki_musk_sa] That month, Musk's own chatbot inserted the "white genocide" claim into answers to unrelated questions; his company xAI blamed an "unauthorized modification" by an employee. [cite:de11_wiki_musk_sa] These statements show what Musk says about South Africa now. They do not show what he believed at seventeen, and they are not his grandfather's.

The life before 1995, then, is documented in outline and contested in detail. The facts that matter for the rest of this part are few and firm: a South African birth, a Canadian passport through his mother, an American degree finished in 1997, and an American career that began, on his own 2005 account, before he had the legal right to stay. The money that career produced, and the first time Peter Thiel's path crossed his, are the subject of [scene:de11_zip2_xcom_paypal|the next section].
