The Courts and the Overhaul

In both countries the highest court can set aside acts of the elected branches, and in both that power has been the centre of political conflict in this decade. The difference lies in what stands behind it. An American court that strikes down a law relies on a written constitution that Congress alone cannot change. Israel's Supreme Court, sitting as the High Court of Justice, built its power of review on the Basic Laws of 1992 and the Bank Mizrahi judgment of 1995, and on standards of administrative law it developed itself; the Knesset that the court reviews can change the Basic Laws themselves, usually by a simple majority. [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws] One of those judge-made standards was "reasonableness", which allowed courts to invalidate decisions by ministers or officials that were "extremely unreasonable". [cite:us2_loc_reasonableness]

Israel, 2023–2026

The government sworn in at the end of 2022 set out to curb that power. The only part of its programme to pass in 2023 was an amendment to Basic Law: The Judiciary, adopted on 24 July 2023, which barred every court, including the High Court, from assessing the reasonableness of decisions by the government, the prime minister or any minister, including appointments and decisions not to act. [cite:us2_loc_reasonableness] It passed by 64 votes to none after the opposition left the chamber. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] On 1 January 2024 the court struck it down by eight justices to seven, the first time in Israel's history that it annulled a Basic Law or an amendment to one. Writing for the majority, the outgoing president, Esther Hayut, said the amendment "does the most severe harm possible to the principle of the separation of powers and the principle of the rule of law"; Justice David Mintz, dissenting, called the doctrine she applied "something out of nothing". A larger majority held that the court may review Basic Laws in exceptional cases: 13 of the 15 justices by the Times of Israel's count, 12 by other accounts, the difference turning on how concurring opinions are read. [cite:us2_toi_reasonableness] [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws]

The coalition then changed how judges are chosen. On 27 March 2025, after the opposition filed 71,023 objections and then boycotted the vote, the Knesset passed a law removing the two Israel Bar Association representatives from the nine-member Judicial Selection Committee, adding one lawyer chosen by the coalition and one by the opposition, and giving the coalition and the opposition each a veto over Supreme Court appointments in place of the three sitting judges' influence. It takes effect only with the next Knesset, the one elected on 27 October 2026. [cite:us2_toi_selection2025] The government also moved against the attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara, who as head of the government's legal advisers had repeatedly told ministers their plans were unlawful. The cabinet voted to dismiss her in August 2025 under a procedure it had just changed. On 14 December 2025 a panel of seven justices, four of them counted as conservatives, unanimously annulled the dismissal because the new procedure had been adopted hastily and without a factual or legal basis; she remained in office. Justice Minister Yariv Levin called the ruling "unlawful intervention", and Communications Minister Shlomo Karhi urged the government to dismiss her in practice regardless. [cite:us2_toi_agfiring]

The United States: a court that checks, and is checked by, opinion

The American Supreme Court's power to review legislation is not written in the Constitution either; it rests on the court's own early judgments. But its decisions bind against a document that elected majorities cannot rewrite, and in this decade it has ruled both for and against presidents. The largest recent check came on 20 February 2026 in Learning Resources v. Trump, decided with Trump v. V.O.S. Selections. By six votes to three the court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, under which President Trump had imposed sweeping tariffs by declaring national emergencies, does not authorise tariffs at all; Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that the statute "contains no reference to tariffs or duties". Customs officials identified about $166 billion collected from more than 330,000 importers, and refunds with interest were ordered in March 2026. The administration replaced the tariffs within days under a different statute, Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which caps such tariffs at 15 per cent for 150 days; challenges to those continued through mid-2026. [cite:us2_wiki_learning] The emergency basis of the original tariffs is part of the next section. [scene:us2_emergency|Emergency powers]

Trust in the courts over time

Public confidence in both courts has fallen, and the fall is measured. Gallup has asked Americans about trust in the judicial branch since 1972; it never fell below 50 per cent until September 2022, three months after the court overturned Roe v. Wade. By September 2026, 54 per cent said they had little or no trust in the judicial branch, and 61 per cent disapproved of the Supreme Court's work, tying the record, against 34 per cent who approved. Among Democrats trust fell from 58 per cent in 2020 to 23 per cent. [cite:us2_axios_gallup2026] Israel's annual Democracy Index, run by the Israel Democracy Institute since 2003, found in its 2025 edition that trust in almost every institution was below its multi-year average. Among Arab citizens the Supreme Court was the most trusted institution, "albeit only at 41%". [cite:us2_idi_di2025] [cite:us2_idi_di2025sf] The two surveys ask different questions of different populations, so they show the direction of change in each country, not a ranking between them.

Leaders before the courts

The courts' record on the powerful themselves is part of the comparison. Israel has imprisoned a former president and a former prime minister. Moshe Katsav was serving a seven-year sentence for rape when Ehud Olmert began a 19-month term in February 2016 for taking about $130,000 in bribes over a Jerusalem housing project during his time as mayor; Olmert, who denied the charge, said he accepted the sentence because "no one is above the law". [cite:us2_voa_olmert] Benjamin Netanyahu is the first sitting prime minister to be tried. Charged with bribery, fraud and breach of trust, which he denies, he has been on trial since 2020. In November 2025 he asked President Isaac Herzog for a pardon, which has no precedent in mid-trial; on 26 April 2026 Herzog said efforts "should first be exhausted to reach an agreement between the parties", and President Trump had repeatedly urged a pardon. [cite:us2_almonitor_pardon] [cite:hm_toi_trialdelay] The attorney general's office agreed in May 2026 to plea talks; Netanyahu finished his testimony on 24 June 2026 after 98 hearings. [cite:us2_ynet_trial] As of October 2026 no verdict, plea agreement or pardon had been reported in the sources checked for this book, and the status should be re-checked after the 27 October election. This volume does not compare American prosecutions of officeholders, a gap left open here.
