The Split That Made the Israeli Right

At the center of this story stands Ze'ev Jabotinsky, a Russian-Jewish writer and Zionist leader [cite:wiki_jabotinsky]. Between 1923 and 1935 he led a breakaway from mainstream Zionism through three dated steps: the founding of Betar in 1923, the Revisionist secession in 1925, and the New Zionist Organization in 1935. Zionism is sometimes pictured as a single, unified movement until statehood produced Israel's party divisions. The institutional record tells otherwise. The movement's foundational split between right and left predates the state by twenty-three years. And the line that runs from those breaks leads, merger by merger, to the party that has dominated Israeli politics for much of the past five decades.

Jabotinsky's positions on territory help explain why the split was so sharp and so lasting. Testifying before the Peel Commission in 1937, he argued against any partition of Palestine and insisted on Jewish rights to the entire Mandate territory, including Transjordan. He died of a heart attack in 1940 while visiting Betar in the United States [cite:wiki_jabotinsky].

Betar came first. Jabotinsky founded it in 1923 as a disciplined, militarized youth movement that stressed hadar, or pride, and training in armed self-defense. It spread through Poland, the Baltic states, Palestine and the Americas. By 1934 the broader Revisionist movement claimed some 600,000 members [cite:wiki_betar]. That is a claimed figure, and it covers members of every degree of commitment. Even so, it describes a mass, international organization, not a fringe.

The formal break followed in April 1925. In Paris, Jabotinsky founded the World Union of Zionist-Revisionists, known as Hatzohar, which seceded from the World Zionist Organization led by Chaim Weizmann [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism]. The Revisionists, as they were called, came with a stated platform: to turn Palestine and Transjordan into a Jewish commonwealth within the British Commonwealth. That stood in direct opposition to the gradual strategy of the mainstream movement [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism]. It was the organization's founding position, though not necessarily every member's view in every detail.

The 1925 secession still left room for some relationship with the mainstream. The second break, a decade later, closed it. In 1935, after the Zionist Executive formally rejected his platform, Jabotinsky resigned from the World Zionist Organization altogether and founded the New Zionist Organization [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization]. It ran its own political and fundraising activity, parallel to and in competition with the mainstream institutions, among them the Jewish Agency and AZEC [cite:wiki_hatzohar].

From this current came the armed organizations that would operate alongside, and often against, the Haganah, the mainstream's armed force, and the Jewish Agency: the Irgun and Lehi, whose story is told in [scene:s07e2|Irgun and Lehi: the paramilitary line]. From it, too, came a political party. Menachem Begin, a former Irgun commander, founded Herut on June 15, 1948, as a successor to the Revisionist paramilitary tradition. It was framed at the time as a challenge to Hatzohar, which after Israel's establishment was itself succeeded by and merged into Herut [cite:wiki_herut] [cite:wiki_hatzohar]. Herut merged with the Liberal Party in 1965 to form Gahal [cite:wiki_gahal]. In 1973 Gahal became the founding core of Likud, the party that has led or co-led Israeli governments for most of the past five decades, including under Netanyahu [cite:wiki_likud].

Political lineages are often asserted loosely, with one party called the "spiritual heir" of another. This one is a dated chain of foundings and mergers, traceable name by name. It is a lineage of institutions, not a claim that today's Likud holds Jabotinsky's original positions; parties change a great deal over a century. But it shows that the mainstream bodies, the World Zionist Organization under Weizmann, the Jewish Agency and AZEC, were never the only political current in Zionism. This was the other one. By the mid-1930s it was organized, international and in open competition.
