The Pure and the World

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The Cathars took their name, as their opponents used it, from the Greek for "pure", and they held that the material world was evil, the work of an evil principle, while the good God was spiritual. [cite:hr_brit_cathari] The Bogomils before them said the visible world was made by the devil. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] Their perfect, after the consolamentum, lived under strict ascetic rules. [cite:hr_brit_cathari]

The inner reading finds in this the same intuition as the Gnostic texts read earlier on this shelf: that the soul does not belong to the world it finds itself in, and that the rulers of this world are not its true home. [cite:vt24_apjohn] Read inwardly, the "world" the Cathars refused is not the earth but the system of compulsions, possessions and powers that binds attention to itself. The perfect's ascetic rule was a way of withdrawing attention from that system, much as the Hermetic soul gives back its borrowed energies.

The reading carries a warning, and this shelf has every reason to state it. A teaching that the world is evil can turn into contempt for the body and for life; the Bogomils condemned marriage. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] The other inner traditions on this shelf mostly chose differently: Kabbalah's sparks are hidden in ordinary things and gathered by ordinary acts; Hasidism placed closeness to God in the social sphere. [cite:kb_brit_luria] [cite:kb_brit_devequt] The inner reading takes the Cathars' refusal of the system, not their refusal of the earth.

The record limits the reading. As Part Six showed, historians disagree about how far the Cathars' beliefs are known from their own words and how far from their prosecutors'. [cite:hr_ihr_moore] The inner reading uses the beliefs as recorded and does not claim to know what each believer held.
