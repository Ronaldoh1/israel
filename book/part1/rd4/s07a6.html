The Peel Commission: Partition's First Draft

Britain's political answer to the revolt was a royal commission. The Palestine Royal Commission, known after its chairman as the Peel Commission, had six members. It was appointed in August 1936, while the general strike was still under way. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] Its task was to investigate the causes of the uprising. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

Its report, published in July 1937, went much further than an inquiry into causes. It proposed ending the Mandate. In its place there would be an independent Arab state, formed by joining part of Palestine to Transjordan, and an independent Jewish state in another part of the country. Jerusalem and some other places would stay under British mandate,, in a British-administered corridor that included the city. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_origins_yehudai] [cite:wiki_peel_commission] It was the first official proposal to partition the territory. The Jewish state would be small. The sources give its size differently: 17 percent of the country according to Ori Yehudai, about 20 percent according to Fred Strickert. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_origins_yehudai] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_ebsco_strickert] The sources consulted here do not explain the difference, and this book gives both figures.

The report did not stop at borders. Beside its partition map it recommended an "exchange of population," compulsory in the last resort. That recommendation, the first time population transfer appeared in an official British document, is the subject of the next section, [scene:pl_s01|Peel's "exchange of population"].

A common belief holds that partition was the United Nations' original idea in 1947, reached through a neutral international process. The record shows otherwise. A British royal commission proposed partition a decade earlier, and both sides had already responded to it, in different ways, before the United Nations took up the question. The UN committee's 1947 map bore a documented resemblance to this earlier partition thinking, which is examined in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the 1947 plan]. [cite:peel_report_1937] The resemblance is one of concept and structure. The two maps were not identical line for line, and the claim here is only that the idea came first from London.

Three responses mattered, and they were not alike.

The first came from the Twentieth Zionist Congress, which met in 1937. The movement was deeply divided over the plan, and the Congress produced a carefully balanced position. It rejected the proposed boundaries. It accepted partition in principle, or at least did not reject it, and it authorized continued negotiation with Britain. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_jvl_riots] [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] Historians do not settle on a single verb for this. Some write that the Congress "accepted" the principle of partition; others that it "did not reject it outright." [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] The difference matters for anyone who later summarizes 1937 in a sentence. A United Nations working paper of 1949 put it more starkly than either: it summarized the outcome as rejection of the plan by both Jews and Arabs. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] The strongest form of the "acceptance" reading is that the movement's leadership accepted the idea of a Jewish state in part of the country and wanted to bargain over its size. The strongest form of the "rejection" reading is that the Congress refused the plan actually on the table, since the boundaries were the plan. Both readings fit the resolution, which was built to allow both. This book does not choose between them.

The second response was the Arab Higher Committee's, and it was unambiguous. The committee, led by al-Husseini, rejected the plan outright, with no conditional language. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] His role in the violence of 1929 is examined in an earlier chapter. The committee's rejection was its own formal institutional position. It does not show that every person in the Arab population held identical views on partition, and the sources do not measure what others thought.

The third response came from the Revisionist movement. Ze'ev (Vladimir) Jabotinsky had founded the Union of Revisionist Zionists, Hatzohar, in 1925. Its core territorial claim was that the Jewish state must cover both banks of the Jordan River: what later became Israel, the West Bank and Gaza, and the whole of Transjordan. [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925] In 1929–1930 Jabotinsky put the argument in geographical terms:

"What does the word Palestine mean? Palestine is a territory whose chief geographical feature is this: that the River Jordan does not delineate its frontier, but flows through its center."

The movement's anthem made the same claim in verse: "Two Banks has the Jordan — This is ours and, that is as well." [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925] The claim was territorial and historical rather than primarily religious; Jabotinsky was not himself religious. He argued that Britain's 1922 separation of Transjordan from the Palestine Mandate had been an arbitrary partition, one that denied Jews the land needed to absorb millions of future immigrants. [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925] Testifying before the Peel Commission, he argued forcefully against any partition and for Jewish rights to the whole of Mandate Palestine, Transjordan included. [cite:wiki_jabotinsky] This was a consistent position, given on the record to the commission itself. It was not the mainstream Zionist position of 1937, which, as the Congress's resolution shows, was far more ambiguous. The later history of this claim, through Herut and Likud, is traced in [scene:gia_src_s02|the versions of "Greater Israel"].

The plan's effect on the ground was immediate. According to Unpacked and the Jewish Virtual Library, Peel's partition proposal prompted rebel groups to restart the revolt in the autumn of 1937. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unpacked] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_jvl_riots] The second phase of the uprising, told in the previous section, began within weeks of the report.

The 1937 rejection is often used as evidence for a later slogan: that the Arabs "rejected every offer." What the record shows is narrower. Arab leaders did reject the 1937 Peel plan, and later the 1947 partition plan. But the slogan compresses a record that runs in two directions. Israel's 1949 refugee offer at Lausanne was made only within a full peace and was later withdrawn. The 2000 Camp David offer is disputed by participants, and some on the American side say it was never written down. Offers in this history were conditional, and both sides refused at different points. Peel, in particular, was refused by both: outright by the Arab Higher Committee, and in its actual boundaries by the Zionist Congress. The slogan is short, and the history behind it is longer and less flattering to whichever side repeats it.

Some questions the record here does not settle. The precise share of the country allotted to the Jewish state differs between sources, and the boundaries of the plan are not set out in the material drawn on for this section. The documents do not say how the Congress's delegates divided, beyond the description of the movement as deeply divided. Nor do they settle whether "acceptance in principle" was a real commitment or a bargaining position. What is firm is the sequence. In 1937 Britain put partition on paper for the first time. The Arab Higher Committee rejected it, the Zionist Congress refused its borders while keeping the idea alive, and Jabotinsky demanded the whole territory on both sides of the river. Within eighteen months, as the next sections show, London abandoned the plan.
