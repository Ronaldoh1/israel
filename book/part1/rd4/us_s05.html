Palestine Enters the Party Platforms, 1944

In 1944, an American presidential election year, Palestine became a campaign issue. The usual account places American political action on Palestine after the war, as a considered response to its outcome. The records of the American Zionist Emergency Council show the question already woven into the election of 1944: in Congress, at both party conventions and in the calendar of the campaign itself. They also show the military and diplomatic arms of the government pulling the other way.

The year opened with a resolution. In early 1944 Senators Robert Wagner, a New York Democrat, and Robert Taft, an Ohio Republican, introduced a resolution calling for unrestricted Jewish immigration to Palestine and for a Jewish commonwealth there. It ran into the War Department. Secretary of War Henry Stimson opposed it on military grounds, and it was deferred [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:aja_azec_1944_record].

The conventions moved where Congress had paused. In the summer of 1944 both the Democratic and the Republican conventions adopted planks on Palestine. The council's own internal appraisal of the year recorded the result as "the first time in American history that Palestine was included in the party platforms" [cite:aja_azec_appraisal_1944]. The phrase comes from the organizers themselves, and it shows how they understood the achievement. Palestine had become a party question in the United States, written into both platforms, four years before there was a state.

Then came a turn that tied the resolution directly to the election calendar. Days before the November 1944 election, the War Department withdrew its objection, in a letter from Stimson to Senator Taft. The House Foreign Affairs Committee approved the resolution on 30 November [cite:aja_azec_dec1944] [cite:aja_azec_1944_record]. The timing of the withdrawal, so close to the vote, is a matter of record. The reasons behind it are taken up in [scene:cg_s01|the section on Congress], where Stimson's letter is described as weighing political considerations against military ones.

The reprieve was brief. In December 1944 action was deferred again, this time at the insistence of the State Department. The second deferral set off a crisis inside the council itself [cite:aja_azec_dec1944]. A version of the resolution finally passed Congress in December 1945, with the support of the American Palestine Committee and the Christian Council [cite:wiki_american_palestine_committee].

The pattern of 1944 is worth fixing in mind, because it recurs. Palestine had become a question of party platforms and election timing. On one side stood the organized campaign, with bipartisan sponsors in the Senate and planks in both platforms. On the other stood the War Department and the State Department, raising military and diplomatic objections and twice holding the resolution back. Four years later, when President Truman faced the decisions of 1947 and 1948, the same alignment would reappear, with the military and diplomatic bureaucracies again on the other side.
