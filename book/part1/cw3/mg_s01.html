Kafr Qasim, 29 October 1956

On the afternoon that Israel's army crossed into Sinai, a curfew was imposed on a row of Arab villages inside Israel, close to the line with Jordan. By half past six that evening, at the western entrance to one of them, Kafr Qasim, a Border Police platoon had shot dead dozens of the village's own people as they came home from work. They were Israeli citizens. The regime under which they lived, the military government that ruled most of Israel's Arab citizens from 1949 to December 1966 through permits, curfews and closed areas, is told in Volume II in [scene:rd6_military_government|the Arabs who stayed, and the military government], and is not retold here. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] This section follows one evening of it: how an order was given, how it travelled down a chain of command, what it produced at one village and not at others, and how the state kept the result from its own public for weeks.

The familiar account holds that the Arab citizens of Israel have enjoyed equal civil rights since 1948. They did vote from the first election, and Volume II gives that fact its due. What happened at Kafr Qasim shows the other half of their position in 1956. A curfew could be laid on their villages by a military commander, at half an hour's notice, under a regime that applied to them and not to their Jewish neighbours, and those who enforced it were armed.

The villages and the curfew. The villages concerned lay in what was called the "Little Triangle," a strip of land near the Jordanian line that Jordan had ceded to Israel under the 1949 armistice. Palestinian citizens of Israel then numbered about 160,000, and most of them lived under the military government. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] Eight months earlier, in February 1956, the Rattner Commission had concluded that the regime should not end until "true peace" existed; the government's case for it was security, while the historian Alina Korn has argued that its main purposes were controlling Arab residents' movement and keeping them from confiscated land. [cite:rs_rd6_korn_adalah] On 29 October 1956, the first day of the war, the Triangle villages were placed under a wartime curfew running from 5 p.m. to 6 a.m. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq]

The timing was the heart of what followed. According to the Jewish Virtual Library's account, the order reached the Border Police at 3:30 p.m., before most villagers could be told of it, and it stated that "any Arab on the streets was to be shot." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] At Kafr Qasim, according to PalQuest, the Institute for Palestine Studies' encyclopedia, and Wikipedia's account of the trial record, a sergeant told the village's mukhtar, its headman, at 4:30 p.m. that the curfew would begin at 5 p.m. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The historian Shira Robinson describes a curfew announced only thirty minutes before it began. [cite:rs_cw3_robinson_2003] On these accounts, villagers already out at work had no way of knowing that the hour at which they would be outside their homes had become a crime.

The chain of command. The man who changed the curfew hours was Issachar Shadmi, the brigade commander. The sources differ on his rank: Wikipedia's account of the trial record gives him as a colonel, other summaries as a brigadier. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] Below him was Major Shmuel Malinki, commanding the Border Police unit, and below Malinki the platoon at Kafr Qasim, under Lieutenant Gabriel Dahan. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq]

What passed between Shadmi and Malinki is the most important single question about the evening, and the two men gave different answers. Malinki testified that he asked how villagers returning from work, unaware of the curfew, were to be treated, and that Shadmi told him: "I don't want sentimentality and I don't want arrests." Malinki then relayed the order to his men in these words: "Anyone leaving his home shall be shot; there shall be no arrests." [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The summary of the court record that underlies this library's account gives Shadmi's reply as being that anyone who left his house would be shot. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] Shadmi, for his part, denied that the question of villagers returning from work had come up at all. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] These are testimonies, given by men whose own liability turned on them, and they are given here as such, from Wikipedia's summary of the trial record rather than from the transcript itself.

The fields. When the sergeant gave the mukhtar his half-hour's warning, the mukhtar raised the obvious difficulty: some 400 villagers were still out in the fields. He was told that they would be taken care of. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq]

Five to half past six. Between 5 and 6:30 p.m., in nine separate shooting incidents at the western entrance to the village, Dahan's platoon shot workers as they returned, on foot, on bicycles and in trucks. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] The village lay about a kilometre from the Green Line. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] The people killed were coming home after a curfew they had not been told of. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60]

The villages where no one was shot. Kafr Qasim was not the only village under the order. The same curfew lay on the other villages under Shadmi's command, and in them no villagers were shot, because the local commanders did not carry out the order as Malinki's unit at Kafr Qasim did. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] Of the three units given the order, two disregarded it. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] That fact cuts in two directions, and both belong in the account. It shows that the order could be read, by officers on the same evening and under the same pressures, as one not to be obeyed literally; that bears on the standard of manifest illegality the court would later set out. And it shows that the outcome at Kafr Qasim depended on choices made at the bottom of the chain as well as at the top.

How many died. The number of dead is given differently in different sources, and the differences are small but should not be hidden. Forty-seven is the figure in the Jewish Virtual Library, the Encyclopaedia Judaica, Jonathan Cook's 2008 article and a JTA report of 2016. Forty-eight is the figure in a JTA dispatch from Jerusalem in November 1957, which spoke of "the murder of 48 Arabs," in Shira Robinson's scholarship ("the border-patrol killing of 48 Palestinian citizens") and in the Times of Israel in 2021, which counted an unborn child as a forty-ninth. Forty-nine is the figure in Kafr Qasim's own commemoration, in a JTA report of 1999, in Ynet in 2014, in the Jewish Virtual Library's page of Knesset highlights, and in President Herzog's words in 2021. Middle East Eye gives "fifty." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] [cite:rs_cw3_ej_kafr_qasim] [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] [cite:rs_cw3_stljl_60th] [cite:rs_cw3_djn_comp] [cite:rs_cw3_robinson_2003] [cite:rs_cw3_toi_herzog] [cite:rs_cw3_jta_1999] [cite:rs_cw3_ynet_rivlin] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_knesset3] [cite:rs_cw3_mee_kq]

The gap between the counts lies mainly in one death. Among the dead was a pregnant woman, and whether her unborn child is counted separately makes the difference between forty-eight and forty-nine; the remaining variation comes largely from the way sources round. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] The breakdown most often given is 19 men, 6 women and 23 children, the children aged between 8 and 17; those figures sum to forty-eight, and the count of forty-nine adds the unborn child. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] Ynet adds that 13 people were wounded. [cite:rs_cw3_ynet_rivlin] This book uses forty-nine, the village's own figure, while noting that serious sources, among them the Encyclopaedia Judaica and Robinson's scholarship, give forty-seven or forty-eight.

The silence. The killings happened on the first evening of a war. According to the Jewish Virtual Library, news of the killings was censored and the public learned of them only weeks later. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] PalQuest records that the government and the army used "a variety of tactics" to conceal what had happened, and that journalists, activists and Communist members of the Knesset nevertheless reached the village through the checkpoints. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq]

The sequence of the following weeks, as Wikipedia's account gives it, ran as follows. An internal inquiry began on 1 November. About two weeks after the killings, two Communist Knesset members, Tawfik Toubi and Meir Vilner, entered the village. Ben-Gurion first referred to the incident in public on 12 November. It then took about two months of lobbying before the blackout on the press was lifted. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] Jonathan Cook reports a remark of Ben-Gurion's from that time: "those who gave the order will get a strict verdict. I don't think the soldiers are guilty." [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] The court's outcome ran the other way: the soldiers and their officers were convicted, while Shadmi, tried separately, was fined a token sum, as the next section shows.

Was it ordered from above? This is the question every reader asks, and the record of the evening itself answers it only in part. It establishes that the curfew and its timing came from the brigade commander, Shadmi; that Malinki testified Shadmi told him he wanted no sentimentality and no arrests; that Malinki passed down an order to shoot anyone leaving home; and that Shadmi denied the question of returning workers was ever raised. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] It establishes that two of the three units given the order did not shoot. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] On that record the order to shoot came at least from Malinki, the officer above the platoon, and the words that framed it are attributed to the brigade commander by Malinki's testimony, which Shadmi denied. Whether anything higher stood behind it, in particular a contingency plan for the Triangle's population in wartime known as Plan Mole, became a separate argument decades later, when trial records were released in 2022; that argument, and what the released records do and do not show, is set out in [scene:cw3_kafr_qasim_memory|the sulha, Plan Mole and the memory of Kafr Qasim].

Why it mattered. One argument about Kafr Qasim, made in this library's own summary of the period, holds that before Israel governed a single Palestinian in the West Bank or Gaza it had already run, for eighteen years, a separate regime for Palestinian citizens inside its own borders, with the absentee laws keeping them from their property and a massacre whose commander, as the next section shows, paid a token fine; on this reading the 1967 occupation inherited a model rather than inventing one. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60] That is an interpretation of the record, not a finding within it, and Volume II's account of the military government sets out the security case on which the government defended the regime. What the evening of 29 October itself shows is narrower and firmer: a curfew imposed on citizens with half an hour's warning, an order that at least one officer relayed as an order to shoot, and a Border Police platoon that carried it out against people walking, cycling and riding home from work.

The record leaves several things open. The trial transcript itself has not been read for this section; the testimony of Malinki and Shadmi is given from a reference summary of it. Shadmi's rank differs between the sources. The precise wording of the order that reached the Border Police at 3:30 p.m. is known only through the Jewish Virtual Library's paraphrase and Malinki's version of it. And why the platoon at Kafr Qasim fired where the units in neighbouring villages did not is not explained by any source used here, beyond the fact that their commanders chose differently. The court that tried the men, the sentences it gave and what became of them are told in [scene:cw3_kafr_qasim_trial|the Kafr Qasim trial].
