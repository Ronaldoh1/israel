Part Twelve separates the two measures the Dream tends to blur: income, what a household earns in a year, and wealth, what it owns minus what it owes. It sets the Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances beside its quarterly Distributional Financial Accounts to show how far apart the two gaps are, by race, age and education and between the top 1 per cent and the bottom half, and it follows the arithmetic by which owned assets compound while wages do not. It then turns to what families inherit, how much inheritance explains, and the tax rules that decide how much of a large fortune passes on untaxed: the estate tax, now $15 million per person, the step-up in basis and the strategy known as "buy, borrow, die". It ends with the housing crash of 2008, when the middle class's main asset was lost on a scale the record can measure, and asks who was charged more for their loans, who lost their homes and who was rescued.
