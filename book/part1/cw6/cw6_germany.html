Bonn's Secret Tanks and the Road to Recognition, 1964–1965

Al-Ard's members were contending with their own state; Israel's government, in the same years, was looking abroad for arms, and not only to Washington. West Germany's material link to Israel began with the reparations agreement signed at Luxembourg in 1952, told in [scene:rd6_financing|financing the new state], and it grew a second, secret strand from 1957, when Shimon Peres and Franz Josef Strauss first met at Strauss's home in Rott am Inn, as Stage Four recounts. This section follows that secret strand to the point where it broke into the open. Between October 1964 and August 1965 a covert tank transfer, arranged among Bonn, Washington and Jerusalem, was exposed, cancelled and converted into cash, and the crisis it set off ended in full diplomatic relations between the Federal Republic and Israel.

The secret channel. According to Marcel Serr, writing in 2015 in Aus Politik und Zeitgeschichte, the journal of Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education, Peres and Strauss first met secretly in the winter of 1957, and in August 1962 Chancellor Adenauer approved a secret Israeli wish list worth 240 million DM that included tanks. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] The arrangement had no public footing. Bonn had no embassy in Israel. What gave the channel its larger significance in 1964 was Washington. Early that year, after an interagency review, the Johnson administration had declined to sell tanks to Israel directly, citing its policy of not becoming a major arms supplier to the region, but had agreed to help Israel obtain them from Europe. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_a] Germany was the European source that was found, and that decision is the background of [scene:cw6_johnson_eshkol|Eshkol's visit to Washington in June 1964].

What Rusk told Johnson, October 1964. The shape of the deal is set out in a memorandum the Secretary of State, Rusk, wrote for President Johnson in October 1964 under the title "FY-1965 Help to Israel," sent with a covering note from three White House aides, Feldman, Bundy and Komer. Rusk told the President that Israel would receive 150 M48A1 tanks from West Germany "as is," together with about 250 British Centurions. The covering memo described the German part as "150 German M–48s, with an option on 82 more." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99]

The United States did not stand aside. Under the arrangement Washington sold Israel kits to modernize the German M48s, worth about $24 million, on Military Assistance Program credit: ten years at 3.5 percent interest, with 10 percent down. "In the interests of secrecy," the tanks were to be fitted in Italy with diesel engines and larger guns. Washington also speeded deliveries of replacement American tanks to West Germany so that the German ones could be released. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] On one reading, the arrangement was built to keep the United States from appearing as a supplier of tanks to Israel, while American credit, American kits and American replacement tanks made the transfer possible.

The secrecy was also written into the paperwork as a condition. A letter exchange of 15 November 1964 provided that if the transaction became public before the United States judged secrecy unnecessary, the United States "might exercise its right of cancellation." [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] Washington had kept the right to walk away if the arrangement came to light.

The leak and the Hallstein trap. The secrecy did not hold. According to the German Federal Archives, the deliveries became public in October 1964; Serr dates the first Egyptian press reports to the autumn of that year. [cite:rs_cw6_barch_triangle] [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] The exposure put Bonn in a bind of its own making, and the mechanism that made it a bind was the Hallstein Doctrine. In force since 1955, it held that recognition of the German Democratic Republic by a third state was an unfriendly act to which Bonn could respond by breaking relations. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] [cite:rs_cw6_barch_triangle] The doctrine was West Germany's main instrument for keeping East Germany isolated. In effect, it also gave every government that Bonn wished to keep from recognizing East Berlin a lever over Bonn: the threat to do exactly that.

Nasser used the lever. In January 1965 he announced that Walter Ulbricht, East Germany's head of state, would visit Egypt. Serr records that after the announcement Chancellor Erhard's government said it would stop arms deliveries to areas of tension, Israel included. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] The sequence suggests that Bonn gave up the tanks in the hope of keeping Cairo from a step toward East Germany.

It did not keep it. Ulbricht arrived in Egypt on 24 February 1965 and was received with military honours, though without formal recognition of the GDR. Bonn then cut its economic aid to Egypt. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] The sources differ by a day on when he left: Serr gives 1 March 1965, while the Konrad Adenauer Foundation and the Federal Archives give 2 March. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] [cite:rs_cw6_barch_triangle] The difference does not affect the sequence. What matters is that Nasser had stopped short of formal recognition of the GDR, and Bonn had lost its tank deliveries and its standing in Cairo all the same.

Erhard's decision, 7 March 1965. Erhard's answer was to turn the crisis around. On 7 March 1965 the Chancellor announced immediate negotiations with Israel on diplomatic relations. According to the Konrad Adenauer Foundation, he used his authority to set policy guidelines and did so without first informing the cabinet or his Foreign Minister, Schröder. His own emissary to Israel, Kurt Birrenbach, learned of the decision from the Israelis in Tel Aviv. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] The decision was a Chancellor's, taken over the heads of the ministers who would have to carry it out, and it reversed a policy that had kept Bonn and Jerusalem without formal ties for more than a decade while money and arms moved between them.

From tanks to cash. The undelivered arms did not simply vanish from the books. Serr writes that they were converted into a payment of 140 million DM, which allowed Israel to buy weapons elsewhere. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] Only 40 of the German tanks had been delivered by then. The gap was filled by Washington. In July 1965 the United States agreed to sell Israel 110 M48A2C tanks to replace the rest, together with kits to convert the 40 that had arrived. [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965] That sale, which the Jewish Virtual Library, citing the Israel State Archives, presents as the first US agreement to sell Israel tanks directly, is told in [scene:cw6_tanks|the first direct tank sale]. [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_mou_1965] The secrecy clause of November 1964 had been designed to keep Washington's role hidden. When the secret broke, the outcome was the reverse: the United States became the direct supplier it had tried not to be, though still in secret.

Recognition, 12 May 1965. Diplomatic relations were established on 12 May 1965. The first West German ambassador, Rolf Pauls, presented his credentials to President Shazar on 19 August 1965. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] [cite:rs_cw6_barch_triangle] The arrival of a German ambassador in Israel was the formal close of the arrangement in which reparations and weapons had moved without an embassy on either side.

The Arab response is recorded differently in the two sources consulted for it, and the difference is one of both number and kind. Serr writes that thirteen Arab states withdrew their ambassadors from Bonn immediately after relations were established. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] GlobalSecurity's history of Jordan says that in May 1965 Jordan joined nine other Arab states in breaking relations with West Germany, which makes ten. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_g] Withdrawing an ambassador and breaking relations are not the same act, and it is possible that both accounts are accurate for different groups of states; neither source consulted here lists the states by name, so the two figures cannot be reconciled from them. The direction of the response is not in doubt: the Hallstein Doctrine had been built to let Bonn punish governments that recognized East Germany, and in 1965 it was Arab governments that withdrew from Bonn because Bonn had recognized Israel.

Two readings of the turn. The documents support two ways of reading what happened, and they are not exclusive. From Bonn's side, the record is of a government losing control of a covert policy: a secret deal leaked, a doctrine designed to isolate East Germany was turned against Bonn, and the Chancellor recovered the initiative only by acting over his cabinet. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations] [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] From Israel's side, the same months turned a covert supply relationship, which could be halted when it became embarrassing (and was), into open relations with a state that was still paying it reparations, together with 140 million DM to buy arms elsewhere and a direct American tank sale. [cite:rs_cw6_serr_bpb] [cite:rs_cw6_komer_jul1965] Egypt, for its part, showed that Bonn's arms to Israel could be made to cost Bonn; but Ulbricht's reception stopped short of the formal recognition that would have triggered the Hallstein Doctrine against Cairo. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations]

The reparations running out. Recognition came as the 1952 agreement was ending. Under the Luxembourg Agreement, as the Federal Agency for Civic Education summarizes it, West Germany committed 3 billion DM to Israel, 450 million DM to the Claims Conference and a special fund of 50 million DM, paid partly in German goods such as ships, locomotives and factory machinery. [cite:rs_cw6_bpb_lux] In March 1965, in the middle of the crisis, the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported from Bonn that 1965–66 was the agreement's last year. Israel was to receive 300 million marks ($74 million) of goods that year, which would bring the goods it had received to 2.4 billion marks ($600 million). [cite:rs_cw6_jta_reparations_1965] That report does not reconcile with the 3 billion DM headline: it leaves 600 million DM unaccounted for, and the dispatch does not say how the difference was settled or whether it reflects a different way of counting. No German or Israeli government record of the final settlement was consulted for this book, so the question stays open here.

What the record does not settle. Several things remain unresolved in the sources used here. The October 1964 plan spoke of about 250 British Centurions alongside the German tanks, and of an option on 82 more M48s; the documents consulted do not say what became of either. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_d99] The two accounts of the Arab response give different numbers for different acts and do not name the states. The end of the reparations is known only from a press report whose total does not match the agreement's. And the reasoning behind Erhard's decision of 7 March is recorded here only in its form, as an exercise of the Chancellor's guideline authority without prior consultation, not in his own explanation of why he chose recognition rather than further retreat. [cite:rs_cw6_kas_relations]
