The Year the Democracy Debated Itself, and the War That Followed

The absence of a constitution was an abstract question until 2023, when a government set out to use it. The coalition formed after the November 2022 election introduced a programme to change the balance between the Knesset and the Supreme Court, and for the next nine months the country argued about what its democracy was. Then, on 7 October, the war began, and the question of who decides moved from the courts to the cabinet room. This section follows both arguments, because together they show where power sits in the Israeli state when it is tested.

The reasonableness law. The part of the programme that became law was an amendment to a Basic Law removing the courts' power to void government and ministerial decisions they judged "unreasonable," a standard Israeli courts had used since the 1980s; it had recently been used to strike down the appointment as a minister of a Netanyahu ally who had pleaded guilty to tax offences. [cite:judicial_cnn] The Knesset passed it on 24 July 2023 by 64 votes to none, after the opposition walked out. [cite:judicial_cnn] The protests against the programme ran for forty consecutive weeks, from January to October 2023, in some 150 towns and cities; military reservists, doctors and business leaders joined them. [cite:judicial_protests_wiki]

The court's answer. On 1 January 2024 the Supreme Court struck the amendment down by eight votes to seven. It was the first time the court had ever annulled a Basic Law, and the majority called the amendment a "severe and unprecedented blow to the core characteristics of the State of Israel as a democratic state." [cite:judicial_cnn] The government called the ruling an overreach. [cite:judicial_cnn] The decision settled the case but not the question underneath it: in a state whose fundamental laws can be changed by a simple majority, can judges strike down a fundamental law? An eight-to-seven court had said yes, once; the coalition that had passed the law said no; and no text existed that could decide between them.

Three cabinets, one war. After the Hamas attack of 7 October 2023, war decisions ran through three bodies at once. The full government kept its authority; the standing security cabinet, which includes the prime minister, senior ministers and, as advisers, the heads of the army, the Mossad and the Shin Bet, kept its own; and on 11 October a war cabinet was formed, of Netanyahu, the opposition leader Benny Gantz and Defence Minister Yoav Gallant, approved by the Knesset 66 to 4 on 12 October. [cite:warcabinet_wiki] [cite:security_cabinet_oct7] The Israel Democracy Institute described the overlap as a "double cabinet" problem; when the war cabinet authorized aid convoys into Gaza, Finance Minister Smotrich insisted the decision needed the full cabinet. [cite:warcabinet_idi] On 9 June 2024 Gantz resigned, citing the absence of any post-war plan for Gaza, and on 17 June Netanyahu dissolved the war cabinet. [cite:warcabinet_gantz] War decisions moved to the security cabinet, a body that includes the far-right ministers who had been kept out of the war cabinet. [cite:warcabinet_idi]

Civilians, soldiers and the line between them. The record also shows the army pushing back. In August 2025, when the security cabinet approved Netanyahu's plan to take Gaza City, Israeli officials told reporters that the military's leadership had strongly resisted a full reoccupation; the army's spokesman said the vote would not affect the chain of command. [cite:security_cabinet_newsweek] The pattern of 2023, a governing coalition pressing against an institution that answers to no voter, recurred in a different form: the elected cabinet decided, and the professional military complied while making its objections known.

What the two arguments show. In 2023 the argument was whether a parliamentary majority should be able to override the courts; in 2024 and 2025 it was who in the executive should decide the war. In both, the decisive fact was the coalition. The judicial programme existed because the coalition's partners wanted it, and war decisions moved to the security cabinet because that was where those partners sat. The same structural point runs through the rest of this part: in Israel's system, the composition of a 61-seat majority decides questions that a written constitution would otherwise settle.
