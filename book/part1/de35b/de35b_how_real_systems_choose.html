How Real Systems Choose and Remove

The design described in the previous section has no working example; Land's quoted passage concedes that the full approach "has never been tried." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Pieces of it have been tried, however. Companies have governed territories, thrones have been bought, families have chosen heirs by council, parties have chosen leaders behind closed doors, and corporate boards fire chief executives every year. Each of these systems had to answer the question the texts leave open: who removes a ruler, and with what power behind them. [scene:de8_tests_it_fails|Part Eight] tested the texts' named models, Singapore and Dubai, and the record of the company-states. This section looks only at the mechanics of choosing and removing, case by case, and at where each failed.

A company that ruled: the East India Company

The nearest historical match to "shareholders elect a board" is the English East India Company, which governed much of the Indian subcontinent. Its shareholders, the Court of Proprietors, elected a Court of Directors of 24 members, six of whom rotated out every April. [cite:de35b_banglapedia_cod] The arrangement did not hold on its own terms. After the Company's misgovernment of Bengal brought it to the edge of bankruptcy, Parliament intervened with the Regulating Act of 1773, which among other things doubled the stock needed to vote. [cite:de8_brit_regact] Pitt's India Act of 1784 went further: it created a parliamentary Board of Control whose consent was needed for revenue and political decisions, and after it the proprietors could discuss the directors' policies but no longer annul them. [cite:de35b_banglapedia_cod] The Court of Directors was abolished in 1858, when the Crown took over the Company's powers. [cite:de35b_banglapedia_cod] [cite:de8_brit_goi] In the one long test of shareholder sovereignty, the shareholders' vote was progressively narrowed and the final check came from outside the company, from a Parliament answerable to an electorate.

A throne for sale: Rome, 193

The texts propose that power should be openly owned and transferable. The Roman Empire once made its highest office transferable for cash, briefly. On 28 March 193, after the murder of the emperor Pertinax, the Praetorian Guard sold its support to the highest bidder. Didius Julianus outbid Sulpicianus, the dead emperor's father-in-law, by offering the largest payment to the soldiers, and was made emperor. He reigned about two months. The legions on the Danube marched on Italy under their commander Septimius Severus, and Julianus was killed in June 193. [cite:de35b_brit_julianus] The episode does not test neocameralism, which proposes shares rather than a one-off auction, but it shows the problem raised in Part Eight in its plainest form: when an office can be bought, the people with weapons decide whether the purchase stands.

Choosing by lot to defeat bribery: Venice, 1268

One long-lived republic designed its selection rule specifically against the buying of office. From 1268 the Venetian doge was chosen through ten rounds that alternated drawing names by lot with voting: colleges of 30, 40, 25 and 45 were drawn by lot, and colleges of 9, 12, 9, 11 and finally 41 were elected, the last needing 25 votes to name the doge. In a 2007 analysis, the computer scientists Miranda Mowbray and Dieter Gollmann noted that the lot made it hard for would-be bribers to predict who the final electors would be. [cite:de35b_mowbray_doge] Venice's electorate was an oligarchy of noble families, not a people. The contrast with the texts is still instructive. Yarvin's "pick one at random" was an argument about the competence of chief executives; Venice used chance for the opposite purpose, to keep any single faction or purse from capturing the choice. [cite:de35b_nyt] [cite:de35b_mowbray_doge]

Family councils and a parliament: Saudi Arabia and Kuwait

Hereditary monarchies in the Gulf, the region the texts point to, choose heirs by decree and family council. On 21 June 2017 King Salman of Saudi Arabia named his son Mohammed bin Salman, then 31, crown prince, removing his nephew Mohammed bin Nayef, 57, as heir and as interior minister. The Allegiance Council, made up of senior members of the ruling family, endorsed the change by 31 of its 34 members. [cite:de35b_aj_mbs] The council confirmed a decision; there is no record of it overturning one.

Kuwait offers a different case: a hereditary ruler removed by an elected body. On 24 January 2006 Kuwait's 65-member parliament voted unanimously, at the government's request, to remove Emir Sheikh Saad al-Abdullah al-Sabah, whom the government said had "lost his health capability" to exercise his constitutional powers; removal required a two-thirds majority. His letter of abdication arrived just after the vote, and the prime minister, Sheikh Sabah al-Ahmad al-Sabah, succeeded him. [cite:de35b_mg_kuwait] [cite:de35b_jurist_kuwait] Even within a dynasty, the removal of an incapable ruler ran through a constitution and a vote.

The party state: China and Singapore

One-party systems choose leaders inside the party, and their stability depends on the party's own rules. In 1982 China capped the presidency at two consecutive terms, a limit associated with Deng Xiaoping and intended to prevent a return to the one-man rule of the Mao years. On 11 March 2018 the National People's Congress removed the limit by 2,958 votes to 2, with 3 abstentions; Xi Jinping had not named a successor at the end of his first term, breaking with recent practice. [cite:de35b_npr_xi] The rule that had governed succession was changed by the body it was meant to bind, which is the failure Land's quoted passage predicts for constitutions generally. [cite:de8_land_de]

Singapore, the texts' own favourite example, works differently. Its leaders change by party procedure: Lawrence Wong became prime minister on 15 May 2024, chosen as leader of the People's Action Party with the unanimous backing of its members of parliament, and the political scientist Netina Tan describes the system's resilience as resting on a planned succession. [cite:de8_cnbc_wong] [cite:de8_tan_pap] Singapore also holds general elections, though Freedom House describes politics there as dominated by the People's Action Party and the Lee family since 1959. [cite:de8_fh_sg2026] Its example shows a rule for succession, not a joint-stock sovereign.

Corporate boards: OpenAI and the founders' trust

The design borrows its vocabulary from the corporate board, so the board's own record is relevant. On 17 November 2023 the board of OpenAI dismissed its chief executive, Sam Altman, saying he had not been "consistently candid in his communications with the board." Nearly all of the company's roughly 800 employees signed a letter threatening to leave for Microsoft unless the board resigned and Altman returned. On 21 November the company announced that he would come back, with a largely new board. [cite:de35b_gma_altman] A board formally empowered to fire its chief executive did so, and was itself replaced within five days by the pressure of the people the executive led and the partner on whom the company depended.

Companies controlled by the network this book maps show the opposite design: founders shielding themselves from shareholders. Palantir's Class F shares are held in a voting trust established by Stephen Cohen, Alexander Karp and Peter Thiel, which gives the founders the ability to control up to 49.999999 per cent of the total voting power, subject to a minimum holding. [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026] [scene:de27_origins_and_control|Part Twenty-Nine] explains the structure. It is a lawful and disclosed arrangement in a company, not a state; it shows that those who build such companies often choose not to be removable by ordinary shareholders.

What the cases have in common

The following is a reading of the cases, not a finding from any one of them. In every case where a ruler was actually removed against his will or without his initiative, the removal came from someone with power of their own: a Parliament with authority over the company's charter, an army, an elected chamber operating under a constitution, or employees and a commercial partner whose departure would have emptied the company. Where no such independent power existed or chose to act, as in the Saudi council or China's congress in 2018, the body that was supposed to check the ruler confirmed the ruler's wishes. The texts name shareholders as the check, but they do not say where shareholders' power would come from if the chief executive commands the police and the army, which is the gap Part Eight identified. [scene:de8_who_enforces_the_charter|Part Eight] The record does not show that shareholders, as such, have ever removed a sovereign ruler.
