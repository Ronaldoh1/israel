Land and Labour, 1922–1933

The findings London adopted and then explained away in 1930–31 were findings about land. Two opposite simplifications surround the land of Mandate Palestine. One holds that the British government sold the country's land to Zionist buyers with little scrutiny or resistance. The other holds that Jewish land purchase was small and sporadic before 1948. The record of the 1920s and early 1930s answers both. The land was not, in the main, sold by Britain. It was bought, privately and lawfully, from landowners, most of them absentees, by Zionist institutions that held it permanently and leased it under terms, in the Jewish National Fund's draft lease, that required Jewish labor. The Mandate tried to protect the tenants who lost their land and failed, and its own inquiries of 1930 measured the result. Resistance was real and documented, in courts and in protest, though it rarely stopped a sale once begun [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] [cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith].

The legal ground for all of it was older than the Mandate. Under Ottoman law, tenant farmers who had worked land for generations usually held no registered title; when the registered owner sold, the tenant had no ownership claim to assert, so a lawful sale and a lawful eviction could be the same transaction [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine] [cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws]. How that law worked, how the Ottoman restrictions on Jewish purchase were met and circumvented, and how the purchasing institutions were built before 1914 are told in [scene:s04j|the land purchase mechanism].

The largest single seller was a family, not a government. The Sursocks of Beirut, a wealthy Greek Orthodox Christian family, had acquired great tracts of the Jezreel Valley under Ottoman rule; the Ottoman government itself sold them Marj ibn Amir in 1872. Between 1901 and 1925, and mainly in the 1920s, the family sold roughly 60,000 to 75,000 dunams to Zionist purchasing organizations, for a fraction of the land's later value [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:sursockhouse_rothschild_purchases]. The main sales fell between 1920 and 1925. The Sursock purchases alone account for about 22 percent of all the land Jews acquired in Palestine up to 1948, and 58 percent of all the land bought from absentee landlords [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases].

The buyers were three institutions with distinct roles. The Jewish National Fund held purchased land as the inalienable property of the Jewish people. The Palestine Land Development Company did the actual purchasing, led by Yehoshua Hankin, who had worked with the Sursock family since 1891, personally negotiated the Sursock deal and most of the major Jezreel Valley acquisitions, and became head of the company in 1932. The Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, PICA, founded in 1924 by Edmond de Rothschild, became through parallel acquisitions the largest single Jewish landowner in Palestine [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]. By the early Mandate years land sold by absentee owners to Jewish buyers went for roughly six Palestine pounds a dunam. Purchasing agents such as Hankin sometimes bought first, at their own financial risk, and were reimbursed by the Jewish Agency or the Fund afterward [cite:emory_zionist_land_acquisition] [cite:palquest_wzo_funding]. The banks and campaigns that supplied the capital are described in the section on the Mandate economy.

What a purchase cost the people living on the land is documented for one group of sales. In the largest single group of Sursock-related purchases, 1,746 Arab farming families, 8,730 people, were evicted. Under the tenure system they were owed no compensation, but they were paid about $17 a person as a goodwill settlement. Across all the Sursock-linked purchases, an estimated 20 to 25 villages were depopulated [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin]. The sources do not date this group of evictions within the sale period, and the figure of $17 describes that group only, not every transaction. Selling land to Jewish buyers was seen by some Arabs as a serious betrayal. In at least one documented case, negotiations stalled for years because the selling landowners did not want to be seen as responsible for the evacuation [cite:wiki_sursock_purchases] [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin].

The Jewish National Fund turned each purchase into a permanent holding. Its land was leased long-term rather than sold, so that no later transaction could reverse an acquisition; its founding resolution called the land "the eternal possession of the Jewish people" [cite:jnf_wiki]. The Fund's own history, from its founding in 1901, is told in [scene:s04d|the Jewish National Fund]. Its holdings in these years show the scale. It held 22,363 dunams in 1920. By 1935 it held about 89,500 acres, some 362 square kilometres, across 108 communities [cite:jnf_wiki] [cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg].

The chart of the Fund's holdings shows how the 1920s and early 1930s changed its course. From a first gift of 200 dunams in 1903, it took seventeen years to reach 22,363 dunams in 1920. In the fifteen years that followed it grew to about 362,000 dunams by 1935, more than sixteen times the 1920 figure, and the curve kept rising, to 516,000 dunams in 1940 and about 936,000 by 1947–48 [cite:jnf_wiki] [cite:wiki_isaac_leib_goldberg]. Because Fund land was never resold, the line could only climb. The 1935 point is the 89,500 acres of the sources expressed in dunams, and should be read as an approximation.

The Mandate's role was that of regulator, not seller, and as regulator it failed. Between 1920 and 1940 Britain enacted five separate laws meant to protect tenant cultivators from eviction after a sale. A documented analysis of the period concludes that they "failed to provide the protection that Palestinian Arabs demanded, often due to loopholes, which allowed individuals and agencies to circumvent the legislation" [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine]. The Shaw Commission said the same in 1930 about the laws then in force: the Land Transfer Ordinances of 1920 and 1921 had failed, the Protection of Cultivators Ordinance of 1929 did not stop evictions, and the evictions were creating a landless class, "a potential danger to the country" [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_e]. Later in the decade British policy moved the other way: in 1938 the Mandate authorities allowed Zionist officials to copy land-registration and tax records of Arab ownership in order to facilitate further purchases [cite:israeled_jnf_cultivators_ordinance] [cite:badil_land_ownership_palestine]. Britain's record on land was inconsistent rather than a single policy, and its restrictions on immigration in 1939 cut in the opposite direction.

The case that tested the system came at the end of the 1920s. In 1929 the Jewish National Fund bought 30,800 dunams at Wadi al-Hawarith at auction from absentee landlords; the historian Weldon Matthews counts it as the third-largest single acquisition the Fund made in the whole Mandate period. The Bedouin Palestinian tenants who lived there refused to leave, and the Fund began legal proceedings to evict them. Awni Abd al-Hadi, a leading Palestinian national figure, represented the residents through a court battle that lasted several years. The final eviction order was issued on 15 June 1933. The response was organized and crossed party lines: the Istiqlal Party, the Palestine Communist Party and the Congress of Youth called strikes and issued public declarations of protest [cite:wiki_wadi_al_hawarith]. The case was decided in the Mandate's own courts, under its own law. It shows that resistance reached formal legal representation and cross-party protest when the stakes were high enough. It does not show that every sale met such opposition; many went through with less.

The fullest measurement of all this came from Sir John Hope Simpson. His report, Palestine: Report on Immigration, Land Settlement and Development (Cmd. 3686), carries a covering letter dated 22 August 1930 and was published in October 1930 [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. It is the most detailed British land study of the Mandate period [cite:w_hopesimpson]. He adopted the Director of Surveys' estimate of 8,044,000 metric dunams of cultivable land, including 1,500,000 in the Beersheba region, and found that only 40 percent of the hill country was cultivable, not the 66.6 percent that had been assumed. He put Jewish-held land in June 1930 at about 1,250,000 Turkish dunams. Deducting about 900,000 metric dunams of Jewish holdings, he left 5,644,000 metric dunams for non-Jewish cultivators [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. The two Jewish figures are in different units, Turkish and metric dunams, and the report as summarized here does not reconcile them further.

His village figures carried the human finding. In 104 surveyed villages, 6,940 of 23,573 families, 29.4 percent, held no land at all. His own enquiry into 688 Arab families displaced by land sales found that only 379 were cultivating land again; the other 309 had joined the landless. He estimated the land available for new settlement at "100,000 dunams at the outside" [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. Without development, he concluded, there was no margin for further Jewish agricultural settlement [cite:w_hopesimpson].

Hope Simpson also examined labor, and here he quoted the movement's own documents. The Jewish National Fund's draft lease required the lessee to cultivate "only with Jewish labour", on penalty of ten Palestinian pounds for each breach and repossession of the land after three breaches. The Jewish Agency's constitution of 1920 committed it to colonization "based on Jewish labour". And fewer than one in three of the immigrants who had arrived since 1921 had settled on the land [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. Read against the purchase chain, the clause meant that, on the draft lease's terms, a tenant evicted from land the Fund bought could not be hired back to work it. Shaw had added two further findings: the allocation of labor-schedule immigration certificates by political creed was indefensible, and Jewish immigration within the country's absorptive capacity had brought material benefits [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_e]. The labor movement's own doctrine of "Hebrew labour" is described in the section on the Histadrut.

The Jewish Agency contested Hope Simpson's numbers. In 1931 it published a memorandum, The Statistical Bases of Sir John Hope Simpson's Report, to challenge the report's figures [cite:rs_rd3_cojs_ja_1931]. The two positions stand side by side. Hope Simpson held that without development there was no margin for settlement, that at most 100,000 dunams were available, and that a landless class was forming [cite:rs_rd3_hope_simpson_unispal]. The Agency held that his statistical foundation was unsound [cite:rs_rd3_cojs_ja_1931]. The research for this book has not verified the memorandum's specific arguments, so they are not summarized here. On the Agency's side, the strongest evidence in the record used here comes from a British inquiry: Shaw's finding that Jewish immigration within the country's absorptive capacity had brought material benefits. What London did with these findings is told in [scene:pf_s01|the Passfield White Paper and its reversal].

Much remains unmeasured. The total number of Arab families displaced by land sales in these years is not established; Hope Simpson's 688 families were those his own enquiry traced, and the 1,746 Sursock-linked families are one group of sales. Whether there was room for more settlement in 1930 is a question on which Hope Simpson and the Jewish Agency disagreed, and the Agency's arguments have not been examined here. The full Jewish holding in 1930 is given in two units that the summarized report does not reconcile.
