A Myth About Authority

A myth about rulers who claim to be the only God, who govern by law, and who keep humanity from knowing its true origin, invites a political reading. The texts make the reading easy in places, and the best-known modern interpreter of the Gnostics made it central. This section sets out what the texts themselves say about earthly authority, what scholars have made of it, and where the evidence stops.

Some of the Nag Hammadi texts attack the church's offices directly. The Gnostic Apocalypse of Peter, from the seventh codex, has the living Jesus "glad and laughing" above the cross while his body is crucified, and it describes those who call themselves bishops and deacons as "dry canals". [cite:vt25_wiki_gapocpeter] The Testimony of Truth, from the ninth codex, attacks the Law, martyrdom, water baptism and procreation, and turns on other Gnostic schools too, the Valentinians, Basilideans and Simonians. [cite:vt25_wiki_testtruth] The Second Treatise of the Great Seth calls the patriarchs and prophets of the Jewish scriptures a "laughingstock". [cite:vt25_wiki_2seth] These are not neutral meditations on cosmology. They are polemics by Christians who believed the church's officers had mistaken the rulers' law for the true God's will.

Elaine Pagels built her influential reading on texts like these. In The Gnostic Gospels she argued that the orthodox doctrines of one God, one bishop and one bodily resurrection reinforced each other, and that the Gnostic alternative, a lesser creator, an inner knowledge and a spiritual resurrection, gave its followers "the theological justification for refusing to obey the bishops". She also wrote that "ideas alone do not make a religion powerful": orthodoxy won partly because it built an organisation. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Critics answered that she simplified a varied literature and read it through modern politics. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] And Ptolemy's moderate creator, a just administrator rather than a tyrant, shows that not every Gnostic writer drew a hostile picture of the lawgiver. [cite:vt24_ect_flora]

The philosophers raised a different objection, and it shows the myth was contested on its own terms. Plotinus, the greatest Platonist of the third century, wrote a treatise against them, known as Against the Gnostics. He attacked their contempt for the cosmos and its maker, their multiplication of divine beings, their promise of salvation without moral effort and their use of magic; his student Porphyry names two of their books, Zostrianos and Allogenes, both of which were later found in the jar. [cite:vt24_bmcr_plotinus] [cite:vt25_wiki_zost] [cite:vt25_wiki_allogenes] Plotinus defended the cosmos and its maker. The texts he attacked treated the same cosmos as the work of lesser powers.

The evidence supports a limited conclusion. The Gnostic myth described the visible order of the world, including its law and, in some texts, its religious officers, as the work of lesser powers that had to be seen through. Whether the myth arose from political experience or simply lent itself to political use cannot be settled from the texts. What can be said is that the bishops understood it as a challenge to their authority, and responded with the instruments described in The Canon: the list, the office and, when the state was willing, the law.
