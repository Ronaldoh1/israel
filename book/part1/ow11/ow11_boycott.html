The Boycott Laws, 1977–2026

Parts One to Ten describe how the United States arms, funds and works with Israel. This part turns to a different kind of law: measures that protect Israel, or those who deal with it, from pressure. Some are old and uncontroversial in their original purpose; some are recent and contested in the courts. Each is set out here with the reason the government gave for it and the strongest case made against it. This first section covers the oldest: the federal law against complying with foreign boycotts, written in 1977 against the Arab League's boycott of Israel, and the state laws of the 2010s that are often confused with it.

The federal law of 1977. The Arab League's boycott of Israel did not only bar Arab states from trading with Israel. Its secondary and tertiary forms asked foreign companies to refuse business with Israel, or with firms on a blacklist, as a condition of trading in Arab markets, and to supply information about their dealings and about the religion or national origin of the people involved, which is what the American law that followed prohibits. [cite:ow11_bis_oac] [cite:ow11_carter_1977] Congress answered in two steps. The Ribicoff Amendment to the Tax Reform Act of 1976 added separate boycott-related prohibitions and reporting duties to the tax law. [cite:ow11_bis_oac] On 22 June 1977 President Jimmy Carter signed H.R. 5840, the Export Administration Amendments of 1977, Public Law 95-52, which barred American persons from complying with unsanctioned foreign boycotts. [cite:ow11_carter_1977] [cite:ow11_bis_oac]

Carter's stated reasons are on the record. He called the boycotts a "profound moral issue from which we should not shrink," tied the law to the special relationship with Israel and to the principle of free trade, and set out its limit: it did not "question the sovereign right of any nation to regulate its own commerce" and was aimed at no particular country. He credited Senators Adlai Stevenson and William Proxmire, Representatives Clement Zablocki, Benjamin Rosenthal, Lee Hamilton, Jonathan Bingham, Stephen Solarz and Charles Whalen, and outside groups including the Business Roundtable, the Anti-Defamation League, the American Jewish Committee and the American Jewish Congress, describing a joint effort of business, Jewish organizations, Congress and the executive. [cite:ow11_carter_1977]

The law today. The 1977 provisions became Section 8 of the Export Administration Act of 1979 and Part 760 of the Export Administration Regulations. On 13 August 2018 the Anti-Boycott Act of 2018, enacted as Part II of the Export Control Reform Act within the FY2019 defence authorization, became their statutory basis. [cite:ow11_bis_oac] The Commerce Department's Office of Antiboycott Compliance enforces it. American persons may not refuse to do business with a boycotted country or blacklisted firm for boycott reasons, discriminate on the basis of race, religion, sex or national origin, or furnish information about their business relationships or about anyone's religion or national origin; they must report boycott requests every quarter. The department states that the Arab League boycott of Israel "is the principal unsanctioned foreign boycott of concern today," though the rules apply to all such boycotts. Administrative penalties reach $374,474 per violation as of January 2025, and criminal penalties $1 million and twenty years in prison. [cite:ow11_bis_oac]

The state laws. The second wave is different in kind. Beginning with Illinois in July 2015, about 38 states passed laws or issued executive orders discouraging boycotts of Israel by the people and companies the state does business with: contractors must certify that they are not boycotting Israel, or state funds must divest from companies that do. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] Their target is not a foreign government's boycott but the boycott, divestment and sanctions movement, a campaign by private actors. That difference is the root of the constitutional challenges they have faced. Federal courts enjoined laws in Kansas and Arizona in 2018, both later narrowed, and a federal court struck down the Texas law in April 2019; the full Eighth Circuit upheld Arkansas's law in June 2022, reasoning that purchasing decisions in a boycott are not expressive enough to be protected speech, and the Supreme Court declined to hear the case on 21 February 2023. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] The laws' origins, text and litigation are told in full in [scene:pm8_laws|thirty-eight states].

The arguments on each side are well defined. Supporters argue that a state, like any purchaser, may choose whom it does business with, and that a commercial boycott is conduct, not speech; the Eighth Circuit accepted that the Arkansas law served commercial purposes. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] Opponents, among them the American Civil Liberties Union, argue that the laws penalize one side of a political debate and that the right to boycott, recognized for civil-rights boycotts, should not depend on the target. [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] Congress has declined to settle the question nationally: the Israel Anti-Boycott Act of 2017 died, and the Combating BDS Act passed the Senate in January 2019 but was not taken up by the House. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds]

The two layers together form the oldest of the legal shields. The federal law of 1977 protects American companies from being drafted into a foreign government's boycott, and was written as a trade and anti-discrimination measure with business support. The state laws of 2015 onward penalize Americans' own boycott decisions, and remain contested. [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] The next section follows a newer shield, built not in commercial law but in civil-rights enforcement.
