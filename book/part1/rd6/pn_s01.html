Seven Founding Narratives, Checked Against the Record

The founding of Israel acquired a story, and much of that story was taught in American schools and repeated in the press as settled history. Volume II has followed the record stage by stage. This section gathers seven founding narratives that many people learned as fact and sets each beside what the documents show. Six of them belong to the familiar founding account. The seventh comes from the opposite direction: the claim that the Rothschilds, or a few Jewish bankers, paid for it all. It is included on purpose. A standard of evidence that corrects only one side's stories is not a standard, and the same documents that complicate the founding slogans also dispose of the conspiracy myth about who paid.

The narratives do not fail in the same way. Some are contradicted outright. Others contain something true and become misleading only in the telling. In each case below, the strongest version of the familiar claim is given first, then the documented figure that tests it.

"A land without a people for a people without a land." The slogan described a country waiting, empty, for settlers. Britain's own census, taken in 1922, two years into the Mandate, counted 757,182 people, about 11 percent of them Jewish. The slogan did not describe the country it named. The figures that followed show how quickly the balance then changed: Britain recorded 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946, and the UN Special Committee on Palestine put the population at about 1.85 million in late 1946, by which time the Jewish share was about a third. The country was neither empty in 1922 nor static afterwards. Its population was counted, and the count was British.

"They left because Arab leaders told them to." This was the standard Western and Israeli official explanation of the refugee exodus through the 1950s, and it placed responsibility on the Arab states. Its strongest form rests on something real: some local Arab commanders did order evacuations of women and children in particular places. But in 1959 the Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi argued from Arab sources that no general order had been given, and in May 1961 the Irish writer Erskine Childers reported in The Spectator that he had examined the British and American monitoring records of Arab broadcasts in 1948 and found no evacuation orders, but rather appeals to stay [cite:w_childers]. In 1988 the Israeli historian Benny Morris, working from Israeli military and state archives, concluded that the main causes of flight were military attack, expulsion and fear, with local evacuation orders in some places and no general Arab order [cite:w_morris_birth]. The account is no longer defended by mainstream scholarship; how it was built and then dismantled is told in [scene:np_s01|how the flight was explained]. What remains disputed is not whether there was a general order but how far the expulsions were intended and planned.

"A miracle against impossible odds." The war of 1948 was fought and won; that part of the story stands. The picture of a few against many does not. A contemporary CIA assessment from 1948, and research since, describe a war in two phases that Israel ended with roughly a two-to-one advantage in manpower. The victory was real. The odds at the end of the war were not those of the familiar image.

"Israel was born of the Holocaust." The genocide in Europe transformed the urgency of the Zionist case, the politics around it and the moral argument for a state, and in 1945–48 the plight of some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in Europe was the central argument made to the American public. But as a statement about origins the claim inverts the sequence. The program, the British pledge and the Mandate all predate Hitler, by between 11 and 37 years; the record dates them to 1896, 1917, 1920 and 1922. The Holocaust accelerated support. It did not create the program, and the institutions that carried it, as the next section shows step by step, were already built.

"Israel accepted partition; the Arabs rejected it and started the war." The first half holds. The vote on partition split as the familiar account says, with acceptance on one side and rejection on the other. The second half compresses two different escalations into one. The fighting began as a two-sided civil war inside Palestine months before the Arab armies entered in May 1948. The claim that the war simply began with the Arab invasion leaves out the months of fighting that came before it.

"The land was bought fair and square." This claim is true in its narrow legal sense and misleading as a description of how the land came to be held. Purchases were made legally, from absentee owners. But in 1947 only about 7 percent of Mandate Palestine's land was in Jewish ownership, roughly 2 million of some 26 million dunams, while the UN plan of that year allotted 56 percent of the territory to the Jewish state. Purchases from absentee landlords under Ottoman land law displaced the tenant farmers who worked the land, and those evictions are documented. After 1948 the larger transfer was not by purchase at all. The Absentees' Property Law of 14 March 1950, building on regulations of 1948, vested the property of those who had left in a state Custodian [cite:rs_rd6_fischbach_2003], and the chain by which that property passed to the Development Authority and the Jewish National Fund is set out in [scene:ap_s01|the absentee property law]. Inside the 1949 armistice lines, which Israel's Foreign Ministry describes as enclosing about 78 percent of the Mandate area, the land that had been bought was a small part of the land that was held [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map].

The opposite myth: "the Rothschilds paid for it all." This story borrows from real names in the record. Edmond de Rothschild in Paris did fund early colonies, and the Balfour Declaration of 1917 was addressed to Lord Rothschild. But the Paris Rothschild who funded colonies opposed Herzl, and the documented money came overwhelmingly from mass giving. Keren Hayesod raised $20.1 million worldwide between April 1921 and December 1929, $11.0 million of it from the United States [cite:jta_kh_decade]. In 1948 alone the United Jewish Appeal raised a record $150 million from more than a million contributors in 4,950 American communities [cite:jta_uja_1948]. Even that figure cannot simply be counted as "funding Israel": $74.7 million went to the United Palestine Appeal, $64.9 million to the Joint Distribution Committee for relief in Europe, and $10.4 million to resettling refugees in the United States [cite:jta_uja_1948]. A founding paid for by a few financiers is not in the ledger. A founding paid for by very large numbers of ordinary donors, much of whose money fed survivors in Europe, is. The full accounting is in [scene:gf_s01|who paid for it all].

Two things follow from the table. The first is that "contradicted" and "complicated" are different findings, and the difference matters. A reader who learns that the land was not bought fair and square may conclude that no land was bought; it was, legally, in a small fraction of the country. A reader who learns that the war was not a miracle may conclude it was never in doubt; the record does not say that either. The second is that the corrections run in every direction. The census that undoes the empty-land slogan, the archives that undo the orders-to-flee story and the campaign ledgers that undo the Rothschild myth are the same kind of evidence, read the same way. That is the only ground on which any of them can be trusted.

The record also leaves some questions open. Historians still disagree about intent and planning in the expulsions of 1948, and nothing here settles them. No audited grand total of the money raised before 1948 exists, so the scale of mass giving can be shown but not summed to a single figure. And the 2-to-1 manpower ratio describes the end of the war, not every front at every moment in it. What the record does settle is narrower and firmer: none of the seven narratives, in its familiar form, survives the documents intact.
