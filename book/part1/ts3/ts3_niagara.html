Crossing the Atlantic: Brookes, Niagara, Moody and Gaebelein

Darby's system reached the United States a generation before the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, and it arrived through people and meetings, not a book. This section follows that route: Darby's own journeys, the Presbyterian pastor in St. Louis who became the system's chief American organiser, the summer Bible conferences that gave it a creed, the evangelist whose schools carried it to a mass audience, and the missionary who supplied the prophetic notes for Scofield's Bible. It ends with what the record shows about how far the system had spread by 1909, which corrects a common claim.

Darby in North America

Darby made at least five journeys to North America between 1862 and 1877, working mostly in New England, Ontario and the Great Lakes region; one extended trip took him on from Toronto across the Pacific. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] Some accounts give the number as six; the index of his correspondence, on which the lower figure rests, supports "at least five."

What Americans took from him was selective. The Brethren way of church life, in which believers left the denominations to meet without clergy, found few takers among American Protestant pastors; Darby's reading of prophecy found many. The usual summary is that "America did not embrace Darby's ecclesiology as it did his eschatology." [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] The historian Timothy Stunt, reviewing Donald Akenson's 2018 study of Darby's American influence, Exporting the Rapture, describes dispensationalist eschatology as "the one aspect of his teaching that really caught on in North America." [cite:ts3_stunt_akenson] The Americans who took it up, such as the Presbyterian Brookes below, stayed inside their own denominations. In this book's reading, that is one reason the system spread as widely as it did: it asked nothing of a congregation's structure, only of its reading of the Bible.

James H. Brookes and the Niagara conferences

The most important of those carriers was James Hall Brookes (1830–1897), a Presbyterian minister from Tennessee who pastored the Walnut Street Presbyterian Church in St. Louis from 1864 and, after a move in 1879, the Washington and Compton Presbyterian Church until his death. He edited a periodical, The Truth, which with the journal Watchword served as an organ of the premillennial movement. [cite:ts3_wiki_brookes]

Brookes's lasting work was the Bible conference. Its origins are usually traced to an informal meeting in New York in 1868 of eight men linked to a premillennial periodical, Waymarks in the Wilderness; in 1875 a small group including Nathaniel West, Brookes, W. J. Erdman and H. M. Parsons met near Chicago, and in July 1876, at Swampscott, Massachusetts, the group named itself the "Believers' Meeting for Bible Study." From 1883 to 1897 it met at Niagara-on-the-Lake in Ontario, which gave it the name by which it is known. [cite:ts3_bt_niagara] Accounts differ on dates: Wikipedia gives 1875 as the founding and describes annual meetings from then to 1897, except 1884. [cite:ts3_wiki_niagara] According to Weber, the meeting ran for two weeks each summer, premillennialists quickly took over its leadership, and Brookes "presided for more than 20 years." [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]

In 1878 Brookes drew up a fourteen-point statement of faith, which the conference formally adopted in 1890. [cite:ts3_bt_niagara] Its first article affirmed that "the Holy Ghost gave the very words of the sacred writings." [cite:ts3_bt_niagara] Its fourteenth set out the end-times view. As reproduced in reference sources, it holds that the world "will not be converted during the present dispensation" but "is fast ripening for judgment," amid "a fearful apostasy in the professing Christian body," and that Christ "will come in person to introduce the millennial age, when Israel shall be restored to their own land." [cite:ts3_ak_niagara] The creed does not set out Darby's full system or a pre-tribulation rapture, but it writes premillennial return and the restoration of Israel into a statement of faith adopted by a conference of American Protestant ministers. [cite:ts3_wiki_niagara]

The same year brought a public platform. "The First American Bible and Prophetic Conference was held in New York City in 1878," Weber records, and it was successful enough that six more followed. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] The premillennial movement was no longer a cluster of readers of British pamphlets; it had annual meetings, a creed and periodicals of its own.

Moody and the Bible institutes

Dwight L. Moody, the Chicago-based evangelist, was by the end of the century "Mr. Evangelical" to nearly everyone, in Weber's phrase, and he "was an early convert to premillennialism," though not a very doctrinaire dispensationalist. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] His best-known summary of his mission reflected the pessimism of that view about the present age: "I look upon this world as a wrecked vessel. God has given me a life boat and said to me, 'Moody, save all you can.'" [cite:ts3_en_moody] Weber notes that nearly every major revivalist from Moody's time to the First World War adopted his eschatology. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]

Moody's lasting institution was a school. At a meeting in Chicago on 22 January 1886 he founded the Chicago Evangelization Society, to train Christian workers; it was renamed the Moody Bible Institute after his death in 1899, added a publishing arm and, in 1926, a radio station, WMBI. The institute has historically been identified as dispensational and as Zionist. [cite:ts3_wiki_mbi] It was one of many. "Almost without exception," Weber writes, "the scores of Bible institutes founded between 1880 and 1940 taught the new premillennialism," and their graduates, as pastors, missionaries and Bible teachers, carried it into congregations across the country. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] How those schools and their successors spread the system is traced in Part Seven. [scene:ts7_schools|Part Seven: the schools]

The road to Scofield

The man who would put the system into the margins of an Oxford Bible came out of exactly this network. Cyrus Scofield came under Brookes's mentorship in St. Louis; by late 1879 he was assisting Moody's evangelistic campaign in the city and became secretary of the St. Louis YMCA. He attended the Niagara conference in 1888, was called in 1895 to pastor Moody's church at East Northfield, Massachusetts, and presided at Moody's funeral in 1899. [cite:ts3_wiki_scofield] His earlier life, including his legal troubles in St. Louis, is set out in Part Four. [scene:ts4_record|Part Four: Scofield's record]

The last link was Arno C. Gaebelein (1861–1945), a German immigrant who became a Methodist minister in New York, began mission work among Jewish immigrants in 1887 and from 1893 published a monthly, Tiqweth Israel (The Hope of Israel), and from 1894 the magazine Our Hope, which he edited from 1896. [cite:ts3_demy_gaebelein] He left the Methodist Episcopal Church in 1899, objecting to theological liberalism. [cite:ts3_wiki_gaebelein] When Scofield prepared his reference Bible, he asked Gaebelein to supply its prophetic portions, writing that on prophecy he would "sit at your feet." [cite:ts3_demy_gaebelein] Gaebelein's own record on Jews is mixed and is stated as the scholarship states it: he wrote that Zionism was "a movement ... of unbelief," his journal reported on Nazi atrocities against Jews from 1937 to 1945, and historians have described some of his writing as anti-Jewish. [cite:ts3_wiki_gaebelein]

Before 1909: the common claim and the record

A common claim holds that before 1909 the dispensationalist framework was virtually absent from American Protestantism. The record above does not support that. By 1909 the system had a creed adopted by a long-running conference, a series of national prophetic conferences beginning in 1878, its own periodicals, a leading evangelist among its converts and a growing number of Bible institutes teaching it. [cite:ts3_bt_niagara] [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] Before 1909 the framework was known and held by some American Protestants; what it lacked was a place in the mainstream. [scene:s05|Road to 1948: the Scofield Reference Bible] Scofield's Bible, the subject of Part Five, did not create the system; it put it into the hands of readers who had never attended a conference.
