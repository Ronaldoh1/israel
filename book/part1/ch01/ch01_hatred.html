An Old Hatred, Before and After Its Name

The word "antisemitism" dates from 1879. The hatred it names is far older. Arguments over the word, and over where legitimate criticism of a state ends, can make it easy to lose sight of the thing itself: a long, documented history of accusation, legal exclusion, expulsion and killing directed at Jews as Jews. This section sets out that record plainly, from the medieval ritual-murder charge to a forged text that is still in print, because no honest account of the words that follow can be written without it.

The oldest of these accusations is the blood libel: the charge that Jews kill Christians in order to use their blood. Earlier references to the idea occur in the writings of Apion. Its first medieval case came at Norwich, in England, in 1144, after the body of a boy named William was found in the woods. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_bloodlibel] [cite:rs_ch01_globe_heng] The charge then spread. Ritual-murder accusations followed at Gloucester in 1168, Blois in 1171, Saragossa in 1182, Bristol in 1183, Fulda in 1235, Lincoln in 1255 and Munich in 1286. In the fourteenth century the accusation was joined to another, that Jews had poisoned wells, during the Black Death. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_bloodlibel]

The case at Trent shows how the charge worked once it took hold. In 1475 a two-year-old named Simon disappeared. The town's Jews were tortured until they confessed, and they were burned. Simon was later counted among the saints. In 1965 the Catholic Church removed him from the list of saints and repudiated the charge. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_bloodlibel] The confession that had condemned the Jews of Trent was a product of torture, and the institution that had honored the supposed victim formally withdrew the accusation.

Alongside the accusations came law. In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council decreed that Jews must wear distinguishing dress and barred them from public office. England, according to reporting on the Church of England's later reckoning with these decrees and to the historian Geraldine Heng, was the first country to require Jews to wear a badge, in 1218. In 1222 church leaders meeting at Osney Abbey, near Oxford, adopted a badge decree of their own; about 3,000 Jews are thought to have lived in England at the time. [cite:rs_ch01_algemeiner_oxford1222] [cite:rs_ch01_globe_heng] England's Statute of Jewry of 1275 barred Jews from living among Christians. In 1290 Edward I expelled the Jews of England, the first such expulsion in Christendom. Jews were permitted to return only in 1656, under Cromwell. [cite:rs_ch01_globe_heng] [cite:rs_ch01_algemeiner_oxford1222]

The mechanism visible in these decrees is worth naming. The badge made Jews legible as a separate category in public. The bar on office closed the instruments of power to them. The Statute of Jewry separated them physically from their neighbors, and the expulsion of 1290 removed the community altogether. Each step was taken by a named authority: a church council, a king, a statute.

The blood libel did not stay in medieval Europe. On 5 February 1840 a Capuchin friar named Thomas and his servant disappeared in Damascus. The Capuchins accused the city's Jews of ritual murder, and the French consul, Ratti-Menton, backed the investigation. Two of the accused died under torture. Sixty-three Jewish children were seized to put pressure on their mothers. [cite:rs_ch01_ej_damascus] On 25 April 1840 Muhammad Ali ordered the torture stopped. After a mission by Moses Montefiore and Adolphe Crémieux, the surviving prisoners were freed on 28 August 1840, without any declaration of their innocence, and the Ottoman Sultan issued a firman repudiating the ritual-murder charge. [cite:rs_ch01_ej_damascus] The Damascus affair is instructive for the way power ran through it: the charge came from a religious order, a European consul lent it official weight, the torture was halted by a ruler's order, and the release came only after Montefiore and Crémieux's mission. The record cited here does not give the date of the Sultan's firman.

In the Russian Empire the charge returned in the twentieth century. The Kishinev pogrom began on Easter Sunday, 19 April 1903, after articles in an antisemitic newspaper; over three days, almost 50 Jews were killed. Between 1911 and 1913 Menachem Mendel Beilis was tried in Kiev on a ritual-murder charge, and acquitted. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_bloodlibel] By then the word "antisemitism" had existed for a generation, coined in Germany to make this same hostility sound modern and scientific; its history, and the Jewish name for the hatred, "Judeophobia," are traced in [scene:s00b0|the history of the word].

The same year as Kishinev saw the first appearance of a forgery that would outlast every exposure. The Protocols of the Elders of Zion, a fabricated plan for Jewish world domination, was first serialized in the St Petersburg newspaper Znamya in the autumn of 1903. In 1905 Sergei Nilus appended it to a book of his own. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_protocols] It did not remain a Russian text. In 1920 Henry Ford's newspaper, the Dearborn Independent, ran a 91-part series based on it. In August 1921 The Times of London showed that the text was largely copied from Maurice Joly's satire of 1864 and called it a forgery. Ford apologized in 1927. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_protocols]

Exposure did not end its circulation. Hitler cited the Protocols in Mein Kampf in 1925, the same year the first Arabic translation appeared in Damascus. In 1935 a Swiss court in Bern ruled against its publishers. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_protocols] In 1943 Hajj Amin al-Husseini, the former Mufti of Jerusalem, echoed the Protocols' themes in Arabic broadcasts from Berlin. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_protocols] The text kept circulating after the war: an Iranian English edition appeared in 1985; Article 32 of the Hamas charter of 1988 drew on it; an Egyptian television series of 41 parts drew on it in 2002, and a series on Hezbollah's Al-Manar channel in 2003. A Russian court ruled it a forgery in 1993. [cite:rs_ch01_ushmm_protocols] Each of these uses is tied to a named publisher, author, movement or broadcaster. The record documents those specific actors, not the peoples or faiths in whose names they claimed to speak.

Taken together, the record shows a hatred with a recognizable structure that long predates any modern dispute over Zionism or Israel: accusations of ritual murder, confessions extracted under torture, legal markers of separation, expulsion, and, in the twentieth century, a forged document that kept circulating after a newspaper, a court in Bern and later a Russian court had all exposed or ruled against it. That history reached its documented extreme in the Holocaust, told in [scene:hol_s05|the scale of the Holocaust].

Several things the sources gathered here do not settle. They do not give casualty figures for most of the medieval cases, nor the numbers of Jews expelled in 1290. They do not name the newspaper whose articles preceded the Kishinev pogrom, or record the date of the Sultan's firman after Damascus. Nor do they set out the causes historians give for why these accusations arose and spread when and where they did. What they do establish, case by case and date by date, is that this was a real hatred, aimed at Jews, enacted through named institutions and documents, and that it existed long before its modern name.
