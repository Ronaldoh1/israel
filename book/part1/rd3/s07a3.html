The Revisionist Line, 1923–1935

While Britain's title was being settled at Lausanne and Geneva, the movement it had promised to help was dividing. Zionism is often described as a single political movement that divided into parties only after the state was founded. The institutional record shows otherwise. Its foundational split, between the movement led by Chaim Weizmann and the one led by Ze'ev (Vladimir) Jabotinsky, opened in the years of this chapter and was documented step by step: a youth movement in 1923, a secession in 1925, a second and final break in 1935. The organization Jabotinsky built is the direct ancestor of Herut and then Likud. It also produced the armed groups, the Irgun and later Lehi, that operated alongside and often against the Haganah and the Jewish Agency [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism] [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization].

Jabotinsky was a Russian-Jewish writer and Zionist leader [cite:wiki_jabotinsky]. He was not religious, and his claims to the land were territorial and historical rather than primarily religious [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925]. In 1923 he founded Betar, a youth movement built on discipline and military training. Its watchwords were hadar, pride or dignity, and armed self-defense, and it spread through Poland, the Baltic states, Palestine and the Americas [cite:wiki_betar].

The same year he set out the argument on which his politics rested, in an essay called "The Iron Wall." Its claim was that the Arabs of Palestine would never voluntarily accept a Jewish majority, and that Zionism could therefore proceed only from a position of overwhelming military strength [cite:wiki_iron_wall_essay] [cite:theconversation_israels_iron_wall]. Its central sentence reads:

Zionist colonization, even the most restricted, must either be terminated or carried out in defiance of the will of the native population.

A follow-up essay, "The Ethics of the Iron Wall," argued that this approach was itself moral [cite:wiki_iron_wall_essay] [cite:theconversation_israels_iron_wall]. What the essay conceded matters as much as what it proposed. It did not pretend the land was empty or that its people would welcome the project; it said plainly that they would not, and drew the conclusion that consent could not be the basis of the plan.

Among the young men drawn to the movement was Benzion Netanyahu, born Mileikowsky in Warsaw in 1910, who had emigrated to Palestine in 1920. In the 1930s he edited the Revisionist newspaper Ha'Yarden [cite:jewishjournal_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:wiki_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:tabletmag_bibi_fathers_footsteps]. His later role belongs to a later chapter: in 1940 Jabotinsky sent him to the United States, where he served as Jabotinsky's personal secretary until Jabotinsky's death that year and then ran the movement's American operations for eight years [cite:jewishjournal_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:wiki_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:tabletmag_bibi_fathers_footsteps]. His son, Benjamin Netanyahu, told a Jabotinsky memorial audience in 2023 that one hundred years after the "iron wall" was set down in Jabotinsky's writings, Israel was "continuing to successfully implement these principles" [cite:counterfire_netanyahu_shadow]. He has also, separately, dismissed the idea that his father's formation under Jabotinsky shaped his own views as "psychobabble" [cite:jewishjournal_benzion_netanyahu]. Both statements are his own, and they sit in tension; this book records both and does not choose between them.

The organized break came two years after the essay. In April 1925, in Paris, Jabotinsky founded the World Union of Zionist-Revisionists, known by its Hebrew acronym Hatzohar [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism] [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925]. Its first president was Vladimir Tiomkin [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. The founding is described as a secession from the World Zionist Organization that Weizmann led [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism]; yet the Revisionists went on sending delegates to Zionist Congresses for the next decade, so in practice the break of 1925 created a rival faction rather than a separate body [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. Its founding platform called for transforming Palestine and Transjordan into a Jewish commonwealth within the British Commonwealth, in direct opposition to the mainstream's gradualist strategy [cite:wiki_revisionist_zionism].

The heart of that platform was the claim to "both banks of the Jordan," a Jewish majority on both sides of the river, covering what is now Israel, the West Bank, Gaza and the whole of Jordan [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925]. Jabotinsky argued that Britain's separation of Transjordan from the Palestine Mandate in 1922 was an arbitrary partition, which denied Jews the territory they would need to absorb millions of future immigrants [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925]. He put the geography in one sentence, writing in 1929–1930:

What does the word Palestine mean? Palestine is a territory whose chief geographical feature is this: that the River Jordan does not delineate its frontier, but flows through its center.

The movement's anthem made the same point in a line: "Two Banks has the Jordan — This is ours and, that is as well" [cite:jabotinsky_revisionist_1925]. This is one of three documented versions of what is called "Greater Israel," and the narrowest in territory: a claim to the whole of the original Mandate area, territorial rather than primarily religious. How later religious-nationalist versions differ, and how the idea is used today, is traced in [scene:gia_src_s02|Greater Israel and its three versions]; the history of the "two banks" claim from 1922 to 1948 is told in [scene:ijr_src_s04|Two Banks of the Jordan].

In 1925 the Revisionists were a small faction. They had four delegates at the Fourteenth Zionist Congress that year [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. Six years later they were a major force. In 1931 they won 17 percent of the vote for the Yishuv's elected assembly, the Asefat ha-Nivharim, and became its second-largest party. At the Seventeenth Zionist Congress in Basel the same year they had 52 delegates, and proposed a resolution for a Jewish state on both sides of the Jordan [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists].

Growth brought internal conflict. In 1933, at Katowice, Jabotinsky suspended the party's executive. A plebiscite of the membership backed him, and his opponents, led by Meir Grossman, seceded to form the Jewish State Party. At the Eighteenth Congress the Jewish State Party had seven delegates to the Revisionists' 46 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. The Revisionists' quarrel with Labour Zionism grew sharper the same year, when Revisionists were accused of the murder of Chaim Arlosoroff (the accused were later cleared), and of the three agreements Ben-Gurion and Jabotinsky signed in London in 1934, the labor agreement was rejected in a Histadrut referendum; that story is told in [scene:rd3_arlosoroff|1933–1935].

How large was the movement by the mid-1930s? The figure most often quoted is 600,000, and it is used in two different ways. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, more than 600,000 Jews in 24 countries signed a Revisionist petition addressed to the British king and Parliament in 1934 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. That is a count of signatures. Other accounts present the same figure as the membership the broader Revisionist movement claimed by 1934 [cite:wiki_betar]. A signature on a petition and a membership are not the same thing, and the first reading rests on the more specific source. Even as a membership figure, it was a claimed number, not a verified one. A smaller figure, for Betar alone, is given as over 70,000 by the mid-1930s in the account of the "two banks" claim cited above. The difference between 70,000 and 600,000 is the difference between a disciplined youth movement and a mass petition.

The final break came in 1935. That year the Zionist General Council adopted a "discipline clause" that barred independent political action by member parties [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. The Zionist Executive formally rejected Jabotinsky's platform [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization]. A Revisionist plebiscite chose secession, and the constituent assembly of the New Zionist Organization met in Vienna in September 1935, said to have been "elected by 713,000 voters," with Jabotinsky as its president [cite:rs_rd3_ej_revisionists]. He resigned from the World Zionist Organization altogether [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization] [cite:wiki_hatzohar]. Where the 1925 secession had left room for a working relationship with the mainstream, the 1935 break did not. The New Zionist Organization ran its own political and fundraising work, parallel to and in competition with the Jewish Agency and, later, the American Zionist Emergency Council [cite:wiki_new_zionist_organization] [cite:wiki_hatzohar]. The "713,000" is reported with a hedge ("said to have been"); the sources do not say how those votes were counted.

The Revisionist case, in its own terms, was argued openly. Its premise was that the Arab population would never consent to a Jewish majority, so that any plan built on consent would fail; better to say so, and to build the strength on which the project could proceed without that consent. Its territorial claim held that Britain had cut the Mandate in two in 1922 arbitrarily, and that a state confined to the western bank could not take in the millions of Jews who would need it. Its method, a militarized youth movement and open political demands such as the 1931 resolution for a state on both banks, was set squarely against the mainstream's gradualist strategy.

The critics' case is in the same record. The Zionist leadership's answer is on record in what it did: the General Council's discipline clause barred exactly the independent political action the Revisionists practiced, and the Executive rejected their platform. Within the movement itself, Grossman's followers broke away in 1933 over Jabotinsky's suspension of the executive. And the Iron Wall essay is, on its face, a statement that the project would be carried out against the will of the people already living in the country. Read from the side of the population it named, it was not only an analysis of their opposition but a program for overriding it by strength. Both readings start from Jabotinsky's own words.

Jabotinsky held his position to the end of the period and beyond. In 1937 he testified before the Peel Commission against any partition of Palestine, insisting on Jewish rights to the entire Mandate territory, Transjordan included. He died of a heart attack in 1940 while visiting Betar in the United States [cite:wiki_jabotinsky]. The line he founded continued as an institution. Herut was founded on 15 June 1948 by Menachem Begin, a former Irgun commander, as a challenge to Hatzohar, which was then succeeded by and merged into Herut after the state was established. Herut joined the Liberal Party in 1965 to form Gahal, which in 1973 became the founding core of Likud, the party that has led or co-led Israeli governments for most of the past five decades, including under Netanyahu [cite:wiki_herut] [cite:wiki_hatzohar] [cite:wiki_gahal] [cite:wiki_likud]. That is a documented chain of foundings and mergers. It is not a claim that Likud's platform today is Jabotinsky's; parties change over a century.

Some questions the record here does not answer. The sources do not give a verified membership figure for the Revisionist movement or for Betar in any year; the 600,000 and the 713,000 are a petition count and a claimed electorate, and the 70,000 for Betar comes from a single account. They do not set out the specific policy disputes behind the Executive's rejection of the platform in 1935 beyond the discipline clause. And they do not show how much money the New Zionist Organization raised in its parallel campaigns. What they do show is that by September 1935 organized Zionism had two rival centers, each with its own leader, program and electorate, thirteen years before the state.
