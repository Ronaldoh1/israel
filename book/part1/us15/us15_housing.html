Homes, Public Housing and Homelessness

Part Fourteen measured how far housing prices have run ahead of incomes in both countries. [scene:us14_housing|Part Fourteen: housing against income] This section looks at what each state does for the households the market does not house: how many own their homes, what public or subsidised housing exists, and how many people end up without a home at all. The two states measure these things differently, and in Israel some of them are measured only occasionally, so the figures below are set side by side within each country and compared across countries only where the definitions allow.

The United States: ownership at 65 per cent, unevenly shared

The Census Bureau's quarterly survey put the American homeownership rate at 65.0 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, unchanged from a year earlier. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] That is below the rate's peak of 69.2 per cent in 2004 and below its 25-year average of 66.3 per cent. [cite:us15_nahb_q226] Ownership is the main store of wealth for most American families, and its distribution is uneven. In the same quarter 74.5 per cent of non-Hispanic white households owned their homes, against 45.4 per cent of Black households and 58.6 per cent of Asian American and Pacific Islander households. Among householders under 35 the rate was 35.2 per cent, down from 36.4 per cent a year earlier. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] The racial gaps are the outcome of documented mechanisms, among them federal lending rules, zoning and wealth passed between generations, which Part Eight sets out; they are not a description of any group's choices. [scene:us8_us|Part Eight: the United States]

For households that cannot buy, the strain shows in the rental market Part Fourteen described: 22.7 million renter households, 49 per cent of all renters, spent more than 30 per cent of their income on housing in 2024. [cite:us14_nlihc_jchs2026]

American homelessness: a single night each January

The federal measure of homelessness is a count taken by local agencies on a single night each January, the point-in-time (PIT) count, of people in shelters and on the street. It is the longest consistent American series on the subject, running from 2007. It counted 647,258 people in 2007, fell to a low of 549,928 in 2016, and then rose, to 653,104 in 2023 and to a record 771,480 in January 2024, of whom 274,224 were unsheltered. [cite:us15_crs_homeless] The January 2025 count, published by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) on 29 May 2026, found 745,652 people, a fall of 3 per cent, with 266,320 unsheltered. [cite:us15_hud_pit2025]

The same release carried the administration's reading of those figures. HUD said homelessness had risen 27 per cent and chronic homelessness 81 per cent since 2013, the year it took as the start of the "Housing First" approach, which places people in housing before requiring treatment or work. Secretary Scott Turner's department said that approach "has failed to meaningfully reduce homelessness", and attributed the 2025 fall to decreases in what it called "Sanctuary Cities". [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] These are the department's stated positions. The count itself excludes people living in housing funded for the formerly homeless, which HUD put at a combined total of 1,456,923 people homeless or in such housing; so a policy that moves people off the street into subsidised housing lowers the PIT count by design. [cite:us15_hud_pit2025]

Israel: public housing that shrank by sale

Israel's public housing is small and shrinking. The State Comptroller found that the stock of public housing apartments fell from about 60,000 in 2014 to about 53,000 in 2019. The main reason was a policy choice: the law lets tenants buy their apartments at a discount, and about 10,000 were sold in those six years, with discounts worth NIS 3.2 billion, while the state bought only about 2,600 to replace them, at a cost of NIS 3.4 billion. [cite:us15_comptroller_ph2020] In 2019, 3,700 eligible people were waiting for a public apartment, and those who moved in that year had waited an average of 31 months. About 200 apartments were expected to be added in the following years, enough for about 4.5 per cent of those waiting. [cite:us15_comptroller_ph2020] The waiting list counts only those eligible under the programme's rules; it is not a measure of the number of households who cannot afford housing.

Israel's main housing programme since 2015 has instead been aimed at buyers. Under Mehir Lamishtaken ("Buyer's Price"), from 2015, and its successor Mehir Matara ("Target Price"), the state markets land for new apartments that first-time buyers can win in a lottery at a discount of 20 per cent or up to NIS 300,000. The April 2022 lottery offered about 10,000 homes in 31 localities and drew 119,207 registered households, nearly twelve for every home. [cite:us15_toi_mehirmatara] Because the land is state land, the programme is a direct use of the land system described in the previous section: the state chooses where to release land and on what terms. [scene:us15_land|Who owns the land]

Israeli homelessness: small counts, thin data

Israel has no annual count like America's. A Knesset report published in 2022 used two sources. The Welfare Ministry's figures showed about 2,000 homeless people receiving aid in 2019 and 2,250 in 2020, 86 per cent of them men. Local authorities reported treating about 3,470 homeless people in 2020, and knew of about 800 more street dwellers not in treatment. Shelters had room for about a third of the street population. [cite:us15_ynet_homeless2022] These are counts of people known to services, not a street census, and they cover a period before the war; this book found no later national figure. Israel's housing emergency after October 2023 took a different form: the displacement of about 60,000 residents from the north, about half of whom had not returned by March 2025, which Part Fifteen's last section takes up with disaster response. [cite:hz7_afp_north_return] [scene:us15_infra|Water, power, transit and disasters]

What the two records show

Even allowing for different definitions and a far larger population, American homelessness is of a different order: hundreds of thousands of people on one January night, against Israeli service counts in the low thousands. [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] [cite:us15_ynet_homeless2022] The American count has a long, consistent series and is the subject of an open policy dispute over Housing First; the Israeli figures are irregular and pre-date the war. Israel's gap lies elsewhere: it sold more than three public apartments for every one it bought between 2014 and 2019 and steered its effort toward discounted ownership for first-time buyers, which helps those who can buy. In the United States, by HUD's own total, almost 1.5 million people were either homeless or living in housing funded for the formerly homeless. [cite:us15_comptroller_ph2020] [cite:us15_toi_mehirmatara] [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] In both, the households least served are those who can neither buy nor reach the head of a waiting list.
