The Arab Revolt, and the Mufti's Road to Berlin

On May 16, 1936, Amin al-Husseini, the Mufti of Jerusalem, called a general strike. He did so in a specific capacity: as president of the Arab Higher Committee, a body that had only just been formed. The strike ran until October 1936. It then gave way to a broader armed uprising against British rule and Jewish immigration that continued until Britain crushed it in 1939. [cite:ushmm_arab_revolt]

The revolt is sometimes remembered as a spontaneous, leaderless eruption that left no lasting political mark. The record does not support either half of that description. It began with a dated call, issued by a named man holding a newly created office. That establishes an organized origin and an overall leadership. It does not mean that every participant in three years of fighting took orders from al-Husseini personally, and the evidence for the revolt's beginning should not be stretched into a claim of central control over every act that followed.

Nor was the revolt without consequence. Its cost can be counted. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives a breakdown across the full uprising: 2,652 Jews, 618 British personnel, and roughly 5,000 Arab combatants and civilians killed. [cite:ushmm_arab_revolt] Those figures rest on the museum's own institutional accounting rather than on independent verification of each death, but they are a far more precise record than a vague reference to "unrest." The toll predates, and helps explain, the security concerns that shaped British and Zionist policy in the years that followed. And the revolt prompted London to send a royal commission to investigate its causes, which produced the first official proposal to partition the country, taken up in the next section on [scene:s07a6|the Peel Commission].

Al-Husseini's own position did not survive the uprising intact. On July 17, 1937, he took sanctuary in the Dome of the Rock to avoid arrest by the British. In September 1937 insurgents killed a British district commissioner; in response the British dissolved the Arab Higher Committee and stripped al-Husseini of his official positions. He fled to Lebanon, then to Iraq, and historians document that he continued to direct the revolt from exile. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] The sources establish that this direction continued; they do not itemize every message or order he sent while abroad.

His path then led to Germany. After a period in Iraq, al-Husseini relocated to Berlin in 1941. On November 28, 1941, he met Hitler in person, asking for German backing for Arab independence and for opposition to any Jewish national home in Palestine. Through the war he broadcast Nazi propaganda to the Arab world over German radio, and he personally recruited and inspected Muslim volunteer units for the Waffen-SS, primarily in the Balkans, recruiting thousands of men for its divisions. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] These facts are drawn from German diplomatic records, wartime broadcast archives and postwar historical research. They are not a matter of dispute.

He was never tried for any of it. Al-Husseini avoided prosecution at Nuremberg, escaped French custody in 1946 and eventually settled in Egypt. Yugoslavia sought his extradition for war crimes connected with the SS recruitment; it was never carried out. He went on to play a diminished but real political role among Palestinian factions into the 1960s. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]

Because this record is so serious, it has often been put to uses it cannot bear. Some have invoked it to indict Palestinians as a people; others have tried to minimize it altogether. Neither move is supported by the evidence. Al-Husseini held particular offices: the post of Grand Mufti of Jerusalem, a religious appointment he had held since 1921, and the presidency of the Arab Higher Committee. The decisions he made in exile were his own. The population he claimed to lead had no vote in them and no mechanism to remove him from his religious post, and the overwhelming majority of Palestinian Arabs had no involvement in, or say over, his wartime alliance. [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] His record matters to the history of this period precisely because it is documented, and it matters as what it is: the record of one man.
