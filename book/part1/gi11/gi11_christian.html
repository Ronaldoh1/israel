Christian Zionist Land Theology in the United States

The third religious current behind the whole-land claim is not Jewish. Christian Zionism, the belief among some Protestants that the modern state of Israel fulfils biblical prophecy and that Christians are bound to support it, has its own history, from John Nelson Darby's dispensationalism in the 1830s to the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, which the Road to 1948 volume sets out. [scene:s05|The Scofield Reference Bible] The verse most often cited, Genesis 12:3 ("I will bless those who bless you"), and the two Christian readings of it are discussed in the opening chapter. [scene:gx_s05|One verse, two readings] A later volume, The Theology Shield, treats the subject in depth. This section confines itself to one question: what named American Christian Zionist organisations and officials have said about the land, and what the record shows of their effect.

CUFI and John Hagee

The largest organisation is Christians United for Israel, which the San Antonio pastor John Hagee incorporated on 7 February 2006, reviving the name of a group founded in 1975. Its membership figures are its own: one million in 2012, two million in 2015, 5.1 million in 2018, and "over 10 million" in an undated claim. Its annual Washington summit sends delegates to lobby members of Congress, and Netanyahu has addressed it, by video in 2010 and again remotely, from Jerusalem, at the 2019 summit where Vice President Mike Pence spoke. [cite:ts8a_inn_netanyahu2019] [cite:gi11_wiki_cufi]

Hagee has stated the land theology directly. In a CUFI webcast on 6 January 2011 he said: "Jerusalem should never be divided for any reason. It is the property of the people of Israel." Citing Genesis 13, 15 and 17, he said that "God did not make a covenant with Washington, D.C. He made a covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob," and that "that covenant stands." [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] His stated policy position is narrower than the theology. The Christian Post corrected its first report to say that Hagee was sceptical of land-for-peace agreements but held that Israel's government should make its own decisions; elsewhere he said that "Israelis – and they alone – have the right to make existential decisions about land and peace," and described CUFI's action as "asking the White House not to pressure Israel into making territorial concessions that she does not wish to make." [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] [cite:gi11_jpost_hagee] He also said that he and other Christian Zionists had grown sceptical of withdrawals after south Lebanon and Gaza. [cite:gi11_jpost_hagee] In policy terms, then, the documented CUFI position is opposition to American pressure for territorial concessions, not a programme of expansion beyond the lines Israel holds.

What American evangelicals told pollsters

The most detailed survey of the belief behind this is LifeWay Research's poll of 2,002 Americans with evangelical beliefs, conducted from 20 to 28 September 2017 and sponsored by Chosen People Ministries and the author Joel C. Rosenberg. Eighty per cent agreed that Israel's founding in 1948 fulfilled biblical prophecy, and 63 per cent said they supported Israel because "God gave the land of Israel to the Jewish people." Yet only 23 per cent said Israel should sign a treaty giving the Palestinians a state in the West Bank and Gaza, 31 per cent disagreed, and 46 per cent were unsure. Support for Israel was 58 per cent among those aged 18 to 34 and 77 per cent among those 65 and over. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] The survey measures belief about the land; it does not show that respondents favoured Israeli rule beyond its present lines.

An ambassador and the Genesis map

The clearest point at which the theology met an office of state came in 2026. The US ambassador to Israel, Mike Huckabee, an evangelical Christian and long-standing supporter of the settlement movement, was asked by the commentator Tucker Carlson, in an interview aired on 20 February 2026, whether Israel had a right to the land promised to Abraham's descendants in Genesis, from the Nile to the Euphrates. He answered: "It would be fine if they took it all." He added that Israel was not seeking to expand, and when Carlson listed Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and large parts of Saudi Arabia and Iraq, replied: "Not sure we'd go that far. I mean, it would be a big piece of land." [cite:gi11_abc_huckabee]

The response was immediate. Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry called the remarks "extremist rhetoric" and "unacceptable" and asked the State Department to clarify its position; Egypt called them a "blatant violation" of international law and said that "Israel has no sovereignty over the occupied Palestinian territory or other Arab lands"; the Arab League called them "extremist and lacking any sound basis"; Jordan, Kuwait, Oman and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation also condemned them. [cite:gi11_abc_huckabee] [cite:gi11a_abc_huckabee] Huckabee then called his answer "somewhat of a hyperbolic statement," said of Israel that "they're not asking to take it over," and complained that the version Carlson posted "edited out my full response"; in the same exchange he said "Area C is Israel," which Al Jazeera noted appears to contradict the official US position opposing annexation. The State Department did not publicly address the remarks. [cite:gi11_aje_huckabee] [cite:gi11a_aje_huckabee] Neither the US government nor Israel's adopted the statement as policy. The territorial claim in Genesis is discussed in Part One and the 2025 interview in which Netanyahu was asked about a "Greater Israel" in Part Seven. [scene:gi1_phrase|Part One: the phrase] [scene:gi7_interview|Part Seven: the interview]

A smaller link runs through the Temple Mount. The red heifers flown to Israel in 2022 for the purification rite that some Third Temple activists consider a precondition for rebuilding were donated by an evangelical farmer in Texas, and an evangelical group, Boneh Israel, was involved in raising them. The Christian counter-record

Christian Zionism is not the position of American Christianity, nor of all evangelicals. In July 2007, 34 evangelical leaders signed a letter in The New York Times supporting a two-state solution. After Hagee's 2011 webcast, Richard Mouw, president of Fuller Theological Seminary, argued that God's promise to bless Ishmael in Genesis 17:20 also binds Christians today and that Israel's blessing depends partly on justice towards its neighbours, and Joel Hunter, a board member of the National Association of Evangelicals, said Hagee's view reflects "a limited portion of Christians who are millennial dispensationalists in their theology." [cite:gi11_cp_hagee2011] The Middle East Council of Churches and theologians in the Anglican and Catholic churches have criticised CUFI's Christian Zionist and end-times theology, and Hagee apologised after claiming that Hitler had been fulfilling God's will by helping to return Jews to Israel. [cite:gi11_wiki_cufi] [cite:gi11a_wiki_cufi] The 2017 survey's age gap points the same way: younger evangelicals were markedly less supportive than older ones. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway]

The finding is limited and documented. Named organisations and a named US official have stated a theology in which the land promised in Genesis belongs to the Jewish people, and CUFI has lobbied Washington against pressing Israel to give territory up. The record examined here does not show that this theology set US policy on borders, and the one official who voiced its widest version, the ambassador, called it hyperbolic within two days while his government stayed silent. Whether the lobby's influence shaped specific decisions, such as the US embassy's move to Jerusalem or the recognition of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan, is a question for The Theology Shield; this book records it as open.
