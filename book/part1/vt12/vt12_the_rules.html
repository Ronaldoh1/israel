The Rules: From the Secret of the Holy Office to Vos estis

Every national inquiry discussed in the next section asked the files it read what the bishop did when a complaint arrived and what the Church's own law told him to do. That law was written in Rome. This section follows it from 1922 to 2026, document by document, and keeps two questions apart that public debate often merges: what the rules required inside the canonical system, and what they said, or did not say, about the police and the civil courts. The documents themselves are on the Holy See's website, and the Holy See has published a historical introduction to them; both are cited here alongside the readings of canon lawyers and critics. [cite:vt12_va_histintro]

Crimen sollicitationis, 1922 and 1962

In 1922 the Holy Office, the Roman congregation responsible for doctrine and for certain grave crimes, issued an instruction called Crimen sollicitationis. Its main subject was a priest who used confession to solicit a penitent for sex. It also set procedure for what it called the "worst crime", a sexual act between a cleric and a person of the same sex, and treated sexual acts with prepubescent children and with animals in the same way for penal purposes. [cite:vt12_va_histintro] [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] In 1962, under John XXIII, the Holy Office reissued it with additions. Cardinal Alfredo Ottaviani signed the new edition, dated 16 March 1962, and addressed it to all patriarchs, archbishops, bishops and other local ordinaries, to be kept in diocesan archives, not published and not commented upon. [cite:vt12_va_crimen1962] [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] The Holy See's own account says copies were meant for bishops attending the Second Vatican Council, but "most of the copies were never distributed." [cite:vt12_va_histintro]

The secrecy clauses are the reason the document became notorious. Under section 11, proceedings were covered by the "secret of the Holy Office", during the trial and after the sentence; everyone attached to the tribunal was bound by it, and breach brought automatic excommunication that only the pope could lift. An accused priest who broke it was suspended. Accusers and witnesses could be put under an oath of secrecy, with excommunication as the sanction, under section 23. [cite:vt12_va_crimen1962] [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] The Holy See describes this as "a strict code of confidentiality which was meant to protect all persons concerned from undue publicity" until a verdict. [cite:vt12_va_histintro]

The document says nothing about reporting to the police. That silence is read in two ways. When it reached the press in 2003, a headline in The Guardian called it a "Vatican order to conceal priests' sex abuse." [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] Canon lawyers answered that nothing in it would have stopped a bishop, or anyone else, from going to the police, and that the secrecy bound people only inside a canonical trial. [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] Thomas Doyle, a canon lawyer and long-standing advocate for survivors, called it in 2006 "an explicit written policy to cover up cases"; he later wrote that he did not believe it "to be proof of an explicit conspiracy." [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen] Both readings agree on the narrower finding: the instruction put canonical proceedings under the strictest secrecy the Church had, and it gave a bishop no instruction to tell the civil authorities. It stayed in force until 2001. [cite:vt12_wiki_crimen]

2001: reserved to the Congregation, and still secret

On 30 April 2001 John Paul II issued the motu proprio Sacramentorum sanctitatis tutela, which replaced the 1922 instruction. [cite:vt12_va_histintro] On 18 May 2001 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith (CDF), the successor of the Holy Office, sent all bishops the letter De delictis gravioribus, signed by its prefect, Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, and its secretary, Archbishop Tarcisio Bertone. It listed the "graver delicts" now reserved to the Congregation, among them a cleric's sin against the Sixth Commandment with a minor under 18. It set a ten-year limitation period that began only when the victim turned 18, and it stated: "Cases of this kind are subject to the pontifical secret." [cite:vt12_va_ddg2001] Like its predecessor, the letter does not mention civil authorities. [cite:vt12_va_ddg2001]

The 2001 rules did two things at once. They centralised: a bishop could no longer close a credible case of child abuse locally, because the file had to go to Rome. And they kept the case under the pontifical secret, a formal confidentiality rule of the Holy See's own administration.Whether centralisation served victims or shielded clergy is the argument of the next two decades; the text of 2001 supports neither reading on its own. In 2014 the Holy See told the UN Committee against Torture that the CDF had confirmed 3,420 credible allegations between 2004 and 2013 and that 848 priests had been dismissed from the clerical state. [cite:vt5_cat_co2014] [scene:vt5_treaty_bodies|Part Five: the 2014 UN reviews]

2010: the civil law sentence

In April 2010, months after the Murphy report on Dublin described in the next section, the Holy See published a short guide to the CDF's procedures. Its first operative instruction read: "Civil law concerning reporting of crimes to the appropriate authorities should always be followed." [cite:vt12_va_cdfguide2010] Benedict XVI approved revised norms on 21 May 2010, which modified a limited number of points in the 2001 text. [cite:vt12_va_histintro] The civil-law sentence was guidance in a guide, not a canon of law; it told bishops to obey whatever reporting duty their own state imposed, and created none where the state imposed none.

2019: the secret lifted

The decisive change came under Francis. In May 2019 he issued Vos estis lux mundi, on a three-year trial basis. It made reporting obligatory for clerics and religious who learned of abuse or of its cover-up, required every diocese to have a public office to receive reports, set a procedure for investigating bishops through their metropolitan archbishop, extended protection to vulnerable adults, and forbade any "obligation of silence" on those who report. [cite:vt12_ewtn_vosestis2023] On 17 December 2019 a rescript signed by Cardinal Pietro Parolin abolished the pontifical secret for reports, trials and decisions on the crimes in Vos estis. Confidentiality remained, but the instruction stated that it does not prevent obligations under civil law, including any duty to report and enforceable requests from civil judges, and that victims and witnesses are under no obligation of silence. The same act raised the age in the child-pornography offence from 14 to 18. [cite:vt12_vn_rescript2019] The seal of confession was not affected.

Francis made Vos estis permanent on 25 March 2023, effective 30 April 2023, and extended it to lay leaders of international associations recognised by the Holy See. [cite:vt12_ewtn_vosestis2023] Separately, the penal book of the Code of Canon Law was rewritten. The apostolic constitution Pascite gregem Dei, signed on 23 May 2021 and in force from 8 December 2021, created a new canon 1398 punishing a cleric's sexual offence against a minor, including grooming and child pornography, with loss of office and other penalties up to dismissal from the clerical state. [cite:vt12_wiki_pascite] The victims' advocate Bart Smeets noted that the revised code still did not oblige Church members to report to secular authorities. [cite:vt12_wiki_pascite]

The Commission and its reports, 2024–2026

Francis created the Pontifical Commission for the Protection of Minors in 2014, under Cardinal Seán O'Malley of Boston; several founding members later resigned, citing resistance inside the Vatican. [cite:vt12_ncr_pcpm2024] In 2022 he asked it for an annual report. The first, presented on 29 October 2024, looked at a dozen countries, two religious orders and two Vatican offices, and said plainly that it was not an audit of how much abuse had occurred. Its sharpest criticism was aimed at the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith: slow procedures and secrecy that retraumatised victims, and a refusal to publish statistics or its own case law that "continues to foment distrust." It called for victims' right to information about their cases, an ombudsman, and compensation, which it framed as "conversional justice." [cite:vt12_ncr_pcpm2024]

The second report, released on 16 October 2025 under the Commission's new president, Archbishop Thibault Verny, ran to about 200 pages and covered 18 bishops' conferences, mainly in Africa and Europe, and two religious institutes. Forty survivors helped shape it, answering the criticism that the first report had heard too few of them. One member, Maud de Boer-Buquicchio, described a "global data gap" on abuse figures, and the Commission acknowledged that it still lacked a mature reporting instrument. Its six recommendations on reparation covered listening, apology, therapy and money. [cite:vt12_vn_pcpm2025] [cite:vt12_ncreg_pcpm2025] Leo XIV told its plenary on 16 March 2026 that listening to victims "must find concrete expression in every ecclesial community and institution," and approved new statutes on 20 May 2026 that place the Commission alongside the Dicastery and make it report directly to the pope; it advises and does not govern. [cite:vt12_va_leo_pcpm2026] [cite:vt12_vn_pcpmstatutes2026] As of 21 October 2026 no third annual report had been located in this research.

The record of the rules, read in order, shows a canonical system that for most of the twentieth century treated abuse as a matter for secret internal trial and said nothing to bishops about the state; a centralisation in 2001 that kept the secret; a first instruction to follow civil law in 2010; and the removal of the secret, with an explicit carve-out for civil law, only in December 2019. What the rules did not do, at any stage, was oblige a bishop to report to the police where his country's law did not.
