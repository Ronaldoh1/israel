The Syrian Front, Spring 1967

By the spring of 1967 the most dangerous of Israel's borders was the one with Syria. The government in Damascus was the product of the coup of February 1966, told in [scene:cw6_syria|Syria's radical turn], and in November 1966 it had signed a five-year defence pact with Egypt. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt] The pact is the hinge of this section. Before it, a clash on the Syrian line was Syria's affair. After it, a clash on the Syrian line was a test of whether Egypt would honour its commitment, and of whether Nasser would act when his new ally was struck.

The Syria that Israel faced. The American government's own view of Damascus was severe. In December 1966 the CIA called Syria "the most bellicose Arab state," and reported that it gave Fatah a base, training and a propaganda outlet. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] Fatah's raids into Israel, from Syrian soil and from the territory of other states, are the subject of [scene:cw6_fatah|Fatah's campaign]. On the Israeli side the raids were the grievance; on the Syrian side, Israel's answers to them were.

7 April 1967. On 7 April 1967 fighting on the Syrian border became a land and air engagement, as GlobalSecurity describes it. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_s] In the air, Israeli aircraft shot down six Syrian fighters, according to Fanack. [cite:rs_cw6_fanack] The sources fetched for this book do not give the incident that began the fighting that day, the losses on the ground, or Israel's own losses, and they are not supplied here.

The historian Henry Laurens reads the day's significance in Cairo rather than in Damascus. The shooting down of six Syrian MiGs, he writes, exposed Nasser's inaction to his Arab critics. [cite:rs_cw6_laurens] Five months after signing a defence pact with Syria, Egypt had watched Syria's air force lose six aircraft and had done nothing. In Laurens's account, that inaction was noticed by Nasser's Arab critics, and became a political cost as well as a military fact. Laurens's reading is an interpretation of Nasser's position, not a record of his deliberations; but it explains why the next alarm from the Syrian front would find Cairo under pressure to be seen to act.

Israel's warnings. In the weeks that followed, Israel's leaders spoke in terms that went beyond the raids themselves. The historian David Tal records that the chief of staff, Rabin, said that the Damascus "authorities are behind the terrorists," which Tal reads as hinting at a change of regime. [cite:rs_cw6_tal_jvl] Tal also records that Prime Minister Eshkol warned that "the last 14 incidents might lead us to take measures no less harsh than those taken on April 7th." [cite:rs_cw6_tal_jvl] Eshkol's words place his warning after 7 April; Tal, as fetched here, does not give the exact date of either statement. The Library of Congress study of Egypt summarizes the period more broadly: in 1967, it says, Israeli leaders repeatedly threatened to invade Syria and overthrow its government if the raids across the Syrian border did not stop. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt]

The word "terrorists" in that sentence is Rabin's, as Tal records it, and this book quotes it as his. The CIA's December 1966 memorandum is the source for who backed the raiders. How the sources of the period used the term, and what they meant by it, is examined in the section on Fatah's campaign.

Who was escalating. The question of who drove the Syrian front toward war is disputed, and the two readings rest on different documents. The first emphasizes Israeli pressure. The Library of Congress study puts Israel's repeated threats against Syria at the centre of its account of 1967, and Laurens describes Rabin's "show of force" against Syria. [cite:rs_cw6_locegypt] [cite:rs_cw6_laurens] On this reading, Israeli threats to strike at the government in Damascus, and an air battle that showed Israel could do so, were what turned a border dispute into a crisis involving Egypt.

The second reading emphasizes Syria's sponsorship of the raids. The CIA, in December 1966, described Syria as the most bellicose of the Arab states and the patron of Fatah: base, training, propaganda. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_s] On this reading, Israel's warnings were a response to a campaign of attacks across its border that a government was sponsoring, and Eshkol's reference to fourteen incidents was a count of what Israel was being asked to absorb. Both readings have documentary support, and the two are not exclusive: the record fetched here shows Syrian sponsorship of raids and Israeli threats against the Syrian government in the same months. What it does not show is which, in the minds of the decision-makers in Damascus, Jerusalem and Cairo, weighed more.

The gap. Several things the reader would want are missing from the sources. The incident that set off the fighting of 7 April is not described; nor are the fourteen incidents Eshkol counted, nor the casualties on either side. The dates of Rabin's and Eshkol's statements are not given. And there is no Egyptian document here recording how Cairo judged its obligations under the November 1966 pact after the air battle. What the record does establish is the position on the eve of the crisis: a defence pact that tied Egypt to Syria, a humiliation in the air that Laurens reads as falling on Nasser, and Israeli warnings that the Library of Congress study and Tal both record as aimed at the government in Damascus itself. On 13 May 1967 a Soviet warning reached Cairo that Israel was about to attack Syria. That warning, and what followed it, is the subject of [scene:cw6_may_crisis|the May crisis].
