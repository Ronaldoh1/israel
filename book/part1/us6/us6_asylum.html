Asylum, Detention and Deportation

Both states have made the people who arrive without permission a central political question, and both have built detention and removal systems to deal with them. The scale is very different. Israel's asylum population, mostly Eritreans and Sudanese who crossed from Egypt, never exceeded about 60,000 people; America's immigration courts alone had a backlog of about 3.8 million cases in 2025. This section sets out how each system has treated people seeking protection, and what each has spent and built to remove people.

Israel: from the Sinai border to Holot

Between 2006 and 2013 about 64,000 asylum seekers and migrants entered Israel across the land border with Egypt's Sinai Peninsula, among them about 50,000 Sudanese and Eritreans. [cite:us6_hrw_holot2014] The number in the country peaked at close to 60,000 in 2012. [cite:us6_972_holot] The government's main legal tool was the Prevention of Infiltration Law of 1954, a statute that depends on the state of emergency in force since 1948, which Part Two describes. [cite:us2_adalah_emergency2003] [scene:us2_emergency|Part Two: emergency powers] Israel completed a fence along the Egyptian border, after which irregular entries, in Human Rights Watch's words, "fallen to near zero". [cite:us6_hrw_holot2014]

The Knesset and the Supreme Court then went back and forth over detention. A January 2012 amendment to the 1954 law allowed new arrivals to be held for long periods without trial; the court struck it down in September 2013. The Knesset passed a new amendment in December 2013 and opened Holot, an "open" facility in the Negev about 65 kilometres from Beersheba, surrounded by a four-metre fence, where residents had to report for roll call three times a day and be inside by 10 p.m. Human Rights Watch called it "a detention center in all but name". On 22 September 2014 the court voided the Holot provisions by 7 to 2 and one-year detention of new arrivals by 6 to 3, giving the state 90 days. [cite:us6_hrw_holot2014] The government responded each time by changing the criteria and time limits, and Holot stayed open until 14 March 2018. More than 13,000 people were held there over its life. [cite:us6_972_holot]

Very few were recognised as refugees. Human Rights Watch reported in 2014 that Israel had rejected every Sudanese asylum claim and more than 99 per cent of Eritrean claims; Minority Rights Group put the recognition rate below 1 per cent in 2025. [cite:us6_hrw_holot2014] [cite:us6_mrg_african] Instead the state encouraged departure: 3,959 Eritreans and Sudanese left under a "voluntary return" programme from 2013 to 2017, and about 1,700 were transferred to Uganda from 2015 to 2018. [cite:us6_972_holot] [cite:us6_mrg_african] In January 2018 the government gave asylum seekers three months to leave or face indefinite imprisonment or deportation to Rwanda or Uganda; the Supreme Court issued an interim injunction on 15 March. [cite:us6_972_holot] On 2 April 2018 Israel and the UN refugee agency (UNHCR) agreed to resettle about half the population in Western states and give the rest temporary status; the government cancelled the agreement the next day under pressure from coalition partners. The court later ruled indefinite detention unlawful without formal agreements with receiving countries. [cite:us6_mrg_african]

About 38,000 asylum seekers remained in March 2018, and an estimated 25,000 in late 2025, most still without refugee status. [cite:us6_972_holot] [cite:us6_mrg_african] They are excluded from Israel's National Health Insurance, which covers citizens and permanent residents. [cite:us16_cwf_israel] [scene:us16_systems|Part Sixteen: health systems]

The United States: refugees, asylum and the border

The United States runs two protection systems. Refugees are selected abroad and resettled under an annual ceiling set by the president. The ceiling for fiscal year 1980, after the Vietnam War, was 231,700, and 207,116 refugees were admitted that year; before fiscal year 2018 the ceiling never fell below 67,000. On 20 January 2025 Executive Order 14163 suspended refugee entry except in individual cases. The ceiling for fiscal year 2025, set by President Biden, had been 125,000, and 38,102 were admitted, almost all before the suspension. For fiscal year 2026 the ceiling was set at 7,500, to be "primarily" allocated to Afrikaners from South Africa; it was raised in May 2026 to 17,500, still the lowest in the programme's history. By 30 June 2026, 7,730 had been admitted, nearly all South African. [cite:us6_crs_refugees]

Asylum, the second system, is claimed by people already in the country or at the border. Its courts are overwhelmed. The Migration Policy Institute counted a backlog of about 3.8 million cases in the immigration courts by mid-2025, more than double the 2021 level; the administration had fired more than 139 immigration judges, and about 68 per cent of people in removal proceedings had no lawyer. [cite:us6_mpi_courts] On his first day in office in 2025 the president also invoked his power to suspend entry to close the southern border to asylum claims. On 24 April 2026 the federal appeals court in Washington held, in RAICES v. Noem, that this power cannot be used to "override asylum laws established by Congress"; the plaintiffs, RAICES among them, expected an appeal to the Supreme Court. [cite:us6_raices_dccir] Status as of October 2026: this book did not find a Supreme Court ruling on the case.

Border crossings fell sharply in the same period. Customs and Border Protection recorded 3.3 million enforcement actions in 2023, 2.3 million in 2024 and 391,000 in 2025. [cite:us6_usafacts_enf]

Removals and detention over time

American removals, deportations under a formal order, have risen over four decades under presidents of both parties. The Department of Homeland Security counts 18,013 removals in fiscal year 1980, 30,039 in 1990, 188,467 in 2000 and 359,795 in 2008. They peaked under President Obama at 432,334 in 2013, a year in which, by Pew's count, 83 per cent of deportations were carried out without a hearing before a judge. Removals fell to 237,364 in 2020 and 85,783 in 2021, when many people at the border were expelled under a pandemic order and counted separately. [cite:us6_dhs_table39] [cite:us6_pew_2013] Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) carried out about 320,000 deportations in fiscal year 2025, 18 per cent more than the year before. [cite:us6_usafacts_enf] The DHS and ICE figures are different counts, the first covering all removals by every DHS agency and the second ICE alone, so the 2025 figure is not directly comparable with the earlier series.

Detention grew with it. ICE held a peak of 68,289 people in early February 2026, 73.6 per cent with no criminal conviction. [cite:us4_trac_ice] [scene:us4_detention|Part Four: detention] The budget act President Trump signed in July 2025 provided about $170 billion for immigration enforcement over four years, $75 billion of it for ICE, including about $45 billion for detention, and nearly $47 billion for the border wall; ICE's funding for fiscal year 2025, about $28.7 billion, was nearly three times its 2024 budget. [cite:us6_brennan_obbba] The data and surveillance tools that ICE uses, including software bought from Palantir, are described in The One-Way Mirror. [scene:ow9_ice|The One-Way Mirror: ICE]

What the two records show

In each country the courts set the outer limits. Israel's Supreme Court twice struck down detention without trial for asylum seekers and stopped the 2018 deportations; America's courts blocked the border asylum closure and the birthright order. [cite:us6_hrw_holot2014] [cite:us6_mrg_african] [cite:us6_raices_dccir] Inside those limits the two governments made different choices. Israel recognised almost no one and pressed people to leave, yet it never carried out a mass deportation and about 25,000 remain in a long-term legal limbo. [cite:us6_mrg_african] The United States has a large asylum and resettlement system on paper, but by 2026 it had cut refugee admissions to the lowest level since 1980, left millions of cases waiting in its courts, and funded immigration enforcement and detention at several times their earlier levels. [cite:us6_crs_refugees] [cite:us6_mpi_courts] [cite:us6_brennan_obbba]
