Aksum: A King, a Captive and a Bishop from Alexandria

Christianity reached the kingdom of Aksum, in what is now northern Ethiopia and Eritrea, through a shipwreck and two captives, and it reached the throne before it reached the people. The story is told by the Latin historian Rufinus, and it begins around 316. Two young Christians from Tyre, Frumentius and Edesius, were sailing on the Red Sea when their ship was attacked; they survived and were taken as slaves to the royal court of Aksum. They rose to positions of trust, and Frumentius helped govern on behalf of the young prince. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius]

When he was freed, Frumentius travelled to Alexandria and asked its patriarch to send a bishop to Aksum. The patriarch was Athanasius, the same man whose Easter letter of 367 would list the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, and he consecrated Frumentius himself, traditionally in 328 or between 340 and 346. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius] [cite:vt22_wiki_frumentius] Ethiopians remember him as Abba Salama and Kesate Birhan. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius] The arrangement he began lasted sixteen centuries: the head of the Ethiopian church was a bishop sent from Alexandria, and until the twentieth century he was always a cleric of the Coptic Church of Egypt. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc]

The king converted first. Ezana, who ruled around 320 to 350, became a Christian, and the change is visible in his money. The crescent and disk that had marked Aksumite coins were removed and replaced with the cross. [cite:vt27_met_aksum] Aksum minted its own coins, and putting the cross on them announced the new faith to everyone who handled them. The Metropolitan Museum notes that Christianity was at first limited to the royal elite and spread more widely only in the later fifth century. [cite:vt27_met_aksum] The faith came down from the throne.

Almost at once, the bishop of Aksum became an object of imperial politics. Around 356 the Roman emperor Constantius II wrote to Ezana and his brother Saizana asking them to send Frumentius to Alexandria to be examined and replaced by Theophilos, a bishop of the emperor's choosing. [cite:vt27_wiki_frumentius] No record shows that Frumentius was replaced. The episode is small, but it is the first documented attempt by a foreign power to decide who would hold the Ethiopian church, and it set a pattern that would recur from Constantinople, Rome, London and Rome again.

After the Council of Chalcedon in 451, the Ethiopian church followed Alexandria in rejecting the council's definition of Christ's two natures; it accepts the first three ecumenical councils and calls itself Tewahedo, "made one". [cite:vt27_eotc_chalcedon] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] Monks known as the Nine Saints, arriving after 451, carried the faith beyond the court and the towns. [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] The break with Chalcedon cut Ethiopia off from the imperial church of Constantinople and, later, from Rome. It is one reason the Ethiopian canon could grow on its own, beyond the reach of the councils that closed the Western lists. Isolation preserved the books. The throne, the next part shows, put one of them to work.
