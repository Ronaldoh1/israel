Inside the Banks: Who Decided

A bank does not decide anything. People inside it do, under a structure of executives, committees and a board. The settlements above name institutions. Testimony and the regulators' findings name the desks where the decisions about Epstein were made, and they show something the institution-level picture hides: the banks were linked to one another through the people who handled him.

JPMorgan: three executives and a board that was never asked

Under oath in May 2023, JPMorgan's chief executive, Jamie Dimon, said the decision to keep Epstein sat with three people. Mary Erdoes, head of private banking, and Jes Staley, then running asset management and investment banking, could have dropped him. The general counsel, Stephen Cutler, was in Dimon's words the "ultimate decider" who could overrule them. In July 2011 Cutler wrote that Epstein was "not an honorable person in any way" and "should not be a client". The bank kept him for two more years. Dimon testified that he was not involved and first learned of Epstein in 2019. When the bank finally let him go in 2013, after Staley left, Erdoes went to the townhouse to tell him. [cite:ep3_ab_dimon2023] These are Dimon's statements; the other executives' accounts are not all public.

The board's role was tested in court and never reached. A pension fund sued JPMorgan's directors for ignoring the warning signs. In August 2023 Judge Jed Rakoff dismissed the case because the fund had not first asked the board to act, without ruling on what the directors knew. [cite:ep3_pw_derivative2023] Nothing released shows the board deciding anything about Epstein.

Deutsche Bank: an approval by email

New York's regulator traced Deutsche Bank's decision to a single chain of emails in 2013. A relationship manager who had worked on Epstein's account before proposed him as a client. The head of wealth management for the Americas said he had consulted the region's head of anti-money-laundering compliance and its general counsel, and that both agreed Epstein could skip the committee that reviewed reputational risk. The general counsel chaired that committee. The bank had no other record of the conversation, and that one approval was then used to open account after account. When the committee did look at him, in February 2015, it decided to continue "business as usual" on conditions that never reached the staff handling his accounts. [cite:ep3_rc_dbfailures2020] The New York Times named the two bankers: the relationship manager was Paul Morris, and the wealth-management head who approved the client was Charles Packard. Both later visited Epstein at home to question him about red flags. [cite:ep3_fox_dbexecs2020]

The banker who moved with the client

Morris is the thread that links three of the four banks. He worked on Epstein's account at JPMorgan from about 2009. He moved to Deutsche Bank in late 2012, and Epstein's money followed him there in September 2013. In 2016 he joined Bank of America's Merrill Lynch. Epstein never became a Merrill client, but Morris kept writing to him; a market note from his account reached Epstein's inbox in July 2019, days before the arrest. The files also record gifts to Epstein and to his assistant, Lesley Groff. Morris is not accused of a crime and was not a defendant in the victims' suits. He did not respond to a request for comment. [cite:ep3_ta_morris2026]

Bank of America is linked by money as well as by Morris. The victims' suit against it alleged that Leon Black sent his $170 million to Epstein through Bank of America accounts, and that Maxwell used the bank too. Black was not a defendant and denies wrongdoing; the settlement meant his scheduled deposition never took place. [cite:ep3_cj_boa2026] Staley carried the JPMorgan relationship into a second bank in a different way: he became chief executive of Barclays in 2015 and left in 2021 as British regulators examined his ties to Epstein. [cite:ep3_pbs_staley2026]

What this layer shows, and what it does not

It shows that the banks did not each discover Epstein on their own. A client relationship, and the banker who ran it, moved from one bank to the next, and at each bank a small number of named executives overrode or bypassed the warnings. It does not show that any board, owner or shareholder directed those decisions. Listed banks are owned by large numbers of shareholders, often the same big investment funds. Shared ownership of that kind would connect the banks to one another whether or not Epstein existed, so it says nothing about him, and this book does not treat it as a link. The links that matter here are the ones on paper: who approved, who carried the account, and whose money passed through.
