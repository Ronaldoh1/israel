How the Flight Was Told: "They Left on Orders" Until the Archives Opened

For more than a decade, the dominant Western account held that the Palestinians of 1948 left voluntarily, on the orders of Arab leaders, and that this had been established at the time. The record shows something different. Parts of what happened were reported promptly. The explanation that took hold for the mass flight, however, was not supported by the documents, and it took some forty years for the documents to displace it.

Some events were known almost at once. The killings at Deir Yassin in April 1948 were reported in Western newspapers within days and documented by the Red Cross delegate who entered the village. News of the massacre spread through Arab Palestine, and the historian Benny Morris records it as a major accelerant of flight. [cite:w_morris_birth]

Objection was public, and some of it came from prominent Jewish figures. On 4 December 1948 a group that included Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and Sidney Hook published a letter in The New York Times condemning Menachem Begin's new Herut party, citing Deir Yassin and calling the party fascist in its methods. [cite:w_arendt_letter] It stands as a documented contemporary Jewish objection, published in America's paper of record.

Yet the explanation for the flight that became standard in the West ran the other way. The claim was that Arab leaders had broadcast orders by radio for civilians to leave. In the 1950s it became the usual explanation in Western and Israeli official accounts, and its effect was to place responsibility for the refugee problem on the Arab states.

The claim was testable, because the broadcasts had been monitored. In May 1961 the Irish writer Erskine Childers published "The Other Exodus" in The Spectator. He reported that he had examined the British and American monitoring records of Arab broadcasts in 1948, including the BBC's, and found no evacuation orders; he found instead appeals to stay. His article set off a long published dispute, with claims and counterclaims running for weeks in the letters pages. The Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi had made the same case, from Arab sources, in 1959. [cite:w_childers]

The decisive correction came from Israel's own records. In 1988 Benny Morris published The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem, working from newly opened Israeli military and state archives. He concluded that the main causes of flight were military attack, expulsion and fear. He found that some local Arab commanders had ordered evacuations of women and children, and that there was no general Arab order to leave. Later editions of his work found more expulsion orders. [cite:w_morris_birth]

The trajectory matters as much as the conclusion. The early account was a political narrative that the documents did not support, and the documents that corrected it were Israeli state archives, not an adversary's claims. That correction has limits. Historians still disagree about intent and planning, including the role of Plan Dalet. But the account that the refugees simply left on orders is no longer defended by mainstream scholarship.

The forty-year gap is itself part of the history. The explanation that took hold in the 1950s was checked against the monitoring records only in 1961, and against Israel's own archives only in 1988.
