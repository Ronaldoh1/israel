The Question

This volume asks one question and answers it as far as the documents allow. For almost ninety years the Holy See publicly refused Zionism. It refused the movement when Theodor Herzl asked a pope for support in January 1904, it argued for an international Jerusalem when the United Nations divided Palestine in 1947, and it declined to recognize the State of Israel from 1948 until it signed the Fundamental Agreement in Jerusalem on 30 December 1993. The question is whether that refusal was what it appeared to be, a real policy with real costs, or a front: a public position that covered private support. Everything else in the book, from the origins of the papal state to its bank, its courts and its archives, is here because it bears on that question or on the questions a reader has to settle before it can be answered fairly.

The refusal, in the Holy See's own record

The refusal begins with a conversation known from a single source. Herzl's diary entry dated 26 January 1904 opens "Yesterday I was with the Pope", and records Pius X's answer to his request: "We cannot prevent the Jews from going to Jerusalem—but we could never sanction it." The pope gave a theological reason: "The Jews have not recognized our Lord, therefore we cannot recognize the Jewish people." He closed with "Non possumus", the formula of papal refusal, and said that if Jews settled in Palestine the Church would have "churches and priests ready to baptize all of you." The audience had been arranged by Count Berthold Dominik Lippay, a papal portrait painter. [cite:vt1_ccjr_herzl] The account of the audience rests on Herzl's diary alone, so the words are Herzl's account of what he heard; the volume treats them as documented evidence of what Herzl recorded, and as one party's report of what the pope said. [cite:dv1_ej_piusx]

That first refusal fused two different things. One was a political judgment about a movement and about who should hold Jerusalem. The other was a theological statement about the Jewish people. The distinction matters for everything that follows, because the Holy See itself later pulled the two apart. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra Aetate of 28 October 1965 said that "God holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers" and that the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today"; it said nothing about land or a state. [cite:ts11_va_nostra4] Twenty years later the Holy See's Commission for Religious Relations with the Jews wrote that the existence of the State of Israel and its political options "should be envisaged not in a perspective which is in itself religious, but in their reference to the common principles of international law." [cite:ts1_crrj_notes1985] From 1985 at the latest, then, the Holy See said in writing that its position on Israel was a matter of diplomacy and law, not doctrine. This volume keeps the two subjects apart in the same way: Zionism is a political movement, Israel is a state, and neither is Judaism.

The political refusal took its clearest form over Jerusalem. The United Nations General Assembly's resolution 181 of 29 November 1947 placed Jerusalem in a corpus separatum "under a special international regime" to be administered by the United Nations, and the Holy See supported that internationalization. [cite:vt1_wiki_corpus] When the war of 1948 divided the city instead, Pius XII's encyclical Redemptoris nostri cruciatus of 15 April 1949 asked that Jerusalem be "accorded and legally guaranteed an 'international' status" and urged Catholics to press their governments for it. [cite:dv1_rnc_1949] The State of Israel, proclaimed on 14 May 1948, was not recognized by the Holy See for the next forty-five years. [cite:vt17_jcpa_schvindlerman] [cite:vt1_va_fa1993]

The end of the refusal

The Fundamental Agreement was signed in Jerusalem on 30 December 1993 by Monsignor Claudio Celli for the Holy See and Yossi Beilin, Israel's deputy foreign minister. Its Article 14 provided for the exchange of special representatives at once and for full diplomatic relations, at the level of an apostolic nunciature and an embassy, once the agreement was in force. [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] A joint protocol signed in Jerusalem on 14 June 1994 established those relations from 15 June. [cite:vt1_jvl_protocol1994] The same agreement also contained a clause that any test of the front hypothesis has to read closely. In Article 11 the Holy See, while keeping the right to teach on moral questions, declared itself "solemnly committed to remaining a stranger to all merely temporal conflicts", a principle that "applies specifically to disputed territories and unsettled borders." [cite:vt1_va_fa1993] That language echoes Article 24 of the Lateran Treaty of 1929, in which the Holy See declared that it would remain outside the temporal disputes of other states unless they jointly asked for its mediation. [cite:vt1_va_lateran]

Recognition did not end the Holy See's separate dealings with the Palestinians. On 26 June 2015 Archbishop Paul Gallagher, the Holy See's Secretary for Relations with States, and Riad Malki, foreign minister of the State of Palestine, signed a Comprehensive Agreement at the Vatican on the life and activity of the Church in Palestine. [cite:vt1_rv_palestine2015] Israel's foreign ministry called the Holy See's recognition of Palestine one-sided. [cite:ts11_wiki_hspal]

As of 21 October 2026 the relations established in 1994 remained in place. On 4 September 2025 Pope Leo XIV, elected on 8 May 2025, received President Isaac Herzog; the Holy See's statement called for the release of all hostages, a permanent ceasefire in Gaza and humanitarian access, and repeated that "a two-state solution is the only way out of the current war." [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] [cite:vt1_vn_herzog2025] On 22 January 2026 Leo XIV named Archbishop Giorgio Lingua apostolic nuncio to Israel and apostolic delegate in Jerusalem and Palestine. [cite:vt1_aleteia_lingua] Those contacts continued after Israeli fire struck the Holy Family church compound in Gaza City on 17 July 2025, killing three people; Netanyahu expressed regret over "stray ammunition", while the Latin Patriarch, Cardinal Pierbattista Pizzaballa, said he believed a tank shell had hit the church directly, adding that he was not sure. [cite:ts11_ncr_holyfamily] [cite:vt1_audit_ncr_holyfamily] Part Eighteen sets out that record and asks why the Holy See has spoken so strongly and yet not suspended relations.

Why the other questions belong here

A reader cannot judge whether a sovereign's public position was a front without knowing what kind of sovereign it is, how it makes decisions, where its money comes from and goes, and what it keeps out of view. That is why the volume begins not in 1904 but with the papal state of the eighth century and the settlement of 1929 that created Vatican City, and why it spends whole parts on the Holy See's bank, its courts, its archives, its handling of abuse and the unsolved disappearance of Emanuela Orlandi. Each of those subjects is a place where the public face of the institution and its private conduct can be compared on the documents, and the comparison shows how far, and in which matters, the Holy See's public statements have been a reliable guide to what it did. The record on Zionism is then read against that background, in Parts Sixteen to Eighteen, and the verdict is given in Part Nineteen.
