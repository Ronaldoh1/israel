In the middle of the twentieth century two large movements of people reshaped the region. In the war of 1947–1949 roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians were displaced, the event Palestinians call the Nakba.[cite:wiki_nakba] Between 1948 and 1972 roughly 850,000 Jews left Arab and Muslim countries. The two are often told as one story, in which each cancels the other. This chapter tells them separately, each on its own record, and then tests the comparison.

It sits in the Baseline, ahead of the history of 1948, because the comparison is made constantly and usually without its evidence, and because the Jewish exodus belongs to many countries and runs through decades that the history of the Mandate does not reach. The Palestinian displacement is told in full in the next volume; here it appears as one side of the comparison.

The first section follows the exodus from Iraq, where the turning point came in Baghdad on 1–2 June 1941, seven years before Israel existed, with the pogrom known as the Farhud.[cite:ushmm_farhud] It traces Iraq's criminalization of "Zionism" in 1948, the law of March 1950 that made departure conditional on surrendering citizenship, the airlift called Operation Ezra and Nehemiah, the disputed Baghdad bombings and Israel's sabotage operation in Egypt in 1954, and then the arrival in Israel: the transit camps, the Yemenite Children Affair and the Ringworm Affair.[cite:yated_ezra_nechemia] [cite:wiki_lavon_affair] The second section goes country by country, through Libya, Egypt, Aleppo, Aden and Bahrain, Yemen, Morocco and Algeria, and sets out the arguments over the total and over the word "refugees."[cite:rs_ch01_beker_jpsr] [cite:rs_ch01_ahram_amayreh] The third compares the two displacements on mechanism, duration, the number of governments involved and citizenship afterwards, and shows why the claim of an even exchange is rejected from both directions.

Throughout, two readings of the Jewish departures are kept side by side: rescue from persecution, and active recruitment by the Israeli state and the Jewish Agency. The record documents both, in proportions that differed from country to country.
