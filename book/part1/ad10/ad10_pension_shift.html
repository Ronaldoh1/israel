From Pension to 401(k)

The retirement half of the Dream was taught as a reward that arrived on its own: work for a good employer long enough and a monthly cheque follows you into old age. For a large share of private-sector workers in the 1970s that was a description of their plan. It no longer describes most plans. No law abolished pensions; what changed, slowly, was which plan employers chose to offer and who carries the risk that the money runs short.

Two terms carry the argument. A defined-benefit plan, the traditional pension, promises a defined amount, usually a monthly payment for life set by a formula of salary and years of service. The employer funds it, invests the money and makes up any shortfall, so the employer bears two risks: that investments do badly and that retirees live longer than expected. A defined-contribution plan, of which the 401(k) is the best-known kind, defines only what goes in. The worker, and often the employer, pays into an individual account; what the worker has at retirement depends on how much was paid in, what the investments earned, what fees were taken, and whether the money was withdrawn early. The worker must then make it last an unknown lifespan: the plan promises an account, not an income.

Studebaker, ERISA and the guarantee

The old pension had a weakness that one bankruptcy made national news. In 1963 the carmaker Studebaker closed its South Bend, Indiana, plant and terminated its pension plan. About 3,600 workers who had already retired received their full benefits. About 4,000 active employees received roughly 15 per cent of what they had been promised, and thousands under 40 received nothing; in all, more than 8,500 workers lost some or all of their pensions. [cite:ad10_pbgc_studebaker] [cite:ad10_pbgc_history] The pension had been a promise backed only by the company that made it.

Congress answered eleven years later. On 2 September 1974 President Gerald Ford signed the Employee Retirement Income Security Act, known as ERISA, which set rules for funding, vesting and the conduct of the people who run plans, and created the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, a federal insurer for private pensions funded by premiums from the plans it covers. PBGC paid its first benefit, $140.75, on 28 February 1975. It now pays about 926,000 retirees from more than 5,000 failed plans, owes benefits to another 428,000 who have not yet retired, and insures about 30 million people in private defined-benefit plans. [cite:ad10_pbgc_history] ERISA protected promises once made. It did not require anyone to make them: in the Labor Department's words, "ERISA does not require any employer to establish a retirement plan." [cite:us18a_dol_erisa]

A tax clause becomes the system

The 401(k) began as a paragraph in a tax bill. The Revenue Act of 1978, signed by President Jimmy Carter on 6 November 1978, was mainly a package of tax cuts; among its provisions was a new section 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code, promoted by Representative Barber Conable, which confirmed that employees could have part of their pay put into a plan before income tax, effective from 1 January 1980. [cite:ad10_napa_1978] Arrangements of this "cash or deferred" kind had existed since the 1950s, approved by an IRS ruling in 1956. [cite:ad10_ici_retro] What turned the clause into a mass product was the IRS's proposed regulation of 10 November 1981, which made clear that ordinary salary reductions qualified; benefits writers date the modern 401(k) from that day. [cite:ad10_ici_retro] [cite:ad10_napa_1978] A long-time retirement-industry editor summarises the record plainly: the 401(k) was "never intended (nor designed) to replace defined benefit pensions." [cite:ad10_napa_1978]

How fast the shift came

The Labor Department's historical tables, built from the annual reports every plan files, show the change year by year. In 1975 private defined-benefit plans had 27.2 million active participants, meaning people still working and earning credit, against 11.2 million in defined-contribution plans. In 1984 the lines crossed, 30.5 million to 30.1 million. By 2000 it was 50.9 million against 22.2 million, and in 2023, the latest year, 96.4 million against 11.1 million. The number of defined-benefit plans peaked at about 170,000 in 1985 and stood at 46,233 in 2023, against 790,610 defined-contribution plans. [cite:ad10_dol_hist] These are counts of plan memberships, not of people: a worker in both kinds of plan is counted twice, and the Department changed its definition in 2004, so the series is best read as two segments. On either segment the direction is the same. The defined-benefit share of active participants, by this book's arithmetic, fell from 71 per cent in 1975 to 28 per cent in 2004 on the old definition, and from 25 per cent in 2004 to 10 per cent in 2023 on the new one. [cite:ad10_dol_hist]

Why it happened is disputed in a way the numbers alone cannot settle. The Investment Company Institute, the mutual fund industry's trade association, argued in its 25-year review that few employers replaced a pension with a 401(k) outright; most of the shift, in its account, came from the decline of the unionised manufacturing industries where pensions were common, from pension sponsors adding a 401(k) on top, and from new firms choosing the newer plan. [cite:ad10_ici_retro] The participant counts, which fall slowly rather than collapse, fit that account; it does not change where the risk now sits. In March 2026, 70 per cent of private-industry workers had access to a defined-contribution plan and 14 per cent to a defined-benefit plan, while 86 per cent of state and local government workers still had a pension available. [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26]

Who is in, and how much they have

Access to any workplace plan has been steady at about seven private workers in ten: 72 per cent in March 2026, with 52 per cent actually participating. [cite:ad10_bls_ebs26] The gap falls by pay. Among the lowest-paid tenth of private workers in March 2025, 38 per cent had access and 15 per cent participated; among part-time workers, 47 and 23 per cent. [cite:us22_bls_ebs25] Inside plans the pattern repeats: in Vanguard's 2025 records, covering about 1,300 plans and 4.6 million accounts, 43 per cent of eligible workers earning under $15,000 participated, against 74 per cent of those earning $30,000 to $50,000 and 95 per cent of those earning over $150,000. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26]

Balances show why the difference between an average and a median matters (both are defined in [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two]). The average Vanguard account at the end of 2025 held $167,970; the median, the account in the middle, held $44,115. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] A few large accounts pull the average up to almost four times the typical one. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, which covers all families rather than one recordkeeper's clients, found that 54.9 per cent of families held any retirement account; among those that did, the median balance was $106,000. [cite:ad10_scf26] Widely shared figures putting the median 401(k) in a worker's fifties above $250,000 do not match either source.

Fees, and the money trail of the risk transfer

An account has costs a pension member never sees directly. Fees are charged as a percentage of assets each year, through each fund's expense ratio and the plan's administration charges, so small differences compound. The Labor Department's long-standing illustration takes a $25,000 balance growing for 35 years at 7 per cent a year: with fees of 0.5 per cent it ends at $227,000, with 1.5 per cent at $163,000, 28 per cent less. [cite:ad10_politifact_fees] A widely repeated version of that example claims Wall Street takes a third of workers' savings; fact-checkers rated it mostly false, because 1.5 per cent was well above the median all-in fee of 0.72 per cent found in a 2009 industry survey. [cite:ad10_politifact_fees] Fees have since fallen sharply. The asset-weighted expense ratio of equity mutual funds held in 401(k)s fell from 0.76 per cent in 2000 to 0.26 per cent in 2024, and target-date funds from 0.67 to 0.29 per cent between 2008 and 2024. [cite:ad10_ici_fees25] The sums remain large: mutual funds held $6.2 trillion of 401(k) assets in mid-2026, and 0.26 per cent of $6.2 trillion is about $16 billion a year, a rough order of magnitude since bond and hybrid funds charge different rates. [cite:ad10_ici_q2_26] [cite:ad10_ici_fees25]

Where it works as promised

One design change has done much of what reformers hoped. When a plan enrols workers automatically and lets them opt out, rather than waiting for them to opt in, participation in Vanguard's plans was 94 per cent in 2025, against 64 per cent in plans without it; 61 per cent of its plans now enrol automatically. [cite:ad10_vanguard_has26] The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 requires most 401(k) and 403(b) plans set up after 29 December 2022 to enrol new workers at 3 to 10 per cent of pay, rising a point a year to at least 10 per cent, from plan years beginning in 2025; small and new employers are exempt, and the Treasury sent final rules for review on 23 June 2026. [cite:ad10_asppa_auto] Seventeen states have passed laws creating automatic payroll IRAs for workers whose employers offer no plan. [cite:ad10_aarp_autoira] Fees, as shown above, have fallen by roughly two-thirds since 2000. Running the other way, on 30 March 2026 the Labor Department proposed a safe harbour for plan managers who add private equity, real estate and crypto-assets to 401(k) menus, carrying out an August 2025 executive order; managers would have to weigh each option's fees, liquidity, valuation and complexity. [cite:ad10_block_alts] Status as of October 2026: proposed.

Interpretation, not finding: the account as a transfer of risk

The Sophia platform reads the move from pension to 401(k) as the clearest case of the Dream's design: the promise of a secure old age was kept in words while the risk of failing to reach it was handed, one account at a time, to the people least able to carry it, and a fee-earning industry grew in the space between. In this reading the 401(k) is not a failed pension but a successful transfer.

The strongest counter-readings are documented. The 401(k) was not designed to replace pensions, and the leading industry account attributes most of the shift to industrial change rather than substitution. [cite:ad10_napa_1978] [cite:ad10_ici_retro] Studebaker showed that an employer's pension promise could itself vanish. [cite:ad10_pbgc_studebaker] Automatic enrolment and falling fees show the account model can be repaired by ordinary legislation. What remains is the risk itself, which no reading disputes has moved.
