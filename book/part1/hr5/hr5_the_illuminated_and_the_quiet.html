The Illuminated and the Quiet

The Alumbrados, the "illuminated", were followers of a mystical movement in Spain between about 1501 and 1700. They taught that the soul, "having attained a certain degree of perfection", entered "into direct communication with the Holy Spirit", and that those who had received the light no longer needed liturgy, good works or the exterior forms of religious life. [cite:hr_brit_alumbrados] Many came from among the reformed Franciscans and the Jesuits, and their ideas reached all social classes. [cite:hr_brit_alumbrados] One of the movement's founders was Isabel de la Cruz, a seamstress of Guadalajara from a family descended from New Christians, converts from Judaism; she was tried by the Spanish Inquisition from 1524 to 1529 and sent to prison. [cite:hr_wiki_isabel] The Inquisition issued edicts against the Alumbrados in 1568, 1574 and 1623. [cite:hr_brit_alumbrados]

The Quietists went further, and closer to the centre of the Church. Miguel de Molinos, a Spanish priest in Rome, published his Spiritual Guide there in 1675: a small handbook teaching that Christian perfection comes through contemplation with divine help. It "caused a sensation". [cite:hr_brit_molinos] In 1685, under a pope who was his friend, Innocent XI, the papal police arrested him; Innocent condemned sixty-eight propositions drawn from his teaching in 1687, and Molinos was sentenced to life imprisonment for heresy. He died in prison in 1696. [cite:hr_brit_molinos]

In France, Quietism reached the court. Jeanne-Marie Guyon, a widow from Montargis, taught "an extreme passivity and indifference of the soul", in which a person became an agent of God, and in 1685 published a short method of prayer. [cite:hr_brit_guyon] After Innocent XI's bull against Quietism she was arrested in 1688 and released after a few months through the intervention of Madame de Maintenon. When Maintenon turned against her, she was arrested again, in December 1695, on the king's authority, and held in various prisons, including the Bastille, until 1703. [cite:hr_brit_guyon] Her chief disciple, Fénelon, defended her against Bishop Bossuet, who argued that the exalted states she taught were rarely achievable and doubted she had experienced them; the Church officially condemned Fénelon in 1699. [cite:hr_brit_guyon]

These were not dualists or rebels. Molinos was a priest in Rome and a friend of the pope; Guyon was released once through the intervention of a woman at the king's side, and later imprisoned on the king's own warrant. [cite:hr_brit_molinos] [cite:hr_brit_guyon] What they shared with the Bogomils and the Beguines was a claim that the soul could reach God without the Church's exterior apparatus, and that claim, made from inside the Church, was treated as heresy just the same. The pope's friendship did not save Molinos; the king's household did not save Guyon.
