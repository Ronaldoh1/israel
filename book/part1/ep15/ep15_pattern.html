Other Networks, Same Questions

Epstein was not the first powerful man accused of using other people, other companies and other institutions to reach and silence victims. Others have been tried, investigated or reviewed in public, and their cases show how such networks work and how they come apart. Part Twelve set several of these cases beside Epstein's to show that he was not the only one. This chapter uses them differently. It does not claim Epstein ran a cartel. It uses those cases as a checklist: each documented feature of another network becomes a question asked of the Epstein files. If the files answer it, the answer goes on the map. If they do not, the gap is named.

What the other cases show

A drug cartel is the clearest model of an organised network, because its structure was laid out in court. At Joaquín Guzmán's 2019 trial in Brooklyn, the man who had built the Sinaloa cartel's encrypted phone system testified that he had become an FBI informant, which let agents read Guzmán's messages. Investigators had concluded they could not see inside without "insider access". [cite:ep15_cnn_chapoit2019] Another witness, a trained accountant, described bribes paid "preferably in U.S. dollars" to police and officials, and jurors were shown a tunnel into Arizona. [cite:ep15_nbc_zambada2018] In 2023 a New York jury convicted Mexico's former security chief, Genaro García Luna, of taking millions from the cartel in exchange for intelligence and safe passage; he was sentenced to more than 38 years. [cite:ep15_aj_garcialuna2024]

Sex-crime networks show the same parts in a different order. Keith Raniere of NXIVM was convicted in 2019 of racketeering and sex trafficking. His secret group recruited women "under the false pretense of joining a women-only mentorship group" and demanded damaging "collateral" to stop them leaving or talking. [cite:ep15_doj_raniere2020] Federal prosecutors charged the fashion executive Peter Nygård in 2020 with using his company's "employees, funds, and other resources" to recruit women, putting some on the payroll as "models" or "assistants", having staff screen guests at his parties, and paying for false statements against accusers. Those are allegations; he was later convicted of four sexual assaults in Toronto and sentenced to 11 years. [cite:ep15_doj_nygard2020] [cite:ep15_abc_nygard2024] Harvey Weinstein hired the intelligence firms Kroll and Black Cube to gather information on accusers and journalists; one contract's stated aim was to stop publication. [cite:ep15_axios_weinstein2017]

The institutions around such men follow a pattern of their own. The Justice Department's inspector general found that the FBI was told about Larry Nassar in 2015 and was slow to act for months, while at least 40 more girls and women said he abused them. [cite:ep15_ap_nassarig2021] A review of the BBC found complaints about Jimmy Savile were "disbelieved, disregarded or covered up", and that a junior worker was told, "he is a VIP". [cite:ep15_itv_savile2016] A Pennsylvania grand jury called the dioceses' methods, from euphemisms to reassigning priests and discouraging police, a "playbook for concealing the truth". [cite:ep15_wral_pagj2018]

One case sets a limit. In 2025 a jury acquitted Sean Combs of racketeering conspiracy and sex trafficking, and convicted him of transporting people for prostitution. [cite:ep15_opb_combs2025] Staff, money and travel around a powerful man are not, by themselves, proof of a criminal enterprise. A function has to be shown in documents, not assumed from the shape of the other cases.

The questions, and what the files answered

What the search found

Asking "were recruiters paid, and by whom?" led to two documents. On 25 April 2014 Richard Kahn emailed a banker at Deutsche Bank, copying Darren Indyke and Bella Klein of his firm, attaching an instruction titled "DB JEE Custody Account_Daniel Siad Euros_4-30-2014": a euro payment from Epstein's custody account to Daniel Siad, dated 30 April. [cite:ep15_doj_siad] The amount is not in the copy read, and the email does not say what the payment was for. Two years later, in May 2016, a lawyer for survivors, Stanley Pottinger, forwarded to a Manhattan federal prosecutor Brunel's own description of Siad as a "scout" or recruiter of girls and women for Epstein. [cite:ep15_doj_pottinger] Siad has not been charged with any crime. The point is the date: federal prosecutors in Manhattan had a named alleged recruiter, from Epstein's own circle, two years before they opened the case that led to his 2019 arrest.

Asking "who funded the front?" led to Epstein's JPMorgan statement for November 2012, which shows a wire to MC2 Model Management at its Fifth Avenue address. [cite:ep15_doj_mc2] Reporting had said he funded Brunel's agency; this is one payment on paper. Asking "were investigators used on accusers?" led to a 2009 federal court record noting that Epstein "has employed private investigators to contact third-parties believed familiar with" the women suing him, and the women's request that it stop. [cite:ep15_doj_piorder]

The money question produced a bank that had been treated as a footnote. A FirstBank memo of April 2016 describes Epstein as a "large non-borrowing deposit Platinum Banking client" with several companies' accounts, an average balance of over $5.3 million and more than seven years with the bank, and waives his fees. [cite:ep15_doj_firstbank] That puts another bank alongside JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank and Bank of America, holding his companies' money from about 2009, while the others were deciding about him.

The influence question produced the sharpest negative result. The cartel cases turned on cash paid to officials. Nothing in the released files shows Epstein paying an official for a decision. What they show is access: donations, a territory's tax breaks, a delegate texting him during a hearing, and a federal deal that immunised "any potential co-conspirators". That difference matters. It is the reason this book describes a system that protected him, rather than a bribe that bought protection.

The last function is the one the other cases say matters most. Guzmán's network came apart because the man who ran its phones changed sides. Epstein's has had no such insider. No one from inside his operation has given investigators the kind of access Guzmán's technician gave the FBI, and the four assistants named in 2008 were covered by that deal's immunity. The records the office kept, the calendars, payment instructions and bank files now being read, are the nearest substitute.
