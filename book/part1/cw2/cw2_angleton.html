The Angleton Channel, 1954–1956

The intelligence relationship between the United States and Israel did not begin with a treaty, and its starting point is told in three different ways, set out in [scene:cw1_cia_liaison|the section on the liaison's origins]. What changed in 1954 was not the existence of a channel but the man who held it. In that year James Jesus Angleton became head of the Central Intelligence Agency's new Counterintelligence Staff, and from that post he controlled the agency's dealings with Israel. [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] For the next two years, the period this stage covers, the channel's most consequential product was a single document: the text of Nikita Khrushchev's secret speech of February 1956.

The familiar account of the relationship presents it as a valuable Cold War partnership, professionally managed, that served American interests in a dangerous period. Another account, carried elsewhere in this library, presents it as a case of one official's capture by a foreign service. The documents of 1954–56 support a narrower statement than either: one CIA officer held an unusual personal control over the Israel account, he regarded Israel as a strategic asset against the Soviet Union, and in 1956 the channel delivered something of real value to Washington. What it cost, and what it may have hidden, are questions for later years.

The administrative anomaly. The clearest description of Angleton's position comes from inside the CIA. David Robarge, the agency's chief historian, writing in the CIA's journal Studies in Intelligence, records that Angleton became head of the new Counterintelligence Staff in 1954, and that his "control of that country's account at CIA, an administrative anomaly, was one of the foundations of his influence." [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] The phrase carries two findings. The word "anomaly" is the agency historian's own acknowledgement that the arrangement was irregular: the Israel account sat with the chief of counterintelligence rather than wherever the agency's normal structure would have put it. And the phrase "foundations of his influence" says that the account was not a side duty but one of the sources of Angleton's power inside the agency.

Robarge also states Angleton's view of the country. Angleton, he writes, "ardently supported Israel" and "saw Israel as a bastion against the Soviet Union." [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] That is the official historian's judgment of Angleton's motive, and it is a Cold War motive: Israel mattered to him as a front against Moscow. The mechanism of the channel follows from the two statements together. The officer who decided how far to trust a foreign service was the same officer who valued that service most and who drew influence from managing it. No one between him and the Israeli liaison held a separate brief to judge the relationship.

The account of Angleton carried elsewhere in this library adds a personal history: it states that he had met future Israeli intelligence leaders in wartime Rome. [cite:adst_pollard] [cite:cia_old_ally] [cite:ips_espionage] [cite:nsarchive_pollard] The same account goes further, charging that over his tenure he passed to the Mossad the identities of CIA sources in Arab countries, against the agency's counterintelligence protocols, and that his dual role made a genuine counterintelligence assessment of Israel impossible. [cite:adst_pollard] [cite:cia_old_ally] [cite:ips_espionage] [cite:nsarchive_pollard] Those charges are undated in the source and cover some twenty years. Nothing in the material used here ties any specific instance to 1954–56, and they are reported as that account's characterization, not as a finding for this period. Robarge's own words, "ardently supported," describe a disposition, not an act.

What kind of agreement. No formal written liaison agreement for 1954–1956 is documented in these sources. Neither the CIA historian nor the other accounts used here describes one. Shammai Siskind, writing in the Middle East Quarterly in 2020, says only that the United States had agreed "a few years earlier" to cooperate with Israel on intelligence. [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] The arrangement that governed the channel in these years, so far as the record shows, was an understanding between services, held together by the officials who ran it.

One description of its terms comes from much later. Samuel Lewis, the United States ambassador to Israel from 1977 to 1985, described in a diplomatic oral history a "gentleman's agreement" during Angleton's tenure: an informal understanding that neither the CIA nor the Mossad would recruit agents in the other's country. Lewis said it "may well have been honored during that period." He added: "By the time I got to Israel in 1977, Jim Angleton had long since retired and the relationship between the American and the Israeli services was less close." Lewis was speaking of a period before his own service, and his "may well have been" is a hedge, not a confirmation. His testimony establishes that such an understanding was believed to exist. It does not establish when it began, who agreed to it, or whether it held in 1954–56. Nor is it a treaty or a written institutional commitment of any kind; on Lewis's account it was a personal arrangement that depended on Angleton.

The speech. In February 1956 Khrushchev delivered his "secret speech" to a closed session of the Twentieth Congress of the Soviet Communist Party. [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] It was meant to stay inside the Communist world. Its route to the West ran through Israel, and the fullest account of that route is Siskind's, which is reported here as his.

According to Siskind, Victor Grayevsky, a Polish Jewish journalist, borrowed a copy from the office of the Polish Communist Party where his girlfriend worked. He gave it to Yaakov Barmor, an agent of the Shin Bet, who photographed it. [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] Ben-Gurion then sent a copy to Washington by courier, to Israel's liaison there, Izzy Dorot. On the American side, Siskind writes, only Angleton was to see it. The text reached Allen Dulles by mid-April 1956, and in early June 1956 the CIA gave it to The New York Times. [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind]

A second account, published when Grayevsky died, agrees on the essentials but leaves one point open. NEWSru.co.il, an Israeli Russian-language outlet, reported in October 2007 that Grayevsky immigrated to Israel in 1957, served for fourteen years as a double agent reporting his KGB contacts to the Shin Bet until 1971, and was honoured by the Shin Bet's chief, Avi Dichter, in August 2006. [cite:rs_cw2_newsru_grayevsky] Its headline says he passed the report to the Mossad; its text names both the Mossad and the Shin Bet as recipients of the speech, without resolving which. [cite:rs_cw2_newsru_grayevsky] Siskind names the Shin Bet. [cite:rs_cw2_meq_siskind] The difference matters for a history of the two institutions. The sources used here do not settle which service first held the document, or whether both did.

The speech shows what the channel was for, from each side. For Washington, Israel offered access the United States did not have on its own: a document out of a closed Communist Party session, obtained through a Jewish journalist in Warsaw and an Israeli agent, and in American hands within about two months. The account of the relationship elsewhere in this library places the speech among the high-value assets Israel provided in exchange for American intelligence on Arab states. [cite:wrmea_cia_mossad_tradeoffs] [cite:michaelcronin_cia_mossad_relationship] For Israel, the delivery demonstrated its worth to the one American officer who controlled its account, at a time, told in the other sections of this stage, when Washington was refusing it arms and courting Egypt. The sources used here do not record what, if anything, Israel received in return for this particular document.

The dispute. The two ways of reading the Angleton years can each be put at full strength. The case for the partnership, as the familiar account presents it, is that in the conditions of the early Cold War a small ally with sources inside the Soviet bloc was worth an unusual arrangement, and the secret speech is its best evidence: a document of world importance, delivered to the CIA and then to the public. The case against, as the account carried elsewhere in this library presents it, is that the same arrangement placed the judgment of a foreign service in the hands of the officer most committed to it, and that the cost appeared only later. [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] [cite:adst_pollard] [cite:cia_old_ally] For 1954–56 the record used here supports the first case's facts and the second case's structural point: Robarge's "administrative anomaly" is both. It does not supply evidence, for these years, of the harms the second case alleges.

The later history of the relationship, including the case of the nuclear material company NUMEC, the Israeli Science Liaison Bureau known as LAKAM, founded in 1960, and the Pollard affair of 1985, belongs to Stages Four to Six and to later volumes. The discipline that governs it is set out in [scene:df_s04|the account of the CIA–Mossad liaison]: two agencies that share an officer, a hub or a channel are not thereby shown to share everything that passes through either one.

What the record does not settle. The sources do not document a written agreement for 1954–56, and they do not show what the "gentleman's agreement" contained in these years or whether it held. They do not agree on which Israeli service first received the speech. They give no Israeli or American document for the speech's route; the sequence rests on Siskind's account and the 2007 obituary report. They do not date the conduct the critical account attributes to Angleton. And they disagree on when the liaison itself began: the account of the CIA–Mossad liaison elsewhere in this library dates a formal liaison to 1951 and has Angleton managing it from that year, while the CIA historian dates Angleton's counterintelligence command to 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_robarge] [cite:wrmea_cia_mossad_tradeoffs] Both may be true, if Angleton held the account before he held the staff; the sources used here do not say.
