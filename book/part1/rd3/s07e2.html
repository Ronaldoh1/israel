The Armed Line: The Haganah, the Irgun and the Jaffa Barrels, 1920–1935

Alongside land and labor, the Yishuv was building a force of its own. A familiar picture of the Mandate years shows one Jewish defense force, the Haganah, acting under a single political direction until it became the army of the new state in 1948. The record for these years shows something less tidy. By the end of 1931 there were already two Jewish armed organizations in Palestine, the Haganah and the Irgun, and by 1940 there would be three. They were divided by documented disputes over strategy and politics, they went through periods of open hostility toward one another, and even at the declaration of statehood they had reached only a partial, negotiated coordination.

The starting point was modest. When the Histadrut, the Jewish labor federation, was founded in December 1920, it took responsibility for defense, and the Haganah was placed under its direct control. Through the 1920s the Haganah had only "a few hundred" volunteer members [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. It was a volunteer body of the labor movement, not an army. In 1924 its senior commanders drew up a "Constitution of the Haganah," a first attempt to give the organization written rules. Nor was the labor camp a single body. In 1926 former members of Ha-Shomer formed a separate underground of their own inside the Gedud ha-Avodah [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. Even before the Revisionist split that is the usual subject of this story, the Yishuv's armed structure was divided.

The violence of August 1929 changed the Haganah's position. In that month its volunteers helped defend the Jewish communities of Jerusalem, Tel Aviv and Haifa. The massacres of that August happened where the Haganah was absent or weak: in Hebron, in Safed and at Motza [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. The lesson the movement drew was organizational. The Encyclopaedia Judaica's account describes the result as a shift toward wider organization and toward public control of the force [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah].

That shift took formal shape in 1931. A national command of six members was set up, with parity between the labor movement and everyone else: three members came from the Histadrut, Eliyahu Golomb, Dov Hos and Meir Rutberg, and three were non-labor members [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. The mechanism matters. A defense force that had answered to one party's federation now answered to a body in which labor held half the seats and the non-labor parties held the other half. The commander who opposed public control was removed [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah].

Not everyone accepted the arrangement. A group led by Avraham Tehomi, who had been the Haganah's Jerusalem district commander from 1929 to 1931, seceded. Together with Revisionist groups it formed the Irgun Zvai Leumi, also known as Etzel [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_tehomi]. Tehomi is the same man whose 1985 account of the 1924 killing of Jacob de Haan is weighed in [scene:dh_s01|the de Haan section]. He did not stay with the organization he founded: he returned to the Haganah in 1937 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_tehomi].

The Irgun's founders were officers aligned with Ze'ev Jabotinsky's Revisionist movement, and the point of principle on which they broke was havlagah, the Haganah's policy of restraint in the face of Arab attacks, which they rejected [cite:wiki_irgun]. Through the 1930s and 1940s the Irgun conducted an armed campaign against British rule and against Arab civilian targets. From 1943 it was commanded by Menachem Begin, who later became Israel's sixth Prime Minister [cite:wiki_irgun]. That is a direct, documented personal line from the command of this organization to the country's highest office. The source establishes the founding rationale and the line of command; it does not, on its own, give a full operational history of the Irgun, and this section does not attempt one.

The two positions can be stated in their own terms, as far as the sources record them. The Haganah's leadership held to restraint and, after 1931, to a command shared equally between labor and non-labor members, removing the commander who opposed that public control. The Irgun's founders rejected restraint in the face of Arab attacks and left the Haganah. One reading, which the sources suggest but do not state, is that both answers came out of the same experience of 1929, which had shown how few defenders there were and what happened where there were none.

Between 1931 and 1935 the Haganah built itself into something more permanent. It had urban branches in Jerusalem, Tel Aviv and Haifa, each with memberships "in the hundreds," it ran annual officers' courses, and it had workshops producing hand grenades [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. It was still small. It was also now an organization that trained its own officers and made some of its own weapons.

The clearest single episode of these years came at the port of Jaffa in 1935. Rifles and ammunition had been shipped from Belgium hidden in barrels of cement. British authorities seized 537 barrels at Jaffa, and the discovery caused significant unrest among Palestinian Arabs [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_cement]. One account, drawing on the Palestine Post and the Peel report among other sources, gives the contents as 25 Lewis machine guns, 800 rifles and 400,000 rounds, found in 359 of the drums, and says they were consigned to a merchant in Tel Aviv who was never identified. The same account dates a protest general strike to 26 October 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_cement]. Those details rest on that one account and are given here as its report.

The sources do not agree on when the shipment was found. The account just cited, relying on Palestine Post reports and the Peel report, dates the discovery to 16 October 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_cement]. The Encyclopaedia Judaica's entry on the Haganah dates it to 18 November 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah]. This book has not resolved the difference. The earlier date sits before the reported strike of 26 October; the later one would fall after it. Neither source, as gathered here, names who in the Yishuv ordered the shipment.

One claim about these years does not hold up as stated. A compiled account of Zionist intelligence before 1948, drawn on elsewhere in this library, says that the first Jewish intelligence organization in Palestine was established under the Haganah in 1929. The same account also dates the founding of the Shai, the Sherut Yediot or Information Service, the Haganah's intelligence and counter-espionage arm, to 1940 [cite:shai_wiki]. The two statements may describe different bodies, an informal service in 1929 and a formal one in 1940, but the account does not say so, and this book has not been able to confirm a founding date for any Haganah intelligence body in 1929. The claim is reported here as unconfirmed. The Shai's wartime and American story belongs to a later period and is not told here.

Where the line led is told in later chapters, but its shape follows from what was built in these years. In 1940 the Irgun split in turn. Avraham Stern led a faction out to form Lehi, Lohamei Herut Israel, "Fighters for the Freedom of Israel," often called the Stern Gang, rejecting the Irgun's decision to suspend operations against the British during the Second World War [cite:wiki_lehi_stern_gang]. Stern's group is documented as having explored contact with Fascist Italy and, most controversially, Nazi Germany, in search of any power willing to oppose British rule in Palestine. The episode was widely condemned at the time, including within the broader Zionist movement. The record shows exploratory contact, not a completed cooperation or alliance [cite:wiki_lehi_stern_gang]. That story is told in [scene:lh_s01|Lehi].

In 1944 and 1945, in what became known as the "Hunting Season," the Haganah, under Jewish Agency direction, moved against members of the Irgun and Lehi through surveillance, exposure to the British authorities and detentions, in an attempt to suppress their independent operations and keep political control unified [cite:wiki_hunting_season]. The source documents the pattern rather than every operation. And at independence, when the Haganah became the core of the Israel Defense Forces and the Irgun and Lehi were formally absorbed, the merger was contested. In June 1948 the Altalena affair, an armed confrontation between the new army and Irgun forces over a weapons shipment, left sixteen Irgun members and three soldiers dead; the ship was shelled and burned off Tel Aviv on Ben-Gurion's direct order. Begin's refusal to let the clash become a civil war, in his words "there will be no civil war with the enemy at our gates," is documented as decisive in preventing a wider fight [cite:wiki_altalena_affair]. Most of the roughly 940 passengers aboard were inducted into the army without incident, so the flashpoint was not the whole of the integration [cite:wiki_altalena_affair]. According to a 2026 report in the Jerusalem Post, the wreck was found intact on the Mediterranean seafloor that year, 78 years later [cite:jpost_altalena_wreck_2026]. That ending is told in [scene:mil_s01|1948].

The reason to tell this line in a chapter about building institutions is a matter of consistency. This library gives full weight to the internal divisions on the political side: the Bund, Reform Judaism's early ambivalence, Neturei Karta. A history that tracked those divisions carefully but treated the armed side as a single force would be holding the two to different standards [cite:jvl_altalena_affair]. The record of 1920 to 1935 shows the armed side divided almost from the start: between the labor federation and the old Ha-Shomer men in 1926, between labor and non-labor in the command of 1931, and between restraint and its rejection in the secession of the same year.

Some things the record gathered here does not settle. It gives no membership figures for the Irgun in its first years and no count of the Haganah beyond "a few hundred" and "in the hundreds." It does not name the three non-labor members of the 1931 command or the commander who was removed. It does not establish who arranged the Belgian shipment, or the date it was found. And it does not support the claim of a Haganah intelligence service founded in 1929.
