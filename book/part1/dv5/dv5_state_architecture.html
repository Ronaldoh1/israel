How the State Is Built: Five Features Together

Several features of the Israeli state are often described as unique. Taken one at a time, most of them are not. Taken together, they are. This section sets out each feature from its source, names the other states that share it, and then describes what the combination does.

No written constitution. The Declaration of Independence promised a constitution by 1 October 1948. The assembly elected to write it renamed itself the Knesset two days after it first met, in February 1949, and in June 1950 it resolved to build the constitution chapter by chapter out of Basic Laws, a process that has never been completed. [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] This is not unique. The United Kingdom, New Zealand, Canada, Saudi Arabia and San Marino also have no single codified constitution. [cite:sa_uncodified] What is unusual is that most of Israel's Basic Laws can be changed by a simple majority of the Knesset, so the constitutional chapters it does have offer little protection against the government of the day; that is told in [scene:dv5_no_constitution|the previous section].

A state of emergency since 1948. On 19 May 1948, five days after independence, the Provisional State Council gave itself power to declare a state of emergency, under which ministers could issue regulations "in the interests of defense of the State." The emergency has been in continuous effect ever since. The Knesset renews it every year, and since 1992 Basic Law: The Government has allowed it to be renewed "without limit." Emergency regulations can change ordinary laws, and for decades they were used for labour disputes and economic measures as well as security. A civil-rights petition of 1999 asked the High Court to end the arrangement; in 2012 the court dismissed it, having accepted the government's progress in moving regulations into ordinary law. [cite:sa_emergency] Many states declare emergencies. Very few have lived under one continuously for more than seventy-five years.

No declared borders. Asked before independence whether the new state should state its borders, Ben-Gurion answered: "When we declare the State we do not have to determine borders nor do we have to seek international recognition." The Declaration names none, and the state's borders have never been fixed in law. [cite:sa_jta_borders] The armistice agreements of 1949 drew demarcation lines that their own texts said were not political boundaries [cite:gz_armistice_text], and the border with Egypt was settled only by the peace treaty of 1979. [cite:gz_treaty1979]

Membership of the United Nations on stated assurances. Israel was admitted on 11 May 1949 by General Assembly Resolution 273, which recorded the assurances its representative had given about the partition and refugee resolutions. [cite:rs_rd6_unga273_text] What those assurances bound the state to do is examined in [scene:rd6_un_admission|the section on admission].

A state defined by one people, open by descent. The Law of Return of 1950 opens citizenship to Jews anywhere, and to their children, grandchildren and spouses. [cite:dv5_jvl_return] Laws of this kind are not unique either: Armenia, Bulgaria, Croatia, Finland, Germany, Greece, Lithuania, Serbia, South Korea, Spain and Turkey, among others, give facilitated citizenship or a right of return to people of their ethnic origin abroad. [cite:sa_jus_sanguinis] What sets Israel apart is the pairing with a Basic Law of 2018 declaring that national self-determination in the state is "unique to the Jewish people," in a country where Arab citizens are about a fifth of the population. [cite:gi5_toi_nslaw] [cite:christiancentury_nsl]

What the combination does. Each feature leaves something open. Without a constitution, the limits on government power are whatever the current majority leaves in place. Under a permanent emergency, ministers keep powers designed for wartime. Without declared borders, the extent of the state is a matter of policy rather than law. The combination means that the questions most states settle in their founding documents, where the state ends, what the government may not do and who the state belongs to, were left to be decided by whoever held power, and in practice have been decided in favour of flexibility for the state. The government of Israel's own explanation is a security one: a small state facing hostile neighbours from its first day needed room to act. Its critics read the same features as an architecture that keeps the state's claims open-ended and its obligations unfixed. The documents establish the features. Which reading fits better is for the reader to weigh, and the later parts of this volume, on the territories and on the rules other states follow, give the evidence for each.
