The Disclosure: December 1960–January 1961

In the first week of December 1960 the United States government reached a formal conclusion about the reactor in the Negev: a national intelligence estimate of 8 December judged that "plutonium production for weapons is at least one major purpose." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] How that judgment was reached is told in [scene:cw4_dimona_discovery|how Washington found Dimona]. The six weeks that followed, from 7 December 1960 to 19 January 1961, when Eisenhower's outgoing administration reported to Congress, turned a secret into a public fact. Washington confronted Israel and France, the story reached American television, and Ben-Gurion stood up in the Knesset to describe a project his government had built without announcing it. What each government said in those weeks, and what the officials on the other side believed, is the record from which every later argument about Dimona begins.

Where this begins. The program at Dimona was built with French technical assistance in the late 1950s and early 1960s, and Israel's government has never confirmed or denied that it possesses nuclear weapons, a policy usually called "nuclear ambiguity" and maintained since the 1960s. [cite:israel_nuclear_fas] The weeks described here are where that policy's public life starts. Its later history, including the estimates of an arsenal that the Federation of American Scientists puts at about 90 warheads and that the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists and the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute have put at between 80 and 400 at various times, the Symington Amendment of 1976, which requires an end to US aid to countries that deliver or receive nuclear enrichment technology outside international safeguards, and the photographs that the Dimona technician Mordecai Vanunu gave to the London Sunday Times in 1986, belongs to later volumes. [cite:israel_nuclear_fas] [cite:symington_amendment] [cite:vanunu_wiki] One claim from that later history must be marked here because it is sometimes attached to these origins. Another part of this library states that the arsenal was built partly with weapons-grade uranium stolen from NUMEC, a nuclear-fuel plant in Apollo, Pennsylvania, and that the CIA and FBI concluded Israel had taken it. That claim is contested, not established. [cite:nsarchive_numec] The FBI investigated NUMEC's missing uranium from 1965 to 1980, and the CIA concluded that environmental sampling near Dimona in 1968 had detected uranium enriched to the same unusual specification as NUMEC's missing material; but no one was ever charged, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission said in 2009 that it had no documents proving a diversion had occurred and could not conclude that one had not, and the question remains officially unresolved. [cite:nsarchive_numec] [cite:apolloaffair_summary] The case begins after this chapter's period and is told in [scene:fis_s07a|NUMEC]; so does the wider pattern of Israeli intelligence activity in the United States that another part of this library dates from the 1960s.

The summons, 7–9 December. On 7 December 1960 the State Department summoned the Israeli ambassador for an explanation. At a luncheon of the Operations Coordinating Board the same day, Livingston Merchant said that the Department would be "the sole spokesman" on the matter. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The aim was one voice, under the Secretary of State. On 9 December Secretary of State Herter showed Ambassador Avraham Harman photographs of the site, and he called in the French chargé d'affaires. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

What France said, 16 December. France answered at a higher level. At the NATO meeting in Paris on 16 December 1960, Foreign Minister Maurice Couve de Murville acknowledged that the Negev reactor was a "replica of [the] Marcoule plant," the French site whose G-1 reactor Henry Gomberg had compared it to a fortnight earlier. He said France would supply raw materials and would receive the plutonium. Asked how the project was being paid for, he said he "assumed the money came from US." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] That last remark landed on a sensitive question in Washington, because the United States was giving Israel aid, and it would be raised at the White House three days later. The question of who did pay, including the disputed claim of West German money, is examined earlier in this chapter, in the section on Bonn's quiet partnership.

Television, 18 December. On 18 December 1960, on NBC's Meet the Press, John McCone, the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, said that Israel had not "revealed" the reactor to the United States. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The State Department had wanted to be the sole spokesman; within eleven days, another agency's head had spoken on national television.

The President's meeting, 19 December. The next day Eisenhower met his advisers. His words were about money. "We do not know where they have obtained the funds," he said, and the United States had an interest in knowing "because of the aid we are giving them." He cited evidence that the project would cost $100–200 million. The Secretary of Defense, Thomas Gates, said, "our information is that the plant is not for peaceful uses." McCone said, "there is a chemical separation plant there." CIA Director Allen Dulles said that Israel was trying to confuse Dimona, "a large production installation," with the small Soreq reactor. Eisenhower agreed that Israel should allow inspection of the kind the International Atomic Energy Agency carried out. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] The meeting shows where the weight of opinion lay among the President's senior advisers: the Pentagon, the Atomic Energy Commission and the CIA all doubted the peaceful account, and McCone, in this meeting, named the separation plant that the intelligence report of 2 December had described.

Harman's account, 20 December. On 20 December Harman gave Herter Israel's version. Israel, he said, was "building an additional research reactor of 24 megawatts capacity in the Negev," and "a reactor of this size is not of industrial importance." It had been begun a year earlier and would take three to four years to complete. Asked what would be done with the plutonium, he said he could not answer. He cited a French Foreign Office announcement of 19 December giving assurances that the reactor was for peaceful use. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d180] The figure of 24 megawatts was the one written into the 1957 agreement with France, according to the sources on that agreement; those sources add that its cooling and waste systems were designed for three times that power. [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] Harman's statement that the work had begun a year earlier sits awkwardly beside the Nuclear Weapon Archive's dating of groundbreaking to early 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist]

Ben-Gurion in the Knesset. Ben-Gurion then made the reactor public in Israel. He told the Knesset it was "exclusively for peaceful purposes," in the words Burr and Cohen quoted in 2015, and that it was "a research reactor ... which will serve the needs of industry, agriculture, health, and science," in the words Cohen and Burr quoted in 2025. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] The Federation of American Scientists and the Jewish Virtual Library summarize the statement as describing a nuclear research center built for "peaceful purposes." [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_nuclear] [cite:rs_cw4_fas_israel] The date of the speech is itself given two ways. The Nuclear Weapon Archive and one passage of the 2015 briefing book date it 21 December 1960; Cohen and Burr in 2025, and another passage of the same 2015 briefing book, date it 22 December. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] The sources used for this chapter do not include the Knesset record that would settle it.

"Misbehaving a little." In private, an Israeli official said something different. On 21–22 December 1960 Addy Cohen of the Finance Ministry, the same official who had once called the site a "textile plant," told American diplomats that Israel had "been misbehaving a little" and that the reactor could be used "for weapons purposes." The embassy reported it in Tel Aviv Telegram 574 of 23 December. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The telegram records a remark to diplomats, not a statement of policy. But it was made on the same days as the Knesset speech, and it conceded to Americans the possibility the speech denied.

Ben-Gurion and Reid, 24 December. Ben-Gurion objected to the publicity. Meeting the American ambassador, Ogden Reid, on 24 December 1960, he asked: "Why in [the] States is everything being told [to] everybody?" [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] Cohen and Burr also quote him telling Reid in this period that "we are not a satellite of America ... and will never be a satellite." [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] The second remark states the Israeli position at its strongest: a sovereign state's defence program was not subject to another government's approval, however close the two were. The first was a complaint about publicity; it came six days after McCone's television appearance, though the record quoted here does not say which disclosure he had in mind.

Washington is not satisfied. The American reply was blunt. A State Department telegram to Tel Aviv at the end of December 1960 said that "Neither Dept nor other interested Washington agencies consider Ben Gurion's statements thus far satisfactory." It stated that "USG policy is unequivocally opposed to proliferation of nuclear weapons capabilities," and it added that Washington would "not welcome new round of alarmist publicity." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d181] Another telegram, of 31 December, cited a "clearly apparent lack of candor." Merchant recorded Allen Dulles's complaint that Israel had "by no means come clean with us." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The two halves of the late-December telegram pull in different directions, and that was the administration's position: it wanted a full account from Israel, and it did not want the public argument to grow.

The money. Eisenhower's question about funds was never answered in these weeks, but the estimates show how far apart the guesses were. On 28 December 1960 the embassy in Tel Aviv estimated Dimona's cost at $17.8 million in foreign exchange and 29.2 million Israeli pounds. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] Herter had put it at $40–80 million on 8 December; Eisenhower cited $100–200 million on 19 December. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The embassy divided its estimate into a foreign-exchange share and a share in Israeli pounds; the documents used here give no rate for combining the two, and none says which estimate was closest. The question of cost mattered politically because of the aid: if a project of $100 million or more was being financed while Israel received American assistance, Eisenhower's advisers wanted to know from where. Reid, on the same day as the embassy estimate, suggested bringing in the International Atomic Energy Agency or internationally known scientists to examine the site. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

No inspection, 4 January 1961. Ben-Gurion answered the call for inspection on 4 January 1961. Israel, he said, "has no plans for producing nuclear weapons," and it would not accept inspection by the International Atomic Energy Agency "until all reactors are treated as equals." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] The condition was framed as a principle of equal treatment among states; in practice it meant no international inspection.

The handover, 19 January 1961. On 19 January 1961 the outgoing administration sent a summary to Congress's Joint Committee on Atomic Energy. It recorded Israel's statement that all plutonium produced at Dimona "will be returned to France." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] That assurance fitted what Couve de Murville had said in Paris a month earlier, that France would receive the plutonium. It answered the question Harman had said on 20 December he could not answer. Burr and Cohen write that Eisenhower's administration, with weeks left in office, kept the matter under State Department control until Kennedy took office, and that Kennedy then "chose the toughest path of struggle and confrontation," while Johnson later "planted the seeds of compromise." [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] That arc is their interpretation of the whole decade, and its 1963 climax is told in [scene:s11b|Kennedy's letters of 1963].

Was the December account true? Two positions stand on the record. Israel's account, given by Harman to Herter, by Ben-Gurion to the Knesset and on 4 January, and in the assurance passed to Congress on 19 January, was of a 24-megawatt research reactor for peaceful purposes whose plutonium would go back to France. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d180] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_reveal] The strongest case for taking it at face value in December 1960 is that it matched what France said, that no outsider had yet been inside the plant, and that the Prime Minister repeated it publicly. Against it stand the judgments of American officials at the time: Gates, McCone and Dulles at the White House on 19 December, and the cables on "lack of candor"; and, decades later, Cohen and Burr's conclusion that the account was false, resting chiefly on the separation plant that Israel concealed. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] Addy Cohen's private remark of 21–22 December sits between the two: an Israeli official conceding that the reactor could be used for weapons, on the days his Prime Minister was telling the Knesset it was for peace.

The record leaves open several things. It does not establish who paid for the reactor, or which of the December cost estimates was nearest the truth. It gives two dates for Ben-Gurion's Knesset statement and does not settle them. It shows what American officials believed about a separation plant in December 1960, but not, from the documents of that month, what they could prove. And Ben-Gurion's statement that Israel had "no plans" for nuclear weapons, made on 4 January 1961, was a statement about intentions that no inspection was then permitted to test. The incoming administration inherited all of these questions. How it set about answering them is the subject of the next section.
