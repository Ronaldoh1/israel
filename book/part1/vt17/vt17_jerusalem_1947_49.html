Jerusalem First: Partition, the Encyclicals and the Refugees, 1947–1958

When the United Nations took up the future of Palestine in 1947, the Holy See had no vote and no seat. It was not a member of the organisation and would not become a permanent observer until 6 April 1964. [cite:dv1_wiki_holysee_un] What it had was a stated interest older than the Zionist movement: the holy places of Christianity, above all in Jerusalem, and the Catholic communities and institutions around them. The record of the years from partition to the death of Pius XII in October 1958 shows that interest expressed in public, in three encyclicals and a relief mission, and, so far as the private papers have yet been read, pursued in private with more caution than force. This section sets out what the Holy See asked for, what it got, and what the private record has added.

Partition and the corpus separatum

The General Assembly adopted resolution 181 on 29 November 1947 by 33 votes to 13, with 10 abstentions. [cite:vt17_ewtn_irishfiles] It recommended an Arab state and a Jewish state and, between them, a third unit: Jerusalem and its surroundings were to be a corpus separatum, a "separate body" under a special international regime administered by the United Nations. [cite:vt1_wiki_corpus] The Holy See supported internationalisation, and accounts of the period credit its diplomacy with helping to keep an international status for Jerusalem inside the partition plan. [cite:vt1_wiki_corpus] [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] Israel's later ambassador to the Holy See, Zion Evrony, summarised the same record from the other side: the Holy See had objected to the partition resolution "on the basis of theological reasons and practical interests." [cite:vt17_evrony_menorah] The two readings are compatible. The Holy See did not want Jerusalem under either new state, and the corpus separatum was the part of the plan that met that aim.

Whether the Holy See went further and pressed Catholic-majority delegations to vote against partition has been asserted and denied, and the documents to settle it have not been published in a form this volume could check. The Latin American bloc was the largest group of Catholic-majority states at the Assembly; twelve or thirteen of them (depending on whether Haiti is counted) voted for partition, six abstained and Cuba voted against. [cite:vt17_tenembaum_sneh] One account, by Baruch Tenembaum of the International Raoul Wallenberg Foundation, says that Moshe Shertok of the Jewish Agency feared the Holy See would turn those delegations against partition, and that the Agency's Moshe Sneh was received on 3 October 1947 by Domenico Tardini, a senior official of the Secretariat of State, in a meeting arranged by the nuncio in Paris, Angelo Roncalli. Tardini, the account says, "did not make any promises." [cite:vt17_tenembaum_sneh] The account cites no archive and rests on later recollections, so it is reported here as disputed. What the vote itself shows is that most Catholic-majority delegations supported partition, which is not what a successful campaign against it would have produced. Whether there was an unsuccessful one, or none, remains open (GAPS: the nunciature papers for 1947 and the Foreign Relations of the United States volumes for that autumn are the places to look).

Three encyclicals

The war that followed partition brought the Holy See's position into public teaching. Pius XII's encyclical Auspicia quaedam of 1 May 1948, two weeks before Israel declared independence, asked for prayers for peace and expressed concern for the holy places. [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] On the day of the declaration, 14 May 1948, the Holy See's newspaper L'Osservatore Romano wrote that "Modern Zionism is not the true heir of Biblical Israel", a line that treated the new state as a political fact rather than a religious one. [cite:vt17_jcpa_schvindlerman] That distinction between theology and politics would become the Holy See's formal position in 1985.

The second encyclical, In multiplicibus curis of 24 October 1948, was devoted to the war. Palestine, it said, "occupies a particular place on account of the war which harasses it"; "thousands of refugees, homeless and driven, wander from their fatherland in search of shelter and food." [cite:vt17_va_imc1948] It lamented damage to churches and charitable buildings around the holy places, and it asked those negotiating peace to give Jerusalem and its surroundings "an international character which, in the present circumstances, seems to offer a better guarantee" for the shrines, with free access to the holy places throughout Palestine assured "with international guarantees." [cite:vt17_va_imc1948] The text named no state. It spoke of the refugees without saying who had driven them out.

The third, Redemptoris nostri cruciatus of 15 April 1949, went further. It asked that Jerusalem and its vicinity be "accorded and legally guaranteed an 'international' status", urged Catholics to press their own governments by legitimate means for that juridical status, and asked that the other holy places in Palestine be protected by international guarantee, with the rights Catholics had acquired over the centuries preserved. [cite:dv1_rnc_1949] It is the only one of the three that told Catholics to act politically, and it is the clearest public statement of the Holy See's aim in these years: an internationalised Jerusalem, not a Christian or papal one, and the security of Catholic institutions under whichever authority emerged.

On 9 December 1949 the General Assembly adopted resolution 303, restating its intention that Jerusalem "be placed under a permanent international regime" as a corpus separatum, with the Trusteeship Council as the administering authority. [cite:vt17_frus_res303] The vote was 38 to 14 with 7 abstentions. The Arab and Muslim states voted for it, having opposed partition in 1947; so did the Soviet bloc, France, Belgium, Australia and several Latin American states; Israel, the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada voted against. [cite:vt17_wiki_res303] Israel declared Jerusalem its capital that month while holding only the western city; Jordan held the east, including the Old City. [cite:vt17_wiki_res303] The resolution was never implemented. [cite:vt1_wiki_corpus] Israel's foreign ministry records that the Vatican backed a further, unsuccessful attempt at internationalisation in 1950. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations]

The Pontifical Mission for Palestine

The refugees named in the 1948 encyclical became the object of the Holy See's one material commitment of these years. In April 1949 Pius XII summoned Monsignor Thomas J. McMahon, national secretary of the Catholic Near East Welfare Association, who had made a fact-finding journey to Palestine the year before, and named him president of a new Pontifical Mission for Palestine. [cite:vt17_cnewa_pmp] The mission, founded that June, was meant to unify the Church's relief among Palestinian refugees and was administered through the Association; it provided food, medical care, housing and training, and acted as the Holy See's liaison with the United Nations agencies. [cite:vt17_wiki_cnewa] [cite:vt17_cnewa_pmp] It was aid, not diplomacy, and its beneficiaries were Arab refugees of all religions. No document in any source shows a comparable transfer from the Holy See to the new state or to the Zionist movement.

What the private record has shown

The papers of Pius XII's pontificate, opened on 2 March 2020, include the Secretariat of State's files for 1945 to 1958, the years of partition and the encyclicals. [cite:vt14_vn_open2020] The first published study devoted to them on this subject is Maria Chiara Rioli's chapter "Archives in War: The Pius XII Papers and the Israeli–Palestinian Conflict", in a volume edited by Simon Unger-Alvi and Nina Valbousquet and published by Berghahn in 2024. [cite:vt17_berghahn_rioli] Its findings could not be checked for this volume, and nothing is attributed to it here. Rioli's earlier book on the Latin diocese of Jerusalem from 1946 to 1956, based on the archives of the Latin Patriarchate, the Franciscan Custody and the Catholic Near East Welfare Association rather than the Vatican's own, describes a local church left without an active patriarch for about two years after 1947, a Holy See unsure how far to commit to its Palestinian faithful, and a church whose first task was refugees, at times arguing for their return. [cite:vt17_robson_rioli]

One private record from these years is available through another government's files. Irish diplomatic papers released in Dublin and reported in January 2004 show that in December 1948 Spain, citing a remark by Pius XII, proposed that Catholic states seek an international mandate for Jerusalem, and that in April 1949 the Irish, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese ambassadors met at the Vatican on the idea. [cite:vt17_ewtn_irishfiles] The plan was dropped. The Irish files attribute that to "the reluctance of the Vatican to disclose its hand" and to its fear of being "saddled with responsibility" for the city. [cite:vt17_ewtn_irishfiles] Here the private record does not contradict the public one; it shows the Holy See declining to turn its public aim into a Catholic political project.

In the same years the two sides had working contacts without recognition. An Israeli delegation went to the Vatican in September 1948 and reached what Israel's foreign ministry calls "various understandings"; Foreign Minister Moshe Sharett met Pius XII in 1952; and in May 1955 the Israel Philharmonic played for the pope at the Vatican, though the occasion was presented without naming Israel. [cite:vt17_mfa_relations] [cite:vt17_wiki_hsisrael] Contact without recognition was the pattern the next four pontificates kept.

The record of 1947 to 1958, read against the front hypothesis set out in [scene:vt1_front_test|Part One], points one way on the evidence so far published. The Holy See's public demand, an international Jerusalem, was opposed by Israel and lost; its one material commitment went to Arab refugees; and the one private record available shows it holding back rather than working against its public line. The Pius XII files may yet change that picture, and Part Nineteen keeps the question open until Rioli's and later studies can be weighed. [scene:vt19_what_stays_open|Part Nineteen: what stays open]
