The Summits and the PLO: The Arab Side of 1964

The previous section followed Washington's year from the American side. The Arab states had their own year in 1964, and it began with water: Israel's project to divert the Jordan, and what the Arab governments would do about it. Out of that decision came three things that shaped the rest of this stage: a pattern of summit meetings among Arab heads of state, a joint military command, and a new Palestinian body with its own charter. This section tells that year, and the two after it, from the documents the Arab governments and the Palestinian congress themselves produced, set beside what American intelligence made of them.

The invitation, 23 December 1963. On 23 December 1963 Nasser invited the Arab heads of state to Cairo to discuss how to confront Israel's project to divert the Jordan. According to the chronology of the Institute for Palestine Studies, Israel had begun that project by the end of 1963. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] The occasion matters to the story. The first Arab summit was not called to found a Palestinian organization; it was called about water, and the Palestinian question entered its agenda through the larger matter of how the Arab states would face Israel together.

Cairo, 13–17 January 1964. The kings and heads of state met at the headquarters of the Arab League in Cairo from 13 to 17 January 1964. Their final communiqué condemned Israel's plan to "divert the course of the River Jordan" as a threat to the riparian rights of the Arab states. It agreed that the governments would suspend the media campaigns they had been waging against one another, a truce among Arab governments that is easy to overlook. And it set the next meeting for August 1964, in Alexandria. [cite:rs_cw6_ecf_communique] What the summit chose to do about the diversion, and why, is told in [scene:cw6_water|the water war].

The communiqué also spoke of the Palestinians. It referred to "organizing the Palestinian people" so that they could take part in liberating their country, and it affirmed "the sacred right of the Palestine Arab people to self determination." [cite:rs_cw6_ecf_communique] It did not, in so many words, name a Palestinian entity. [cite:rs_cw6_ecf_communique] [cite:rs_cw6_jvl_summit_1964] [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] The wording was a mandate, not yet an institution, and the man the summit entrusted with organizing the Palestinian people was Ahmad al-Shuqairi. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron]

The Unified Arab Command. An Arab summit also created a Unified Arab Command. The State Department's editors record its effect from the American side: King Hussein of Jordan's request for American arms in 1964 came under pressure from the Command. [cite:rs_cw6_frus18_sum_plo] A country-history account published by GlobalSecurity.org, an unattributed text, says the Cairo summit of January 1964 created the Command under an Egyptian commander. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_a] The mechanism is the point. A joint command was a new channel through which the Arab states, and on the GlobalSecurity account Egypt in particular, could press a neighbour of Israel on how it armed itself, and the editors' note shows it bearing on Hussein's own request for American arms; that request reappears in [scene:cw6_tanks|the first American tank sale].

Jerusalem, 28 May–2 June 1964. Shuqairi's mandate became an organization within five months. The first Palestinian National Congress met in Jerusalem on 28 May 1964, under the sponsorship of King Hussein, with nearly 400 delegates, all of them appointed. On 2 June it announced the Palestine Liberation Organization, approved a National Charter and a Basic Law, and elected Shuqairi as president. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] Each of those details points in a different direction. The meeting was Palestinian in membership and took place in a Palestinian city; its delegates were appointed, not elected; and the city lay under Jordanian rule and the congress met under the Jordanian king's sponsorship. The organization it founded was the creation of all three facts at once.

The Charter in its own words. The Charter of 1964 is the clearest statement of what the new organization claimed, and its key articles are best read as written. Article 2 defines the territory: "Palestine with its boundaries at the time of the British Mandate is a regional indivisible unit." [cite:rs_cw6_charter_1964] Article 17 calls the partition of 1947 and the establishment of Israel "illegal and false." Article 18 calls the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate "fraud." [cite:rs_cw6_charter_1964] Article 7 addresses Jews: Jews of Palestinian origin "are considered Palestinians if they are willing to live peacefully and loyally in Palestine." [cite:rs_cw6_charter_1964]

Taken together, these articles denied Israel's legitimacy outright. The Charter did not dispute Israel's borders or its policies; it declared the state's founding illegal and its legal foundations fraudulent, and it defined the whole territory of the Mandate as one indivisible Palestinian unit. Article 7 placed the Jews it would count as Palestinians inside that unit on a condition, origin and loyalty, that the text set for no other group. That is what the document says, and it should be stated plainly. It is equally a statement of what one organization's founding text said in 1964, adopted by a congress of appointed delegates; it is not evidence about the views of Palestinians or Arabs as peoples, and the sources here do not treat it as such.

Article 24 and the dispute over it. One clause of the Charter has had a long second life. Article 24 states that the organization "does not exercise any regional sovereignty over the West Bank in the Hashemite Kingdom of Jordan," and it names the Gaza Strip and the Himmah area in the same clause. [cite:rs_cw6_charter_1964] Critics of the PLO cite this article to argue that Palestinians did not claim the West Bank and Gaza before 1967: in their reading, the organization's founding text left those territories outside the Palestine it sought to liberate, which suggests the claim to them arose only after Israel took them. [cite:rs_cw6_rabbani_charter] [cite:rs_cw6_charter_1964] The analyst Mouin Rabbani, writing in 2025, reads the same text the other way. Article 2, he argues, already defines Palestine as the whole territory of the Mandate, and Article 24 only defers to the administration Jordan, Egypt and Syria were then exercising over those areas. He adds the context of authorship: the 1964 Charter was drafted by delegates chosen by Arab governments, one of which, Jordan, ruled the West Bank and hosted the congress; the Charter of 1968, drafted mainly by the guerrilla movements, dropped the clause. [cite:rs_cw6_rabbani_charter]

Both readings rest on the text, and the text supports each in part. Article 24 does disclaim sovereignty over the West Bank and names Gaza and Himmah; Article 2 does define Palestine as the whole Mandate territory. Whether the first article limited the claim or merely postponed its exercise is the question the two sides answer differently, and the Charter alone does not settle it.

Alexandria, September 1964. The second summit met not in August, as Cairo had planned, but at Alexandria from 5 to 11 September 1964. According to the Institute for Palestine Studies, it recognized the PLO over the opposition of Syria and Saudi Arabia. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] The Washington Institute's survey of the summits dates the formal approval of the PLO to 11 September 1964. [cite:rs_cw6_winep_summits] The objections matter as much as the approval. The new organization did not enter Arab politics with every Arab government behind it; it was recognized by a summit in which two governments opposed it.

Casablanca, 1965. The third summit, at Casablanca, ended on 18 September 1965. The Washington Institute's survey records that it took no notable decisions. [cite:rs_cw6_winep_summits] After two years of summitry the institutions the meetings had created, the joint command and the PLO, existed; the unity on which they depended had not deepened.

Who created the PLO? The question has two documented answers, and each is supported by a different kind of source. The first answer is that the Arab League and Nasser created it. A memorandum of the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence dated 2 December 1966 says the PLO was formed in 1964 with the sanction of the Arab League, and that Nasser had considerable influence over it. Its radio station, the "Voice of Palestine," broadcast from Cairo. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_plo] On this account the PLO was an instrument of the states, and above all of Egypt: the summit gave the mandate, Cairo gave the voice. The second answer is that a Palestinian congress created it. The Institute for Palestine Studies stresses that a Palestinian national congress, meeting in Jerusalem, founded the organization and elected its own leadership, though with appointed delegates and under Jordanian sponsorship. [cite:rs_cw6_palquest_chron] On this account the states made the occasion, but Palestinians made the organization. The two answers are not exclusive. The documents show a League mandate, a Jordanian host, Egyptian influence and a Palestinian congress, and the weight a reader gives each depends on whether the question is who made the PLO possible or who constituted it.

The army. The CIA's memorandum of December 1966 also describes the organization's attempt to build an army. The PLO, it says, tried to form a Palestine Liberation Army around the Palestinian units already serving in the Egyptian, Syrian and Iraqi armies. [cite:rs_cw6_cia_dec1966_plo] The detail shows how far the organization depended on host states two years after its founding: its army was to be assembled from units that belonged to other governments' forces.

Hussein's break, July 1966. The organization's Jordanian sponsor turned against it within two years. In July 1966, according to the GlobalSecurity account, Hussein withdrew official endorsement and support from the PLO after friction over its taxing Palestinians and distributing arms in the West Bank and in the refugee camps. The PLO and the Syrian government then turned against him. [cite:rs_cw6_gs_jordan_a] This account rests on a single unattributed country-history text and is reported here as that source's account. If it is accurate, the break set Article 24 against practice: an organization that disclaimed sovereignty over the West Bank was taxing and arming there, and the government that ruled the West Bank withdrew its support.

What the record does not settle. The sources fetched here leave several questions open. They do not say how the nearly 400 delegates to the Jerusalem congress were chosen, or by whom, beyond that they were appointed. They give the Unified Arab Command's founding to the January summit only on the authority of one unattributed account, and they do not describe its forces or its record. They date Hussein's break and its causes from that same single source, without a Jordanian or PLO document. And the two great disputes, over Article 24 and over who created the PLO, cannot be closed from the texts, because each side reads the same documents for a different question. What the record does establish is the sequence: a summit called about water produced a mandate to organize the Palestinians; a congress in Jerusalem turned the mandate into an organization whose Charter denied Israel's legitimacy; and within two years that organization was at odds with the king who had hosted its founding, while the guerrilla movements that would draft its Charter of 1968 were beginning their own campaign, told in [scene:cw6_fatah|Fatah's raids].
