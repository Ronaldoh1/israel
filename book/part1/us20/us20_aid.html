The Government Channels: Grants, Guarantees, Missile Defence and Intelligence

The memorandum of understanding (MOU) is the channel everyone knows, and it is set out in full elsewhere: Part One of this volume weighs it against Israel's economy, and Part Fifteen of the Greater Israel volume follows the emergency money that came after October 2023. [scene:us1_aid|Part One: the aid] [scene:gi16_aid|The aid in detail] This section lists every channel through which the American government moves money, guarantees or information to Israel, states what each is worth in today's dollars, and marks which ones run the other way. "Today's dollars" in this part means the September 2026 consumer price index for all urban consumers (CPI-U), 336.940, set against the annual average for the year a sum was committed. [cite:us20_bls_cpi_sep26] [cite:us20_bls_cpi_annual] The conversions are this book's arithmetic and measure purchasing power in the United States, not the value of the goods to Israel.

The grant: fixed in dollars, shrinking in value

The 2016 MOU promised $38 billion for the fiscal years 2019 to 2028: $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing (FMF) and $5 billion for missile defence, or $3.8 billion a year. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] The sum is fixed in nominal dollars. Prices rose by about 40 per cent between the 2016 annual average and September 2026, so the $3.8 billion paid in 2026 buys what about $2.7 billion bought when the agreement was signed, by this book's arithmetic. [cite:us20_bls_cpi_annual] [cite:us20_bls_cpi_sep26] The long view shows the same erosion. American aid in 1985, $3.68 billion including an emergency economic grant, equals about $11.5 billion in September 2026 dollars; the Congressional Research Service (CRS) estimate of all aid since 1946, about $298 billion in 2024 dollars, equals about $320 billion today. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Measured in what it buys, the regular grant is now about a third of its 1985 size, though the wartime supplemental of 2024 lifted that single year to about $12.5 billion. [cite:us1_jvl_aidtable]

Where the grant may be spent

Under the 2007 MOU Israel could spend up to 26.3 per cent of its FMF inside Israel, a practice called offshore procurement; in 2016 that was $815 million. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_ni_osp] The 2016 MOU phases it out, slowly until fiscal 2024 and then sharply, "ending entirely in FY2028"; the fiscal 2025 appropriation allowed $450.3 million. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] The White House said in 2016 that ending offshore procurement and the use of FMF for fuel would move up to $1.2 billion a year more into American-made goods. [cite:ow1_wh_mou2016] Two details in the CRS account matter for the flow. Israel's Defence Ministry reportedly spends about one third of its FMF on direct commercial contracts with American firms rather than through government-to-government sales, and under a "Special Billing Arrangement" Israel may draw on its own cash reserves for sales costs beyond the MOU's amounts or term. If no successor agreement is reached and Congress stops appropriating FMF, Israel would have to pay for existing contracts with national funds, find other financing or cancel them. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Interest earned on the early lump-sum payment cannot be used for purchases inside Israel. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025]

Missile defence: the channel that runs both ways

Missile defence is the one large channel in which money and goods move in both directions. CRS counts $14.8 billion in American missile-defence funding for Israel from fiscal 2006 to 2025, including about $6.1 billion for Iron Dome batteries, interceptors, co-production and maintenance and $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser, all of the latter in the 2024 supplemental. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] From 2016 production money for David's Sling and Arrow 3 was split about evenly between the Israeli makers and their American partners, Raytheon and Boeing; a 2014 agreement let Iron Dome parts be made in the United States and gave the Missile Defense Agency full access to the technology. [cite:ow8_dn_coprod2016] [cite:ow8_crs_irondome2014] The United States has also bought the product. On 6 February 2019 the two defence ministries confirmed that the US Army would buy two Iron Dome batteries, with 240 interceptors, for $373 million as an interim defence against cruise missiles. [cite:us20_toi_armyirondome] On 24 October 2023 Washington transferred two American-owned Iron Dome batteries to Israel. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] The One-Way Mirror volume sets out the co-production pipeline in detail. [scene:ow8_coproduction|Co-production]

Loan guarantees: credit, not cash

Loan guarantees move no American money unless the borrower defaults. Congress authorised $10 billion in 1992, in five annual tranches of $2 billion, for loans tied to Israel's effort "to resettle and absorb immigrants" arriving from the former Soviet Union; that is about $24 billion in today's dollars. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] [cite:us20_bls_cpi_annual] The law allowed the guarantees to be cut by an amount equal to Israel's spending on settlements in the occupied territories. Washington cut them by $1,358.8 million in 1994–97, restored $585 million for security reasons, and left $9,226.2 million available. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] A second programme of $9 billion followed in 2003, about $16.5 billion in today's dollars, and was cut by $289.5 million in 2003 and $795.8 million in 2005 on the same settlement test. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] [cite:gi16_jvl_loans] By 2025 Israel had issued $4.1 billion in American-guaranteed bonds under it, and the remaining authority, up to $3.814 billion, runs to the end of fiscal 2030 under the 2025 appropriations law. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] By the account of the CRS data compiled by the Jewish Virtual Library, Israel has never defaulted and paid annual fees for the guarantees, so the direct cost to the American government has been nil; the benefit to Israel lay in borrowing on American credit. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] The settlement deductions are the only case this book found in which the American government priced a specific Israeli policy and subtracted it from assistance. [scene:gi16_aid|Testing the fungibility argument]

A smaller channel sits beside these. The United States also keeps a stockpile of its own munitions in Israel, War Reserve Stocks for Allies–Israel; additions are capped at $500 million a year through fiscal 2027. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] Israel has held the status of major non-NATO ally since February 1987. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025]

Intelligence: the channel with no price

Intelligence sharing has no dollar value in any public record, and its terms are not published; the American Civil Liberties Union lists data-sharing agreements with Israel among those whose texts it has sought. [cite:ow4_aclu] What is documented is its history. The liaison began in the early 1950s, and James Angleton, who became head of the CIA's new Counterintelligence Staff in 1954, took control of the Israel account. [cite:ow3_robarge] [cite:ow3_tablet] A strategic cooperation agreement signed in November 1981 was temporarily suspended by the Reagan administration weeks later, after the Knesset extended Israeli law to the Golan Heights without consulting Washington. [cite:gi8_wiki_golanlaw] In September 2013 The Guardian, drawing on documents leaked by Edward Snowden, reported that the National Security Agency had agreed in principle in March 2009 to share raw signals intelligence with Israel, including unfiltered communications of American citizens, under a memorandum that did not require such data to be removed first; the NSA said American data were handled under procedures "designed to protect privacy rights". [cite:us20_aje_nsa] According to reporting by David Sanger of The New York Times in 2012, the two governments built the Stuxnet cyber-weapon against Iran's enrichment plant together from 2006; neither government has officially confirmed it. [cite:ow3_sw_sanger] After 7 October 2023 a White House memorandum expanded sharing to include live drone feeds over Gaza, The Wall Street Journal reported in April 2024; Reuters reported in December 2025 that in the second half of 2024 the United States briefly cut one feed and restricted some uses over concern about civilian deaths, while the Israeli military said "the strategic intelligence cooperation continued throughout the war." [cite:ow3_wsj_sharing] [cite:ow3_reu_withheld] The relationship also carries counterintelligence concerns: in June 2026 NBC News, citing unnamed officials, reported that the Defense Intelligence Agency had raised its counterintelligence threat rating for Israel to "critical". [cite:ow13_nbc_dia] The One-Way Mirror volume follows this channel in full. [scene:ow3_channel|The intelligence channel]

Status as of October 2026

Israel's Defence Ministry announced on 5 June 2026 that talks had opened on a framework to replace the MOU, aiming to "gradually transition from aid to a completely reciprocal partnership", and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said that month that he wanted the grant to begin falling toward zero. [cite:ow1_mod_mou2026] [cite:gi16_ji_netanyahu] As of October 2026 no new agreement had been reported in the sources this book found. Israel votes on 27 October 2026 and the United States on 3 November 2026, and both governments that negotiate the successor may change. [cite:gi6_aje_dissolve] [cite:us1_wjla_midterms] For each country the record shows a gap. On the American side, the government's largest channels are set by an executive agreement and annual appropriations, and the one documented price signal, the settlement deduction, was last applied in 2005 on the record this book found. [cite:us20_jvl_loans] On the Israeli side, a defence ministry that has relied on the grant for decades would carry the full cost of contracts signed against it if the money stops without a successor. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025]
