The Iraqi Militias and the American Bases

Iraq's armed groups are the part of Iran's network that has most often fought the United States directly. Their history is bound up with the American occupation of Iraq: armed militias began to emerge after the US invasion of 2003 and grew stronger when they organised to fight the Islamic State. In 2014 the Popular Mobilisation Forces (al-Hashd al-Shaabi) were established as an umbrella for mainly Shia paramilitary units, and became the main such force confronting the group; after its territorial defeat they turned increasingly to American targets. [cite:hz10_at_farida] The groups describe themselves as muqawama, "resistance," against the United States and its allies. [cite:hz10_at_farida] Washington has designated three of them, Kata'ib Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba and Asa'ib Ahl al-Haq, as Foreign Terrorist Organizations; the Revolutionary Guards and their Quds Force are under US terrorism sanctions. [cite:hz10_crs_if12587]

The man who held the network together was Qasem Soleimani, an IRGC major general who rose in the Iran–Iraq war and commanded the Quds Force, the Guards' external arm, from 1998. [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] His Iraqi counterpart was Abu Mahdi al-Muhandis, deputy head of the Popular Mobilisation Forces. [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] [cite:hz10_airwars_soleimani] The way both men died shows how a single exchange between the militias and the United States can climb to the top of two governments in about a week.

From a contractor's death to Soleimani's, December 2019 – January 2020

In late December 2019 a rocket attack on an Iraqi military base killed an American contractor; Defense Secretary Mark Esper dated it to 27 December, and the United States blamed Kata'ib Hezbollah. [cite:hz10_fox_pentagon2020] US airstrikes on 29 December killed 25 of the militia's fighters. Militia members and supporters then stormed the US embassy compound in Baghdad, breaching the reception area and setting parts of it on fire in a siege that ended on 1 January 2020; President Trump ordered about 750 more soldiers to the region. [cite:hz10_fox_pentagon2020] [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] Shortly after midnight on 3 January, a US drone strike on a convoy leaving Baghdad airport killed Soleimani, al-Muhandis and others; Airwars records 11 to 13 combatants and possibly one civilian killed. [cite:hz10_airwars_soleimani]

The Pentagon called the strike "decisive defensive action," said Soleimani "was actively developing plans to attack American diplomats and service members in Iraq and throughout the region," and said it "was aimed at deterring future Iranian attack plans." [cite:hz10_fox_pentagon2020] [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] Iran's supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, warned of "harsh retaliation." The New York Times reported that the strike was carried out without congressional approval, and it was unclear whether Iraq's government had been consulted. [cite:hz10_rudaw_soleimani] The strike was a United States operation.

The Gaza war and Tower 22

After 7 October 2023 the Iraqi groups, operating under the banner of the Islamic Resistance in Iraq, an umbrella that includes Kata'ib Hezbollah, launched more than 160 attacks on US forces in Iraq and Syria in under four months, by the count cited in early February 2024. [cite:hz10_at_farida] On 28 January 2024 a drone struck Tower 22, a small US base in north-eastern Jordan near the Syrian border, and killed three American soldiers, Sergeant William Jerome Rivers and Specialists Kennedy Ladon Sanders and Breonna Alexsondria Moffett; many more were wounded. President Biden blamed Iran-backed militia groups. [cite:hz10_abc_tower22] The United States named Kata'ib Hezbollah, which announced within days that it was "halting" its military operations, under pressure, it said, from Iran and the Iraqi government. [cite:hz10_at_farida] In the following week the United States struck targets in Iraq, Syria and Yemen; the White House described the message to Iran as a demand that it rein in its proxies. [cite:hz10_npr_roule]

Iran's stated position is that it did not order the attacks and that each faction acts on its own to oppose "aggression and occupation"; the head of the Revolutionary Guards said Tehran was "not looking for war." [cite:hz10_at_farida] Analysts read the same events differently. Norman Roule, the former CIA manager for Iran, told NPR that Iran controls the militias' weapons, money and key political relationships and so has enough leverage to shape their behaviour, while preferring "the product of militia actions" to day-to-day command; he ranked the Iraqi groups' closeness to Tehran as variable. [cite:hz10_npr_roule] Kata'ib Hezbollah's quick halt after Tower 22, which the group itself attributed partly to Iranian pressure, is evidence of leverage; the attacks before it are consistent both with Iranian direction and with the groups' own long campaign against the American presence. [cite:hz10_at_farida]

The network also coordinated across borders. In June 2024 the Houthi leader confirmed that his movement was coordinating military activities with the "so-called Islamic Resistance in Iraq," and the UN Panel of Experts on Yemen reported joint operations rooms in Iraq and Lebanon with Houthi representation. [cite:hz10_un_poe2024]

Status as of October 2026

The Iraqi groups' role in the 2026 war has been harder to pin down than the Houthis'. On 27 July 2026 drones hit Saudi Arabia's Abqaiq energy facility; the Houthis claimed the attack, while Riyadh blamed Iran-backed Iraqi militias. [cite:hz10_lwj_saudi2026] By late September 2026 the Critical Threats Project's daily Iran update reported "nothing significant" in Iraq.
