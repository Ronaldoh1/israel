Who Named the Heretics

Every book on this shelf has asked who held sacred knowledge. This one asks the reverse: who had the power to say that someone else's knowledge was heresy, and what that power did.

Three findings stand out. The first is that the common charge, across six centuries and very different beliefs, was a claim to reach God without the Church's mediation: the Bogomils and Cathars by rejecting its sacraments, the Beguines by living without its approved rule, Porete by a book on the soul's union with God, the Alumbrados and Quietists by teaching that the illuminated or quiet soul needed no exterior forms. [cite:hr_brit_bogomils] [cite:hr_brit_cathari] [cite:hr_brit_beguines] [cite:hr_brit_alumbrados] The second is that the decisive offence was refusal. At Montségur those who renounced walked free; Porete died because she would not speak or unsay; Molinos admitted error and was imprisoned, not burned. [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur] [cite:hr_wiki_porete] [cite:hr_brit_molinos] The third is that the prosecution of heresy served other interests too: a crusade paid in land ended with the French crown owning Languedoc. [cite:hr_brit_crusade]

The continuity with the rest of the shelf is direct. The Canon traced how the Church came to hold the list of books; this book traces how it held the definition of right belief, and what it did to those who would not accept the definition. The Gnostics' books were buried; the Cathars were burned; Porete's book survived without her name. In every case the institution claimed the sole right to say how the soul reaches God, and in every case someone said otherwise.
