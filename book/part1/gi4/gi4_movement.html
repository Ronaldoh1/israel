The Movement for the Whole Land of Israel

The phrase "Greater Israel" entered English largely as the translation of one organization's name. HaTnu'a LeMa'an Eretz Yisrael HaShlema, "the Movement for the Whole Land of Israel," usually rendered the Movement for Greater Israel or the Land of Israel Movement, was formed in the summer of 1967 to keep the territories Israel had just taken and to settle them. [cite:gi4_wiki_mgi] Unlike the biblical border texts and the founders' maps discussed in Parts One and Two, this is a case where a named body stated the programme in so many words, so it can be documented directly. This section sets out what of its founding statement can be read today, who signed it, what became of the movement, and who answered it at the time.

When it was founded

The sources give three dates, and they describe different events. Wikipedia, citing the political scientists Myron Aronoff and Ehud Sprinzak, dates the founding to July 1967. [cite:gi4_wiki_mgi] The political scientist Ian Lustick places it "two months after the Six Day War", that is, in August. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] The manifesto itself appeared as a newspaper advertisement in Yedioth Ahronoth on 22 September 1967, signed by 57 cultural figures. [cite:gi4_972_twoads] The essays that prepared the ground, collected that year by Aharon Ben-Ami under the title HaKol: Gvulot HaShalom shel Eretz Yisrael ("Everything: The Peace Borders of the Land of Israel"), had already run in Maariv, Haaretz, Davar and Lamerhav within about two months of the war. [cite:gi4_inss_einav] The most economical reading is that the circle formed in July or August and went public in September.

What the manifesto said

Few sources reproduce the manifesto, and only part of it is publicly available. The full Hebrew text is held in the Posen Library of Jewish Culture and Civilization, whose public page reproduces the opening, and in Ben-Ami's 1977 history of the movement. [cite:gi4_posen_manifesto] [cite:gi4_inss_einav] The rest of the Posen text is available only to registered readers. What is publicly available online is the following.

The manifesto opened by declaring that the army's victory "placed the nation and the state in a new and fateful period." Its central sentence, in the Posen Library's translation, reads: "Now the whole Land of Israel is in the hands of the Jewish people, and just as we have no right to give up the State of Israel, so too we are commanded to maintain what we have received from its hands: the Land of Israel." [cite:gi4_posen_manifesto] The historian Omer Einav, writing for Israel's Institute for National Security Studies (INSS), quotes the same sentence from Ben-Ami's book and notes that the word metzuvim, "commanded," gives a secular document a religious tone. [cite:gi4_inss_einav] Lustick, writing in 1988, renders the opening clause as "The whole of Eretz Yisrael is now in the hands of the Jewish people." [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] Its operative conclusion, in the translation the Israeli journalist Noam Sheizaf took from the historian Nitza Erel, was that "no government in Israel is entitled to give up this wholeness." [cite:gi4_972_twoads]

So the document stated a programme in plain terms: the territory between the Jordan and the sea, and by implication the other areas taken in June, was not to be bargained away by any government. Beyond these sentences the public record available online does not show the full text. This book does not reconstruct it, and does not attribute to the manifesto wording that cannot be checked. A dealer's catalogue of the movement's papers from 1967 to 1969 confirms that a handwritten platform, a council list, protocols with Nathan Alterman's participation and a petition against withdrawal from the territories survive in private hands. [cite:gi4_kedem_archive]

Who signed

The signatories matter because they do not fit the usual picture. Einav names Labor-movement figures (the kibbutz leader Yitzhak Tabenkin, the novelist Moshe Shamir, a Palmach veteran and former member of the left-wing Mapam, and the poet Zerubavel Gilad); writers and intellectuals including the poet Nathan Alterman, Haim Gouri, Hayim Hazaz, S.Y. Agnon, Yaakov Orland and Rachel Yanait Ben-Zvi; the secular intellectual Aharon Amir; Shmuel Tamir of the Free Center; and Rabbi Zvi Yehuda Kook of the Merkaz HaRav yeshiva. About half the signatories were writers or academics. [cite:gi4_inss_einav] Wikipedia's list adds Revisionists, among them Shmuel Katz and Israel Eldad, together with the poet Uri Zvi Greenberg, Isser Harel, Yitzhak Zuckerman, Zivia Lubetkin and Avraham Yoffe, who became its leader. [cite:gi4_wiki_mgi] The historians Amir Goldstein and Elchanan Shilo describe its founders as "activist Labour movement members." [cite:gi4_biu_goldstein]

The finding, then, is that the post-1967 whole-land programme was not born in one camp. It joined the Revisionist claim described in [scene:gi1_versions|Part One] to a strand of the Labor movement's own activist wing and to the religious current around Kook, whose lineage [scene:gi11_religious|Part Eleven] follows. It was the programme of these named people and this named movement. It was not the position of the government, which was then led by Labor and had just voted to offer Sinai and the Golan back ([scene:gi4_conquest|above]).

What became of it

As a party the movement failed. Running in the 1969 Knesset election as the "List for the Land of Israel," it won 7,561 votes, 0.6 per cent, below the threshold, and took no seat. Before the 1973 election it joined the new Likud alliance of Herut, the Liberals, the Free Center and the National List; Yoffe sat as its one member from 1973 to 1976, when it merged into the La'am faction, which remained in Likud until it merged into Herut in 1984. [cite:gi4_wiki_mgi] As a cause it did better. Lustick describes it as an elite body of writers, intellectuals and generals that aspired to be "a respected pressure group", and which Gush Emunim eclipsed after 1973, absorbing many of its participants, so that by 1977 it had "virtually ceased to exist." [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] Goldstein and Shilo's study is built on the same handover: Gush Emunim, they write, "effectively took the lead" in the movement during the 1970s. [cite:gi4_biu_goldstein] How a cause carried by Labor-movement poets passed to religious settlers is the subject of the next section, and the party line that runs through La'am into Likud is taken up in [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five].

The answer the same day

The opposition to keeping the territories was published on the same date. On 22 September 1967 the small socialist group Matzpen, founded in 1962 by four former members of the Communist Party, placed a 52-word advertisement in Haaretz: "Occupation leads to foreign rule," it said; holding the territories "will make us a nation of murderers and victims"; "Let's leave the occupied territories now." [cite:gi4_972_twoads] That month also saw the first publication of Siach Lochamim (published in English as The Seventh Day), conversations with kibbutz soldiers edited by Avraham Shapira. Its invitation, drafted by Amos Oz, promised "not a victory album, nor a collection of heroic deeds." [cite:gi4_inss_einav]

Einav sets the two currents side by side as the start of a split. The movement spoke in a collective "we" and offered policy; the soldiers' book spoke in the first person and offered doubt. Before the war, he argues, disagreements over the state's borders had been "largely theoretical"; afterwards they divided most of the Zionist camp along a single line, with far-reaching changes inside both the Labor movement and religious Zionism. [cite:gi4_inss_einav] [cite:gi4_inss_einav_abs] That is an interpretation, and it is offered as one. What the documents establish is narrower: in September 1967 a named group of Israelis demanded that the whole land be kept, and other named Israelis demanded the opposite, in the same newspapers, on the same day.
