Licensed and Left Alone

In 1979 Israel's military government recognized the Mujama as a charity and then registered it as an association. It allowed the network to build schools, clinics and mosques and an Islamic university in Gaza. Israeli officials who served there at the time told The Wall Street Journal in 2009 that their main concern then was Fatah and the PLO, which were fighting Israel, and that they did not want to appear to be enemies of Islam; when the Islamists clashed with secular Palestinians, Israel often stayed out of it. [cite:hm_wsj_spawn]

Brigadier General Yitzhak Segev, the military governor of Gaza in the early 1980s, later described the arrangement in his own words: "The Israeli government gave me a budget, and the military government gives to the mosques." [cite:hm_intercept_2018] He was describing a budget for religious institutions in an occupied territory, which Israel administered, not a grant to an armed group, and there was no armed Islamist group in Gaza for Israel to fund until 1987.

What Israeli Officials Said Later

The officials who were there did not agree about what the choice amounted to. Avner Cohen, who worked on religious affairs in Gaza for more than twenty years, told the Journal: "Hamas, to my great regret, is Israel's creation." In the mid-1980s, he said, he had written to his superiors urging them to break up "this monster" before it was too late, and nothing was done. David Hacham, an Arab affairs adviser in Gaza in the 1980s, said: "I think we made a mistake." [cite:hm_wsj_spawn]

Others disputed it. Shalom Harari, an intelligence officer in Gaza at the time, said Israel never financed or armed Hamas, and that its failure was one of neglect, not design. Arieh Spitzen, another officer, doubted that political Islam could have been stopped by acting sooner. [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] [cite:hm_intercept_2018]

What the Record Supports

The record supports a narrow finding and not a broad one. It shows that Israel licensed and tolerated the Brotherhood's charity network in Gaza for most of a decade, at a time when it regarded the PLO as the threat, and that some of the officials responsible later called that a mistake. It does not show that Israel created, funded or armed Hamas as an armed movement: the Brotherhood came first by half a century, its turn to armed struggle in 1987 was its own decision, and the officials closest to the question disagree about what Israel's tolerance amounted to.
