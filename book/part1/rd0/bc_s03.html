The 1831 Contract: The Rothschilds and the Papal Treasury

For much of the nineteenth century, the Holy See, the government of the papacy in Rome, did not manage its own money through its own institutions. It depended on outside bankers, first a Roman house and then the Rothschilds. The familiar picture of a Vatican that always kept its finances apart from any private banking family does not survive the record. The principal source for the arrangement is Niall Ferguson's The House of Rothschild: Money's Prophets 1798–1848 (Viking, 1998). [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see]

The bridge was the Torlonia family. Giovanni Torlonia (1754–1829) was born to modest circumstances in the Auvergne region of France. Through cloth trading, money-lending and papal patronage, he built one of the largest banking fortunes in Rome. He opened his banking house there in 1780, and by the 1790s it held the primary banking relationship with the Holy See. On 9 March 1794, according to records in the Archivio di Stato, Pope Pius VI ennobled him as Don Giovanni Torlonia, 1st Prince of Civitella Cesi. The exchange was plain: financial service to the papacy, answered by aristocratic rank from the papacy. The Torlonia house managed papal funds through the French occupation of Rome in 1798–1799 and the restoration that followed, keeping the Vatican's operations running on its loans. By Napoleon's final defeat in 1814 it was the Vatican's chief financial manager.

In 1823 Giovanni's son Alessandro married into the Orsini, among the oldest documented noble dynasties of Rome. The marriage joined the papacy's leading banking family to its old nobility. It is recorded in Roman diocesan records, in Orsini genealogical materials, and in Pompeo Litta's Famiglie celebri d'Italia. The sources assembled for this book name the bride in two ways, as Teresa and as Maria Luisa Orsini; that discrepancy is left unresolved here.

Then came the contract. On 30 November 1831, in Rome, Alessandro Torlonia co-signed an agreement with James Mayer de Rothschild of the Paris house. According to the account used here, a clause kept secret gave the Rothschilds an exclusive right of first refusal on Vatican sovereign debt. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] As Ferguson describes the terms, they amounted to exclusive management of papal loan issues and key financial operations through the London and Paris houses, with the Torlonia house acting as local intermediary. The need was real. The Papal States were in fiscal distress after the Napoleonic wars, and the Torlonia house lacked the capital to meet the Vatican's needs on its own. A letter of Pope Gregory XVI dated 15 January 1832 acknowledges the Rothschilds' assistance. A loan of £400,000 to Gregory XVI in 1832 is documented; it was negotiated through Carl Mayer von Rothschild, with James serving as official papal banker. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see]

What happened in 1834 is less clear, because the accounts gathered here tell it two ways. In one, Carl Mayer de Rothschild travelled to Rome and invoked the secret clause to block an attempt by Cardinal Tosti to refinance with six other banks. In the other, the episode was an embezzlement case involving a papal official named Antonio Tosti. The Rothschilds' records of their dealings with the Vatican, set against the Vatican's own accounts, helped expose irregularities. Ferguson is cited for the Tosti affair, but the two versions do not agree, and this book does not choose between them. Either way, the episode shows how deeply the bank was involved in papal finance before the Vatican had internal institutions of its own.

The relationship was no secret. The Jewish Encyclopedia, published by Funk & Wagnalls between 1901 and 1906 and compiled by some 400 scholars, described the Rothschilds in its entry on the family as "guardians of the papal treasure." Some read the phrase to mean the bank managed Vatican assets rather than merely lending money, but that is an interpretation of a single phrase, not a finding from the accounts. Catholic critics at the time, as a remark by the poet Alfred de Vigny illustrates, fixed on the fact that the bankers were Jewish. That line of attack is a trope, and this book does not adopt it. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see]

The research behind this book traces a line forward from 1831. In 1942 the Vatican founded its own bank, the IOR, bringing its banking in-house. In 2026 François Pauly, a former chief executive of Edmond de Rothschild Suisse, part of the Edmond de Rothschild group, became president of the IOR's supervisory board. [cite:wiki_rothschild_loans_holy_see] That appointment should be read at its actual strength. It is an institutional adjacency: a former executive of a bank that bears the Rothschild name. It is not a family relationship, and it does not establish an unbroken thread of Rothschild control running across nearly two centuries. What the record does establish is narrower and firmer. In 1831 a papacy short of money turned to a Roman banking family, and through it to what was by then the dominant private banking operation in Europe.
