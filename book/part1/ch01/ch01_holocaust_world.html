The Holocaust in the World's Classrooms

The previous section asked what American schools teach about the Holocaust, who supplies the material and what young Americans actually know. [scene:ch01_never_again|"Never Again" and the Classroom] The same questions can be put to every other country, in the same way: what the curriculum requires, who wrote it, how the event is framed, and what the public knows. A familiar assumption holds that the Holocaust is a fixed lesson taught alike wherever history is taught. The documents show a mandatory topic in some systems, a line about human rights in others, and nothing at all in some.

The fullest global picture is a mapping for UNESCO by the Georg Eckert Institute, published in 2015. It analysed 272 curricula valid in 2013–2014 from 135 of a possible 195 countries, and 89 textbooks from 26 countries published since 2000. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] [cite:rs_ch01_ucl_unesco] Its summary reports that the Holocaust appears in the history curricula of approximately half of the countries investigated, and that about a quarter of all curricula do not name the Holocaust but refer to its context, such as the Second World War or National Socialism. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] A report on the study in The Conversation gave the count differently: 57 curricula directly stipulate the Holocaust with words such as "Holocaust" or "Shoah," eight countries address it only partially, and 46 provide only the context of the war or National Socialism. [cite:rs_ch01_conv_unesco] Both summaries mix units, counting countries in some figures and curricula in others, so their numbers cannot be lined up directly; they are two summaries of one study, not two measurements.

Most curricula, the summary found, do not specify which victim groups to cover; where groups are named, Jews are usually the only ones explicitly listed. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] Which curricula leave the Holocaust out entirely is one point on which the two summaries diverge. The UNESCO summary gives Brunei Darussalam, Iceland and Thailand as examples of curricula with no reference to it; the Conversation report on the same study names Egypt, Palestine, Iraq, New Zealand and Thailand. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] [cite:rs_ch01_conv_unesco] Only Thailand appears on both; the full list that would reconcile them has not been published in these summaries. The summary also lists Argentina, Belize, Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico and Slovenia as countries whose curricula refer to the Holocaust only partially, framing it as a violation of human rights; in Mexico it appears as one aspect among others of human-rights education. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] [cite:rs_ch01_conv_unesco]

Framing varied as much as coverage. In the textbooks the study examined, the Holocaust was generally located in "Europe" and "Germany," while Russian and Indian textbooks cast it in transcontinental terms, setting Europe against Asia. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] Rwandan textbooks apply Holocaust vocabulary to the genocide of 1994. Chinese textbooks use the terms "genocide" (datusha) and "kinds of crimes" (zhongzhong zuixing) rather than "Holocaust" or "Shoah," and apply such language to the Nanjing massacres of 1937. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] [cite:rs_ch01_conv_unesco] The authors drew the conclusion in a single sentence: "educational media provide a foundation not for education about the Holocaust, but for education about the Holocausts." [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum]

In Germany the requirement is set out officially. The Conference of Education Ministers of the sixteen Länder, the KMK, states that the Holocaust and National Socialism are firmly anchored in the History curricula, or history-heavy social-science curricula, of all 16 Länder: a mandatory topic in grade 9 or 10 of lower secondary school, occasionally grade 8, and a mandatory, more in-depth topic in upper secondary. [cite:rs_ch01_kmk_holo] The instrument is a series of KMK recommendations. The first dates from 20 April 1978, "Behandlung des Nationalsozialismus im Unterricht," on the treatment of National Socialism in teaching. The "Erinnern für die Zukunft" recommendations on memory culture followed on 11 December 2014, and in October 2013 the KMK signed a declaration of intent with Yad Vashem and Israel's education ministry. [cite:rs_ch01_kmk_holo]

The live German argument is whether students should be made to visit a former camp. In May 2025 Bavaria announced, according to the education site News4teachers, that from the 2025/26 school year students at all secondary schools must visit a concentration-camp memorial at least once during their schooling; before that the requirement applied only to Gymnasium and Realschule students. [cite:rs_ch01_n4t_bavaria] The same month the Federal Education Minister, Karin Prien, called for compulsory memorial visits for school students. [cite:rs_ch01_iac_prien] The memorials themselves were divided. In April 2024 the directors of Buchenwald, Sachsenhausen, Dachau, Bergen-Belsen and Neuengamme were reported as opposing or doubting compulsory school visits, arguing that voluntariness is essential and that coercion can reinforce rejection; the director of the Flossenbürg memorial reported very good experience with obligatory visits. [cite:rs_ch01_ja_memorials] The International Auschwitz Committee answered Prien that funding for school trips and teacher training mattered more than compulsion. [cite:rs_ch01_iac_prien] No study in the record settles which side is right.

Israel built remembrance into law early. On 4 March 1959 the Knesset passed a law requiring public observances in tribute to Holocaust victims on Holocaust Remembrance Day, and a 1961 amendment required places of entertainment to close on the eve of the day. [cite:rs_ch01_ejud_1959] In October 2013, according to the Times of Israel, Education Minister Shai Piron announced a programme designed by Yad Vashem with Holocaust lessons in each of the 12 years of compulsory schooling, starting in first grade; at the time, students began in-depth Holocaust study only in 11th grade. [cite:rs_ch01_toi_piron]

Israeli Holocaust education also took place abroad, on school trips to Poland. According to Notes from Poland, tens of thousands of Israeli youths visited Poland each year before the pandemic. Israel suspended the trips in June 2022 amid a dispute in which, according to the same outlet, the Polish government was reportedly trying to dictate what could be taught. [cite:rs_ch01_nfp_trips] Polish officials argued that the trips focused narrowly on the war, strengthened false and negative stereotypes of Poles, and relied on armed Israeli security guards, and that they should include Polish history and Jewish heritage. The agreement to resume them, signed on 22 March 2023, added exactly that: Polish history and Jewish heritage, meetings with Polish youth, and responsibility for security placed on the host country. [cite:rs_ch01_nfp_trips] In April 2023 Yad Vashem criticised the agreement, according to the Associated Press. It said the recommended site list included sites that should not be visited in an educational context, that it had had no input into the list, and that the trips must keep complete historical accuracy, including Poles' roles both in persecuting and in rescuing Jews. [cite:rs_ch01_ap_yv_trips] Read side by side, the two positions turn on who decides what students see.

The United Kingdom differs by nation. The genocide of European Jews has been a mandatory subject in the Key Stage 3 National Curriculum for History in England since 1991, in the description of University College London. [cite:rs_ch01_ucl_impact] The current statutory programme of study for England, published on 11 September 2013, lists "In addition to studying the Holocaust" under Key Stage 3, within its content on challenges for Britain. [cite:rs_ch01_dfe_nc] The requirement has limits that the Holocaust Educational Trust sets out: academies need not follow the National Curriculum, and Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have no formal requirement. [cite:rs_ch01_het_uk]

England is also the one country in this record with a large study of what school students know. The UCL Centre for Holocaust Education's October 2016 study of more than 8,000 students aged 11 to 18 found that only 37% knew what "antisemitism" means; that 8.8% correctly identified the pre-war German Jewish population as under 1%; and that only 7% understood that the invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 triggered organised mass killing. [cite:rs_ch01_ucl_2016pub] [cite:rs_ch01_ucl_keyfind] Fifty-five percent thought the mass murder took place in Germany. Seventy-one percent associated Auschwitz with the Holocaust, against 15% each for Treblinka and Bergen-Belsen, and fewer than 10% suggested that the German people bore any responsibility. [cite:rs_ch01_ucl_keyfind]

France begins in primary school. In an answer to a parliamentary question, published on 16 April 2019, the education ministry stated that the genocides of the Jews and the Roma are taught for the first time in CM2, the final year of primary school; that two parts of the troisième programme, for students aged 14 to 15, address antisemitism and the genocide of the Jews; and that the genocide is studied in the vocational-track première programme. [cite:rs_ch01_an_fr] Among the curricula described here, it is the only one documented as naming the Roma alongside the Jews.

Poland legislated on how the war may be described. In February 2018 an amendment to the law on the Institute of National Remembrance, signed by President Andrzej Duda, provided prison sentences of up to three years for accusing Poland of complicity in the Holocaust, according to NPR. On 27 June 2018 both houses of parliament voted to remove the prison penalty, leaving such cases as civil offences subject to fines. [cite:rs_ch01_npr_poland] A joint statement by the Polish and Israeli prime ministers accompanying the change said they "acknowledge and condemn every single case of cruelty against Jews perpetrated by Poles during the World War II." [cite:rs_ch01_npr_poland] Both readings of the law are on the record. The Polish government, through Prime Minister Morawiecki, held that those who say Poland may be responsible for wartime crimes deserve penalties, and presented the law as a defence of the country's reputation. Critics in Israel, the United States and Europe, the World Jewish Congress among them, said it whitewashed history and stifled discussion of local complicity. [cite:rs_ch01_npr_poland] The research does not record what Polish curricula require.

For Russia the record runs from the Soviet period to the present. The historian Ilya Altman of Russia's Holocaust Center told a Yad Vashem conference in 1999 that the Holocaust was omitted from Soviet school and university textbooks from the mid-1940s to the late 1980s, that wartime reports replaced "Jews" with "peaceful Soviet citizens," and that post-Soviet textbooks mentioned the six million only in passing. [cite:rs_ch01_altman_yv] In 2008, according to the World Jewish Congress, a joint study by the Russian Academy of Sciences and the Russian Jewish Congress found that many Russian school history textbooks completely avoid the subject. [cite:rs_ch01_wjc_russia] The current state textbooks for grades 10 and 11, by Medinsky and Torkunov, published in 2023, frame the war as the Great Patriotic War; a 2024 study of them by Aleksander Wawrzyńczak found no discussion of the Holocaust, and the only Jewish reference it noted was Tsezar Kunikov, listed among Soviet heroes. [cite:rs_ch01_wawrz_russia] In April 2025, according to the Moldovan news agency IPN, Russia promulgated a law defining "genocide against the Soviet people" during the Second World War, due to enter into force on 1 January 2026; the report on it does not mention Jews or the Holocaust. [cite:rs_ch01_ipn_sovgen] How the law has been applied in schools since it took effect has not been checked for this edition (as of October 2026).

Iran is documented here through a state act rather than a curriculum. On 11 December 2006, according to an AFP report, a government-sponsored conference questioning the Holocaust opened at the Foreign Ministry's Institute for Political and International Studies in Tehran. Foreign Minister Manouchehr Mottaki said its aim was "not to deny or confirm the Holocaust," and participants included David Duke. [cite:rs_ch01_mg_tehran] The research holds no study of what Iranian schools teach.

For the Arab governments, the evidence is of uneven kinds. For the Palestinian Authority, Samira Alayan's peer-reviewed study of 2016 in the Comparative Education Review found no mention of the Holocaust in its history textbooks, and compared them with Arab-Israeli curricula. [cite:rs_ch01_alayan_pal] The EU-commissioned Georg Eckert Institute study of Palestinian textbooks, published in June 2021, examined 156 of 309 volumes and found UNESCO-style human-rights content alongside "anti-Semitic narratives and glorifications of violence"; its published FAQ does not address the Holocaust. [cite:rs_ch01_gei_paltex] The Conversation summary of the UNESCO mapping names Egypt, Palestine and Iraq among curricula with no reference. [cite:rs_ch01_conv_unesco] For Saudi Arabia the only finding comes from an advocacy group: IMPACT-se reported on 1 May 2024 that in Saudi textbooks "The Holocaust is absent from the curriculum." [cite:rs_ch01_impact_saudi] No ministry response or independent study of that point is in the record. Two governments announced the opposite course. In January 2023 the embassy of the United Arab Emirates in Washington said the UAE would include the Holocaust in the curriculum for primary and secondary schools, with its education ministry consulting Yad Vashem and IMPACT-se; Yad Vashem said at the time, according to Al-Monitor, that the initiative was still being developed. [cite:rs_ch01_almon_uae] In September 2018, according to Ynetnews, Morocco's Education Minister, Said Amzazi, announced at a round table at the UN General Assembly that King Mohammed VI had decided to integrate the study of the Holocaust into the national curriculum. [cite:rs_ch01_ynet_morocco]

Latin America appears in the UNESCO summary chiefly as a region of partial, human-rights framing, and Argentina is on that list. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] Its own documents show more. The Priority Learning Cores, the NAP, approved by the Federal Council of Education Ministers in 2004, list the Holocaust as a topic. [cite:rs_ch01_arg_nap] Argentina has been a full member of the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance since 2002, and its foreign ministry describes it as the only full member in Latin America. [cite:rs_ch01_arg_ihra] The province of Córdoba has had a law on teaching the Shoah since about 2008, under which more than 2,000 teachers have been trained, according to the Córdoba paper Hoy Día, and in 2026 (as reported in July 2026) it added mandatory analysis of hate speech and terrorism. [cite:rs_ch01_hoydia_cba] The research does not explain how the UNESCO classification relates to Argentina's own documents.

In Africa, South Africa's national History curricula for Grades 9 and 11 have included the study of Nazi Germany and the Holocaust since 2007, according to the South African Jewish Board of Deputies' report on the SA Holocaust & Genocide Foundation. [cite:rs_ch01_cjc_sa] Rwanda, as noted above, applies the Holocaust's vocabulary to 1994; for East, South and Southeast Asia the record holds only the UNESCO findings on China, India, Thailand and Brunei. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum]

In this record, what publics know worldwide comes from two surveys by the Anti-Defamation League. Its first Global 100 survey interviewed 53,100 adults in 102 countries and territories between July 2013 and February 2014. Only 54% worldwide had ever heard of the Holocaust, against 94% in Western Europe. [cite:rs_ch01_adl_g100_2014] According to NBC News' report on that survey, 32% of respondents believed the Holocaust was a myth or greatly exaggerated, and only 38% in the Middle East and North Africa had heard of it, of whom 63% doubted it or considered it exaggerated. [cite:rs_ch01_nbc_g100] The second round, published in January 2025, surveyed more than 58,000 adults in 103 countries and territories, with fieldwork from 23 July to 13 November 2024. It found that 20% worldwide had not heard of the Holocaust and that 48% recognised its historical accuracy; the share was 39% among those aged 18 to 34, 16% in the Middle East and North Africa and 23% in Sub-Saharan Africa. [cite:rs_ch01_adl_g100_2025] The research does not record whether the two rounds asked identical questions of comparable samples, so their figures should not be read as a measured trend.

A narrower survey by the Claims Conference covered eight countries, with 1,000 adults each in the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Austria, Germany, Poland, Hungary and Romania; fieldwork ran from 15 to 28 November 2023, and the results were released in January 2025. Across the European countries, 93.5% had heard the word "Holocaust," 44% knew that about six million Jews were killed, and 81% could name a camp or ghetto. [cite:rs_ch01_jvl_cc8] [cite:rs_ch01_cc_keyfind] The figures for the young varied sharply by country. In France, 46% of those aged 18 to 29 had not heard of the Holocaust or were unsure, against 15% in Romania, 14% in Austria and 12% in Germany, and 53% of young Romanians said the number killed had been greatly exaggerated, according to San Diego Jewish World's report on the release. [cite:rs_ch01_sdjw_cc8] [cite:rs_ch01_cc_keyfind] In the Netherlands, a Claims Conference survey of 2,000 adults in January 2023 found that 23% of those born after 1980 believed the Holocaust was a myth or that the number of victims was greatly exaggerated, according to an AFP report. [cite:rs_ch01_malay_nl]

Governments have also committed themselves collectively. The International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance was founded in 1998 on the Stockholm Declaration, which pledges to promote Holocaust education in schools and universities; it states that it has 35 Member Countries and 8 Observer Countries. In 2019 it published Recommendations for Teaching and Learning about the Holocaust, organised around why, what and how to teach. [cite:rs_ch01_ihra_members] [cite:rs_ch01_ihra_recs] [cite:rs_ch01_arg_ihra] The United Nations General Assembly adopted resolution 60/7 on 1 November 2005. It designated 27 January as an annual International Day of Commemoration, urged Member States to develop educational programmes on the Holocaust's lessons, and "Rejects any denial of the Holocaust as an historical event, either in full or part." [cite:rs_ch01_un_60_7] Resolution 76/250, on Holocaust denial, was sponsored by Israel and co-sponsored by 114 nations; it was adopted by consensus without a vote on 20 January 2022, and Iran disassociated itself from it. [cite:rs_ch01_euronews_76250] These instruments urge and recommend; none of them, in the texts summarized here, sets a curriculum.

The gaps are large. Sixty of the world's 195 countries were outside the UNESCO mapping, and its data are from 2013–2014, before many of the changes described here. [cite:rs_ch01_unesco_sum] For Iran, Poland and most of the Arab world, no study of current school curricula is in the record at all; for Saudi Arabia the only finding is an advocacy group's; and for the UAE and Morocco the record holds announcements, not evidence of what classrooms now teach. Whether Israel's twelve-year programme was carried out as announced, and how Russia's 2025 law has been applied in schools since January 2026, are not documented here. The surveys measure what adults know, not what they were taught, and no study in the record connects a country's curriculum to its survey results. Nor does any study here measure how classrooms outside the United States connect the Holocaust to the State of Israel.

The question this library puts to the story of Israel, what is taught, who writes it and through what instrument, can be put to the Holocaust in the same terms. A ministers' conference in Germany, a minister and a state memorial authority in Israel, a statutory programme in England, a speech law in Poland, a state textbook in Russia, a foreign ministry's conference in Iran, an advocacy group's review and an embassy's announcement in the Gulf: each is an identifiable author, and the versions they produce differ. How the story of Israel itself reaches students is told in [scene:s00a|How the Story Is Taught].
