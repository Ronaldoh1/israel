The Routes

Every reform in this book has to pass through one of six doors: a city charter or ordinance, a state statute, a state ballot measure, an act of Congress, an amendment to the Constitution, or a ruling by a court. The doors differ in who holds the key and in how hard they are to open, and a reform's prospects depend as much on its door as on its merits. Some of the reforms most popular in polls sit behind the hardest door, and some of the most effective sit behind the easiest. This section sets out each route, what has passed through it, and what is moving through it as of October 2026.

Cities

The city door has carried the most reform. New York City's voters adopted ranked-choice voting for primaries and special elections by 73.61 per cent in November 2019. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] Seattle's voters created democracy vouchers and renewed them in 2025; Takoma Park lowered the voting age in city elections to sixteen in 2013; Oakland's voters did the same for school board races in 2020. [cite:dt7_bp_seattle] [cite:dt7_wm_vote16] [cite:dt7_74_vote16] In November 2026 ranked-choice voting is used in eleven cities and two counties, as well as Alaska, Maine and the District of Columbia. [cite:dt6_fv_2026use] The limit of this route is that a state can close it. Eighteen states have banned ranked-choice voting, all since 2022, Indiana most recently in February 2026. [cite:dt6_bp_bans2026] [cite:dt6_bp_bans2024]

States

State legislatures hold the second door. The popular-vote compact passes through it one state at a time; Virginia's governor signed it on 13 April 2026, bringing its members to 222 electoral votes. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia] Where a state allows citizen initiatives, voters can go around the legislature. Maine's voters adopted ranked-choice voting in 2016 by 52.12 per cent, and when the legislature tried to delay it they overruled it by a people's veto in 2018; Alaska's voters adopted top-four primaries and ranked-choice general elections by 50.55 per cent in 2020; Arizona's created their redistricting commission in 2000. [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] [cite:dt6_bp_ak2026] [cite:dt7_bp_airc] This route is bound by the state's own constitution: Maine's highest court advised in 2017 that ranked-choice counting conflicted with the constitution's plurality rule for governor and the legislature, so it applies in Maine only to primaries and federal elections. [cite:dt6_bp_me_advisory] It is also bound by the voters' willingness, which in 2024 ran mostly against reform: voters in Oregon, Colorado, Idaho, Nevada, Montana, Arizona and South Dakota rejected eight ranked-choice, open-primary or majority-winner measures, Alaska's voters narrowly kept their system, the District of Columbia adopted ranked-choice voting and Missouri's voters adopted a ban. [cite:dt6_bp_2024results] Some efforts never reach the ballot: Michigan's ranked-choice campaign withdrew from 2026 to aim at 2028, and open-primary measures in Oklahoma and Nevada failed to qualify. [cite:dt9_bp_mirc] [cite:dt9_prt_ok836] [cite:dt9_ni_nv2026]

Congress

Congress can change the rules for federal elections where the Constitution and the Court allow. It has done so recently, and with members of both parties: the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 raised the threshold for objecting to a state's electoral votes from one member of each house to one-fifth of each, and made clear that the vice-president's role in the count is ministerial. [cite:dt2_bp_ecra] The bills that would do more have one-party sponsorship. The Fair Representation Act, introduced in July 2025, would elect the House by ranked-choice voting in multi-member districts and ban mid-decade redistricting. [cite:dt6_fra_text] The DISCLOSE Act failed 49–49 in 2022 and was reintroduced in March 2026 with 186 sponsors, all Democrats or independents who caucus with them. [cite:dt7_senate_disclose2022] [cite:dt7_whitehouse_disclose2026] Neither can pass the Senate without 60 votes or a change to its rules.

The Constitution

Three of the reforms in this book need an amendment: abolishing the Electoral College outright, limiting the terms of members of Congress, and allowing limits on independent spending. [cite:dt7_fed_thornton] [cite:ad15_lii_cu] An amendment needs two-thirds of both houses and ratification by three-quarters of the states, 38 of 50; there have been 27, the last in 1992. [cite:us2_annenberg_art5] The nearest attempt at direct presidential election passed the House 339–70 in 1969 and failed to overcome a Senate filibuster in 1970. [cite:dt2_wiki_bayh] The Democracy for All Amendment won 54 Senate votes in 2014, short of the 60 needed to end debate and far short of the 67 needed to pass, and was reintroduced in 2025. [cite:dt5_senate_sjres19] [cite:dt5_duckworth_dfa] This is the route with the broadest public support behind some of its proposals and the least movement.

The gap between opinion and movement is wide. In 2024, 63 per cent of Americans told Pew they favoured electing the president by popular vote, and 58 per cent told Gallup they would back an amendment to do it; in 2018, 66 per cent of Republicans and 85 per cent of Democrats backed an amendment to overturn Citizens United; in 2023, 72 per cent wanted limits on campaign spending. [cite:dt2_pew_ec2024] [cite:dt2_ktvu_gallup2024] [cite:dt4_time_umd] [cite:dt4_pew_money2023] Support in a poll is not the same as a supermajority in Congress and in 38 legislatures, and on the Electoral College it divides by party: Republicans were 32 per cent in favour in Gallup's survey, Democrats 82. [cite:dt2_ktvu_gallup2024] Article V also allows a second path, a convention called when two-thirds of the state legislatures apply for one, with the same three-quarters needed to ratify. [cite:us2_annenberg_art5] The routes that bypass this door, the compact and state ballot measures, exist because this one is so hard to open.

The courts

Courts open and close the other doors. The Supreme Court kept the commission route open by 5–4 in 2015, closed federal courts to partisan-gerrymandering claims by 5–4 in 2019, and in 2023 rejected the theory that state legislatures act free of their own state courts when setting federal election rules. [cite:dt7_bp_airc] [cite:dt3_bp_rucho] [cite:dt3_ts_moore] A federal judge upheld Maine's ranked-choice count in 2018, writing that "the Constitution does not require an easy ballot." [cite:dt6_baber] On money, apart from upholding the ban on party soft money in 2003, the Court has moved one way since 1976, and consistently since 2010, most recently by striking down limits on party coordinated spending on 30 June 2026. [cite:dt4_wiki_buckley] [cite:dt7_audit_mcconnell] [cite:dt7_pac_nrsc] The compact's supporters expect its legal test to come in court if it ever reaches 270 electoral votes. [cite:dt2_mpr_virginia]

Read as a whole, the table shows where effort meets least resistance. The reforms that need only a city or a state are moving, and being reversed, every year; those that need Congress move slowly and along party lines; those that need an amendment have not moved in decades. The Court sits above all of them, and its direction on money since 2010 has been steady.
