The Core Texts, 2007–2014, and Their First Readers

Unqualified Reservations was not an ordinary blog. Its posts were essays, often thousands of words long, frequently published as numbered series over weeks, written in a dense, allusive and deliberately provocative style that mixed history, software metaphors and jokes. Yarvin posted regularly from 2007, largely stopped in 2013 when he turned to building his computer platform Urbit, and in April 2016 announced that the blog had "completed its mission." [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] Out of hundreds of posts, a handful carry most of the ideas that later writers quote. This section reads four of them, then Land's serial of 2012, and then the first critics and journalists who read both. The hardest passages in these texts are quoted here briefly and in context; [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|Part Six] and [scene:de4_the_hard_passages|Part Four] treat them in full.

"A formalist manifesto" (23 April 2007)

The blog's opening essay set out a theory of peace. Its definition of the problem was legal rather than moral: "Violence, then, is anything that breaks the rule, or replaces it with a different rule." Conflict, on this account, comes from disputes over who owns what, including who holds political power, and the cure is to settle those disputes by recognising the present distribution exactly as it is. "Formalism says: let's figure out exactly who has what, now, and give them a little fancy certificate." [cite:de2_le] The argument's appeal and its difficulty are both visible here. It promises an end to struggle by ending argument about justice: whoever holds power now gets a deed to it. It does not ask how the present holders came by what they hold, or what happens to people who hold nothing. Princeton University Library keeps the essay as a 14-page booklet prepared with the author's permission. [cite:de2_princeton_fm]

"An Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives" (from 17 April 2008)

The second core text was a serial in many chapters, addressed to the kind of reader its author expected to disagree with him. Its first chapter, "A Horizon Made of Canvas," opened with the question "Are you an open-minded progressive?" and the observation that the reader probably had friends who were. [cite:de2_upcarta] The form matters as much as the content: rather than preaching to readers who already agreed, the Open Letter addressed the ones most likely to object. It is also where Yarvin, as Part One showed, accepted "reactionary" as a fair description of himself, while preferring "restorationist." [cite:de1_wiki_yarvin]

"Patchwork" (from 13 November 2008)

In November 2008 Yarvin turned from diagnosis to design. The series called "Patchwork: A Positive Vision" proposed replacing large states with a patchwork of small ones, "each governed by its own joint-stock corporation without regard to the residents' opinions." Residents would have one remedy: "If residents don't like their government, they can and should move." [cite:de2_le] This is the exit-over-voice idea of Part One in its most concrete form, and it is the model Land later summarised as "No voice, free exit." [cite:de1_land_ebook]

The series also asked what a profit-seeking sovereign corporation should do with residents who cost more than they pay. In the first instalment, writing of a hypothetical company-run San Francisco and its surplus residents, Yarvin's answer began: "Sell their slums out from under them, obviously," and went on to demolition and "possibly a little aerial bombing." [cite:de2_le] In the second instalment, on 20 November 2008, he raised converting such people "into biodiesel, which can help power the Muni buses," said he was "just kidding," and then stated: "Our goal, in short, is a humane alternative to genocide." The alternative he went on to describe was permanent confinement connected to virtual reality. [cite:de2_tnr] Yarvin has described the biodiesel line as a joke, and the text itself says so; the passage that follows it, stating the goal, is not framed as a joke. These are his words, and they belong in any honest account of what Patchwork proposed. [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|Part Six] gives the surrounding text and his later explanations.

The 2012 talk and the end of the blog

In 2012 Yarvin carried the ideas from the page to a stage. At a technology conference called BIL he gave a talk proposing that a new American leader "Retire All Government Employees," abbreviated RAGE. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] The proposal marks a turn worth noting. Patchwork had imagined new small sovereigns that people could choose between; RAGE imagined taking over the existing American government and emptying it. [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six] reads the talk, and the question of how far later events resemble it, without being derived from it, runs through Book Three. In 2013 Yarvin largely stopped posting to concentrate on Urbit, and the blog fell quiet. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin]

Land's serial (2 March–20 July 2012)

Land's "The Dark Enlightenment" was published in ten instalments over five months. Read as a whole, it does three things. It restates Yarvin's diagnosis and design, with long quotations, crediting him with the Cathedral, neocameralism and Universalism. It places them in a longer line of thought running "From Thomas Hobbes to Hans-Hermann Hoppe," and in the contemporary turn of some libertarians, Peter Thiel and the seasteading advocate Patri Friedman among those quoted, from voting toward exit. And from the fourth instalment onward it turns, at length, to race, which the serial treats under headings such as "A multi-part sub-digression into racial terror." [cite:de2_land_ebook] Those passages, including Land's rejection of the slide from white identity politics into antisemitism, which he calls a "trap," are examined in [scene:de4_the_hard_passages|Part Four]. The serial ends with speculation about human genetic self-engineering and the line "It's time to move on." [cite:de2_land_ebook]

The first readers

For six years these texts circulated mainly among blog readers. The first account written for a broad technology audience was Klint Finley's "Geeks for Monarchy: The Rise of the Neoreactionaries," published by TechCrunch on 22 November 2013. Finley described the writers' preference for monarchy and hierarchy, defined the Cathedral, quoted Yarvin's "all 'exit,' no 'voice,'" and quoted the investor Balaji Srinivasan saying that a much-discussed speech of his was "more about emigration and exit." [cite:de1_finley_tc] A month earlier the blogger Scott Alexander, writing on Slate Star Codex, had published "The Anti-Reactionary FAQ" on 20 October 2013, a long point-by-point rebuttal that defined neoreaction as "a political ideology supporting a return to traditional ideas of government and society." [cite:de2_ssc_faq] That rebuttal came from inside the online rationalist community where many of Yarvin's early readers were found, which is one reason the two are often confused and one reason they should not be.

The critical reception hardened over the following years. The journalist Corey Pein wrote about the current for The Baffler on 19 May 2014 under the title "Mouthbreathing Machiavellis Dream of a Silicon Reich," and returned to it on 9 October 2017, writing that Yarvin's "Dark Enlightenment dogma is steeped in pseudoscientific racism" and calling neoreaction "a subset of what is now euphemistically termed the alt-right." [cite:de2_pein2017] His 2018 book, Live Work Work Work Die, became a standard source for Yarvin's 2012 talk. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] The writer Elizabeth Sandifer crowdfunded a book-length essay, Neoreaction a Basilisk, first published on 24 June 2016, which read Yarvin and Land alongside the rationalist writer Eliezer Yudkowsky. [cite:de2_sandifer_gr] [cite:de2_sandifer_ep] Each of these readers is a named critic with a stated view, and each is quoted as such; their characterisations are theirs, set beside the texts they describe.

The worst-case reading, tested

The texts in this part bear on the hypothesis in two ways. The destination is documented in them: Patchwork's sovereign corporations govern "without regard to the residents' opinions," and Land's summary of the model is "No voice, free exit." [cite:de2_le] [cite:de1_land_ebook] So is a harsh answer to the question of people a profit-run sovereign does not want, stated in Yarvin's own words in November 2008, with one line marked as a joke and the stated goal not. [cite:de2_tnr] The shift from exit to takeover is also documented, in the move from Patchwork's new small states in 2008 to RAGE's emptying of the existing American government in 2012. [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] That shift matters for the hypothesis, because a programme of leaving existing states poses little danger to them, while a programme of capturing one does.

What the texts of 2007–2014 do not show is any connection to the occasions the hypothesis names. They contain no plan for immigration enforcement and no link to any later government; the readers who first wrote about them, from Finley to Sandifer, described a small online body of writing, not an operation. That a later policy resembles RAGE is, on this part's material, not supported as provenance: rhyme is not descent, and the test of whether anything passed from these texts into office begins in [scene:de12_the_thiel_years|Part Twelve] and [scene:de17_money_to_office|Part Seventeen]. Part Three goes back further, to the older writers these texts drew on.
