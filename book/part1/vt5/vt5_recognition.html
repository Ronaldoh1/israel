Why It Is Still Treated as a State

Part Four described the machinery inside the walls. This section asks a question from outside them: why other governments, the United Nations and foreign courts treat a papal office with a city of 44 hectares as a sovereign at all. The short answer is that they do not, strictly, treat the city as the sovereign. The subject of international law is the Holy See, the office of the pope and the central government of the Catholic Church, and Vatican City State is the territory over which it has ruled since 1929. [scene:vt4_holy_see_vs_city|Part Four: the Holy See and the city] [scene:dv1_sovereignty|The Divine 3+1: the sovereignty tests] The statehood tests applied to the city in The Divine 3+1 are not repeated here; this section follows how the Holy See is recognised, where that has been challenged, and what immunity it has claimed in court.

A claim older than the city

The Lateran Treaty did not create the Holy See's international standing; it acknowledged it. In Article 2 of the treaty signed on 11 February 1929, Italy recognised the sovereignty of the Holy See in the international field "as an attribute inherent to its nature," and only in Article 3 did it recognise the Holy See's sovereign power over the Vatican, creating the city. [cite:vt1_va_lateran] [scene:vt2_pacts_signed|Part Two: the Pacts signed] The order of the two articles mirrors the order of events. Between the fall of Rome to the Italian army in September 1870 and the treaty of 1929 the Holy See held no territory, yet it continued to send and receive diplomats: the number of states with which it had relations rose from 16 to 27, it concluded 29 concordats and other agreements, and it was asked to mediate 13 international disputes. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] Some jurists of the period doubted whether a sovereign without land could remain an independent actor in international law; in practice most governments kept dealing with it. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] [scene:vt2_roman_question|Part Two: the Roman Question]

That practice is the real basis of the Holy See's standing today. International law does not issue certificates of statehood; a subject exists to the extent that other subjects deal with it as one, by exchanging ambassadors, signing treaties and admitting it to conferences. On that measure the Holy See's position is strong. The Holy See Press Office's annual note of 9 January 2026 recorded diplomatic relations with 184 states, plus the European Union and the Sovereign Military Order of Malta, and 93 embassy chancelleries accredited to the Holy See with their seats in Rome; the offices of the League of Arab States, the International Organization for Migration and the UN refugee agency accredited to it are also in Rome. [cite:vt5_pv_relations2026] It listed no new relations in 2025. [cite:vt5_pv_relations2026] Older figures in circulation, "183" or "about 180", are out of date.

Recognition and its gaps

The 184 are not the same set as the 193 members of the United Nations. They include 181 UN members, the State of Palestine, the Republic of China on Taiwan and the Cook Islands, and they leave out twelve UN members. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] Among those twelve are the People's Republic of China, Saudi Arabia, Vietnam, Afghanistan and North Korea. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] Several of the absences are themselves acts of recognition policy. The Holy See keeps its relations with Taipei, not Beijing, while dealing with Beijing through the Provisional Agreement on the appointment of bishops, signed on 22 September 2018, renewed for two years in 2020 and 2022 and extended for four years on 22 October 2024. [cite:vt5_vn_china2024] [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] On 26 June 2015 it signed a Comprehensive Agreement with the State of Palestine, a step Part Eighteen examines. [cite:vt1_rv_palestine2015] [scene:vt18_state_of_palestine|Part Eighteen: the State of Palestine]

Recognition also came late from the two governments most often cast in popular accounts as the Holy See's partners. The United Kingdom established full relations on 16 January 1982 and the United States on 10 January 1984. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] Israel and the Holy See established full relations by a protocol signed on 14 June 1994, after the Fundamental Agreement of 30 December 1993 that is the subject of Part Seventeen. [cite:rs_cw2_holysee_israel_protocol] [scene:vt17_fundamental_agreement|Part Seventeen: the Fundamental Agreement] Oman, on 23 February 2023, was the most recent state to establish relations before the 2026 note. [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] Recognition has been extended state by state, for each state's own reasons, and remains incomplete.

The United Nations seat

The Holy See became a Permanent Observer State at the United Nations on 6 April 1964 and has chosen not to seek membership. [cite:vt5_hsm_58314] [cite:dv1_wiki_holysee_un] [scene:dv1_three_cities|The Divine 3+1: the three cities] Its rights were set out forty years later in General Assembly resolution 58/314, "Participation of the Holy See in the work of the United Nations," adopted without a vote on 1 July 2004. [cite:vt5_hsm_58314] [cite:vt5_va_migliore2004] The annex gave the Holy See the right to take part in the Assembly's general debate, the right of reply, the right to raise points of order on matters that concern it (without the right to challenge the chair's ruling), and the right to co-sponsor draft resolutions that refer to it, which go to a vote only if a member state asks. It "shall not have the right to vote or to put forward candidates." [cite:vt5_hsm_58314] It is a voice without a vote.

The challenge: "See Change"

That voice was contested in the years before 58/314. On 14 March 2000, at a press conference at UN Headquarters, Frances Kissling, president of Catholics for a Free Choice, and four other speakers presented the "See Change" campaign, which asked Secretary-General Kofi Annan to open a review of the Holy See's status as a non-member state permanent observer and to give it instead the standing of a non-governmental organisation, as the World Council of Churches has. [cite:vt5_un_seechange2000] Kissling said almost 400 organisations had endorsed it. One speaker, Anika Rahman, argued that the Holy See met only one of the four criteria of statehood, the capacity to enter into relations with other states; the others criticised its positions on contraception, condoms and abortion at UN conferences since the 1995 Beijing conference on women. [cite:vt5_un_seechange2000] [cite:vt5_rns_seechange]

The response was organised and immediate. The United States bishops' conference said Catholics for a Free Choice merited "no recognition or support," a former US ambassador to the Holy See, Ray Flynn, defended its diplomatic role, and counter-campaigns gathered endorsements of their own. [cite:vt5_rns_seechange] No member state proposed a review. Four years later the General Assembly confirmed and widened the seat by consensus. [cite:vt5_va_migliore2004] The contest shows where the Holy See's standing is weakest: on the Montevideo test of a permanent population, which the city's small resident population meets only thinly. [scene:dv1_three_cities|The Divine 3+1: the city's residents] It also shows that the test did not decide the outcome. Recognition practice did.

Sovereign immunity in foreign courts

Statehood has a practical consequence that matters most to people who have sued the Holy See: a foreign state cannot ordinarily be sued in another state's courts. In the United States that rule is set by the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, and two abuse cases tested how far it protects the Holy See. In O'Bryan v. Holy See, brought in Kentucky, the Sixth Circuit held on 24 November 2008 that the Holy See is a foreign state under the Act, and that its role as head of a religious body "cannot be separated from its position as a foreign state." [cite:vt5_jurist_obryan2008] [cite:vt5_findlaw_obryan] The court let claims proceed on the conduct of bishops and Holy See personnel in the United States who supervised abusive clergy, under the Act's exception for torts committed in the country, but barred claims based on the abuse itself and on acts of the Holy See abroad. [cite:vt5_findlaw_obryan] In August 2010 the plaintiffs' lawyer, William McMurry, dropped the case, citing the high bar of sovereign immunity and the lack of further plaintiffs; the Holy See's US counsel, Jeffrey Lena, said the outcome confirmed "there has never been a Holy See policy requiring concealment of child sexual abuse." [cite:vt5_ncr_obryan2010]

Doe v. Holy See, brought in Oregon over abuse in about 1965 by a Servite priest, Andrew Ronan, went further. On 3 March 2009 the Ninth Circuit allowed the claim against the Holy See to proceed, on Oregon's definition of employment, and on 28 June 2010 the Supreme Court declined to review that ruling; Lena said the refusal was "not a comment on the merits." [cite:vt5_wiki_doe] [cite:vt5_am_doe2010] Back in the district court, Judge Michael Mosman held on 20 August 2012 that the facts did not establish that Ronan was an employee of the Holy See, and dismissed the claim. [cite:vt5_jurist_doe2012] Neither case found the Holy See beyond the reach of US courts; both turned on where the acts took place and whether a priest is the pope's employee. What became of the abuse claims outside these suits is followed in Part Twelve. [scene:vt12_accountability|Part Twelve: accountability]

In Europe the same question reached the European Court of Human Rights. Twenty-four people who said they had been abused as children by Catholic priests in Belgium sued the Holy See, among others; the Ghent court of first instance declined jurisdiction over it in 2013 and the Ghent Court of Appeal upheld its immunity in February 2016. [cite:vt5_eapil_jc2021] In J.C. and Others v. Belgium, decided on 12 October 2021, the Strasbourg court found no violation of the right of access to a court. It held that the Belgian courts had not acted arbitrarily in treating the Holy See as having the characteristics of a foreign state, and accepted their reasoning that the bishops' conduct could not be attributed to the Holy See and that the failures alleged against it had taken place in Rome, not Belgium. [cite:vt5_eapil_jc2021] Immunity covers the Holy See itself, not local dioceses and bishops, which are separate legal persons under national law.

The Holy See is treated as a state because, for more than a century and through a period when it held no land, other states have treated it as one, and courts follow that practice; challenges on the statehood criteria and on its conduct have not changed it.
