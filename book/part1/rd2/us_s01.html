The First American Campaigns and the Fight Over the Money

In March 1921 the Zionist leader Chaim Weizmann invited Albert Einstein to accompany him to the United States. That spring the two spent two months touring the country, raising money for the Zionist Organization's new Palestine Foundation Fund, Keren Hayesod, and for the Hebrew University. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro] [cite:hnn_isaacson_einstein] American support for a Jewish state is often dated to the years after the Second World War, as a response to the Holocaust. The organized American campaign began much earlier. By 1921 the movement was running national fundraising tours, quarrelling over who would control the proceeds, and securing a meeting with the President.

The tour exposed a struggle for control inside American Zionism, one documented in the Einstein Papers. Weizmann wanted Keren Hayesod centralized under the Zionist Executive, the movement's leadership. The faction led by Supreme Court Justice Louis Brandeis and Judge Julian Mack wanted the American money kept under American control and managed on businesslike lines. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc168] [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro]

The conflict came to a head in 1921, at the Cleveland convention of the Zionist Organization of America. Weizmann's side won, and the Brandeis group withdrew from the leadership. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_intro]

A second delegation followed that November. Nahum Sokolow, Otto Warburg and Vladimir Jabotinsky arrived for a Keren Hayesod tour that raised nearly $5 million. On 13 January 1922 Sokolow met President Warren Harding for nearly an hour. [cite:israeled_sokolow_harding] Within a year of the first tour, the campaign had produced both substantial sums and access to the White House.

The record also preserves the limits of that success. Much of established American Jewry remained non-Zionist. Paul Warburg kept his distance from the cause. When Einstein invited about fifty non-Zionist notables to engage with it, few responded. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252] The campaign was raising money and winning political attention, but it did not yet speak for the whole community it drew on.

Seen from 1948, these years look like an early stage of a long effort. Seen from 1921, they show a movement that had already built the machinery of a national American campaign: celebrity tours, organized fundraising, a contest for control fought out at a national convention, and a meeting with the President, some twenty-seven years before statehood. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc252] The political side of that effort, an endorsement of the Balfour Declaration by Congress, followed in 1922, as described in [scene:us_s02|the section on the Lodge–Fish Resolution].
