Borrowed Prestige: Science and the Universities

The memo told Epstein to rebuild himself as a patron of science. He had begun long before, and the record shows that, after his conviction, science went on taking his money.

The universities

The two universities that later examined their ties reached very different findings. Harvard's review found about $9 million in Epstein gifts between 1998 and 2007, the largest a $6.5 million grant in 2003 to the mathematician Martin Nowak's Program for Evolutionary Dynamics, and nothing after the 2008 plea, though Epstein had been made a visiting fellow in 2005. [cite:dailymaverick_harvard_mit] [cite:the_harvard_mit_bacow] MIT's outside review found the reverse: ten donations totalling $850,000, nine of them after the conviction, including $525,000 to the Media Lab and $225,000 to the physicist Seth Lloyd. Three vice presidents learned of the gifts in 2013 and approved them, in the absence of any policy, on condition that they stayed anonymous; Epstein visited the campus nine times between 2013 and 2017. The Media Lab's director, Joi Ito, resigned, and Lloyd was placed on leave for failing to report gifts. [cite:axios_mit_lloyd] [cite:bostonglobe_mit_report] Harvard's money came before the conviction; MIT's came after.

The salon

Outside the universities, Epstein paid for a place in the scientific conversation itself. Tax filings show that foundations linked to him gave about $638,000 of the roughly $857,000 that John Brockman's Edge Foundation received between 2001 and 2017. The gifts stopped during his prosecution, resumed at $50,000 or more a year from 2009 to 2011, and ended with $30,000 in 2015. Epstein also funded Edge's one large award, a $100,000 prize to the physicist David Deutsch. Edge hosted the "billionaires' dinners" held during the TED conference, where scientists met the founders of the technology industry; in 2002, before the conviction, Steven Pinker, Richard Dawkins and Daniel Dennett were photographed on Epstein's plane flying to TED with Brockman. [cite:ep6_buzzfeed_edge]

The grants, and what the scientists said

Smaller grants reached many researchers, and their accounts of why they took the money differ. The strongest version of their side deserves stating first. Several said they did not know of the conviction, or of its nature: the University of Arizona, the University of British Columbia and others said they were unaware of Epstein's involvement in gifts made through other foundations. Others knew and judged it closed: a Columbia dental researcher who took about $100,000 in 2012 said he had understood that Epstein "subsequently paid his debt". Some apologised. The Harvard geneticist George Church, whose funding ended in 2007, apologised for his "poor awareness and judgment"; Seth Lloyd apologised and disclosed that he had visited Epstein in jail; the biologist Robert Trivers called his own earlier remark minimising the abuse "stupid and offensive". Others did not: the physicist Lawrence Krauss, who received at least $250,000, the last of it in April 2017, had publicly defended Epstein in 2011; a neuroscientist who took $25,000 for an online interview series in 2014 said he knew of the conviction and did not regret the money. Several institutions, among them the Santa Fe Institute and Mount Sinai, pledged to give matching sums to anti-trafficking work. [cite:ep6_buzzfeed_science]

The friendships went beyond grants. Released emails show the linguist Noam Chomsky in close contact with Epstein into at least 2017. An undated letter of recommendation, likely written in 2017 or later, calls Epstein "a highly valued friend" and credits him with arranging a meeting with Ehud Barak; it is not known whether it was sent. [cite:ep6_wbur_chomsky2025]

What the gifts bought was not research. It was the right to be photographed among serious people, which is exactly what the 2011 memo had prescribed.
