Accountability, Grapes of Wrath and Qana

Through the 1990s the war in the south ran on a pattern that both sides described openly. Hezbollah attacked Israeli and SLA positions in the zone. When Israeli or SLA fire killed civilians in Lebanese villages, Hezbollah fired Katyusha rockets into northern Israel. When the rockets hit Israeli towns, Israel struck far beyond the zone. Twice, in 1993 and 1996, that cycle produced a week or more of bombardment, and each time it ended in an unwritten or written set of rules about civilians. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns]

Operation Accountability, July 1993. On 25 July 1993 Israel began a week of air and artillery strikes across southern Lebanon. Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin told the Knesset on 28 July that Hezbollah, backed by Iran, had fired Katyushas at the zone and at Kiryat Shmona and Nahariya, that Syria had "turned a blind eye," and that 92 Israeli soldiers and civilians had been killed since the zone was created. He named the operation's goal as restoring "security to the lives of residents in communities of the north." [cite:hz4_jvl_rabin93] He also stated its method: Lebanese civilians would be moved north, away from villages where Hezbollah operated, so that, he said, the army could fight Hezbollah without harming them, and the Lebanese and Syrian governments would be pressured to restrain it; residents could return only "if fire at the northern communities ceases." [cite:hz4_jvl_rabin93] The commander of Israel's artillery corps said the aim was "to destroy the villages and the houses of the activists and the locations from which the rockets are fired." [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns]

Israel's artillery fired about 25,000 rounds; Hezbollah fired about 150 rockets into northern Israel. [cite:hz4_wiki_accountability] Human Rights Watch counted about 120 Lebanese civilians killed, close to 500 injured and some 300,000 Lebanese villagers and Palestinian refugees displaced; Hezbollah's rockets killed two Israeli civilians and injured 24. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] The fighting stopped on 31 July under a US-brokered understanding that was never written down: Hezbollah would not fire rockets into northern Israel, and Israel and the SLA would not attack civilians in Lebanon. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns]

The rule both sides stated. The rules broke down repeatedly. In 1995, Human Rights Watch recorded, Katyusha attacks killed two civilians and injured 31 in Israel. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] Hezbollah's deputy leader, Naim Qassem, set out its policy in April 1995: "whenever the Israeli enemy shells and harms civilians in our villages, we will shell northern Palestine." [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] Human Rights Watch's report on these years, titled Civilian Pawns, treated both the rocket fire on Israeli towns and Israel's shelling of Lebanese villages as violations of the laws of war, whatever reprisal each side claimed. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns]

Grapes of Wrath, April 1996. On 11 April 1996, after weeks of escalating attacks, Israel launched Operation Grapes of Wrath. By 15 April, warnings to leave had gone to 86 Lebanese communities. [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] An unnamed Israeli official told the Wall Street Journal that the policy was to force civilians out to pressure the Lebanese government: "let's just say we hope Lebanon understands the message." [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] The UN estimated that at least 400,000 people, about one in eight Lebanese, were displaced. The Lebanese army counted 154 civilians killed and 351 injured over the sixteen days. [cite:hz4_hrw_grapes] Hezbollah kept firing rockets at northern Israel; on 9 April alone, five Israelis were wounded and 38 treated for shock. [cite:hz4_hrw_grapes]

Qana, 18 April 1996. On the afternoon of 18 April, Israeli artillery shelled the compound of the UN peacekeeping force's Fijian battalion at Qana, where hundreds of villagers had taken shelter. The UN's investigator recorded "an estimated 100 persons" killed; Human Rights Watch counted 102 by 24 April; the figure most often given since is 106. [cite:hz4_un_qana] [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] [cite:hz4_army_qana]

The UN Secretary-General sent his military adviser, Major-General Franklin van Kappen of the Netherlands, to investigate. His report of 1 May 1996 found that Hezbollah fighters had fired mortars from about 220 metres from the centre of the compound, about 15 minutes before the shelling, and had fired rockets from nearby earlier that day. [cite:hz4_un_qana] It found that an Israeli drone had been over the area, contrary to earlier Israeli denials, and that almost all the proximity-fused shells, which burst in the air above their target, fell on the compound. It concluded that "it is unlikely that the shelling of the United Nations compound was the result of gross technical and/or procedural errors," while adding that this "cannot be ruled out completely." [cite:hz4_un_qana] An addendum recorded Israel's correction that a drone had been operating but was sent to Qana after the shelling ended. [cite:hz4_un_qana]

Israel's position, given to the UN, was that the deaths resulted from "a sequence of operational mistakes and technical failures, compounded by chance," and that it was "not Israeli policy to target civilians or the United Nations." [cite:hz4_un_qana] Prime Minister Shimon Peres said, "We did not know that several hundred people were concentrated in that camp," and that Hezbollah had "used the U.N. as a shield." [cite:hz4_hrw_pawns] A 2016 article on the US Army's website argued that the pattern of impacts fits fire-direction and map errors rather than intent. [cite:hz4_army_qana] Human Rights Watch did not find intent proven but called the barrage at minimum "a reckless and negligent attack," and disputed Israel's statement that it did not know civilians were sheltering there. It also noted that UNIFIL counted about 270 incidents of fire at or near UN positions in the operation, about 15 of them by Lebanese guerrillas and the rest by Israeli or SLA forces. [cite:hz4_hrw_grapes] Whether the shelling was deliberate is contested and the record does not settle it. That it was Israeli fire, that it followed Hezbollah fire from beside a crowded UN base, and that the UN found error "unlikely" are all documented. The UN General Assembly later requested that Israel pay for the damage, and draft resolutions put the sum at some $1.12 million; the UN Secretary-General reported that Israel had not responded. [cite:hz4_un_qanacost]

The April Understanding. The operation ended on 27 April 1996 with a written text brokered by the United States and France. Armed groups in Lebanon would not attack Israel with Katyushas or any other weapon; Israel "and those cooperating with it", meaning the SLA, would not fire at civilians or civilian targets in Lebanon; both sides committed that civilians would never be targeted and that populated areas would not be used as launching grounds; and nothing in it barred self-defence. [cite:hz4_jvl_april] A Monitoring Group of the United States, France, Syria, Lebanon and Israel would hear complaints filed within 24 hours. [cite:hz4_jvl_april] The text said nothing about attacks on soldiers. That silence left the fighting between Hezbollah and Israeli and SLA forces inside the zone outside its terms, and within that frame the fighting went on for four more years.
