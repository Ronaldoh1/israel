A State Without a Constitution: Basic Laws, the Law of Return and the Nation-State Law

The Declaration of Independence of 14 May 1948 promised that a constitution would be adopted by October 1948. It never was. The First Knesset deadlocked over what the document should say, above all over the place of religion in public life, and instead resolved to pass "Basic Laws" one at a time, each to become a chapter of an eventual constitution. [cite:gov_legalclarity] That decision, known as the Harari Resolution of 1950, is still the framework more than seventy-five years later. [cite:dv5_unpacked_torah] The way its Basic Laws work explains much of what happened to its politics in the 2020s.

What a Basic Law is. The Basic Laws cover the Knesset, the government, the presidency, the army, the judiciary, the state's lands, Jerusalem and, since 1992, a partial bill of rights in the Basic Law: Human Dignity and Liberty. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] The Supreme Court treats them as having constitutional weight. But with a few exceptions they can be passed or amended by a simple majority of the Knesset, unlike constitutions elsewhere that require a supermajority or a referendum to change. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] [cite:gov_legalclarity] There is, in other words, no constitutional floor that a governing coalition cannot lower. The question that follows, whether the court may strike down a Basic Law itself, became the centre of the crisis of 2023 described in a later section.

Who may belong. The state's most distinctive law is not a Basic Law at all. The Law of Return, passed on 5 July 1950, opens with one sentence: "Every Jew has the right to come to this country as an oleh," an immigrant entitled to citizenship. [cite:dv5_jvl_return] An amendment of 1970 extended the right to the child and grandchild of a Jew, to a Jew's spouse, and to the spouses of a Jew's child and grandchild, and defined a Jew for the law's purposes as a person born of a Jewish mother or converted to Judaism, and not a member of another religion. [cite:dv5_jvl_return] The effect is a citizenship open by right of descent to people living in other countries. Supporters see in the law the core purpose of the state, a refuge after the Holocaust; critics see in it the clearest legal statement that the state belongs to one people. Both descriptions are accurate accounts of what the text does.

The Nation-State Law. On 19 July 2018 the Knesset passed, by 62 votes to 55 with two abstentions, the Basic Law: Israel, the Nation-State of the Jewish People. Its eleven articles state that "the exercise of the right to national self-determination in the State of Israel is unique to the Jewish people"; make Hebrew the state's language while giving Arabic "a special status"; declare the state open to Jewish immigration; and call "the development of Jewish settlement" a "national value" the state will encourage. [cite:asianews_nsl_passed] [cite:jta_nsl_upheld] It contains no clause on equality. On 8 July 2021 the Supreme Court rejected petitions against it by ten votes to one, holding that it does not negate the state's democratic character; the one dissent came from Justice George Karra, the court's only Arab member. [cite:jta_nsl_upheld] [cite:gktoday_nsl_upheld] Supporters say it codifies what the state has always been; critics, among them Arab rights groups and some Jewish organizations, say it lowers the standing of Arab citizens, about a fifth of the population. [cite:christiancentury_nsl] [cite:jta_nsl_upheld] In a state with no constitution, it is the nearest thing to a clause defining whom the state is for, and it names one people.

This volume's earlier parts insisted on a distinction that the Nation-State Law makes harder to keep in view. Zionism is a political movement; Judaism is a religion and a people's heritage; many Jews, in Israel and elsewhere, have opposed the movement or criticized the state. The law speaks of "the Jewish people," but it was passed by a parliamentary majority of 62, opposed by 55 members, and criticized by Jewish as well as Arab organizations. [cite:asianews_nsl_passed] [cite:christiancentury_nsl] It is the act of a governing coalition, and should be read as one.

Religion in the law, 2026. The pressure that blocked a constitution in 1950 has not gone away. In July 2026 the Knesset passed a Basic Law: Torah Study, declaring that "Torah study is a fundamental value in the heritage of the Jewish people and the State of Israel"; petitions against it are before the High Court. The next day it passed a law freezing the arrest and prosecution of ultra-Orthodox men who had not reported for military service until 30 November 2026, and the High Court suspended that law by interim order. [cite:dv5_unpacked_torah] The fight over who serves in the army, and on what religious terms, is the same fight that kept the First Knesset from writing a constitution.

Why it matters for the volume. Each of the three cities in Parts Two to Four is unusual because of its relationship to the state around it. Israel is unusual in the relationship between its state and its constitution. A simple majority can change its fundamental law; its citizenship law reaches far beyond its borders by descent; and its one statement of national purpose is, by its own words, the purpose of one people. Those features are the background to everything in the rest of this part: how its governments are formed, how they govern the territories, and how they deal with the rules and the guardian outside.
