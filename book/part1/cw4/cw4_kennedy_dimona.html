Kennedy's First Months and the First Visit to Dimona, 1961

Eleven days before Ben-Gurion resigned, John F. Kennedy took office, on 20 January 1961, with the Dimona question already open on his desk. The outgoing administration had told Congress on 19 January that Israel promised to return all of the reactor's plutonium to France, and Ben-Gurion had refused international inspection, as told in [scene:cw4_dimona_december|the disclosure month]. [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] Between Kennedy's inauguration and the end of May 1961 the new administration settled on a course: it accepted Israel's assurances provisionally, asked for an American visit to the site in place of international inspection, waited four months for it, and received a report that the reactor was what Israel said it was. Whether that report was right is one of the sharpest disputes in the history of the program.

Rusk's memorandum, 30 January 1961. Ten days into the new administration, Secretary of State Dean Rusk sent Kennedy a memorandum titled "Israel's Atomic Energy Activities." Ben-Gurion, Rusk wrote, had given "categoric assurances supported by appropriate public statements" that "Israel does not have plans for developing nuclear weaponry." The Israeli and French assurances "appear to be satisfactory." But the Department regarded the matter as "not a single episode but a continuing subject," requiring "a continuing watch." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d5] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_rusk] The United States, Rusk added, was encouraging Israel to permit "a qualified scientist from the United States or other friendly power to visit the Dimona installation." He recalled that in 1955, under Atoms for Peace, the United States had undertaken to help Israel's atomic energy program. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_rusk] [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d5]

The memorandum set the terms of the whole year. Its first half accepted Israel's word; its second half asked to see for itself. The reference to 1955 can be read as a reminder that American help to Israel's atomic program under Atoms for Peace was a matter of record, giving Washington a standing to ask.

"On a secret basis," 31 January. The next day Kennedy met Ogden Reid, who had been ambassador in Israel through the disclosure in December. Reid told him an inspection could be arranged "if it is done on a secret basis," and that "we can accept at face value Ben Gurion's assurances that the reactor is to be devoted to peaceful purposes." [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] Reid's advice combined trust with a condition: a visit, kept secret. The condition echoed the December episode, when Ben-Gurion had asked Reid, "Why in [the] States is everything being told [to] everybody?" [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

Waiting, January to April. The invitation did not come quickly. On 28 March 1961 Assistant Secretary of State G. Lewis Jones told Ambassador Avraham Harman that the United States had been "faithfully waiting since early January" for the invitation Israel had promised. Harman suggested that nothing be done before Passover. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] On 12 April, according to the National Security Archive, Harman proposed a meeting between Kennedy and Ben-Gurion; the archive's editors read the Dimona visit as its tacit condition. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] The linkage, if that is what it was, made sense from both sides. Israel sought a meeting with the new President; Washington sought a visit to the reactor; on the archive's reading, neither was offered without the other.

The invitation and its terms, April 1961. In April Harman delivered "an invitation for an American to visit the Dimona reactor site during the week of May 15." He asked that the visit be kept secret, because publicity "could have a most undesirable effect," and he described it as the discharge of Ben-Gurion's specific promise. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d31] Two American officials raised a difficulty. Philip Farley warned that labelling the visit secret "might be counter-productive," and Jones noted "a good deal of Congressional interest." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d31] Secrecy protected Israel from publicity; Jones's note about Congress shows why it was awkward for Washington, which had a domestic audience waiting for an answer.

Signals before the visit. Two Israeli signals in these weeks point in different directions. On 16 May, according to the National Security Archive, the Israeli diplomat Mordechai Gazit hinted to Farley that Israel might be interested in a nuclear option for deterrence. The archive's editors read the remark as a possible "trial balloon." [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] It was a hint, recorded by the American side, and its meaning is an inference by the editors. And Foreign Minister Golda Meir reportedly objected to the visit itself, "concerned about the implications of misleading the American scientists." [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] That report, if accurate, is the more striking. It does not come from a document Meir wrote, and it should be read as reported; but it describes an Israeli minister worried before the visit that the visitors would be misled.

The visit, May 1961. The two visitors were scientists of the Atomic Energy Commission, Ulysses M. Staebler and Jesse W. Croach. Even the dates of their visit are given three ways. The editors of the State Department's documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States, say that the visit, accepted on 28 April for 15 May, was moved to 18 May. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d31] The National Security Archive says that the two men arrived on 17 May and visited Dimona on 20 May, escorted by Ephraim Katzir and Manes Pratt, the officer who had directed the reactor's construction. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] The Wilson Center's archive summary says they toured Israel's nuclear sites from 17 to 22 May 1961. [cite:rs_cw4_wda_123836] The three accounts can be partly reconciled, since a tour of several sites from 17 to 22 May would contain a day at Dimona, but the sources used here do not settle whether that day was 18 or 20 May.

What the scientists reported. Staebler and Croach briefed the State Department on 25 May. They said they had been received "with cordiality" and that "nothing was concealed from them." The complex occupied a square 750 metres on each side. The reactor might cost about $15 million, with about $20 million more for supporting plant. The Israelis told them that ground had been broken in 1959 and that the reactor would "not be completed before 1964." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d53] Their conclusions were measured. "There is no present evidence that the Israelis have weapon production in mind." The reactor would eventually produce small quantities of plutonium suitable for weapons, as other reactors of its size did. Israel's secrecy was "regrettable, but perhaps understandable." And there was "strong evidence of close French scientific collaboration and support." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d53] The report that Executive Secretary Battle sent to Bundy at the White House on 26 May said the reactor was "of the scope and peaceful character previously described to the United States." [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d53] [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] [cite:rs_cw4_wda_123836]

The figures can be set against those of December. The scientists' estimate, about $35 million for reactor and plant together, was above the embassy's foreign-exchange figure of $17.8 million and below Herter's $40–80 million and the $100–200 million Eisenhower had cited. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d53] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510] The date Israel gave them for the groundbreaking, 1959, also differs from the Nuclear Weapon Archive's early 1958. [cite:rs_cw4_nwa_isrhist] And the report said nothing of the "plutonium separation plant" that the Joint Atomic Energy Intelligence Committee had described on 2 December 1960, or of the "chemical separation plant" McCone had named at the White House. [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2024] [cite:rs_cw4_ebb510]

Did they see what was there? The dispute over this visit has two positions, and each deserves its full weight. The first is the scientists' own. Staebler and Croach were technical officers of the Atomic Energy Commission, sent to look at a reactor; they reported that nothing was concealed from them and that there was "no present evidence" of weapons intent. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61_d53] [cite:rs_cw4_wda_123836] Their wording was careful. "No present evidence" is a statement about what they saw, not a guarantee about the future, and they noted that the reactor would in time produce plutonium suitable for weapons. Read narrowly, their report may have been accurate about the reactor building they were shown.

The second position is Avner Cohen and William Burr's. Writing in 2025, they argue that the account Pratt gave the visitors, that Dimona would give "experience in the construction of a nuclear facility which would prepare (Israel) for nuclear power in the long run," was false. They write that Dimona's personnel spent weeks preparing before American teams arrived, and that Israel concealed the underground separation plant. They attribute the finding of deception to a State Department intelligence memorandum of 1967 and to later reporting. [cite:rs_cw4_cohen_burr_2025] Meir's reported concern about "misleading the American scientists" fits their case, as does the 2 December 1960 report of a separation plant that the visitors did not mention. [cite:rs_cw4_nsa_first] [cite:rs_cw4_cns_2024] The strength of this position is that it rests on later American government findings and on the Israeli side's own reported worries; its limit, in the sources used here, is that the 1967 memorandum itself is known through Cohen and Burr's account of it, not reproduced.

The two positions are not strictly opposed. The scientists could have reported truthfully what they were shown, and what they were shown could have been arranged so that the decisive facility was not among it. That is in substance what Cohen and Burr argue. Whether it was so in May 1961 specifically, as distinct from the later visits of the 1960s, depends on evidence that the documents of 1961 do not contain.

The record leaves several questions open. It does not show what Kennedy himself made of the report in the days after 26 May. It does not settle the date of the visit, or the year of the groundbreaking. It records Gazit's hint and Meir's objection only as the American side and later historians report them. And it does not show, from documents of 1961, whether the separation plant then existed in a form that two visitors could have been kept from seeing. What it does show is that by the end of May 1961 the Kennedy administration had its visit and a reassuring report, and that both were in hand when Kennedy met Ben-Gurion in New York four days later, as told in [scene:cw4_waldorf_1961|the Waldorf meeting]. The confrontation over regular inspections came two years later, in [scene:s11b|Kennedy and Dimona, 1963].
