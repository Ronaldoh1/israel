Independence and the Nakba, 1948

On Friday 14 May 1948, at four in the afternoon, David Ben-Gurion stood in a hall of the Tel Aviv Museum and read out the Declaration of the Establishment of the State of Israel. The British Mandate was due to expire at midnight, a few hours later. [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal] [cite:morasha_czech_arms] The document itself notes the day: it was signed "on this Sabbath eve." Its dating is not quite consistent. The signing line reads "5th day of Iyar, 5708 – 14th May 1948," while the body of the text, referring to the end of the Mandate, gives "6th Iyar, 5708 (15th May, 1948)." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] The discrepancy stands in the text as it is reproduced.

Ben-Gurion, born in 1886 and dead in 1973, was a Polish-born organizer of the labour Zionist movement. He became the new state's first Prime Minister and the most consequential single figure in its creation. [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal] Palestinians call what followed the Nakba, Arabic for "catastrophe." [cite:morris_1948] The founding of the state and the displacement of much of the Arab population from the territory it came to hold took place in the same months, and this section follows both. Along the way it tests two claims that have long been taught about 1948. The first is that the Palestinians left of their own accord, on instructions broadcast by Arab radio. The second is that America created and armed Israel. Both claims rest on something real, and the record contradicts both.

The Declaration began with its grounds. It based the state on "our natural and historic right," on the Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917, on the Mandate of the League of Nations, and on the General Assembly resolution of 29 November 1947. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] The order matters. The first ground was a claim of right that did not depend on any outside power. The other three were international instruments: a British pledge, a League mandate and a United Nations vote. Taken together, the text claimed the state both as an inheritance and as an act of international law.

The document also made promises. It pledged equality of social and political rights to all inhabitants "irrespective of religion, race or sex." It appealed to the Arab inhabitants of the state "to preserve peace" and to take part in building it on the basis of "full and equal citizenship." To the neighbouring states it extended "an offer of peace and good neighbourliness." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] These clauses are the strongest written form of the founders' case. They promised a state for the Jewish people in which its Arab citizens would be full citizens, and they offered peace to its neighbours. Whether and how the first promise was kept is a question for the later volumes of this library, because the record of the Arabs who stayed inside Israel begins after 1948. The answer to the second came within hours: armies from Lebanon, Syria, Iraq and Egypt invaded, and Transjordanian forces entered the areas designated for the Arab state and Jerusalem. [cite:rs_rd5_oh_war_b] That war is the subject of a later section of this chapter.

Two further features of the text are often missed. The Declaration stated no borders. It referred to Eretz-Israel, the Land of Israel, and it pledged steps toward "the economic union of the whole of Eretz-Israel." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] Economic union between the two states was what the UNSCOP majority had recommended in 1947. [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] And it set a deadline for its own replacement. The People's Council became the Provisional Council of State, and a constitution was to be adopted by an elected Constituent Assembly "not later than the 1st October 1948." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration]

That deadline passed. When the First Knesset met, it deadlocked over what a constitution should contain, above all over the place of religion in public life. Instead it resolved to enact Basic Laws one at a time. [cite:gov_legalclarity] The first came in 1958. There are now fourteen, covering the Knesset, the government, the military, the judiciary and civil rights, and the Supreme Court treats them as having constitutional weight, though a simple Knesset majority can amend them. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] The constitution promised in May 1948 has never been adopted. The consequences of that gap belong to a later period and are traced in [scene:ilgov_s01|No Constitution, Only Basic Laws].

The structures of the new state appeared quickly, because most of them already existed. The institutions the Jewish Agency had built under the Mandate became, almost overnight, the institutions of a sovereign government. The Haganah, the pre-state militia, became the Israel Defense Forces; a state intelligence service, the Mossad, was established in 1949. The United States extended de facto recognition within eleven minutes, a timing arranged by Clark Clifford over the State Department's objection. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] Recognition is examined in the next section. What matters here is a distinction the familiar account often blurs. Recognition was a political act. Weapons were another matter.

The United States imposed an arms embargo on all parties to the 1948 war. The Truman administration recognized Israel within minutes, but it did not lift the embargo. [cite:morasha_czech_arms] The weapons that reached the new army in the decisive months came instead from communist Czechoslovakia. By this account they were former German arms: Mauser rifles, Messerschmitt Bf 109G fighters and ZK 383 submachine guns, flown in by C-54 transports from Žatec airfield in an airlift code-named Operation Balak. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] [cite:wiki_czech_arms_shipments] Israeli military historians have documented the operation, and so has Zeev Sternhell in his work on the founding. Ben-Gurion's verdict, quoted from his memoir, was that "Czech arms saved the State of Israel." [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal]

The detailed record of the pipeline, set out in [scene:cz_s01|the Czechoslovak arms section], adds some precision. It describes the fighters as Avia S-199s, a Czech-built derivative of the Messerschmitt Bf 109, of which about 25 were delivered. It dates the first contract to 14 January 1948, before the Security Council's arms embargo of 17 April (Resolution 46). It records about 35,000 German-pattern Karabiner 98k rifles sent by sea, and a 1952 report by the Czechoslovak Foreign Minister, Viliam Široký, that put receipts at almost $14.5 million. [cite:wiki_czech_arms_israel] [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] It gives Ben-Gurion's words in their 1968 form: "Czechoslovak arms saved the State of Israel, really and absolutely." [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] It also records that Britain supplied aircraft and training to the Arab air forces. That sits awkwardly with the shorter statement that the United States, Britain and the United Nations all embargoed. Both versions are given here. Nor was Czechoslovakia the only source. The clandestine American procurement network, and the money Golda Meyerson raised in the United States early in 1948, are described in earlier sections of this chapter.

Czechoslovakia did not act alone. It lay inside the Soviet sphere, and the sales went ahead with Moscow's permission. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] The motive usually given is the same one read into the Soviet vote for partition in November 1947: anything that hastened the end of the British Mandate weakened Britain's position in the eastern Mediterranean. [cite:wiki_operation_balak] On that reading, Soviet interest and the new state's survival ran in the same direction for a few years and then parted. Two conclusions are sometimes drawn from this episode, and both go too far. The first is that the state later regarded as America's closest ally was armed at birth by the Soviet bloc while Washington held back. That is accurate for 1948, but it was a dated episode, and Soviet-Israeli relations changed markedly in the years that followed. The second is that Israel was a Western implant. The arms record is the strongest evidence against that view: at the decisive moment, the Western power most often credited with Israel's birth was withholding weapons from it.

The same months produced the Nakba. The figures given in the main account in this book's sources are between 700,000 and 750,000 Palestinians displaced, with about 500 villages depopulated or destroyed. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] Benny Morris estimates 700,000. Walid Khalidi gives 750,000, and Rashid Khalidi accepts the higher figure. Walid Khalidi's All That Remains (1992) documents the villages one by one. [cite:walid_khalidi_all_that_remains] Much of the sustained documentation comes from Israeli historians writing from inside the country's own archives: Morris's 1948: A History of the First Arab-Israeli War, Ilan Pappé's The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine, Tom Segev's 1949: The First Israelis and Avi Shlaim's The Iron Wall. [cite:morris_1948] [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing]

One account in this library calls the 750,000 figure undisputed by any credible historian. The wider record does not bear that out. The UN Conciliation Commission's technical estimate of 1949 was about 711,000; a later summary gives the same commission's figure as 726,000. Other figures run from a past Israeli government estimate "as low as 520,000" up to 850,000 from Palestinian researchers. UNRWA's rolls of 1950 held 957,000, but the commission had already warned that relief rolls were inflated by duplicate ration cards and by people who were not refugees. [cite:rs_rd5_unccp_1949] [cite:rs_rd5_prrn_1996] Counts of depopulated villages also vary with method, from 369 in Morris's 1987 count to 418 in Khalidi's and 531 localities in the Palestine Land Society's list, which includes towns and tribal lands. [cite:rs_rd5_plands_villages] There is no dispute that the displacement happened, or about its rough scale. The exact numbers are disputed, and [scene:rd5_refugees|the section on the refugees] sets out each estimate and its method.

The territorial figures show what the war decided. By 1948, land in Jewish ownership, bought parcel by parcel over decades, amounted to roughly seven percent of Mandate Palestine. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] The same account elsewhere puts legally purchased land at 9.8 percent, so the sources disagree on the exact figure, but both are under a tenth. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] Resolution 181 had allotted about 55 percent of the country to the Jewish state (56 percent in other sources used in this chapter). By the 1949 armistice, about 78 percent of Mandate Palestine was under Israeli control. [cite:morris_1948] Purchase accounts for the first figure. The difference between it and the last was made, in this account, by the war and by the displacement this section describes. The familiar phrase about making the desert bloom on land that was bought and settled describes the first figure. The rest has a different history.

How the departure happened is the core of the dispute. Plan Dalet, or Plan D, is the central document in it. The Haganah finalized it on 10 March 1948; according to the account in this book's research it was later released from the Israeli archives in the 1980s. Walid Khalidi published its text as an appendix to his article "Plan Dalet: Master Plan for the Conquest of Palestine," republished in the Journal of Palestine Studies in 1988. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The plan states its purpose plainly: "The objective of this plan is to gain control of the areas of the Hebrew state and defend its borders." That aim included "areas of Jewish settlement and concentration which are located outside the borders." Section 4(b) provides for "Destruction of villages (setting fire to, blowing up, and planting mines in the debris)" and states that, where resistance is met, "the population must be expelled outside the borders of the state." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] One account in this book's sources summarizes the plan as authorizing three things. The first was securing the areas assigned to the Jewish state by expelling villages that resisted. The second was taking Arab-majority cities in those areas. The third was the use of a stated vocabulary of destruction (haricha) and expulsion (gerush). [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] The plan, its predecessors and the operations carried out under it are set out in detail in the section on Plan Dalet.

What the plan meant is where historians divide. Walid Khalidi argues that it created "the military fait accompli upon which the state of Israel was to be based," and that the exodus was its product. He faults Morris for treating the exodus in "an historical vacuum." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Pappé argues in The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine (2006) that Plan Dalet meets the UN definition of ethnic cleansing. [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] Morris, who stands politically to Pappé's right and does not use that term, documents many of the same operations but reads the plan differently: as a military plan to secure territory and lines of communication before the Arab states' invasion, whose village provisions gave local commanders discretion, used in some places and not others, rather than a central order to expel. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] In Zachary Lockman's summary of his wider findings, Morris concludes that there was no explicit, general Israeli decision to expel. He finds that the flight up to June 1948 came mainly from direct military action, and that deliberate expulsions from June onward helped create about 300,000 further refugees. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] Shlaim writes that Morris rejected both the "Arab order" and the "Jewish robber-state" explanations and treated the refugee problem as "a by-product of the war." Shlaim himself holds that the evidence "suggests a far higher degree of Israeli responsibility." [cite:rs_rd5_shlaim_hnn_ref]

One summary in this book's sources holds that "the debate is over the term, not the facts," and that the Haganah carried out a plan to secure and expel which produced the outcome of 750,000 displaced. [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] That is one reading. The fuller record shows the disagreement reaching further, into the facts of intent. Was there a central decision to remove the Arab population, or a military plan whose village clause left expulsion to local commanders and the course of battle? The documents cited on each side are real. The disagreement is over what they show.

The record names particular episodes. On 9 April 1948, Irgun and Lehi forces, the two right-wing militias later folded into the Israel Defense Forces, attacked the village of Deir Yassin west of Jerusalem. [cite:morris_1948] Current scholarship puts the toll at about 110, generally between 107 and 120, many of them women, children and elderly people; one account in this book's sources gives about 107. The figures given at the time and afterwards were higher. For decades the toll was cited as 254. The letter of December 1948 signed by Albert Einstein, Hannah Arendt and others gave 240. [cite:rs_rd5_einstein_nyt] A Red Cross representative reported seeing some 150 bodies in a cave. The section on the massacres of 1948 gives the fuller picture. Haganah and Palmach forces supported the attack, and Israeli military archives on it remain partly classified. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] [cite:aljazeera_deir_yassin_75] According to Yehuda Slutsky's official history of the Haganah, the attackers announced the killings by radio to nearby villages in order to spread panic, and in the following weeks many families in those villages fled. Ben-Gurion later wrote in his diary that Deir Yassin "had a decisive effect" on Arab flight. [cite:morris_1948] The account in this book's sources argues that the panic was the purpose, not a by-product: terror used as an instrument of removal. That is an inference from Slutsky's record and from the flight that followed, and it is presented here as such. Four days after Deir Yassin, on 13 April, Arab forces attacked a convoy to the Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus and killed 78 Jews, most of them doctors and nurses, in reprisal. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The two killings belong in the same account.

The same account argues that the mechanism did not depend on any one militia, and points to Lydda and Ramla in July 1948. There, it says, about 50,000 civilians were expelled on written orders signed by Yitzhak Rabin, with no Irgun or Lehi involvement. [cite:morris_1948] It calls the force that carried this out the Haganah. By July, however, the Haganah had been merged into the Israel Defense Forces under the provisional government's order of 26 May. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] The research on the war gives a wider range of numbers. The two main accounts in the research on the war give competing figures, and neither settles the other. Lockman, summarizing Morris, puts the two towns' combined population at 50,000 to 70,000, about 15,000 of them refugees already, and the dead at 250 to 400 Palestinian civilians and four Israeli soldiers. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war] Palquest estimates about 70,000 driven out on 13 July, between 80 and 176 people killed at the Dahmash mosque and more than 400 in Lydda, and close to 1,000 dead in the towns and on the march that followed. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] Citing Morris, both sources report that on 12 July Ben-Gurion answered a question about the inhabitants with the words "Expel them," and that Rabin then signed the expulsion order. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda] [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_war] The operation is told in full in the section on the Arab states' war.

Ben-Gurion appears on both sides of this record. He led a community that included Holocaust survivors and refugees from the Middle East to statehood against invading armies. He also held executive authority over a founding that included the documented expulsions. The account in this book's sources calls him the architect of the new state's territorial and demographic policy, and treats his own diaries and directives as part of the evidence for the expulsions. [cite:hasbara_czech_arms_deal] That account also admits that it does not trace every decision he made about them. Whether his authority amounted to a general policy of expulsion is the point on which Morris and his critics differ. Neither the heroic version of his career nor the purely damning one can be kept without leaving out half of what the record shows.

The first of the two taught claims was put to a direct test in 1961. For years after the war, it was widely said that Arab governments had broadcast instructions telling Palestinians to leave their homes for a short time, with the promise that they could return once the Arab armies had won. If true, the claim placed the cause of the refugee problem with Arab leaders, not with events on the ground. The Irish journalist Erskine Childers went to the monitoring records. The BBC Monitoring Service had recorded and transcribed Middle Eastern radio throughout the period for British intelligence, and American monitors had done the same. These records were made at the time, independently of any later political narrative. Childers searched them for the evacuation orders and published his findings in The Spectator: [cite:spectator_childers_1961]

There was not a single order, or appeal, or suggestion about evacuation from Palestine from any Arab radio station, inside or outside Palestine, in 1948. There is a repeated monitored record of Arab appeals, even flat orders, to the civilians of Palestine to stay put.

He challenged anyone to produce the broadcasts, and no one did. [cite:decolonizepalestine_childers] Walid Khalidi, citing the BBC monitoring and its American counterpart, the FBIS, called the evacuation-order claim "a piece of mythmaking." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] One summary in this book's sources says that Morris, working in the archives in the 1980s, found no evidence of the broadcasts in Israeli, Arab or British records either. [cite:decolonizepalestine_childers]

The Israeli archival record Morris used adds one detail. In Lockman's summary, an IDF intelligence report of June 1948 estimated that about 240,000 Arabs had fled the areas of the Jewish state and about 150,000 other areas by 1 June. It attributed about 70 percent of that flight to direct Jewish military operations, 2 percent to psychological warfare and 2 percent to expulsion orders, which Morris considers an underestimate. It attributed no more than 5 percent to evacuation orders from Arab leaders. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] So some local evacuation orders were given; the Israeli army's own estimate at the time counted them as a small cause, and they were not the blanket radio campaign of the later story. Lockman also writes that Yosef Weitz's report of October 1948 falsely blamed Arab leaders for organizing the flight. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref] Childers's finding is narrow, and its narrowness is its strength. It does not explain every departure, village by village; the record shows many different circumstances of flight and expulsion. What it settles is that a single blanket explanation, repeated for years, was not in the broadcast record when someone checked.

The account of 1948 in this book's sources closes with a moral argument, stated here as its authors' own. Jewish need for safety after the Holocaust was real. The Zionist claim that Europe's Jews needed a place of refuge after 1945 was not false. That need was met in a land whose Arab inhabitants bore no responsibility for the crimes committed in Europe, and the cost of meeting it fell on them. On this view, the founding was at once the rescue of one people and the dispossession of another. Neither half cancels the other, and an account that drops either one is incomplete.

The same sources set a second contrast beside this one, again as an interpretation, not a finding. Jewish communities had lived among Arab populations across the Muslim world for more than a millennium: in Andalusia's Golden Age, under the Ottoman millet system, in Baghdad, with a community of 140,000, and in Damascus, Cairo, Sana'a and Fez. That life was not a utopia, and pogroms occurred. [cite:morris_righteous_victims] The claim that this coexistence was "substantial" and of a kind "Europe never matched" is a reading of a long and varied history, and is offered as one. The exodus of roughly 850,000 Jews from Arab lands between 1948 and 1972 had several documented causes, including active recruitment by Israeli and Jewish Agency operations. [cite:morris_righteous_victims] The violence against Jews in Arab cities after the partition vote, which was among those causes, is set out in its own section of this chapter.

A smaller decision closed the founding year. On 28 October 1948 the Provisional Council adopted the state flag. It chose the design of the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897, a Magen David between blue stripes, and placed the ancient Menorah on the state seal. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] [cite:wjc_flag_unveiled_1948] The date and the choice are documented. One symbolic reading holds that the choice shows which lineage the new state saw itself as continuing: the political project of 1897 rather than the ancient Temple. That is an interpretation, not a finding. Placing the Menorah on the seal did not mean it was disregarded. It shows only the order of precedence the Council set between two available symbols.

The refugee question did not end in 1948. On 11 December 1948 the General Assembly's Resolution 194 affirmed a right of return for the refugees. It has not been implemented as of 2026. Israel has refused it on the ground that a return would end the Jewish majority in the state. [cite:morris_1948] According to the same account, the Palestinians registered with UNRWA as descendants of the displaced numbered some 6 million as of 2026, most of them stateless. Resolution 194 and the negotiations that followed it belong to the next stage of this volume.

The account of the Nakba in this book's sources goes further, and its argument should be stated in full and labelled for what it is. It holds that the Nakba was not a single event but a structure that has continued for 78 years. It lists, as of 2026, what it treats as later expressions of that structure. Among them are the occupation of the West Bank since 1967 and the blockade of Gaza since 2007, with some 2 million people in 365 square kilometres. West Bank settlers numbered 110,000 in 1993 and more than 500,000 in 2025, plus 250,000 in East Jerusalem, according to Peace Now and Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics. The list includes the war in Gaza since October 2023, South Africa's genocide case at the International Court of Justice, filed on 29 December 2023, and the International Criminal Court's arrest warrants for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant, issued on 21 November 2024. It also cites the conclusions of Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and B'Tselem that the two legal systems in the West Bank meet the international-law definition of apartheid, and Israel's unilateral annexation of East Jerusalem in 1980 under Basic Law: Jerusalem. Those conclusions are the organizations' own legal judgements and are attributed to them here. The account then traces the effects across the region. In Jordan, more than 2 million Palestinians hold citizenship, about 40 percent of the population. Lebanon hosts about 500,000 refugees in 12 UNRWA camps, barred from most professions and from owning property. Syria hosts about 500,000, many displaced again since 2011. Egypt administered Gaza from 1948 to 1967. In Egypt's 1979 treaty, Jordan's of 1994 and the Abraham Accords of 2020 with the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco and Sudan, Arab states normalized relations with Israel without resolving the Palestinian question. The book's sources do not attach citations to these present-day figures. They are reported here as that account gives them, with the date, and they are examined in the volumes that cover those years. The opposite reading treats each of these episodes as a separate event with its own causes. This volume does not settle between the two. It records that the refugee problem created in 1948 was not resolved in 1948, and that every later chapter of this history inherits it.

The year-by-year ledger for this stage sets 1948 beside the two years before it. In 1946 and 1947 the entries are the Anglo-American Committee, the King David Hotel bombing, UNSCOP, the CIA's estimate ORE-55 and Resolution 181, a sequence of pressure, inquiry and warnings overruled. The entry for 1948 lists Deir Yassin, Plan Dalet, independence, the eleven-minute recognition, the Czech arms and the Nakba under a single heading: execution. Its money column holds two sums. The first is about $50 million reported raised on Golda Meyerson's American tour, spent largely on weapons. The second is almost $14.5 million in Czechoslovak arms receipts. [cite:jwa_golda_50m] [cite:jpost_czech_arms_saved_israel] The objectors' column for the year names the letter of December 1948, signed by 28 people including Albert Einstein and Hannah Arendt, which protested Menachem Begin's visit to the United States and described his new party, which had grown out of the Irgun, as bearing "the unmistakable stamp of a Fascist party." [cite:rs_rd5_einstein_nyt] The two sums show the connection the ledger draws. Money raised in America paid in part for weapons sold by the Soviet bloc, under an American embargo.

Much remains unsettled. The number of refugees is known only within a range, and the count of villages depends on what is counted. Whether the expulsions followed a central decision or accumulated from local orders under a plan that allowed them is still the main point of disagreement between Morris and his critics, and the documents do not close it. The death toll at Deir Yassin is now put at about 110, but part of the Israeli military record remains classified. The tolls at Lydda and Ramla vary by source. This book has not yet compiled the Arab military and civilian dead of the war. The radio claim is settled in its broad form and not in every local detail: the Israeli army's own estimate allowed a small share for Arab evacuation orders. On the arms, the scale of Czechoslovak supply is clear, but the sources differ on the aircraft, on Britain's role and on whether any other supplier mattered. What is not in doubt is the pairing at the centre of this section: in the same months, one people gained a state and hundreds of thousands of another were displaced from their towns and villages. Both facts are documented, and much of the documentation is Israeli.
