What the System Teaches About Israel and the Church

Dispensationalism is often described by its most dramatic doctrine, the rapture, but its core is a rule for reading the Bible. This section sets that rule out in plain terms, using Darby's own lectures and the account of Timothy Weber, a historian of American premillennialism, and then tests two claims that circulate about it: that Darby taught the Jews must return to their land before Christ could come back, and that his system made its followers political Zionists from the start.

Two peoples, two plans

A "dispensation," in this usage, is a period in which God deals with humanity on particular terms. Dividing history into such periods was not new; the dispensationalist theologian Charles Ryrie argued that the scheme Scofield later printed followed the eighteenth-century hymn-writer Isaac Watts's division of the ages rather than Darby's. [cite:ts3_wiki_scofield] What was distinctive in Darby's system was the claim, in Weber's summary, that "God had two completely different plans operating in history": one for an earthly people, Israel, whose promises concern a land, a throne and a nation; and one for a heavenly people, the church, whose hope lies outside this world altogether. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]

Darby stated the distinction directly in eleven lectures he gave in Geneva in 1840, published under the title The Hopes of the Church of God in connection with the destiny of the Jews and the nations as revealed in prophecy. "The church is in relationship with the Father, and the Jews with Jehovah," he said; the church's calling was "participation in a coming heavenly glory," and believers should learn to separate the prophecies about Israel "from those which refer to us Gentiles." [cite:ts3_darby_hopes1] In the tenth lecture he put the consequence for the reading of the Old Testament prophets plainly: "The gospel does not occupy itself with the earthly blessings of the Jews, which is the matter of these prophecies." [cite:ts3_darby_hopes10]

This cut against the older reading, discussed in Part One, in which the church inherited the promises made to Israel and the prophecies of a restored Israel were fulfilled spiritually in Christianity. [scene:ts1_defined|Part One: the shield defined] But it was not the first Protestant challenge to that reading. The English Puritans and their successors, described in Part Two, had already taught that the promises to the Jews would be fulfilled literally, including a return to the land. [scene:ts2_puritans|Part Two: the Puritan readers] Darby's contribution was to make the separation total and permanent: Israel's promises were not transferred to the church, and the church's hope was not Israel's.

The paused clock

The second element is a timetable. Dispensationalists read the "seventy weeks" of the Book of Daniel, chapter 9, as a prophetic calendar for Israel. In Weber's account of Darby's scheme, when the Jewish nation rejected Jesus, God "suspended the prophetic timetable at the end of Daniel's sixty-ninth week" and "began building a new and heavenly people—the church." The present age is therefore a gap in Israel's calendar, not a stage of it. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]

The rapture closes the gap. Because the church belongs to heaven, it is removed from the earth in a "secret, any-moment, pre-tribulational Rapture," and only then does Israel's calendar resume. "Once the heavenly people of God have been raptured, Darby believed, the divine script can be played out to the end": the seventieth week of Daniel, a seven-year tribulation, the rise of the Antichrist, and finally the visible return of Christ to reign on earth for a thousand years. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]

A Jewish return as precondition: what Darby taught

A widely repeated account holds that Darby taught that the Jews had to return to their land before Christ could come back, and that this turned a political programme into prophecy. The system as Darby set it out does not quite say that. Its central hope, the rapture, was "any-moment": nothing had to happen first. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] The restoration of Israel belonged to the events that follow the church's departure. In his 1840 lectures Darby described that restoration in grim terms. "Though the people of Israel will be restored in their land, there will only be a remnant saved," he said; "those who rejected the Christ will be subjected to the Antichrist," and "Two thirds of the inhabitants will be cut off in the country of Israel itself after their return." [cite:ts3_darby_hopes10]

Those lines are Darby's reading of the prophets in 1840, quoted so that the system is described in its founder's words rather than an opponent's. They are not a statement of what later dispensationalists, still less Christian Zionist organisations, teach or want; the stated positions of those organisations, including CUFI's, are set out with their own words in Parts Eight and Ten. [scene:ts10_mechanism|Part Ten: how the shield works] What the lines do show is that in Darby's own scheme a Jewish return to the land was not a reward, nor a precondition for Christ's return, but the opening of a time of judgment.

That had a practical consequence for how the first generation of American dispensationalists read the Zionist movement when it appeared in the 1890s. Premillennialists, Weber writes, read "signs of the times" in current events, and "the rise of Zionism" was among them. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] But a sign was not an endorsement. Arno Gaebelein, a dispensationalist teacher who had worked as a missionary among Jewish immigrants, told an audience in 1905 that "Zionism is not the divinely promised restoration of Israel," but "a political and philanthropic undertaking," and that "The great movement is one of unbelief." [cite:ts3_wiki_gaebelein] [cite:ts3_demy_gaebelein] His magazine was nonetheless sympathetic to Zionism's progress. [cite:ts3_demy_gaebelein] The move from reading Zionism as a sign to backing the state of Israel as a religious duty came later, and is the subject of Parts Six to Eight.

The finding for this part is therefore narrower than the familiar one. Darby did not invent the expectation of a Jewish return, and he did not make it a political cause. What he built was a framework in which Israel, as a people distinct from the church, kept its own future on earth, centred on its land, and in which world events could be read as the stage being set for that future. Later teachers, organisations and governments would draw political conclusions from that framework that Darby did not draw; how they did so is traced in the parts that follow.
