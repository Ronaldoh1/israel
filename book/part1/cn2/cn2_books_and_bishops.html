Four Gospels and a Line of Bishops

In the middle of the second century there were two kinds of authority in the Christian communities of the Roman world, and they did not always sit easily together. One was the teacher: a philosopher-like figure who gathered students, wrote commentaries and claimed a deeper understanding of the faith. The other was the officer of the local congregation, the overseer or bishop, who presided at the weekly gathering and handled its funds. Justin Martyr, writing in Rome around 155, describes the Sunday assembly: the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, the president gives an instruction, and those who are well off give what they choose. What is collected is deposited with the president, who uses it to help orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. [cite:vt21_justin67] The president held the purse.

The teachers held something else. Valentinus, the most successful Christian teacher of the period, came to Rome and attracted a large following; later writers credit his school with the Gospel of Truth and a body of commentary on the scriptures. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] Tertullian, hostile as always, preserves a revealing detail about why Valentinus broke with the church. He "had expected to become a bishop", Tertullian writes, but another man obtained the office on the strength of having suffered for the faith, and Valentinus left in resentment. [cite:vt24_tert_adval] Whether or not the motive was as petty as Tertullian makes it, the story shows where the line of conflict ran. The teacher wanted the office, and the office went to someone else.

Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, wrote the five books of Against Heresies around 180 to defeat the teachers, above all the Valentinians. His argument had two halves, and the history of the canon cannot be understood without both. The first half was about books. The church, he argued, had four gospels and only four, as there are four winds and four quarters of the world, and the Valentinians' additional writings were forgeries. [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] The second half was about people. The true teaching, Irenaeus said, was not secret knowledge passed among initiates but public tradition, guaranteed by the bishops whom the apostles had appointed and by their successors. He proved the point with a list. In Rome the apostles had handed the office to Linus, then came Anacletus and Clement, and so on to "Eleutherius", who "does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate". [cite:cn_iren_3_3]

The phrase Irenaeus used for the result has been argued over ever since: "it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its preeminent authority". [cite:cn_iren_3_3] Catholic writers read it as early evidence for the primacy of Rome; others read it as a remark about the reliability of a well-connected church. On either reading, the argument did something new. It joined the question of which books were scripture to the question of who held office, and made the office the guarantee of the books. A teacher who appealed to other writings, or to a private tradition, was by definition outside the line.

The cost of being outside the line was not yet physical. Irenaeus was a bishop in a province of a pagan empire, with no power beyond his pen and his congregation's loyalty, and his books are polemic written by an opponent. [scene:vt24_the_heresy_hunters|The heresy hunters] But the structure he described would later be backed by law. In the next century and a half, the bishops in the line acquired something the teachers never had: the support of the Roman state. When that happened, the line between the canon and the excluded books became a line between legal and illegal assembly, as Part Three shows.

The fruit of the argument can be measured in what survived. The gospels Irenaeus named are in every Bible. The books he attacked, the Gospel of Truth among them, vanished from use and were known for sixteen centuries only through his quotations, until a jar dug up near Nag Hammadi in Egypt in 1945 returned some of them in Coptic translation. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] The teachers had written books. The bishops had built an institution that decided which books would be copied.
