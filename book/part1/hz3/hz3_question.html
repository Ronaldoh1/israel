The Case That Israel Made Hezbollah

The argument that Israel's invasion and occupation created Hezbollah is an argument, not a finding: it is a claim about what would have happened in a Lebanon that Israel never entered, and no record can test that directly. What makes it more than a slogan is that it has been made, in plain words, by people who ran Israel's war in Lebanon, and by the leading American scholar of Lebanon's Shia politics. This section sets out that case and separates the facts it rests on from the inference it draws.

The facts it starts from. Israel invaded on 6 June 1982, as Part Two describes. [scene:hz2_invasion|The invasion] Two days later, on 8 June, a group of about fifty Shia fighters ambushed an Israeli armoured column at Khalde, south of Beirut; Hezbollah has since treated that ambush as the founding story of its military wing, an account recorded by the journalist Robert Fisk and by the Israeli general and historian Shimon Shapira. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] In the weeks after the invasion, about 1,500 Iranian Revolutionary Guards arrived in the Bekaa valley with Syrian permission and began training Lebanese fighters at a base there. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] When Hezbollah published its founding manifesto on 16 February 1985, read by Sheikh Ibrahim al-Amin, the first aim it named was a fight against American and Israeli power, including Israel's occupation of the south. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] [scene:hz2_letter|The Open Letter] Those are documented events. They show that the organisation took shape during the invasion and named the occupation as its first enemy. They do not, by themselves, show that it would not have taken shape without it.

What Israeli officials said. The most quoted statement comes from Ehud Barak, the prime minister who ordered the withdrawal of May 2000. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] In 2006 he was quoted as saying: "When we entered Lebanon, there was no Hezbollah. We were accepted by perfumed rice and flowers by the Shi'a in the south." And: "It was our presence there that created Hezbollah." [cite:hz3_statesman_barak] [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] The exact venue is less certain than the words. The remark is usually attributed to a 2006 magazine interview, and the Meir Amit Intelligence and Terrorism Information Center quotes a shorter version without a date; a variant wording dated 2010 also circulates. This book could not confirm the original outlet, and gives the quotation as reported. [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] The substance is not in dispute: a senior Israeli commander and prime minister described the occupation as the cause.

Yitzhak Rabin, defence minister when Israel pulled back to a strip of the south in 1985, said much the same in the language of warning. In February 1985, in remarks reported by the Jewish Telegraphic Agency, he said that if Israel had merely swapped the threat of the Palestine Liberation Organization for a Shia one, "we have done the worst (thing) in our struggle against terrorism", and that "the Shiites have the potential for a kind of terrorism that we have not yet experienced." [cite:hz3_djn_rabin85] A month later the Christian Science Monitor reported that Shia fighters in the south had "sworn to drive the Israelis out of Lebanon", and that Rabin feared their hatred of Israel more than Palestinian attacks. [cite:hz3_csm_mar85] Rabin's policy was not to leave. On the strip Israel kept, he said, "If there is a problem we will bomb them, we'll shell them." [cite:hz3_djn_rabin85] In September 1986 he said Israel would back the South Lebanon Army "with our firepower to permanently break any future offensive". [cite:hz3_ina_rabin86] That militia, according to the Congressional Research Service, was one Israel "fully funded and equipped"; its rank and file came to be mostly Shia, drawn in part by salaries. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [cite:hz4_olj_sla] [scene:hz4_zone|The security zone]

What the occupation did, in documented acts. The case does not rest on officials' remarks alone. When Israel pulled back in April 1985, it took more than 1,000 Shia detainees, most of them held at the Ansar camp, across the border into Israel; the International Committee of the Red Cross and the United States condemned the transfer as a breach of the Geneva Convention. [cite:hz3_csm_jun85] The sources consulted for this book do not record Israel's stated reason for the transfer. Over the next fifteen years, Israel and its militia held a strip of the south, kept the Khiam prison, for whose abuses Human Rights Watch held Israel responsible in 1999, and fought Hezbollah there; [cite:hz4_hrw_khiam99] about 250 Israeli soldiers were killed there between 1985 and 2000, by a Reuters count cited by the Congressional Research Service. [cite:hz4_crs_sla] [scene:hz4_zone|Part Four] The argument is that each of these acts gave the new organisation recruits, a cause the wider public in the south could share, and a battlefield on which to prove itself.

What the leading scholar argued. Augustus Richard Norton, a Boston University professor and the author of a widely used short history of Hezbollah, put it plainly on American radio in July 2006: "Hezbollah didn't exist in the early 1980s and was in effect created by the Israeli occupation of Lebanon and by Iran." [cite:hz3_npr_norton] He added that "the Israeli presence in Lebanon basically created Hezbollah", while Iran "was attempting to extend its revolutionary goals to places like Lebanon". [cite:hz3_npr_norton] In his book, as reviewers summarised it, Norton argued that the "profoundly destructive Israeli invasion of Lebanon in 1982" broke the PLO and helped create a "far more formidable" opponent, and he quoted both Barak and Rabin conceding that the occupation "provided the context in which Hezbollah was created". [cite:hz3_divine_norton] [cite:hz3_aljadid_norton] Norton's own formula names two causes, not one, a point the next section takes up.

Hezbollah's own account. Hezbollah tells its origin both ways, and that is itself part of the record. Its founding story centres on Khalde and the occupation, and its leaders have long described the fight against Israel as the reason the organisation exists; Naim Qassem, long its deputy leader, has called that struggle the central rationale of the party. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] Yet the same 1985 manifesto pledged allegiance to Iran's supreme leader and to Ayatollah Khomeini's doctrine of rule by the senior jurist, and set the goal of making Lebanon part of the project of Iran's 1979 revolution. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] And Qassem has also written, as quoted by the Israeli researcher Shapira, that "the Israeli invasion of Lebanon was not the reason Hezbollah was founded." [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] Hezbollah, in other words, presents itself as a resistance to occupation and as part of a revolutionary religious movement led from Tehran, and its own statements support both readings.

What the case shows and what it assumes. The documented part is strong: Hezbollah formed during the invasion, fought the occupation for eighteen years, and was described as its product by an Israeli prime minister and foreseen in the warnings of Israel's defence minister at the time. The inference, that without the invasion there would be no Hezbollah, assumes that the other forces at work in 1982 would not have produced an armed movement on their own. Even Barak's statement is a judgement made in hindsight, not evidence about the alternative. The record also shows that the organisation outlived the occupation, a fact the other side of the argument presses and the next section examines.
