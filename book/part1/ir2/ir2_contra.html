Iran-Contra: the Israeli Leg, 1985–1986

The best-documented Israeli arms deliveries to the Islamic Republic are those of 1985, because they became part of the largest American political scandal of the decade and were examined by an independent counsel, Lawrence Walsh, whose final report was published in 1993. [cite:ir2_walsh18] [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] Unlike the earlier sales, they are itemised by date and number in official records.

How it began

The channel opened through Israel. Prime Minister Shimon Peres first suggested contacts with Iran, and David Kimche, director general of Israel's Foreign Ministry, pressed the idea on the US national security adviser, Robert McFarlane, in mid-1985. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] Michael Ledeen, a consultant to the US National Security Council, had met Peres on 3 May 1985 to discuss Iran and weapons transfers. [cite:ir2_wiki_contratl] Kimche met McFarlane in Washington on 3 July 1985 on behalf of Israelis who were talking with the Iranian businessman Manucher Ghorbanifar and the Saudi financier Adnan Khashoggi. [cite:ir2_brown_begin] According to a chronology published in 1987, Kimche said that arms sales "probably will be a condition" of any opening to Iranian "moderates." [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] The Israeli side of the operation was run by Peres's counter-terrorism adviser Amiram Nir, with Adolph Schwimmer, founder of Israel Aircraft Industries, and Yaakov Nimrodi, an arms dealer with long experience of Iran. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra]

The American aim was to free seven American hostages held in Lebanon by groups linked to Iran, among them the CIA's Beirut station chief, William Buckley. [cite:ir2_csm_interests86] [cite:ir2_jc_sharon] Those kidnappings, and Hezbollah's emergence behind them, are described in the Hezbollah volume. [scene:hz2_1983|The Hezbollah Architecture: 1983] The plan agreed in August 1985 was that Israel would sell American-made TOW anti-tank missiles to Iran from its own stocks and the United States would replace them. McFarlane said President Reagan approved this orally; Reagan and Attorney General Edwin Meese later said the president had not known of the shipments before a written finding of January 1986. [cite:ir2_brown_begin] [cite:ir2_csm_interests86]

The 1985 shipments

On 20 August 1985 Israel delivered the first 96 TOW missiles. No hostage was released. A second shipment of 408 TOWs followed in September, and the Reverend Benjamin Weir was freed in mid-September (14 or 15 September in different accounts). [cite:ir2_brown_begin] [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] Walsh's final report records that "with U.S. acquiescence" Israel shipped 504 American-made TOWs to Iran in August and September 1985, and that Weir "was released as a result." [cite:ir2_walsh18] Earlier reporting gave the first shipment as "100" missiles, the approximate number Reagan approved; the itemised record is 96. [cite:ir2_jc_sharon] [cite:ir2_brown_begin]

In November 1985 Israel tried to send HAWK anti-aircraft missiles. An El Al 747 that was to carry 80 of them toward Iran by way of a European country turned back, and 18 were flown from Israel to Iran on a CIA-owned aircraft around 25 November. [cite:ir2_walsh18] They were not what Iran had asked for. Iranian officials found that the missiles were an older model and bore Israeli markings, and Iran sent them back. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] [cite:ir2_csm_interests86] Rafsanjani later recalled that the Israeli HAWKs "remained at the airport for a long time and had to be returned." [cite:ir2_iranwire_oil] After this failure, American officials took over the operation themselves. [cite:ir2_csm_interests86]

From Israeli sales to American sales

On 4 December 1985 Oliver North, a National Security Council aide, proposed selling arms to Iran directly rather than through Israel, with part of the proceeds going to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua, whom Congress had barred the administration from funding; on 6 December he told Israeli defence officials that future profits would help the Contras. [cite:ir2_wiki_contratl] [cite:ir2_jc_sharon] On 17 January 1986 Reagan signed a secret finding authorising dealings with Iran, including arms sales, and instructed CIA Director William Casey to withhold the details from Congress. [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] From then on the sales were American. About 1,000 TOWs went in February 1986 under US direction; in May McFarlane, North and others, among them Amiram Nir, flew to Tehran with HAWK spare parts; Lawrence Jenco was released in July; more TOWs went in October, some of them through Israel, and David Jacobsen was freed on 2 November. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] [cite:ir2_wiki_contratl] Three more Americans were kidnapped in Lebanon in the same period. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra]

The money moved through a chain of intermediaries. By Encyclopaedia Iranica's account, Israeli middlemen paid Israel's defence ministry $6,000 per TOW, Ghorbanifar paid $10,000, and the Iranian government was charged $12,000; Iran later discovered it had been heavily overcharged. Residual funds from the February 1986 shipment were used partly for the Contras and for commissions to the businessmen Richard Secord and Albert Hakim. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] Rafsanjani later said Iran paid $14 million of a $22 million deal and refused the rest because of the price. [cite:ir2_iranwire_oil]

On 3 November 1986 the Lebanese weekly Ash-Shiraa disclosed McFarlane's visit to Tehran. [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] The next day Rafsanjani, then Speaker of parliament, confirmed that the United States had shipped military equipment; his account to parliament did not satisfy some members, and when eight deputies summoned the foreign minister over the affair Khomeini rebuked them, saying their tone was "harsher than that of Israel, harsher than that of the US." [cite:ir2_iranwire_rafs]

How many weapons, and through whom

The totals are often misstated. Over seven shipments from August 1985 to November 1986, Iran received 2,004 TOWs, 18 HAWKs (which it returned) and more than 200 HAWK spare parts, and three hostages were freed. [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] Of these, Israel sold from its own stocks 504 TOWs and the 18 HAWKs, all in 1985; the rest were American sales under the January 1986 finding, although Israeli territory and personnel continued to be used in some deliveries. [cite:ir2_walsh18] [cite:ir2_iranica_contra] [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] Some accounts, including the simulation on which this book draws, say that more than 2,000 TOWs and 235 HAWK parts kits went "via Israel." That figure traces to reporting of 20 November 1986, before the investigations, which attributed the whole programme to Israeli shipments with US approval; the itemised record of the Walsh investigation does not support it. [cite:ir2_csm_chron87] [cite:ir2_walsh18]

Who urged whom

Whether Israel drew the United States into the arms-for-hostages trade, or American officials drew Israel in, was disputed at the time and remains so. Israeli officials in November 1986 described Israel as a "willing helper" to Washington and did not deny reports that Israel had conceived the scheme; Peres, then foreign minister, repeatedly said Israel had acted only to help free American hostages. [cite:ir2_csm_plays86b] The record shows that a CIA draft of May 1985 had already proposed easing the arms embargo to reach Iranian "moderates," and that the NSC consultant's approach to Kimche followed it. [cite:ir2_csm_interests86] Zbigniew Brzezinski suggested that Kimche may have taken the first steps but that "there may have been a strong temptation on the US side to move in that direction as well." Prime Minister Yitzhak Shamir rejected American suggestions that Israel had helped channel money to the Contras. [cite:ir2_csm_relat86] Both readings rest on documents; neither has been settled by them.

The Americans who ran the operation faced prosecution. Fourteen people were charged and eleven convicted; every conviction was either overturned on appeal or pardoned. [cite:ir2_jc_sharon] On 24 December 1992 President George H. W. Bush pardoned six officials, among them former Defence Secretary Caspar Weinberger and McFarlane, saying he was doing what "honor, decency, and fairness require." [cite:ir2_procl6518] The public record this book examined does not show charges in the United States against any Israeli official.
