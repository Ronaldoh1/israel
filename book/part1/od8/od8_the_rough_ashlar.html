The Rough Ashlar

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

In the first degree of Freemasonry the new member meets two stones. The Entered Apprentice lecture, as quoted by the Grand Lodge of Illinois, calls the rough ashlar "a stone in its rude and natural state, as taken from the quarry", and the perfect ashlar one "prepared by the workmen, to be adjusted by the working tools of a Fellow Craft". The first stands for a person's natural state; the second for the character shaped by "a virtuous education, our own endeavors, and the blessing of Deity". [cite:od_ilmason_ashlar] The lodges took the tools and lore of the working masons and made them an allegory of building a self. [cite:vt30_wiki_pgle]

The inner reading sets the two stones beside the temple that runs through this whole shelf. Paul asked his readers whether they knew that they were God's temple. [cite:cn_bh_1cor316] The lodge's ritual, which takes its legends from the building of Solomon's Temple, turns the same image into a discipline: the member is the stone, and the temple is built one dressed stone at a time. The work is done with tools, by hand, over years. Nothing in the allegory says it can be done for someone else.

The reading notes what the allegory refuses. The rough stone is not condemned; it is the material of the work. That is a different picture of the person from the one in Humanum genus, which divided the human race into two kingdoms. [cite:vt30_va_humanum] The stone does not need to be saved from outside; it needs to be worked.

The record limits the reading. The ashlar lecture is a moral allegory taught in a fraternal order, and Masonic bodies state its meaning in terms of virtue and education, not mystical union. [cite:od_ilmason_ashlar] The link to Paul's temple and to the other inner temples on this shelf is the reader's.
