How Ranked-Choice Voting Counts

Most American elections use plurality voting: each voter marks one candidate, and whoever has the most votes wins, whether or not that is a majority. With two candidates the rule works well. With three or more it can elect someone a majority voted against, and voters who prefer a weaker candidate must weigh whether a vote for that candidate is "wasted". Ranked-choice voting is one answer to this problem; in November 2026 it is in use for state or federal races in Alaska and Maine and for local races in the District of Columbia, eleven cities and two counties. [cite:dt6_fv_2026use] This section explains how it counts, step by step, with an invented example and three real elections, and sets out exactly what kind of majority it produces.

The rule

The form used in American single-seat races is instant runoff. Voters rank candidates in order of preference, first, second, third and so on, up to the limit the ballot allows; in New York City the limit is five. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] Counting proceeds in rounds. In the first round each ballot counts for its first choice. If a candidate has a majority of the ballots still in play, that candidate wins. If not, the candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated, and each of that candidate's ballots moves to the next candidate it ranks who is still in the race. The rounds repeat until one candidate has a majority of the ballots still counting. A ballot that ranks no remaining candidate drops out of the count. It is called an exhausted ballot, or in New York City's official tables an "inactive" ballot. The ballots still counting in a given round are called continuing ballots. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_fra_text]

The name "instant runoff" describes the purpose. A conventional runoff is a second election, held weeks later, between the top finishers when no one wins a majority. Instant runoff asks voters for their fallback choices in advance, so that the elimination can be run from the same ballot without a second trip to the polls. A federal judge, upholding Maine's version in 2018, noted that the Constitution is "perfectly silent" on how states count votes for Congress, and that "the Constitution does not require an easy ballot." [cite:dt6_baber]

A real count: Maine's 2nd District, 2018

Maine's 2018 general election for its 2nd congressional district was the state's first general election for Congress counted by ranked choice, after a court ruling and a referendum described in the next section. In the first round the Republican incumbent, Bruce Poliquin, had 134,184 votes (46.3 per cent) and the Democrat, Jared Golden, 132,013 (45.6 per cent). Two independents, Tiffany Bond and Will Hoar, had 16,552 and 6,875. With no one above half, both independents were eliminated and their 23,427 ballots moved to their next choices. Golden gained 10,427, Poliquin gained 4,747, and 8,253 ranked neither major candidate and were exhausted. The final round stood at Golden 142,440 (50.6 per cent of continuing ballots) to Poliquin 138,931. [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] The first-round leader lost. On the 289,624 ballots cast in the race, Golden's final total was about 49.2 per cent, so his majority was a majority of the continuing ballots, not of everyone who voted. Poliquin sued in federal court on 13 November 2018, lost, and withdrew his request for a recount on 14 December. [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] [cite:dt6_baber]

New York City: eight rounds in 2021, three in 2025

New York City adopted ranked-choice voting for its primary and special elections by a charter vote in 2019. [cite:dt6_bp_nyc2019] Its first open mayoral primary under the system, the Democratic primary of 22 June 2021, took eight rounds. Eric Adams led the first round with 289,403 votes (30.7 per cent), ahead of Maya Wiley at 21.4 per cent, Kathryn Garcia at 19.6 per cent and Andrew Yang at 12.2 per cent. Candidates were eliminated one round at a time until two remained. In the final round Adams had 404,513 votes (50.4 per cent) and Garcia 397,316, a margin of 7,197. By then 140,202 ballots were inactive, because they ranked neither finalist. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] On the official figures, Adams's final total was about 43 per cent of the ballots counted in the first round.

Four years later the same system produced a fast result. In the Democratic primary of 24 June 2025 Zohran Mamdani led the first round with 469,642 votes (43.8 per cent), against 387,137 (36.1 per cent) for Andrew Cuomo and 11.3 per cent for Brad Lander. The count took three rounds. Mamdani finished with 573,169 (56.4 per cent of continuing ballots) to Cuomo's 443,229, with 55,332 ballots inactive. [cite:dt6_nyc_2025] Here the first-round leader won, as he would have under plurality, and his final total was a majority, about 53 per cent, even of all ballots counted in the first round.

What kind of majority this is

The three counts show what the system does and does not promise. It always produces a winner with a majority of the ballots still counting in the last round, and it lets voters support a candidate unlikely to win without helping elect the candidate they like least: a Bond or Hoar voter in Maine could rank an independent first and still count in the final contest. It does not guarantee a majority of all ballots cast, because some voters rank only one or two candidates, or none of the finalists. In Maine and in New York in 2021 the winner's final total fell just short of half of everyone who voted; in New York in 2025 it cleared half. [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_nyc_2025]

The design also carries a practical cost that its critics stress. Because a candidate's elimination depends on every ballot in the race, the rounds cannot be run until all ballots that could transfer have been counted, and in New York and Maine the final tallies are published as round-by-round tables rather than as a single total. [cite:dt6_nyc_2021] [cite:dt6_bp_me2_2018] Supporters answer that a separate runoff election takes weeks longer still.
