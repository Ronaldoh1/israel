The Apostolic Archive: What "Secret" Meant and Who Gets In

For most of its history the pope's central archive was called the Archivum Secretum Vaticanum, the Vatican Secret Archive. The name has done more to shape popular ideas about the Holy See than almost anything kept inside the building. This section explains what the word meant, why it was dropped in 2019, how large the archive is, what it is known to hold, who may read in it, and the rule that decides which decades are open. It ends with the Vatican Library, a separate institution that is often confused with the archive.

Private, not hidden

The archive was created at the wish of Pope Paul V around 1610 to 1612, when papal records that had been kept with the Apostolic Chamber and the Apostolic Library were gathered in one place. The name Archivum Secretum is attested from about 1646. [cite:vt14_va_mp2019] In the Latin of the period, secretum meant separate or reserved: this was, in Pope Francis's words, "the Pope's private, separate, reserved archive", as distinct from the archives of curial offices. [cite:vt14_va_mp2019]

Francis changed the name by an apostolic letter issued motu proprio (on his own initiative) dated 22 October 2019 and published on 28 October 2019. He gave as the reason that the word "secret" had acquired "ambiguous, even negative nuances" in modern languages, a reading he called the opposite of the archive's purpose. The new name, Vatican Apostolic Archive, was meant to stress its tie to the Roman see, as the name of the Vatican Apostolic Library already did. The letter changed the name, not the archive's structure or its rules of access. [cite:vt14_va_mp2019] Claims that still speak of a "Secret Archive" in the present tense are describing an institution that no longer uses that name, and the old name never meant that its contents were concealed as a class.

Size and holdings

The archive holds about 85 linear kilometres of shelving, part of it in an underground store known as the Bunker, beneath the Cortile della Pigna of the Vatican Museums. Its more than 600 archival collections, or fonds, cover about twelve centuries, from the eighth to the twentieth. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] The papal registers, the copies of outgoing papal letters, survive in a continuous series from Innocent III (1198–1216), with a few earlier volumes. [cite:vt14_wiki_vaa]

Among the documents reference works list in the archive are Henry VIII's petition for the annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, a transcript of Galileo's trial, letters of Michelangelo about unpaid work on the Sistine Chapel, and the bull of 1521 excommunicating Martin Luther. [cite:vt14_wiki_vaa] These are famous items in a mass of routine administrative paper: appointments, dispensations, correspondence of nunciatures and the accounts of the papal government.

The Apostolic Archive is not the only Holy See archive. When the papers of Pius XII's pontificate were opened, they included, besides the Apostolic Archive, the historical archive of the Secretariat of State's section for relations with states, the archive of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, the historical archive of Propaganda Fide, the archive of the Congregation for the Eastern Churches and the archives of other offices and tribunals. [cite:vt14_cchq_brownfleming] Much of the most consulted diplomatic material for the twentieth century sits in the Secretariat of State's archive, not in the Apostolic Archive proper. [cite:vt14_cchq_brownfleming]

Who may read, and how

Leo XIII opened the archive to researchers of every country in 1881; the reading room opened on 1 January of that year. [cite:vt14_va_mp2019] [cite:vt14_wiki_vaa] Today, according to Vatican News, access is free of charge and open to scholars of all nationalities regardless of religion. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] In practice it is restricted to qualified researchers, usually from universities, who apply with a letter of introduction. Readers order a limited number of items at a time, the reading room admits a limited number of people a day, and ink pens and personal cameras are not allowed. [cite:vt14_wiki_vaa] These are reading-room rules of the kind most major archives apply to fragile originals; they limit who may come in and how, not which documents exist.

The real limit is chronological. The Holy See opens its archives by whole pontificates, and only a pope can authorise access to his predecessors' papers. [cite:vt14_vn_open2020] [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] The papers of Pius XI (1922–1939) were opened on 18 September 2006 by decision of Benedict XVI, after a partial release of German material under John Paul II in 2002. [cite:vt14_cna_piusxi2006] [cite:vt14_wiki_vaa] Those of Pius XII (March 1939 to October 1958) were opened on 2 March 2020 by decision of Francis, who had announced the date a year earlier with the words "The Church is not afraid of history; rather, she loves it." [cite:vt1_va_archive2019] As of August 2025 Vatican News gave the end of Pius XII's pontificate, October 1958, as the current limit. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] We found no announcement, as of October 2026, of a date for opening the papers of John XXIII (1958–1963), the next pontificate in line. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025]

That is the accurate content of the common claim that "everything after 1958 is classified". Papers after October 1958 are closed, but under a rolling rule that has applied to every earlier period too, and that has so far opened each pontificate six or more decades after its end (Pius XI's after 67 years, Pius XII's after 61). One earlier claim, that the Pius XII papers were held back for eighty years "due to the beatification process", gives a motive for which we found no source; the documented practice is that whole pontificates open together, by papal decision. [cite:vt14_vn_open2020] [cite:vt14_cna_piusxi2006] The rolling rule is a real restriction on what can be known about the Holy See after 1958, including its relations with Israel and the Palestinians, and the volume says so wherever it bears on a finding. It is not evidence that any particular document is being hidden.

The Library

The Vatican Apostolic Library is older and separate. It descends from the early papal library; Nicholas V (1447–1455) enlarged it greatly and opened its manuscripts to scholars, and Sixtus IV formally established it by the bull Ad decorem militantis Ecclesiae of 15 June 1475. [cite:vt14_brit_bav] [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] Under Paul V the Library and the Archive were separated as institutions. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025] The Library holds more than 80,000 manuscripts, mostly Latin and Greek, more than 1.6 million printed volumes and some 8,600 incunabula (books printed before 1501), together with coins, medals, prints and drawings. [cite:vt14_brit_bav] About 6,000 scholars use it. [cite:vt14_vn_libarch2025]

The archive therefore answers the question of "hidden history" in a specific way: the papers up to October 1958 can be read and are being read, and what they show about the Second World War is the subject of the next section. [scene:vt14_pius_files|The Pius XII files]
