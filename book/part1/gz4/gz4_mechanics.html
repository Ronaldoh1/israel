How the Closure Worked

The closure of Gaza was not a single act but a system of rules, permits and limits, maintained by Israel on three sides of the strip and by Egypt on the fourth, and adjusted many times over sixteen years. This section sets out its parts in turn: the decision that founded it, and what it did to goods, people, the sea and the land. Israel's stated aims were to prevent the smuggling of weapons into Gaza, to stop the rocket fire from the strip, and to exert economic pressure on Hamas. [cite:gz_wiki_blockade]

The founding decision. On 19 September 2007 Israel's Security Cabinet, meeting under Prime Minister Ehud Olmert, unanimously decided that Hamas was "a terrorist organization that has taken control of the Gaza Strip and turned it into hostile territory." It cited the continuing Qassam rocket fire at Sderot and other communities, and it authorized reducing the supply of fuel and electricity to Gaza, restricting the passage of goods, and restricting the movement of people in and out, to be carried out after legal review "taking into account both the humanitarian aspects relevant to the Gaza Strip and the intention to avoid a humanitarian crisis." [cite:gz_un_hostile2007] Egypt had closed its crossing at Rafah after the takeover. [cite:gz_wiki_blockade]

Goods. For the first three years Israel admitted a restricted list of goods. Items it classed as "dual-use", which included construction materials and computer equipment, were allowed only as part of approved projects run by donors, and Israel banned virtually all exports; Israeli security officials, as reported, described the export ban as "a political decision to separate Gaza from the West Bank." [cite:gz_wiki_blockade] In January 2008 an Israeli government document titled "Food Consumption in the Gaza Strip — The Red Lines", estimated that each Gazan would need 2,279 calories a day to avoid malnutrition. It became public in 2012 after the Israeli human rights group Gisha filed a freedom-of-information petition and Israel's Supreme Court ordered its release; the Defense Ministry said the plan it described was never put into effect. [cite:gz_jta_redlines]

The flotilla, and the easing of 2010. On 31 May 2010 Israeli naval forces boarded a flotilla of ships sailing to break the blockade, and killed nine passengers aboard the Mavi Marmara. [cite:gz_wiki_blockade] [cite:gz_palmer] Facing mounting international calls to ease the blockade, Israel announced in June 2010 that it would allow nearly all civilian goods into Gaza while continuing to block weapons and dual-use items, and Egypt partially reopened Rafah, opening it on a regular but limited basis from May 2011. [cite:gz_wiki_blockade] The UN Secretary-General's panel of inquiry, chaired by Geoffrey Palmer, found in September 2011 that the naval blockade was "a legitimate security measure" whose implementation complied with international law, but that the decision to board with such force was "excessive and unreasonable"; it also found that the soldiers met "significant, organized and violent resistance from a group of passengers", that the flotilla "acted recklessly", and that Israel had given no satisfactory explanation for any of the nine deaths. It recommended that Israel keep easing its restrictions "with a view to lifting its closure." [cite:gz_palmer]

People. Leaving Gaza meant leaving through Israel's Erez crossing with an Israeli permit, or through Rafah when Egypt opened it. Israel considered exit applications from only a small number of labourers, less than five percent of the number considered in 2000. [cite:gz_wiki_blockade] Patients needing treatment outside the strip, whose referrals tripled between 2006 and 2016 to 24,616, saw the share of exit permits approved fall from 90 to 62 percent. [cite:gz_un_gaza10]

Sea and land. The Oslo agreements had envisaged that Gaza's fishermen could fish up to 20 nautical miles from shore; from 2006 the limit Israel enforced varied between 3 and 6 miles, occasionally 9. Along the land fence Israel declared "access-restricted areas" that at times covered up to 35 percent of Gaza's farmland and up to 85 percent of its fishing waters. Between 2007 and 2017, according to the UN, Israeli fire in those areas killed 389 people and injured 2,829. [cite:gz_un_gaza10]
