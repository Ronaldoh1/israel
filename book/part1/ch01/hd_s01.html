How Historians Argue About 1948

A common assumption holds that historians agree on what happened in 1948, and that only politicians and activists dispute it. They do not agree. The scholars whose work is cited throughout this library divide in two separate ways. The first division is over events and intent: what happened to the Palestinian Arabs who left or were driven from their homes, and how far it was planned. The second is over framing: what kind of movement Zionism was in the first place. Knowing where each scholar stands does not settle either question, but it allows a reader to see, when a later chapter cites a historian, whose interpretation is being offered and what the other camp would say in reply. This section maps the camps. It is a guide to the interpreters, not a verdict on them.

The first debate has a history of its own, and it turns on access to documents. Until the 1980s the dominant Israeli account held that the Palestinians had largely left on their own, or at the urging of Arab leaders. In the 1980s Israel began to declassify its state and military archives, and a group of scholars who came to be called the "new historians," Benny Morris and Ilan Pappé among them, went into those files. What they documented was expulsions, massacres and the deliberate prevention of Palestinian return. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The older account had denied these things; the archives recorded them.

The new historians did not reach one conclusion. Morris and Pappé, working with the same newly opened material, came to sharply different judgments about how far the expulsions were planned. That disagreement matters because the documents of the period can be read in more than one way: an order or a pattern of operations can be read as evidence of a design, or not. The arguments over one such document are traced in [scene:pd_s01|Plan Dalet and the argument about what it was]. The historian Walid Khalidi, writing on Deir Yassin, argued that the Haganah, a Jewish armed force of the period, was involved there at every stage. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] These are claims about intent and responsibility, and some of the archival material bearing on them remains classified.

The new historians have their own critics. Historians such as Efraim Karsh reject the expulsion-centered account altogether. In their reading, the decisive facts of 1948 are the choices of Arab leaders and the Arab rejection of partition; the departure of the Palestinian population follows from those choices rather than from a design on the Jewish side. [cite:dailyalert_194_karsh] The strongest form of this case is that a war launched against partition carries responsibility for its consequences, and that an account which begins with expulsions has already chosen where the story starts. The strongest form of the new historians' case is that the expulsions, massacres and the barring of return are recorded in Israel's own state and military files, and that no account of the war's causes removes what those files show was done. The two camps are not arguing about whether the archives exist. They are arguing about what the archives show regarding intent, and where responsibility lies.

A second debate concerns framing rather than events, and it runs alongside the first. Scholars such as Joseph Massad describe Zionism as a settler-colonial project. Their evidence is, in part, the movement's own vocabulary: Zionist leaders used the word "colonization" of their own work, including Vladimir Jabotinsky in his 1923 essay "The Iron Wall." [cite:massad_settler_colonial] On this reading, the events of 1948 are one phase of a longer process of settlement and displacement. Others describe Zionism as a national liberation movement, and as a refugee movement: the effort of a people with ancient roots in the land to find safety after centuries of persecution. On that reading, the same events belong to the founding of a refuge and to a war over partition. Each frame can point to documents. Neither is settled by them, because the dispute is over which category best describes a set of facts that both sides can read.

How the older account came to be told, and how it changed as the files opened, is the subject of [scene:np_s01|how the Nakba was told]. For further reading across these positions, the works this library draws on include Shlaim's The Iron Wall, [cite:shlaim_iron_wall] Masalha's Palestine: A Four Thousand Year History [cite:masalha_four_thousand] and Said's work on the question of Palestine. [cite:said_question_palestine]

Part of the record is still closed. Material in Israeli military archives on some events, including Deir Yassin, remains classified. That means some of the questions above cannot be answered by any historian on the documents now available: claims about planning and about responsibility for particular events rest, in part, on files that no one outside the archive has read. The framing dispute will not be resolved by new files at all, because it is a disagreement over interpretation rather than over evidence.

The working rule that follows is to read the document first and the interpreter second. Facts drawn from the archives, such as an order, a report or a dated record of what was done in a given village, are shared ground, and historians on every side work from them. Judgments about intent, and the choice of frame, are where scholars divide. The chapters that follow cite the documents and say whose interpretation is whose, so that a reader can keep the two apart and reach a judgment of their own.
