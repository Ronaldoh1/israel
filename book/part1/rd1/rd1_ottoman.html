The Country Before the Program: Ottoman Palestine, 1872–1914

When Theodor Herzl and his colleagues spoke of Palestine in the late 1890s, they were speaking of a place that had no border and no government of its own. From 1516 to 1917, apart from a period of Egyptian rule in 1831–40, the land was governed as part of the Ottoman Empire. It was administered chiefly as the district of Jerusalem and as parts of the provinces of Beirut and Damascus, and nothing separated it from the territories that are today Syria, Lebanon and Jordan. Damascus, Baghdad, Jerusalem and Beirut were provincial cities of the same state, and people and goods moved between them without passports. The national borders that frame every later chapter of this book did not yet exist. Syria, Iraq, Lebanon, Jordan and Israel were not states. Almost every line that matters in the region's conflicts was drawn between 1916 and 1922, in a window of six years, by Britain and France. Iran, a separate Persian state with its own dynasty, its own language and a Shia majority, was never Ottoman at all.

Two misreadings of this period are common, and they point in opposite directions. One holds that the Ottomans "occupied" Palestine in the way later settlers did. The other holds that Palestine was an empty backwater of the empire, waiting to be filled. The record supports neither. Ottoman rule was imperial administration: governors, garrisons and taxation imposed over a population that remained, for four centuries, largely Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians, alongside long-established Jewish communities. The Ottomans ruled the country; they did not replace its people. A third misreading, that the Middle East has always been a set of hostile nations permanently at war, fails for a simpler reason. For four hundred years most of the region was one state.

The administrative shape that mattered in Herzl's lifetime dated from 1872. In that year the Ottoman government created the Mutasarrifate, or Sanjak, of Jerusalem as an independent administrative unit. It comprised the districts of Jerusalem, Jaffa, Hebron, Beersheba and Gaza. To its north lay two further sanjaks, Nablus and Acre. This arrangement lasted until Britain occupied Palestine in December 1917, when the Mutasarrifate was dissolved.[cite:rs_rd1_jerusalemstory] The country that Zionist delegates debated at Basel was therefore, in legal terms, one Ottoman unit centred on Jerusalem plus parts of two others. When later writers speak of "Palestine" in this period, they are describing a region that the Ottoman state itself divided among several administrations.

Inside this imperial frame, religion rather than nationality organized much of daily law. Under the millet system the empire recognized religious communities, Muslims, Christians of several churches, and Jews, and allowed each to govern marriage, inheritance and schooling through its own religious law, its own courts and its own leaders.[cite:w_ottoman_millet] The arrangement gave communities real autonomy over their internal lives. It was not, however, a golden age, and the record does not present it as one. The order carried heavy taxation and conscription, and it saw periodic violence. Non-Muslims paid special taxes and held a lesser legal status, and revolts were suppressed. The point is not that the Ottoman order was just. It is that it was a different order from the one the twentieth century built: an empire of religious communities, not a map of rival nation-states.

The Jews already living in this country when the Zionist movement was founded had their own long history, and their own institutions, quite separate from the movement that would arise in Europe. Historians call this community the Old Yishuv. According to the National Library of Israel, the Jewish population of the land grew from about 7,000 in 1800 to about 27,000 in 1880.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_oldyishuv] It was concentrated in the four cities that Jewish tradition held holy, Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias, and in the nineteenth century most of it lived in Jerusalem. The same four cities appear in the record of the wider Ottoman population as the places where Jewish communities lived among a mostly Arabic-speaking Muslim and Christian majority.

The Old Yishuv lived largely on money from abroad. It depended almost completely on the halukka, funds collected from diaspora donors and distributed through kollels, which were organized by the donors' and recipients' country of origin and by Hasidic affiliation. The money therefore moved through communal channels rather than through any political body. Before the Ottoman authorities, the Jews were represented by the Sephardi chief rabbi, the Hakham Bashi, a post later titled Rishon LeZion. At the end of the nineteenth century the Ashkenazi kollels united under a single body, the General Committee of Knesset Israel.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_oldyishuv] These were the Jews of Ottoman Palestine as the empire knew them: a recognized religious community within the millet order, Sephardi and Ashkenazi, supported by charity.

That began to change before Herzl. The National Library of Israel dates the beginning of a "New Yishuv" to the First Aliyah, the wave of immigration that began in 1881, and describes it as costing the Old Yishuv its leading position among the Jews of the country.[cite:rs_rd1_nli_oldyishuv] The Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question counts about 25,000 Jewish immigrants in 1882–1903, and more than 84,000 in 1850–1914, noting that not all of them stayed.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_chron] The Ottoman Land Code of 1858, which governed how land in the country was held, is examined in the previous stage of this book: [scene:ot_s01|the Ottoman Land Code].

Palestine's Jews were also one community among several in the Ottoman Middle East with deep roots. Jewish life in Mesopotamia, modern Iraq, went back to the Babylonian conquest of Judah in 586 BCE, and the scholars of that community compiled the Babylonian Talmud, still the primary text of rabbinic study.[cite:huc_farhud][cite:ushmm_farhud] That continuous presence predated the Iraqi state of 1932 and the Israeli state of 1948 by millennia; its later history belongs to a different part of this library: [scene:irq_e01|the Jews of Iraq].

How many people lived in Palestine on the eve of the First World War, and how many of them were Jews, is a question on which the sources disagree, and the disagreement is not a technicality. The numbers below are the main ones in the record, with what each source actually counted.

The Ottoman census of 1914–15 recorded 722,000 people: 602,000 Muslims, 81,000 Christians and 39,000 Jews. Roberto Bachi's reconstruction for 1914 gives a smaller total, 689,000, made up of 525,000 Muslims, 70,000 Christians and 94,000 Jews.[cite:rs_rd1_demog_wiki] The gap between the two Jewish figures, 39,000 against 94,000, is larger than the whole Jewish population of 1880. The difference begins with who was counted. The Ottoman register recorded 38,754 Jews, and on the analysis of the historian Justin McCarthy it apparently counted only Ottoman subjects. Many Jews in Palestine were foreign nationals and did not appear in it. McCarthy, adjusting the Ottoman figures, gives a total of 722,143 for 1914–15 and puts the true Jewish figure, including foreign nationals, closer to 60,000. Against this stands the figure long accepted in the literature, 85,000, as summarized by Roger Owen in his review of McCarthy's work.[cite:rs_rd1_mccarthy_owen] The Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question agrees with McCarthy's order of magnitude: about 60,000 Jews at the start of the war in 1914, 39,000 of them Ottoman citizens, against more than 700,000 Muslim and Christian Ottoman subjects.[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_chron]

The same pattern appears a generation earlier. For about 1890, Bachi's reconstruction gives 532,000 people, 43,000 of them Jews; the Ottoman census of 1890–91 gives 516,000, with 18,000 Jews.[cite:rs_rd1_demog_wiki] Here the reconstruction is higher on both counts, and its Jewish figure is more than double the census's. The direction of McCarthy's corrections is also on record. He adjusts Ottoman registers chiefly for the undercounting of young children, especially girls, and for categories of people the registers missed, and on the same reasoning he re-estimates the population of 1922 at 823,684, above the official British census total, arguing that modern methods correct the census undercount.[cite:rs_rd1_mccarthy_owen]

Each side of this dispute has a serious case. The lowest figure, 38,754, is a contemporary official register, but on McCarthy's reading it counted Ottoman subjects only. McCarthy's estimate of about 60,000 adds back the foreign nationals the register left out, and his corrections are stated: young children, especially girls, and missing categories. Those who hold to the higher figures rest on the figure long accepted in the literature, 85,000, and on Bachi's reconstruction, 94,000. The sources available here do not set out Bachi's method in detail, so the case for his figure cannot be tested against the record in this book.

What none of the figures disputes is the order of magnitude that mattered politically. On every count, the Jewish population of Palestine in 1914 was a minority, living among a majority of Arabic-speaking Muslim and Christian Ottoman subjects. On every count, too, it had grown several times over since 1800, first through the Old Yishuv's slow increase and then through immigration after 1881. How far it fell during the war that began in 1914 is itself disputed, with one account holding that about a third left and McCarthy arguing the fall was much less than the commonly cited quarter, since many who fled to Alexandria returned; that story is told in [scene:rd1_wwi|Palestine at war].[cite:rs_rd1_palquest_chron][cite:rs_rd1_mccarthy_owen]

The empire that governed all of these people did not survive the war. British forces captured Jerusalem in December 1917, ending Ottoman rule in the city. The Republic of Turkey, founded in 1923, renounced all rights over the former Arab provinces in the Treaty of Lausanne that same year.[cite:w_lausanne] Modern Turkey is thus the empire's successor state, not a claimant to Palestine. The layers that followed, oil, the Anglo-French carve-up, installed rulers and revolts, stacked on top of the Ottoman order, and the map below traces them.

Several things this record leaves open should be said plainly. It does not give a single agreed figure for the Jewish population in 1914, and no figure in it can be treated as settled: the range runs from 38,754 to 94,000 depending on who was counted and how. It does not say how many of the more than 84,000 Jewish immigrants of 1850–1914 stayed. Nor does it measure how the Old Yishuv's members themselves regarded the newcomers of 1881 and after, beyond the National Library's judgment that the New Yishuv displaced them from leadership. What it does establish is the country as it was when the program adopted at Basel set its sights on it: an Ottoman land without borders, a majority of Arab Muslim and Christian subjects, an old Jewish community living on charity in four holy cities, and a new wave of immigrants who had begun to change it.
