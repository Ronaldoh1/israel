Jacob Becomes Israel: What the Text Says

Before the word "Israel" named a movement, a territory or a state, it named a man. The text in which it first appears says something narrower and more personal than the nation or the Promised Land. In the Hebrew Bible, the word begins as a renaming, given to one individual after a single night.

The setting is Genesis 32. Jacob is alone at the ford of the Jabbok river on the night before he is to meet his brother Esau, whom he had deceived years earlier. All night he wrestles with a figure the text calls a man, an angel and, in Jacob's own words afterward, God. When the struggle ends, he receives a new name:

"Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed."

This verse, Genesis 32:28, is the word's first documented occurrence anywhere in the Hebrew Bible.[cite:genesis_32_biblegateway] The word appears 2,362 times across the Hebrew and Christian scriptures, 2,284 times in the Hebrew Bible and 78 in the New Testament, and every later use traces back to this verse. Jacob names the place of the struggle Peniel, "face of God," because, he says, "I saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared." Centuries later the prophet Hosea retold the story (Hosea 12:3–4), a sign that later biblical writers understood this episode as the origin of the name.

How the name is built is well established. Yisra'el joins El, the ancient Semitic word for God, to a root related to sarah, meaning to strive, contend or prevail. The standard scholarly reading is "he who struggles with God," or, if El is read as the subject, "God strives." The grammar allows both, and the text does not say which was meant.[cite:wiki_israel_name] El did not belong to Hebrew alone; it was shared across Canaanite, Ugaritic and other Northwest Semitic languages, a point developed in [scene:s00b|the discussion of the word "Semitic"]. The derivation is not contested. Hebrew lexicons, Brown-Driver-Briggs among them, rabbinic commentary and Christian biblical scholarship all hold it.

The text also builds in a contrast. Jacob's original name, Ya'akov, is related to akev, "heel": he was born grasping the heel of his twin, Esau, and the root carries overtones of crookedness and of following from behind. The letters of Yisra'el can also call to mind yashar, "straight" or "upright." Several Hebrew-language sources read this as deliberate: the grasping, deceptive Jacob and the upright, prevailing Israel are the same person, transformed.[cite:wiki_jacob] Before the name belongs to a nation, it describes a change in one man's character, earned through struggle.

From there the name gathers layers. When it is given, it belongs to one person. Generations later it names the descendants of his twelve sons. Centuries after that it attaches to a specific territory. Only in 1948 does it become the name of a modern nation-state. Each is a separate, later development. Each deserves to be tracked on its own, not read backward into the verse as though they had always been one thing.

Reading the verse in its original context makes no claim about how later biblical authors, ancient Israelite communities or modern political movements would use the word. Those uses are real and documented, and this book takes them up elsewhere. What the primary text establishes is the starting point: a personal name, given after a night of wrestling, meaning one who has struggled with God and with men and prevailed.
