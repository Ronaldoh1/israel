The answer depends on which definition is used, and the leading definitions disagree. The 2016 IHRA working definition, adopted by the US government and, as of August 2024, by 35 states and the District of Columbia, lists "denying the Jewish people their right to self-determination" as a potential example of antisemitism; seven of its 11 examples concern Israel [cite:reformjudaism_five_definitions] [cite:peacenow_ihra_faq]. The Jerusalem Declaration of March 2021, signed by more than 200 scholars of Jewish history and the Holocaust, holds that anti-Zionism is not antisemitic on its face, while listing examples that are; the Nexus Document sits between the two [cite:jwa_jda] [cite:thirdnarrative_three_defs].

Both concerns in this argument are real. IHRA's lead drafter, Kenneth Stern, says it has been weaponized against critics of Israel [cite:stern_guardian_2019], while Britain's Community Security Trust says the Jerusalem Declaration legitimizes calls for Israel's elimination [cite:cst_jda_critique]. Some anti-Zionist speech does cross into hatred of Jews, and the ADL recorded a record 9,354 US antisemitic incidents in 2024 [cite:algemeiner_adl_2024]. The practical test is the target: a state's policies, or Jewish people.
