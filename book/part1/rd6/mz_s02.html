The Yemenite Children Affair

Between 1948 and 1954, families living in Israel's immigrant transit camps say their babies were taken from them. The young state's treatment of its own newcomers is part of the founding record, and this episode has been fought over inside Israel for decades. A common view holds that the founding generation treated all Jewish immigrants alike. The record of the camps, and of the children who disappeared from them, complicates that view.

Jews who arrived from Arab and Muslim countries after 1948 were often placed in transit camps, the ma'abarot, shantytown settlements for new arrivals. According to the Times of Israel, these immigrants were largely sidelined by the European-born leadership of the ruling Labor party. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021]

The affair began in these camps and the clinics that served them. Families, mostly from Yemen but also from the Balkans, North Africa and elsewhere, say that between 1948 and 1954 their infants were taken from camps and clinics. Doctors told the parents their children had died, but would not give them bodies or death certificates. The families allege that the babies were given to Ashkenazi families, Jews of European origin, in Israel and abroad. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] The children who vanished this way are counted in the hundreds.

The state investigated more than once. Three official inquiries, the first in 1967, concluded that most of the children had died of disease. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] That did not end the dispute. The newspaper Israel Hayom later published documents that, it said, pointed to possible organized involvement by state bodies. [cite:israelhayom_yemenite] As described, those documents show a possibility, not an established finding. The official inquiries and the families' accounts remain in conflict.

The government's formal answer came in February 2021. The cabinet approved NIS 162 million, about $50 million, in compensation, with about 1,050 families eligible. Its decision stated that the government "regrets" the events and recognizes the families' suffering. [cite:tps_yemenite_2021] [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] Prime Minister Netanyahu called the affair one of the most painful in the state's history and asked that it be taught in schools. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021]

The families' organizations were not satisfied. The decision, they said, lacked the essential element: an official apology and an acceptance of responsibility. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] That gap between regret and responsibility is the heart of the matter. The compensation acknowledged suffering. It did not accept responsibility for what had happened.

The affair belongs in an account of 1948 and its aftermath for a reason beyond the individual cases. It is documented evidence of a hierarchy inside the new state: an Ashkenazi establishment, and Mizrahi immigrants, Jews from Arab and Muslim countries, placed in camps under its administration. Readers raised in Israel often learn this part of the record late. It sits beside the laws governing the state's Arab citizens, described in [scene:ap_s01|the Absentees' Property Law]. Together they show that building the state meant deciding how different groups of its people would be treated, and that those decisions were not the same for all.
