A Meeting in Manhattan, July 1945

In July 1945 the war in Europe had just ended, and the full scale of the Holocaust was becoming documented and public. That summer David Ben-Gurion was already thinking about a different war, one not yet declared. He expected Arab nations to go to war against a future Jewish state. He judged that the Haganah, the pre-state militia, had neither the weapons nor the money to survive such a conflict. It was a strategic assessment made nearly three years before independence, not a response to fighting already under way.

On a July afternoon that year, Ben-Gurion convened a small, secret meeting of American Jewish businessmen in the Manhattan apartment of Rudolf Sonneborn. [cite:sonneborn_toi] Sonneborn was a wealthy industrialist who had traveled to Palestine himself and become a friend of Ben-Gurion's. Accounts describe roughly 17 prominent businessmen in the room. Ben-Gurion told them that Arab nations would soon go to war against the Jews of Palestine, and that the Haganah could not meet the threat as it stood. With the news of the Holocaust still fresh, the men agreed on the spot to build what became a Jewish-American arms underground. [cite:sonneborn_toi]

The familiar account of Israel's founding war holds that its weapons arrived only after May 1948, once the state existed and could ask for international support. The documents tell a different story. The supply effort began in 1945, in a private apartment, and ran for three years before there was any state to ask for anything.

What came out of the meeting was not a fundraising drive in the ordinary sense. It was the Sonneborn Institute and its front company, Materials for Palestine. Together they became the financial and logistical backbone of the Haganah's transatlantic supply network, operating under the guise of ordinary commercial activity. [cite:sonneborn_jps] Materials for Palestine presented itself as a commercial and charitable enterprise. Under that cover it bought and shipped machinery, tools and industrial materials, all legally purchasable surplus. Their destination was Ta'as, the clandestine arms-production effort of the Yishuv, the Jewish community of Palestine. [cite:sonneborn_jps] The network would run continuously until 1949, entirely outside any registered or public channel.

The group's most important innovation was legal rather than logistical. It did not operate as an identifiably Zionist body, or as an arm of the Jewish Agency, the body that represented the Yishuv. It was built to look like ordinary business and charity. This was deliberate. The network stood apart from the Jewish Agency specifically to shield the Agency from direct legal exposure for what was, under US law, illegal arms export. [cite:sonneborn_jps] The separation was real but not total. Historians who have documented the network note that high-level figures such as Abba Hillel Silver, chairman of the Jewish Agency's American section, still served as a liaison between the two. [cite:sonneborn_jps] On paper the structure insulated the Agency; in practice it kept a working connection. That does not establish how much any particular Agency official knew. The record documents the insulating design and one named point of contact, not a full accounting of what each individual knew.

Some caution is needed about the meeting itself. Its occurrence, its convener and the organization that grew out of it are well documented. The motives and understanding of individual participants are less certain. The record does not show that every donor fully grasped that the network was operating illegally. Much of the vivid firsthand description of the afternoon, including Leonard Slater's 1970 book The Pledge, was written decades later by a sympathetic author who interviewed participants after the fact. Any participant's account of why he joined, recorded long afterward, deserves the caution due any memory.

Even with those limits, the July 1945 meeting marks the moment when preparation for statehood acquired a material base in the United States. The next question was how that base actually moved weapons, aircraft and money across the world, and how long it could do so before American authorities noticed. That story is taken up in [scene:s07e|the arms network in operation].
