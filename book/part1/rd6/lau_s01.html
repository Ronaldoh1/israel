Lausanne, 1949: Where Return and Borders Were Negotiated, and Failed

In April 1949, a few months after the fighting, the refugee question came before a formal negotiation at Lausanne. Two opposite accounts of what happened there are still repeated. One says Israel never offered anything on refugees. The other says the Arab states refused every offer. The United Nations' own report on the talks supports neither. It shows two sets of conditions, each arranged in an order the other side would not accept.

The forum was the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine, created by Resolution 194 in December 1948 to help the parties reach a settlement. It convened talks at Lausanne from 27 April to 12 September 1949. The Arab delegations refused to negotiate directly with Israel, so the Commission shuttled between the parties. On 12 May they signed the Lausanne Protocol, which took the 1947 partition map as a basis for discussion. [cite:wiki_lausanne_1949] [cite:pij_lausanne]

The positions were set out early. The Arab states demanded that Israel first accept the return of refugees under Resolution 194, and they wanted the 1947 partition lines. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_israel_studies_peace] Israel held that the Arab invasion had nullified the partition plan and that refugees could be dealt with only inside a final peace. Israel first floated annexing Gaza along with its refugee population; Egypt refused.

Then came the offer. On 3 August, according to the Commission's own progress report, Israel offered to accept the return of 100,000 refugees, which would have brought its Arab population to a maximum of 250,000. The offer was made "only as an integral part of a general and final peace settlement." [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report] [cite:jvl_ccp_fourth_report] The Arab delegations called it contrary to Resolution 194 and to the Protocol. Israel later withdrew it. The Commission's report also records Israel blocking a committee on orange groves owned by refugees. [cite:un_ccp_fourth_report]

The conference ended in September without agreement. The United States had already sent Ben-Gurion a secret warning in May. Now it told Israel it might revise relations over the deadlock. What became of that warning is traced in [scene:tr_s02|Truman's post-election policy].

The shape of the failure explains why it lasted. Israel would discuss return only after peace. The Arab states would discuss peace only after return. Each side's sequence was the reverse of the other's, and neither moved. The offer of 100,000 shows that Israel was ready to put a number on the table. The conditions attached to it, and the Arab rejection of those conditions, show why the number never became policy. With no agreement, roughly 700,000 refugees remained refugees.

That is why Lausanne matters. It was not the last negotiation on the question. But it was the one at which the refugee problem stopped being a consequence of the war and became a permanent feature of the conflict. The question it failed to settle has outlived every negotiation since, and in the end it turns on how one reads a single paragraph of Resolution 194.
