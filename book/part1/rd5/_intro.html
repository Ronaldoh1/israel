When the Second World War ended in Europe in 1945, there was no Jewish state and no United Nations decision on Palestine. Within three years both existed. This stage covers that interval, from the summer of 1945 through the founding year of 1948, and asks how the decisive pieces were put in place.

The answer runs along several tracks at once. In a Manhattan apartment in July 1945, David Ben-Gurion set in motion an arms and supply network that operated for three years outside US law. In Washington, a president who had been Vice President for only 82 days inherited the Palestine question and made his choices amid White House advisers, an old friend's personal access, and an approaching election. In Palestine, Britain's mass raid of June 1946 was followed by the King David Hotel bombing, which hardened British opinion toward disengagement and contributed to London's decision to hand the problem to the United Nations. There, the Jewish Agency arrived with a prepared plan, while the Arab Higher Committee boycotted the inquiry.

Throughout, warnings were written down. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Secretary of Defense, the new CIA and critics outside government all objected, in time and on the record. This stage traces who acted, who objected, and why the objections did not prevail.
