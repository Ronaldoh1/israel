Part Thirty follows the institution's record from the medieval heresy trials into the modern world, where its targets were no longer mainly rival churches but fraternities, accused witches and the arts it called forbidden. It takes four subjects in turn. The Rosicrucian manifestos of 1614 to 1616 and the Freemasons, condemned by a papal bull of 1738 and still, in a Vatican note of November 2023, declared incompatible with the faith. The Bavarian Illuminati, a short-lived Enlightenment society banned by its own state in the 1780s, and the conspiracy myth that grew around it and was grafted onto antisemitism. The witch trials of about 1450 to 1750, with what a papal bull and an inquisitor's handbook did, what secular and prince-bishops' courts did, and how many people actually died. And the forbidden arts themselves: alchemy, astrology, divination, tarot and the books placed on the Index. For each the part sets out what the group or practice actually taught, what the Church's documents said about it and when, what states did separately, and where the popular story parts company with the record.
