Annexation Without a Declaration, 2026

By the start of 2026 the formal route was blocked by Washington's stated veto and by Likud's refusal to carry the Knesset bills. The government's measures in the West Bank that year did not declare sovereignty. They changed who records land, who may buy it and where building is tendered. This section sets out three of them, the land-registration decision of 15 February 2026, the related decisions of the same month, and the E1 tender of August 2026, with the government's stated reasons, the objections, and where each stood as of 21 October 2026, six days before Israel's general election.

The February 2026 decisions

On 8 February 2026 the cabinet transferred some demolition powers in Areas A and B, the zones the Oslo agreements left to the Palestinian Authority, to Israeli authorities, and changed the rules on Israelis buying land in the West Bank. [cite:wb_pn_mirabilis] One week later, on 15 February, it approved the registration of land in Area C, the zone under full Israeli control, for the first time since 1967. Area C covered 72–74 per cent of the West Bank when the Oslo II agreement drew the zones in 1995; after the later redeployments it was about 63 per cent by a 2013 count. [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] [cite:wb_wiki_oslo2areas] [scene:wb5_map|The West Bank, Part Five: Oslo's map]

The decision allocated NIS 244.1 million, about $78 million, over four years and up to 35 staff, placed the process in the Justice Ministry rather than the military government, and set a target of completing registration for 15 per cent of Area C within five years. [cite:gi6_fmep_feb2026] Only about a third of the West Bank's land had been registered and titled when Israel took it in 1967, after which Israel froze registration; the rest has remained without a completed title record. [cite:gi6_fmep_feb2026] The mechanism by which Israel has declared unregistered land "state land" since 1979 is described in the West Bank volume. [scene:wb4_land|The West Bank, Part Four: how the land was taken]

The government gave two reasons. A joint statement by the defence minister, Israel Katz, the justice minister, Yariv Levin, and Smotrich said the decision meant "the registration of extensive areas in Judea and Samaria that belong to the state," and Levin called it "a real revolution in Judea and Samaria." [cite:gi6_fmep_feb2026] Katz called registration "a vital security measure designed to ensure control, enforcement, and full freedom of action," and the cabinet described it as an "appropriate response to illegal land registration processes promoted by the Palestinian Authority" that would end disputes. Smotrich said: "We are continuing the revolution of settlement and strengthening our hold across all parts of our land." [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026]

The objections were as direct. The Palestinian Authority's presidency called the decision "a de-facto annexation of occupied Palestinian territory and a declaration of the commencement of annexation plans." [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] Peace Now, the Israeli settlement monitor, called it "a mega land grab of Palestinian property," arguing that most of Area C would pass to the state. A land lawyer for the Israeli rights group Yesh Din, Shlomo Zacharia, said that because unclaimed plots pass automatically to the state and possession weighs heavily, the process favours whoever holds the land in practice, chiefly the Civil Administration and settlers. Arab governments, including some that had normalized relations with Israel, and the UN criticized the month's measures. [cite:gi6_fmep_feb2026] Reuters noted that Trump had ruled out annexation but had not moved to curb settlement. [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026]

E1: the tender before the election

E1, the land between East Jerusalem and the settlement of Ma'ale Adumim, received final planning approval for 3,401 homes in August 2025, after two decades in which successive Israeli governments had held it back under international pressure; Smotrich said then that it would "permanently bury the idea of a Palestinian state." [cite:wb_itv_e1] [scene:wb9_annex|The West Bank, Part Nine: E1] On 18 August 2026 the Housing Ministry published a tender for 1,234 of those homes, about a third, with bids closing on 19 October, eight days before the election. Peace Now called it "a last-minute maneuver" and said the aim was to sign contracts that a new government would find harder to cancel. [cite:gi6_pn_e1tender]

The tender then met two Israeli institutions. Residents and three Israeli organizations, Ir Amim, Bimkom and Peace Now, had petitioned the Jerusalem District Court against the E1 plans, alleging a defective and discriminatory approval process; the state and the Ma'ale Adumim municipality rejected the claims. On 15 June 2026 the court refused the state's motion to dismiss. [cite:gi6_pn_e1tender] On 8 September Judge Nimrod Flax declined to freeze the tender, finding no irreversible harm while construction was distant, without ruling on the legality of the plans, which remained before the court. [cite:gi6_jpost_e1court] On 26 September the Attorney General's Office ordered the bid deadline postponed by a month, past the election, citing the area's diplomatic sensitivity and the aim of "reducing the concern that the tender could be used for prohibited election propaganda"; it did not cancel the tender. Ma'ale Adumim's mayor, Guy Yifrach, said the town would petition against the delay: "We will not give up and will not allow E1 to be turned into a hostage." [cite:gi6_ih_e1postpone]

Where the track stood, 21 October 2026

The Knesset dissolved on 17 July 2026 and set the general election for 27 October. A Channel 12 poll at the time projected neither Netanyahu's bloc nor his opponents reaching the 61 seats needed to govern. [cite:gi6_aje_dissolve] This volume found no report of a further reading of the 2025 sovereignty bills before the dissolution; neither had become law. The International Court of Justice's advisory opinion of July 2024, which found Israel's continued presence in the territory unlawful and called for an end to new settlement, stood unchanged, and Netanyahu's answer to it, "The Jewish people are not occupiers in their own land," remained his government's stated position. [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] [scene:wb10_rulings|The West Bank, Part Ten: the rulings]

Two readings of the same record

What the record establishes as fact is the set of decisions above, each dated and sourced, and the statements of the ministers who made them. Smotrich has described the purpose in his own words, in 2024 a change of the system's "DNA" that would not look like annexation, and in 2026 "strengthening our hold across all parts of our land." [cite:gi6_bg_nyt_smotrich] [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] Read with his 82 per cent map, those statements make annexation of most of the West Bank a documented programme of a named minister and party, the Religious Zionism party, which held the civil powers needed to advance it. [cite:gi6_toi_82pct]

Whether it is also the government's programme is an argument with two sides. The Palestinian Authority and Peace Now argue that the cumulative measures amount to annexation in fact, and the International Court of Justice found in 2024 that Israel's continued presence in the territory was itself unlawful. [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] [cite:gi6_fmep_feb2026] [cite:wb_jurist_icj2024] The government's stated position is different: its prime minister has refused the bills, its statements present registration as order and security, and its official line in 2024 was that the territory's final status would be settled in negotiations. [cite:gi6_toi_readings] [cite:gi6_reuters_reg2026] [cite:gi6_bg_nyt_smotrich] Between them stands a documented pattern that both readings accept: formal sovereignty has been declared over no part of the West Bank since 1967 outside East Jerusalem, [cite:wb_wiki_ejannex] and the administrative changes of 2023–2026 have moved in the direction the sovereignty motions asked for. The election of 27 October 2026 will decide who holds the ministries that made them.
