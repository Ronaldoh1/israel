From the Dam to the Canal, December 1955–26 July 1956

The stage ends with Washington's offer to Egypt withdrawn. For seven months the United States, Britain and the World Bank held out an offer to finance the High Dam at Aswan. On 19 July 1956 the United States withdrew it. A week later Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal Company. The familiar story runs these two events together as cause and consequence. The documents show the sequence clearly enough; what they do not show with one voice is why the offer was withdrawn, and the officials who took part gave different answers in their later recollections.

The offer dated from 16 December 1955. It was a joint undertaking of the United States, the United Kingdom and the World Bank. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike] Its terms are known in detail only from later recollection. Parker Hart, in an oral history recorded for the Association for Diplomatic Studies and Training, recalled that the United States was prepared to make a grant of about $56 million at once, with roughly 85 per cent of the grant share coming from the United States and 15 per cent from Britain. The American loan that was to follow carried conditions: Egyptian fiscal reforms, and a prior agreement between Egypt and Sudan on the Nile waters. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] Hart remembered Nasser's reaction to the conditions in one line: "This is a denigration of Egypt." [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] These are the figures of one participant's oral history, recorded long afterward. No primary text of the offer is in the record used here.

The objection can be read against the year before. In 1954 the same government had negotiated British troops out of the Canal Zone, in an agreement that recognized the Canal as "an integral part of Egypt"; on one reading, conditions on its fiscal policy and on its dealings with Sudan touched the same question of who decided Egypt's affairs. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_suez_base] The offer was nonetheless kept open, and by July 1956 Egypt's ambassador, Ahmed Hussein, was returning to Washington to announce that Egypt accepted it. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike]

The decision to withdraw was taken before he arrived. At 9:40 on the morning of 19 July 1956 Eisenhower met John Foster Dulles and Herbert Hoover Jr. at the White House. The meeting lasted twelve minutes. The President agreed with Dulles that "we should withdraw the U.S. offer upon the occasion of the Ambassador's visit." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike] The memorandum records four reasons: Egypt's economic conditions had changed since the offer was made; the dispute over the Nile waters was unresolved; the austerity the project would demand would make whoever built the dam unpopular in Egypt; and it had become harder to work with Nasser's government. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike]

That afternoon, from 4:10 to 5:07, Dulles received Hussein at the State Department. He told him it was "not feasible at present" to go ahead. He put the cost of the dam at $1.3 billion, of which $900 million would have to be found inside Egypt. He said that "no single project in the Mutual Security Program was as unpopular today," and that the dam should be "put on the shelf." Then he gave a reason that had not appeared in the morning's list in these words: the basic opposition, he said, came from a feeling that Egypt was working with those "hostile to us." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein]

Hussein made Egypt's case in reply, and it deserves to be heard at the strength he gave it. The dam, he said, was "the only solution to the population problem." Moscow was making a "very generous" offer. And "there were strong pressures which Nasser might not be able to resist." Dulles answered that any Soviet offer would be made "for political purposes." [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein] The exchange set out the two positions: for Egypt's ambassador the dam was the answer to the population problem, and for Dulles the choice of financier was a political question.

Why the offer was withdrawn is the question this section cannot close, because those who took part answered it differently. The first answer is the one in the documents of the day: a policy judgment about Egypt itself. Its economy had changed, the Nile question was open, the austerity would turn Egyptians against the builder, and Nasser's government was drawing closer to the Soviet bloc. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein] The compilers of the oral histories add that the Czech arms deal of September 1955 ([scene:cw2_czech_arms|the arms race]) and Egypt's recognition of Communist China had angered Dulles. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan]

The second answer is domestic politics. Robert Bowie recalled that Congress was turning hostile to the project and that the hostility came from several directions at once: cotton growers, supporters of Israel and the China lobby. Members threatened legislation that would forbid the use of aid funds for the dam. Fraser Wilkins called Congress the "real reason." [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] Read carefully, this account names three separate constituencies, cotton growers, supporters of Israel and the China lobby, converging in one Congress; it does not describe a single hand behind the decision, and Dulles's own remark to Hussein about the project's unpopularity is consistent with it without proving it. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein]

The third answer is personal. Parker Hart called Dulles's decision "impulsive." James Cortada, in the same oral-history collection, read it the other way round, as a trap that Nasser had set for Washington. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] On one reading, the record of the morning meeting does not support the word impulsive in any simple sense, since the decision came with four stated reasons and was taken in advance of the ambassador's visit; nor does it rule out that the reasons were assembled for a conclusion already reached.

A week later, on 26 July 1956, Nasser announced the nationalization of the Suez Canal Company, and offered full compensation. The State Department's own historians note that the company had been jointly British and French owned and operated. [cite:rs_cw2_state_suez] Bowie recalled that the nationalization came "within a matter of ten days" of the withdrawal. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] The interval was in fact seven days, from 19 to 26 July; the dates are those of the primary record. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_aswan_ike] [cite:rs_cw2_state_suez]

The money in this episode moved, or failed to move, along clear lines. On the offer as Hart recalled it, the United States would have carried most of the grant and Britain the rest, with the World Bank alongside. Dulles's own figures put the greater part of the cost on Egypt: $900 million of $1.3 billion was to be raised internally. [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein] When the offer was withdrawn, by the oral histories' account, Egypt turned to the Soviet Union; Wilkins later described the dam as a "$600 million project under the Russians." [cite:rs_cw2_adst_aswan] That figure and Dulles's $1.3 billion are not reconciled in the sources used here, and they may not measure the same thing. The Canal Company passed from its British and French owners to Egypt, with full compensation offered to them. [cite:rs_cw2_state_suez]

Egypt's case, as far as the record used here gives it, rested on sovereignty and on need: the 1954 accord with Britain had recognized the Canal as "an integral part of Egypt", and the dam was, in its ambassador's words, the only answer to the country's population problem. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_suez_base] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_dulles_hussein] The British and French case, and what both governments did about it, belong to the next stage.

What remains open is the weight of each reason. The memoranda of 19 July record what Eisenhower and Dulles said to each other and to Egypt's ambassador; the oral histories record what officials remembered later; neither tells us which consideration was decisive, or whether the threatened congressional ban would have passed. The record used here also gives no primary text of the December 1955 offer itself. On the evening of 26 July 1956 the Canal Company had been nationalized by Egypt, the dam had no Western financier, and the crisis that followed is told in Stage Three, with Egypt's side of it in [scene:egi_src_s02|Egypt and Suez].
