Who Pays, and How It Compares

Two questions follow from the totals. Where does the money come from? And how large is it beside the other interests that spend in American elections? This section answers both from the disclosures. It names donors only where the public record names them as the largest givers to the committees studied here, and it keeps separate a kind of giving that is often added to pro-Israel totals but does not belong in them.

The super PAC's donors. A super PAC must report its donors, and the reports show that AIPAC's super PAC depends on a small number of very large gifts. In its first cycle AIPAC itself gave it $8.5 million, Jan Koum, a co-founder of WhatsApp, $2 million, and Jonathon Jacobson, Paul Singer, Bernard Marcus and Haim Saban $1 million each. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] In 2024 the largest donors were Koum ($5 million), Jacobson (nearly $4.6 million), Marcus ($3 million) and David Zalik ($2 million). [cite:factcheck_udp_2024] In 2026 FactCheck.org named Singer, Blair Frank, Marc Rowan and Saban among its top donors. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] The PAC is different: its money comes in earmarked contributions from more than 10,000 members, each capped at the individual limit. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024] The network's two committees therefore rest on two different bases, a broad membership for the money given to candidates and a narrow group of very wealthy donors for the money spent against them.

A kind of giving kept separate. Some of the largest donors associated with the cause of Israel in American politics give most of their money not to pro-Israel committees but to general party committees. Sheldon and Miriam Adelson are the leading example. New York magazine's tally of their lifetime political giving is about $273 million for Sheldon Adelson, who died in 2021, and $284 million for Miriam Adelson; in 2018 they spent $123 million on the midterm elections, nearly all of it benefiting Republicans, and in 2024 Miriam Adelson gave about $100 million to a super PAC supporting Donald Trump. [cite:wiki_miriam_adelson] [cite:pm_sludge_2019] [cite:wiki_preserve_america] Their stated priorities included moving the American embassy to Jerusalem, which Trump did in his first term. [cite:aj_adelson_knesset] That giving is real and large, but it went to committees whose purpose was to elect Republicans, not to support Israel, and adding it to "pro-Israel money" would mix two different things. This volume reports it here, names it as what it is, and leaves it out of the pro-Israel totals. The same rule applies to Haim Saban's gifts to Democratic committees, $3.3 million in 2018. [cite:pm_sludge_2019] The single exception in the totals is Miriam Adelson's $5 million to the Republican Jewish Coalition's super PAC in 2024, which was a pro-Israel committee's money and is counted. [cite:pm_israelite_2024]

The scale in proportion. Against other single-issue spenders, pro-Israel money is large but not the largest. The cryptocurrency industry's super PAC, Fairshake, and its affiliates spent more than $133 million in 2024, more than AIPAC's two committees by most counts, and had raised more than $193 million for 2026 by January of that year. [cite:pm_wiki_fairshake] Against the great economic interests it is smaller still: in 2018 the securities industry spent $389 million on federal elections and real estate $186 million. [cite:pm_guardian_2019] Against other foreign-policy causes, on the other hand, it has no near rival in the record used here. In 2018 the US-Cuba Democracy PAC, one of the best known of them, gave about $171,000. [cite:pm_guardian_2019]

One more channel, counted elsewhere. The Guardian's 2018 analysis added a figure from a different law. Agents registered under the Foreign Agents Registration Act on behalf of Israeli principals reported about $46.3 million in 2017 and 2018, the third-largest country total after Japan and South Korea; about $2.1 million of it was for political activity and $44.2 million for tourism promotion and other commercial work. [cite:pm_guardian_2019] That money is the Israeli state's and Israeli companies' own, spent openly through registered agents, and it is the subject of Part Nine.

The comparisons point in two directions, and both should be kept. Measured against all the money in American politics, pro-Israel spending is a middle-sized interest, smaller than finance or real estate and, in recent cycles, than cryptocurrency. Measured against other causes concerned with a foreign country, it is in a class of its own. What makes it unusual is less its size than its use: spent through every channel, in both parties, and, at its largest, against sitting members of Congress.
