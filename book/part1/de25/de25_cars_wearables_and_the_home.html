The Car, the Wrist and the Living Room

The phone is not the only object that reports on its owner. A connected car, a watch, a television, a speaker and a doorbell each send some of what they sense to their maker. The distinction that matters here is between what a device could collect, a matter of its sensors, and what its maker says it collects, keeps and hands over, a matter of policy that can change with an update. The state can reach that data by three routes: a legal demand on the company, seizure of the device, or purchase in the market. This section takes the car, the body and the home in turn, and closes the part with the book's test of its worst-case reading.

Cars: the driving record and who gets it

Telematics is the term for the data a connected car sends home: location, speed, braking, acceleration, mileage. In March 2024 the New York Times reporter Kashmir Hill showed that General Motors' OnStar Smart Driver feature had passed drivers' trip records, including hard braking and rapid acceleration, to risk-profiling companies such as LexisNexis, which supplied insurers; some drivers' reports ran to hundreds of pages, and a salesman told her he always clicked "yes" on the enrolment screen himself. GM stopped sharing the data with brokers and ended Smart Driver. [cite:de25_mkt_gm] Texas sued GM in August 2024, alleging that trip data from most of its 2015-and-later vehicles had been sold to companies that built "Driving Scores" for insurers, affecting more than 1.5 million Texans. [cite:de25_tx_gm] In January 2026 the Federal Trade Commission, by two votes to none, finalised a 20-year order that bars GM for five years from sharing geolocation and driving-behaviour data with consumer reporting agencies, requires express consent for collecting or sharing connected-car data, and lets owners delete it and switch off precise location. [cite:de25_rdn_gm] [cite:de25_tc_gm] The case was about insurers, not police; no government purchase of car telematics has been reported.

Police reach car data by asking the manufacturer. On 30 April 2024 Senators Ron Wyden and Edward Markey published the automakers' answers to their questions about location data. Five companies, GM, Honda, Ford, Tesla and Stellantis, said they required a warrant unless there was an emergency or the owner consented. Hyundai accepted a warrant or another court order. Toyota, Nissan, Subaru, BMW, Mazda, Mercedes-Benz and Kia disclosed location on a subpoena, which a prosecutor or agency can issue without a judge; Volkswagen did so for up to six days of data. Hyundai kept location for up to 15 years, Toyota 10 and Honda 7, while Mercedes-Benz kept only the last parked position. Only Tesla told its customers when the government asked for their data. [cite:de25_wm_letter]

The second route is the car itself. Berla's iVe kit, plugged into a vehicle's infotainment system, recovers texts, call logs and contacts synced from a driver's phone, GPS tracks, and the times doors opened and the ignition turned on, across 20,752 vehicle types by March 2022. Customs and Border Protection paid more than $450,000 for five kits, as The Intercept reported in 2021, and courts in at least six states have allowed police to download such data without a warrant. A Homeland Security spokesperson said in 2023 that the department "no longer partners" with Berla. [cite:de25_rec_berla] In practice its investigative arm, Homeland Security Investigations, placed a roughly $130,000 order with Berla in 2025, posted a notice on 28 August 2026 to renew it without competition, and in September 2025 paid $311,250 for 325 covert GPS trackers made by Ensurity. [cite:de25_bu_berla]

Tesla: what the notice says and what has happened

Tesla's cameras, satellite positioning and permanent cellular link could in principle record a continuous history of where a car went and what was around it. What Tesla says it does is narrower. It told the senators in 2024 that it receives a car's location only for "a critical safety event (such as a collision, an airbag deployment, or automatic emergency braking event)." [cite:de25_wm_letter] Its customer privacy notice says that camera recordings shared for development "remain anonymous and are not linked to you or your vehicle." [cite:de25_reu_tesla] Its law-enforcement guidelines require a valid legal basis or the customer's consent, allow emergency disclosure only on a good-faith belief of "danger of death or serious physical injury," and promise "reasonable efforts" to tell customers unless a court order forbids it. [cite:de25_tesla_ler]

Three episodes test those words. In April 2023 Reuters reported, from nine former employees, that groups of Tesla staff had shared customer camera recordings in internal chats between 2019 and 2022, among them a crash in which a car hit a child on a bicycle; seven said the labelling tool could show where a recording was made. Tesla did not respond. [cite:de25_reu_tesla] In 2024 Oakland police obtained court orders in at least three cases to tow Teslas and copy the Sentry Mode footage that parked cars record onto a drive inside the vehicle; footage of a shooting on 13 July helped charge two people with murder. [cite:de25_p1_oakland] After a Cybertruck exploded in Las Vegas on 1 January 2025, the county sheriff said Elon Musk had sent footage from Tesla charging stations across the country "directly to us" and explained how the vehicle had locked, and Musk posted that the vehicle's telemetry was normal at the time of the blast. No legal process was mentioned. [cite:de25_bi_vegas] Whether that was an emergency disclosure under Tesla's own rules is not documented.

Musk's role in the government in 2025, described in [scene:de11_tesla_spacex_and_the_state|Part Eleven] and [scene:de19_the_access|Part Nineteen], raised a further question. On 5 February 2025 Senator Richard Blumenthal asked Tesla and five other Musk companies whether they had received "any nonpublic data" from the Department of Government Efficiency or supplied it with servers or AI models. [cite:de25_blum_letters] The letters are questions. No transfer of federal data to Tesla, and no bulk transfer of Tesla customer data to the government, had been documented as of October 2026.

Wearables and health apps

Most Americans assume their health information is protected by HIPAA, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996. It binds doctors, hospitals and insurers, not the makers of fitness bands, watches, rings or apps sold to consumers, whose data is governed by their own terms of service. [cite:de25_ahcj_rfk] That gap widened in importance when Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. told a House subcommittee on 24 June 2025 that wearables are "a way people can take control of their own health" and announced a large campaign to encourage them; reporters paraphrased his goal as every American wearing one within four years. [cite:de25_ahcj_rfk]

The record shows wearable data used in criminal cases and health data leaking into advertising. Connie Dabate's Fitbit recorded her moving for about an hour after the time her husband said an intruder had shot her in Connecticut on 23 December 2015; he was sentenced on 18 August 2022 to 65 years. [cite:de25_ct_dabate] On 5 August 2025 a California jury found that Meta had violated the state's Invasion of Privacy Act by collecting Flo app users' menstrual data; Google and Flo had settled, and Meta said it would explore "all legal options." [cite:de25_tc_flo] No case of police obtaining a period-tracking app's records had been reported as of October 2026. Company design makes a difference here: Apple says Health app data on a current, passcode-protected device with two-factor sign-in is end-to-end encrypted and "not readable by anyone — even Apple," and that it "does not sell" health data, though the data remains on the phone, where the extraction tools in [scene:de25_spyware_and_phones|the previous section] can reach it. [cite:de25_apple_health] Washington State's My Health My Data Act, in force since March 2024, covers health data including location that suggests a person sought care, and lets individuals sue; the first suit was filed against Amazon on 10 February 2025. [cite:de25_wh_mhmda]

The living room and the front door

Many smart televisions use automatic content recognition, or ACR: at intervals the set takes a sample of the picture or sound, reduces it to a digital fingerprint and matches it against a database of programmes and advertisements, so the maker knows what is being watched and when. [cite:de25_mb_samsung] In 2017 Vizio paid $2.2 million to the FTC and New Jersey for capturing "second-by-second" viewing on 11 million televisions and selling it without consent. [cite:de25_ftc_vizio] In December 2025 Texas sued Sony, Samsung, LG, Hisense and TCL, alleging screen captures "as often as every half second"; Samsung settled by March 2026, agreeing not to collect such data from Texans without explicit, informed consent. [cite:de25_pg_acr] [cite:de25_mb_samsung] As with cars, the documented buyers are advertisers; no government purchase of television viewing data has been reported.

Voice assistants listen for a wake word and send what follows to the company. On 28 March 2025 Amazon ended an option that let some Echo devices process requests locally, so that all recordings would go to its cloud for the new Alexa+. [cite:de25_tc_alexa] In an Arkansas murder case of 2015, Amazon refused to release a suspect's Echo recordings without "a valid and binding legal demand," then released them when he consented; prosecutors also used his smart water meter's readings, and dropped the charges in November 2017. [cite:de25_npr_bates] In 2023 the FTC obtained $25 million from Amazon over children's Alexa recordings and $5.8 million in refunds from Ring, one of whose employees had watched thousands of female customers' private videos, including in bathrooms and bedrooms. [cite:de25_thn_ring]

Ring, Amazon's doorbell company, built the most direct channel between household cameras and police. In July 2022 Amazon told Senator Markey that 2,161 agencies were on its Neighbors service and that Ring had given footage to police without the owner's consent 11 times that year, each on its own finding of "imminent danger of death or serious physical injury." [cite:de25_ax_ring] Local police can ask Ring owners for video through the evidence platform of Axon, a police-equipment company; owners may decline. In February 2026 Ring cancelled a planned integration with the licence-plate camera company Flock after public criticism, and said that it "has no partnership with ICE" and that federal agencies cannot submit such requests. [cite:de25_cnn_ring] Google's Nest cameras raised a different question. In February 2026 FBI Director Kash Patel said video from a missing woman's Nest doorbell in Arizona had been "recovered from residual data located in backend systems," although the sheriff said she had no subscription that saved video; Google did not explain its retention. [cite:de25_nbc_nest]

The devices in an ordinary home could, between them, record where a family drives, how it sleeps, what it watches and who comes to the door. What the record shows the state doing with them is narrower and older in form: case-by-case demands, warrants, a forensic kit for cars, and voluntary or emergency disclosures.

The worst-case reading, tested

The book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, reads immigration enforcement as the occasion for building a surveillance machine that could serve a sovereign executive without a veto. This part bears on the machine's eyes and ears.

Documented: federal agencies buy phone location data drawn from apps and advertising auctions without a warrant, and in 2026 both the FBI and ICE said or showed that they do; the products can list every device seen at a place. [cite:de25_tc_patel] [cite:de25_404_webloc] Documented: ICE now holds remote spyware that reads encrypted messages, forensic tools for seized phones and cars, covert trackers, and cell-site simulators. [cite:ow10_tc_lyons] [cite:de25_fs_forecast] [cite:de25_bu_berla] [cite:de25_tc_tosv] Documented: these purchases ran under both parties. Location buying began in 2019 and 2020 under Trump's first administration and continued under Biden; ICE bought simulators and signed the Paragon contract under Biden; the one sharp political reversal was the revival in August 2025 of a spyware contract the previous administration had paused. [cite:de25_vice_venntel] [cite:de25_oig_ctd] [cite:ow10_poulson_ice] Documented too: the agencies broke their own privacy rules in using bought location data. [cite:de25_oig_ctd]

Probable: that bought data is now the route of least resistance. The courts have narrowed the compelled routes, through Carpenter, Chatrie and, in one district, the end of tower dumps, while no court has ruled on purchase and the FTC's orders bind sellers only. [cite:de25_crs_chatrie] [cite:de25_wlox_tower] [cite:de25_ftc_gravy] This is a structural reading of documented law, not a documented policy decision by any agency. Probable as well: that ICE's location tools are used to find people for removal, since CBP's purchases were reported to have led to arrests at the border; the specific uses of Webloc have not been published. [cite:de25_vice_venntel] [cite:de25_404_webloc]

Not supported: that any of these tools has been used in the United States against a journalist, an activist or a political opponent; the documented spyware abuse in this part took place in Italy and Serbia. [cite:de25_tc_italy] [cite:de25_ci_serbia] Not supported: that the state buys car, television or wearable data, or that Musk's companies received government data through DOGE or handed customer data over in bulk. [cite:de25_blum_letters] Not supported: a single system joining a person's phone, car, home and body into one live picture; each layer exists, and their joining is the subject of the Palantir part. Where the record runs against the hypothesis it does so concretely: the FBI tested Pegasus and refused to deploy it; the Supreme Court upheld nearly $200 million in fines on carriers that sold location; Ring says federal agencies cannot use its request channel, and Tesla tells its customers when police ask. [cite:ow10_ai_nytmag] [cite:de25_rec_fcc] [cite:de25_cnn_ring] [cite:de25_wm_letter] Capability, on this part's evidence, is well ahead of documented intent. How the identification of faces, the view from drones and satellites, the code and cloud underneath, and the money rails add to it is the subject of [scene:de26_faces_drones_satellites|Part Twenty-Six]; the platform that joins the layers is examined in [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Seven], and the running tally of these grades is kept in [scene:de40_worst_case_scorecard|the final part].
