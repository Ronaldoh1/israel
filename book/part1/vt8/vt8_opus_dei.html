Opus Dei: a Prelature, Its Critics and a Pope's Reform

Opus Dei is younger than the Jesuits by almost four centuries and much smaller in priests, but it carries a reputation for quiet influence that rivals theirs. Its reputation comes from three sources: its unusual legal form inside the Church, the public careers of some of its members, and the testimony of former members. This section takes each in turn, sets out how Pope Francis reduced its standing in Church law in 2022 and 2023, and places the criticism beside the organisation's own stated position.

From a Madrid foundation to a personal prelature

Josemaría Escrivá, a Spanish priest, founded Opus Dei ("the Work of God") in Madrid on 2 October 1928. Its message was that ordinary lay people could seek holiness through their daily work, without becoming monks, nuns or priests. The Holy See approved it as a secular institute, provisionally in 1947 and definitively in 1950 under Pius XII. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus]

John Paul II gave it a form no other body has held. By the apostolic constitution Ut sit of 28 November 1982 he erected Opus Dei as a personal prelature. [cite:vt8_va_charisma] A diocese is defined by territory: its bishop has authority over the Catholics who live in it. A personal prelature is defined by persons: its prelate has authority over the priests incardinated in it and over the lay members for the purposes the prelature serves, wherever they live, while those lay members remain parishioners of their local dioceses. Opus Dei remained the only personal prelature. John Paul II beatified Escrivá in 1992 and canonised him in 2002, and Fernando Ocáriz has been the prelate since 23 January 2017. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus]

Who belongs

Opus Dei states that it has about 93,000 members, of whom about 2,095, or 2 per cent, are priests. About 70 per cent are supernumeraries, mostly married people living ordinary family lives. Numeraries are celibate and usually live in Opus Dei centres, available for the organisation's apostolic work and the formation of other members; associates are celibate but live with their families or where their work requires. [cite:vt8_od_members] Female "numerary assistants" do the domestic work of the centres; their testimony is at the centre of the criticism discussed below. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus] Opus Dei has no monks or nuns, despite its portrayal in The Da Vinci Code. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus]

Francis's reform, 2022 and 2023

On 14 July 2022 Francis issued the motu proprio Ad charisma tuendum ("to protect the charism"), which took effect on 4 August 2022. It moved Opus Dei from the Dicastery for Bishops to the Dicastery for the Clergy: "the Prelature depends on the Dicastery for the Clergy". It required the prelate to report to that dicastery every year. It ruled that "the Prelate shall not be honoured with the episcopal order", so the head of Opus Dei would no longer be made a bishop, and gave him instead the title of Supernumerary Apostolic Protonotary, a rank of monsignor. [cite:vt8_va_charisma] The change took the prelature out of the line of bodies treated like dioceses and placed it beside clergy associations.

A second motu proprio, in force from 8 August 2023, rewrote the two canons of the Code of Canon Law that govern personal prelatures. Canon 295 now says a personal prelature is "similar to public clerical associations of pontifical law with the ability to incardinate clerics", headed by a Moderator with the faculties of an Ordinary. Canon 296 now says that lay people "can dedicate themselves to the apostolic works of the personal prelature", language that describes them as cooperating in its works rather than as its members in the sense of a flock. [cite:vt8_vn_prelatures2023] Opus Dei was required to revise its statutes to fit. After an extraordinary internal congress in April 2023 it submitted revised statutes to the Dicastery for the Clergy on 11 June 2025; its prelate said the prelature "awaits the Holy See's response". [cite:vt8_exaudi_statutes]

The response had not been published as of October 2026. On 16 February 2026, according to a prelature statement, Leo XIV told Ocáriz that the updating of the statutes "continues in its study phase and that no publication date can yet be foreseen". [cite:vt8_ncr_leo_statutes] No later approval was found in the sources checked for this volume.

Influence: what is documented

The best-documented case of political influence is Spain. Several Opus Dei members served as ministers under Franco, among them Alberto Ullastres, and critics have cited their presence as evidence of the organisation's power inside the regime. The historians Paul Preston and Brian Crozier concluded that these ministers were chosen for their technical ability rather than their membership. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus] The finding the record supports is that individual members reached high office; the claim that Opus Dei as an organisation directed Spanish policy through them is contested and is not settled by the appointments themselves.

The same distinction applies to Rome. Opus Dei's rise in Church law under John Paul II, from secular institute to the only personal prelature, and the canonisation of its founder in 2002 are documented. So is its reduction under Francis. [cite:vt8_va_charisma] [cite:vt8_vn_prelatures2023] A body that controlled the papacy would not have been moved to a lower dicastery, lost the episcopal rank of its head and been made to rewrite its statutes, which as of October 2026 still awaited the pope's decision. [cite:vt8_ncr_leo_statutes]

The criticism and the response

Criticism of Opus Dei comes mostly from former members. María del Carmen Tapia, who held senior posts in the organisation, and Vladimir Felzmann, a former Opus Dei priest, published accounts describing strict control of members' lives, correspondence and reading. Critics also describe recruitment through friendships and social events and the practice of corporal mortification, including the cilice, a spiked band worn on the thigh. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus] In 2024 a joint investigation by the Financial Times and El País, based on testimony from former numerary assistants, reported that women had been "coerced into domestic servitude" through a system of psychological control; the same year Gareth Gore's book Opus described the organisation's finances and recruitment. Opus Dei denied the allegations, said that membership requires repeated, explicit personal consent over several years, and called Gore's book "a false picture of Opus Dei based on distorted facts, conspiracy theories and outright lies". Its spokespeople have also admitted mistakes in the treatment of some members. [cite:vt8_wiki_opus]

The most serious case is in Argentina. Forty-three women who were members between 1974 and 2015 complained; prosecutors described a system that targeted girls and alleged that they had been made to work without pay under false promises of education and employment. After a two-year investigation with the federal office for trafficking and exploitation, prosecutors named four former regional vicars, and on 11 June 2025 asked to question Mariano Fazio, the organisation's auxiliary vicar, who had led it in Argentina from 2010 to 2014. [cite:vt8_ncr_argentina2025] Opus Dei "categorically rejects the accusation of trafficking and exploitation" and says the complaint misrepresents a vocation the women freely chose. [cite:vt8_od_argentina] As of 11 September 2026 the judge in charge, Daniel Rafecas of Federal Court No. 3 in Buenos Aires, had not carried out any of the requested questionings, and a survivors' organisation, Ending Clergy Abuse, had asked him to move the case forward. [cite:vt8_lcmdp_2026] No one has been charged; the accused are entitled to the presumption of innocence.

The documented picture is of a lay organisation of modest size whose standing in Church law rose under one pope and fell under another, and whose treatment of some of its own members is the subject of an unresolved criminal investigation that it denies. That is a record of influence and of serious allegations. It is not a record of control over the Holy See.
