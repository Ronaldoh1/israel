From Programmer to Mencius Moldbug

Yarvin left Berkeley in the early 1990s for the technology industry, and the company he joined is the first employer in his record that can be named. Siegel reported in Tablet that he worked at a mobile software company founded in 1996 as Libris, renamed Unwired Planet and then Phone.com, one of the firms that built the first systems for reading the web on mobile phones. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] Kofman reported in The New Yorker that he helped design an early mobile browser there. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] Programming remained his trade and his preferred self-description: Siegel noted that he calls himself a computer engineer or programmer because he thinks of himself as a builder. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel]

The two profiles give different figures for what the job paid him, and both are reported here. Yarvin, quoted by Siegel, said that after the dot-com crash a buyout left him with "a few hundred thousand dollars," which he described as considerably less than enough never to work again. [cite:de5_audit_tablet] Kofman wrote that the company's stock-market listing brought him roughly a million dollars. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] The accounts may describe different payments, a share of the listing and a later buyout, or the same money remembered differently; no filing examined here settles it. Both agree on what the money did. It gave a man in his late twenties several years in San Francisco without an employer, and he spent them reading. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] The crash, in his own words, also left him with "a newly acquired girlfriend (who would become my wife)," Jennifer Kollmer. [cite:de5_audit_tablet] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin]

The reading

His own account of the reading has been consistent across interviews. He began, he told Siegel, with the Austrian school of economics, Ludwig von Mises and Murray Rothbard, and then moved to Hans-Hermann Hoppe, a German-born libertarian economist whose 2001 book Democracy: The God That Failed argued that a hereditary monarch, as the owner of his realm, has stronger reasons to preserve it than an elected caretaker who holds power for a term. "Hoppe opened a kind of door to the pre-revolutionary world for me," Yarvin said. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] The historian Joshua Tait, in the first scholarly account of Yarvin's thought, reported that the lawyer and blogger Glenn Reynolds had introduced him to Mises and Rothbard, and that Thomas Carlyle, James Burnham and Hoppe together prompted his turn to authoritarianism. [cite:de3_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de3_tait] Kofman, too, credits Hoppe's book with pushing him towards monarchy. [cite:de3_wiki_dtgtf]

The influence ran one way. Hoppe, asked in 2025, said he did not follow Yarvin's writing closely, "too much rambling for my taste," that he had met him once at a gathering to which Peter Thiel had invited them both, and that his own ideal of many small private-law territories was not the same thing as a state run like a business. [cite:de3_hoppe_reply] A reader's debt to an author is not the author's endorsement of the reader, and [scene:de3_lineage|Part Three] traces the inheritance in full.

He was also a long-standing participant in early online argument. Siegel described a formative period in late adolescence on Usenet, the pre-web discussion network, including a group called talk.bizarre. [cite:de5_audit_tablet] The habits of that culture, long posts, irony, provocation and an appetite for argument with strangers, are visible in everything he later wrote; his admirer Nick Land would write in 2012 that "irony is Moldbug's method, as well as his milieu." [cite:de4_land_de]

Two projects: a computer and a blog

In 2002, by the usual account, he began designing Urbit, a personal computing system built from scratch, with its own programming languages and its own network of permanent digital identities. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] It stayed a private project for years; its first public code release came in January 2010, announced in a post titled "Urbit: functional programming from scratch." [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] Urbit would become his company and the subject of the next section.

The other project made him known. In 2007, under the pen name Mencius Moldbug, he started a blog called Unqualified Reservations. Its first post, "A formalist manifesto," dated 23 April 2007, set out the idea he called formalism: that the way to end political conflict is to recognise, and then formalise as property, whatever distribution of power actually exists. "We need a rule that tells us whose wallet is whose," he wrote. [cite:de6_le_contra] Over the next two years came the texts on which his reputation rests: the posts defining neocameralism in 2007, a book-length "Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives" from April 2008, which Kofman estimates at about 120,000 words and which proposed a "CEO-monarch," the "Patchwork" series of November 2008, a "Gentle Introduction" series in 2009, and three essays on Carlyle in 2009 and 2010. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de3_princeton_carlyle] [scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two] reads the canon in order; [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six] sets out the program and the hardest passages.

The blog's reach in its first years was small and specialised, a conversation among libertarian and contrarian bloggers. It widened in 2012, when Nick Land's long serial essay "The Dark Enlightenment" presented Moldbug as the central figure of a "neo-reactionary" current, credited him with the Cathedral and neocameralism, and called him, half in jest, the movement's "supreme Sith Lord." [cite:de2_land_ebook] It widened again in November 2013, when the technology site TechCrunch ran a piece on the "neoreactionaries" and connected the pen name to Yarvin, a San Francisco software entrepreneur. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] [cite:de1_finley_tc]

The end of Moldbug

By the time he was identified, the blog was winding down. In 2012 he gave his best-known public talk, at a technology "unconference" called BIL, proposing that a new national leader "retire all government employees"; the program it set out is read in [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]. [cite:de6_pein_2014] He largely stopped posting in 2013 to concentrate on Urbit, went quiet in 2014 after seven years, and in April 2016 announced that Unqualified Reservations had "completed its mission." [cite:de2_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] The pen name did not disappear; it became a label others used about him, and he returned to political writing four years later under his own name, as the last section of this part describes.

Two things about this period bear on the questions this book asks. The first is that the writing and the engineering were separate enterprises in public: Urbit was presented to investors and programmers as a technical project, and the political blog was pseudonymous until a journalist connected the two. When the connection became public, it followed him into his company: the Strange Loop programming conference withdrew an invitation to speak about Urbit in 2015, and speakers and sponsors left LambdaConf in 2016 when its organisers kept him on the programme. [cite:de5_dailydot] The second is that the money behind both enterprises in these years was his own. The documented outside money, from Peter Thiel's Founders Fund and others, arrived with the company in 2013, and the next section follows it. [cite:de5_wiki_urbit]
