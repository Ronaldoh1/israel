Roosevelt's Position: Warm in Public, Cautious in Private

A common account of Franklin Roosevelt holds that he was a committed supporter of Jewish statehood, and that his successor simply failed to honour the one specific promise he had made to the Saudi king, Ibn Saud, before he died. The record for the war years supports a more complicated picture. It shows two Roosevelts, each documented: one who sent warm, friendly messages to American Jewish organizations, and one who, in private meetings and in writing to Arab governments, held a cooler and more cautious line.

The first piece of evidence comes from a man with every reason to hope for the opposite. Rabbi Stephen Wise, a leader of Reform Judaism who co-chaired the American Zionist Emergency Council, met Roosevelt several times during the war, as did Chaim Weizmann, both seeking firmer support for a Jewish state. Wise left at least one of these private meetings, by his own account, "surprised and shocked" by the president's actual position, which was considerably cooler than the messages Roosevelt sent to Jewish audiences. [cite:brandeis_center_fdr_zionism] The account is first-person and comes from an invested, sympathetic witness rather than a hostile critic. Its limit is equally plain: it records Wise's characterization of his own reaction, not a transcript of what Roosevelt said.

The second is a decision. Roosevelt approved a State Department plan to suppress public discussion of the Palestine question for the duration of the war. The approval is documented in declassified records examined by historians studying the gap between Roosevelt's stated and actual policy. [cite:brandeis_center_fdr_zionism] There is a real difference between a president who simply does not raise a subject and one who approves a plan to keep it out of public debate; this was the second kind of choice. The records cited here establish that he approved the plan. They do not describe how it was carried out in practice.

The third is the State Department's own later description of its correspondence. On 5 April 1945 Roosevelt wrote to Ibn Saud, following their meeting aboard the USS Quincy in February, which is told in full in [scene:s07ca|the Great Bitter Lake meeting]. When the department confirmed the text of that letter in a press release of 18 October 1945, it described it as one of "several letters addressed by President Roosevelt to Arab leaders," each carrying the same assurance that the United States would consult before acting. [cite:state_dept_oct1945_release] This is not a later historian's reconstruction of scattered evidence. It is the government's own contemporary statement that the promise went to more than one Arab leader. The release does not name every recipient government beyond Ibn Saud, and the sources here do not supply the list.

Put together, the three pieces describe a pattern rather than an exception. Historians working on this period, including work cited in the Brandeis Center's review of newly available documents, note that Roosevelt's warm, boilerplate pro-Zionist messages to Jewish organizational events served political narratives better than his actual Palestine policy did, which was more cautious and committed to consultation. [cite:brandeis_center_fdr_zionism] The two registers ran at the same time, and both are documented.

That changes how the Ibn Saud letter should be read without changing anything about the letter itself. The meeting, the letter and the later departure from its terms under Truman remain as documented. What the wider record adds is that the commitment to Ibn Saud was not an exceptional promise from an otherwise pro-Zionist president. It was consistent with a repeated, more cautious position that Roosevelt held throughout the war, expressed privately and in writing to several governments, and distinct from his warmer public rhetoric. [cite:state_dept_oct1945_release] Seen this way, the single promise matters more, not less, because it was one of several.

There are two ways to read Roosevelt, and each has a serious case. The first reads him as an instrumentalist on Palestine: a president who gave Jewish audiences the language they wanted, gave Arab rulers written assurances of consultation, and approved silence on the question at home while the war lasted. Its evidence is the evidence above, two pieces of it in Roosevelt's own government's records and one in the words of a Zionist leader who had expected better. The second reads the same record as wartime statecraft rather than duplicity. A politician's public messages and working policy can diverge without hypocrisy, because they serve different audiences and different purposes. On this reading the warm public messages and the cautious private commitments were both genuine, each fitted to its setting. The documents establish the gap between the two registers. They do not establish which motive produced it, and the sources here make no claim about why Roosevelt chose to speak in two voices.

What the record leaves open, then, is Roosevelt's purpose rather than his conduct. It is clear that he approved the suppression plan, that a leading Zionist came away from a private meeting shocked by his position, and that he sent the consultation assurance to more than one Arab leader. It is not clear what was said in the meetings that shocked Wise, how the suppression plan worked in practice, which governments received the other letters, or what Roosevelt himself intended the future of Palestine to be. Because he died in April 1945, the record also cannot say what he would have done once the war ended, and this book does not speculate.
