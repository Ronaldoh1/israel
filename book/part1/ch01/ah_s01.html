Three Thousand Years in Brief: Israel, Judah, Rome and the Name "Palestine"

Two short claims circulate whenever the deep history of this land is argued. One says that Jews were absent from it for two thousand years. The other says that Palestinians appeared only in the twentieth century. Both treat the past as though only one people could have one. The documented record does not support either. It shows Jewish kingdoms in the first millennium BCE and a Jewish presence that survived Rome's suppression of two revolts. It also shows centuries in which the majority of the land's people became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians, the ancestors of today's Palestinians.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] [cite:encyclo_israel_history] This section traces that record from the first inscriptions to the end of Ottoman rule, follows the history of the two names, "Israel" and "Palestine," and sets out where scholars disagree.

Israel in the stones. The earliest known mention of Israel outside the Bible is on a victory stele of the Egyptian pharaoh Merneptah, dated about 1207 BCE. Flinders Petrie found it in 1896 in the first courtyard of Merneptah's mortuary temple at Thebes; it is now in the Cairo Museum.[cite:rs_ch01_cojs_merneptah] The line that names Israel uses a stock phrase for a conquered place, rendered "Israel is wasted, its grain (or seed) is not." A detail of the writing gives it special weight. Egyptian scribes added to names an unpronounced sign, called a determinative, that told the reader what kind of thing was named. Names such as Ashkelon and Gezer carry the sign for a place. "Israel" carries the sign for a people or ethnic group.[cite:rs_ch01_cojs_merneptah] At the end of the thirteenth century BCE, then, an Egyptian royal scribe knew of a people called Israel.

Kingdoms appear in the outside record nearly four centuries later, and from the testimony of their enemies. The Tel Dan stele, an inscription in Old Aramaic dated about 840 BCE from its script, was found in 1993 at Tel Dan in northern Israel during excavations led by Avraham Biran; two smaller fragments followed the next year. It is held at the Skirball Museum in Jerusalem. On it an Aramaean king, most likely Hazael of Damascus, boasts of victories over the king of Israel and over the "House of David." Line 9 is the earliest known mention of David's dynasty outside the Bible.[cite:rs_ch01_cojs_teldan] From the other side of the Jordan comes the Mesha Stele, also called the Moabite Stone, of about the same date and written in Moabite. The missionary F. A. Klein saw it in 1868 near Dibon in Transjordan. It states that "Omri was the king of Israel, and he oppressed Moab for many days." It is now in the Louvre.[cite:rs_ch01_cojs_mesha] Two inscriptions from neighbouring peoples, written in their own languages to celebrate their own victories, named the kingdom of Israel and the dynasty of David. These are the outside witnesses to the Jewish kingdoms of Israel and Judah that existed in the first millennium BCE.[cite:encyclo_israel_history]

Where the name "Palestine" comes from. The earliest surviving text to use the name Palestine is the Histories of the Greek writer Herodotus, from the second half of the fifth century BCE. He speaks of "Syria Palaistine" for the coast between Phoenicia and Egypt.[cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] [cite:rs_ch01_totf_hadrian] According to one secondary account, Herodotus also mentions "Syrians who are in Palestine" who practise circumcision (Histories 2.104.3), and both Philo of Alexandria, who died about 50 CE, and Josephus, writing about 93–94 CE, used "Palestine" when writing about Jews.[cite:rs_ch01_totf_hadrian] The Greek name is generally linked to the Philistines, in Hebrew peleshet, an Aegean people who settled the southern coastal plain in the twelfth century BCE. The scholar G. W. Bowersock calls the older variant forms, such as Peleset and Phlishtu, etymologically obscure.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_palname] [cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] The name, in other words, was in Greek use more than five centuries before Rome attached it to a province.

Rome, the revolts and the renaming. Rome conquered the region in 63 BCE and in 6 CE created a province of Judaea whose population was predominantly Jewish.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] Two great revolts against Roman rule failed. The first ended with the destruction of the Second Temple in Jerusalem in 70 CE. The second, led by Bar Kokhba, is dated 132–135 in some accounts and 132–136 in others, the end date being uncertain. After it Rome killed, enslaved or expelled much of Judea's Jewish population. It rebuilt Jerusalem as a pagan colony, Aelia Capitolina, and barred Jews from the city.[cite:wiki_syria_palaestina] [cite:wiki_palestine_region] Roman inscriptions show that around the time of this revolt Judaea was merged with Galilee into a province called Syria Palaestina.[cite:rs_ch01_totf_hadrian] [cite:rs_ch01_bas_feldman]

Why Rome made that change is a real dispute, and the evidence is thinner than either side of the modern argument tends to admit. No ancient source states when or why the change was made.[cite:rs_ch01_totf_hadrian] [cite:rs_ch01_bas_feldman] The case that it was punitive is the more widely held. Reference summaries describe the renaming as one that most scholars see as aimed at severing the Jewish connection to the land, and many historians link it to the punishment of the Jewish rebels.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] [cite:w_syria_palaestina] Its strongest scholarly statement is Louis Feldman's, from 1990: the emperor Hadrian was the likely author, given his decrees against Jewish national and religious life. Feldman conceded that only circumstantial evidence links Hadrian to the change.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_palname] [cite:rs_ch01_bas_feldman] The case against a punitive motive starts from the name's age. It had been in Greek and Latin use for centuries, including by Jewish writers. Ronald Syme suggested that the change may have come in 130 CE, before the revolt began, and David Jacobson, writing in 1999, and others have read it as an administrative or Hellenizing step rather than a punishment. These positions are known here through a secondary account that summarizes them.[cite:rs_ch01_totf_hadrian] Both cases agree on the facts that matter most: Rome did rename the province after, or around, a revolt it crushed, and the name it chose was not new.

The presence that remained. Rome's victory did not end Jewish life in the land. A continuous, smaller Jewish presence remained, and Galilee became its religious centre. Much of classical Jewish literature was produced there.[cite:encyclo_israel_history] In the sixteenth century Jewish communities took root in the four holy cities of Jerusalem, Hebron, Tiberias and Safed, forming what came to be called the Old Yishuv, the pre-Zionist community. It was a minority whose size rose and fell.[cite:encyclo_israel_history] Its numbers, institutions and place in Ottoman Jerusalem are the subject of [scene:ch01_old_yishuv|the Jewish community before Zionism].

Byzantium, the caliphate and the empires that followed. The land passed next to Byzantine Christian rule.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] Under Byzantium the region was divided into First, Second and Third Palestine. After the Muslim conquest in the seventh century, First Palestine became Filastin and Second Palestine became al-Urdunn.[cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] [cite:rs_ch01_jvl_palname] Under the Umayyad caliph 'Abd al-Malik, in the late seventh century, Palestine existed as a province of the caliphate, Jund Filastin.[cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] The Arabic form of the name was used for the region from the early Islamic period onward.[cite:w_name_palestine] Crusader kingdoms followed, then the Mamluks in the thirteenth century, and from 1516 the Ottoman Empire, which ruled for four centuries until 1917.[cite:wiki_palestine_region] Over these centuries the majority of the population became Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians.[cite:wiki_palestine_region]

Under the Ottomans, "Palestine" was an informal term rather than the name of a province; how the land was in fact administered is told in [scene:s00h|Ottoman Palestine]. The word "Palestinian" for Arabic speakers of the land is attested from 1898, in the writing of Khalil Baydas, and in 1913 Ruhi al-Khalidi wrote of "the Palestinian race" in the newspaper Filastin. Both dates come from Zachary Foster, as cited by the Jewish Virtual Library.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_palname]

Four thousand years of Palestine? A second dispute concerns continuity. Nur Masalha, in Palestine: A Four Thousand Year History (2018), argues that Palestinian place-names and identity persisted through Roman, Byzantine and Islamic rule.[cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] Reviewing the book, Bowersock called the four-thousand-year title indefensible. The documented name, he pointed out, begins with Herodotus, and its location and meaning kept changing from one empire to the next. He nonetheless praised the book's erudition.[cite:rs_ch01_nyrb_bowersock] The Jewish Virtual Library takes a further position: that the name was imposed by Rome, and that Palestinian nationalism dates from the 1910s and 1920s, as a reaction to Zionism.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_palname] The record supports parts of each. Bowersock's point about the name is documented: it is about 2,500 years old in writing, not 4,000, and it described different territories at different times. The persistence of the name itself, through the Byzantine provinces and Jund Filastin, is also documented. And whatever the date of modern national feeling, which is taken up in [scene:s00i|a later section on presence and identity], the people who lived in the land through those centuries were real and are documented.

"Stolen from Rome." One form of the argument circulates widely in comment threads: that every Palestinian symbol was stolen from someone else, the keffiyeh, the flag and the name itself, the last taken from Rome to erase Judea. Checked against the record, the claim is mostly false, with one half-truth. The keffiyeh is a traditional headdress worn across the Arab world; its black-and-white pattern became associated with Palestinian nationalism during the revolt of 1936–39.[cite:w_keffiyeh] The Palestinian flag derives from the flag of the 1916 Arab Revolt, as do the flags of Jordan and other Arab states. That is a shared origin, not a theft.[cite:w_pal_flag] [cite:w_arab_revolt_flag] The half-truth is the name. Rome did rename Judaea "Syria Palaestina" after the Bar Kokhba revolt, and many historians link the change to punishing the Jewish rebels.[cite:w_syria_palaestina] But the name was already more than 500 years old, found in Herodotus, and the Arabic Filastin was in use for the region for centuries from the early Islamic period.[cite:w_name_palestine] A related line, that Arafat was born in Cairo, is largely accurate, though some sources dispute it, and it says nothing about a people's history.[cite:w_arafat]

What the record does not settle. Some questions remain open on the evidence. Why Rome renamed Judaea, and exactly when, is not stated in any ancient source; the punitive reading is the more widely held, and the administrative reading has serious scholarly support, but neither can be proven from the surviving texts. The end date of the Bar Kokhba revolt is given as 135 in some sources and 136 in others. How much continuity of identity, as opposed to continuity of residence and of place-names, ran from the Byzantine and early Islamic provinces to modern Palestinians is the substance of the Masalha–Bowersock exchange, and the record of names alone cannot decide it. The size of the Jewish population that remained at any given point between Rome and the Ottomans is described in the sources here only as smaller, and fluctuating.

What the record does settle is that two histories are both documented. A people called Israel is named in an Egyptian inscription of about 1207 BCE, and the kingdom of Israel and the House of David by its enemies about 840 BCE; Jews remained in the land after Rome. Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians formed its majority across many centuries of residence, under a name that Greek writers used before Rome and Arab rulers used after it. Neither history erases the other. The modern conflict is about how those two documented claims were handled after 1897.
