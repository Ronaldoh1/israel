Zionism Is Not Judaism

In August 1897, in Basel, Switzerland, a political movement was founded. That movement, Zionism, is often treated as the same thing as Judaism, a religion and a set of ethnic and cultural lineages thousands of years old. The record draws a line between the two, and Jews drew it first. Zionism's founder said as much in his own words, and Jewish religious and political bodies across several traditions have kept the distinction alive ever since.

Theodor Herzl was an assimilated Viennese Jew, secular in outlook. He was a correspondent for the Neue Freie Presse, not a rabbi or a religious scholar. He wrote Der Judenstaat in 1896 and convened the First Zionist Congress in Basel on August 29–31, 1897. Afterward he wrote in his diary:

At Basel I founded the Jewish State.

"Founded" was his own word, written in a private diary rather than for public messaging. Herzl also gave serious consideration to Argentina and Uganda before Palestine was settled on. At its founding, then, political Zionism treated location as a practical question. [cite:herzl_diaries] [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Herzl's own secularism does not mean every early Zionist was secular; the movement drew both religious and secular support from early on.

The most formal Jewish rejection of a national return came before Zionism existed. In November 1885, twelve years before Basel, American Reform Judaism adopted the Pittsburgh Platform, which declared:

We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine, nor a sacrificial worship under the sons of Aaron, nor the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state.

This was the public, signed position of the largest Jewish denomination in America at the time. [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] It stood until the 1937 Columbus Platform softened it, and substantial Reform opposition to political Zionism continued even then. The familiar story holds that Judaism had always longed for a return to Zion, and that Zionism simply organized that longing. This document is hard to square with that story. Reform Judaism's institutional position later shifted considerably; the platform records 1885, not the present.

In October 1897, a few months after Basel, Jewish workers in Vilna founded the General Jewish Labor Bund. The Bund rejected Zionism as a political solution. It argued instead for Jewish national-cultural autonomy inside the countries where Jews already lived, with Yiddish as the everyday language, socialist politics, and doikayt, "hereness": building here rather than going there. [cite:wiki_bund] Accounts of its size differ. One figure puts membership above 400,000 across the Russian Empire by 1917, larger than the Zionist movement in Eastern Europe. Henry Tobias's history of the Bund in Russia describes a genuine mass movement with tens of thousands of members in the early twentieth century. Either way, it was no fringe. Its destruction came from the Holocaust and from Soviet suppression, not from losing an open argument with Zionism. The Bund's existence does not make its position the authentic Jewish one. It shows that Jews contested the equation of Jewish with Zionist, at scale, from the movement's first year.

Criticism also came from inside the Zionist idea. Asher Zvi Hirsch Ginsberg (1856–1927), who wrote as Ahad Ha'am, "One of the People," argued for cultural Zionism: a Jewish spiritual and cultural center in Palestine. At the same time he warned against Herzl's political version. His 1891 essay "Truth from Eretz Israel" recorded the Arab population already living in the land and warned that the project would produce conflict: "We are used to believing abroad that Palestine is nowadays almost totally desolate... but in fact this is not the case. It is difficult to find in the land any tillable land which is not already tilled." [cite:wiki_ahad_haam] The warning does not prove his alternative more workable. It shows that the population, and the risk, were seen from inside the movement from the start.

A religious argument followed in 1958, when Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, the Satmar Rebbe, published VaYoel Moshe. The book rests on the Three Oaths of the Talmud, Ketubot 111a, and holds that Jewish tradition forbids re-establishing sovereignty in the land by human political force before the Messiah comes. In this reading, political Zionism violates a religious commitment rather than fulfilling it. The Satmar community numbers about 130,000 in the United States alone, in Williamsburg, Kiryas Joel and Monsey, with communities in Israel, London, Antwerp and Montreal. It is the largest Hasidic dynasty in the world, and it still teaches this position. [cite:wiki_satmar] It is not the majority Orthodox position, and this book does not present it as one.

Laid on one timeline, the distinction runs from 1885 to the present: the Pittsburgh Platform, Ahad Ha'am's warning in 1891, Basel and the Bund in 1897, Satmar in 1958. [cite:basel_program] In contemporary Jewish life, Judith Butler's Parting Ways, Peter Beinart's The Crisis of Zionism and the organization Jewish Voice for Peace carry the argument on. [cite:butler_parting_ways] [cite:beinart_crisis_zionism] [cite:jvp_org] The distinction matters because collapsing it turns criticism of a political project into what reads as an attack on a people, the move traced in [scene:s00b|the section on the word "Semitic"]. Holding the distinction does not mean Zionists are wrong to see their support as bound up with their Jewish identity, nor that every Jew draws the line in the same place.

Two further arguments extend the point; they are arguments, not findings. The first is a thought experiment built on Herzl's alternatives. Uganda's population spoke Bantu languages. Argentina's spoke Spanish and Indigenous American languages such as Quechua, Guaraní and Mapudungun. None of these is Semitic. Palestine's population spoke Arabic, which is. [cite:wiki_bantu_languages] [cite:wiki_languages_argentina] [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] On this reasoning, the charge that criticizing the state is "antisemitic" found a surface foothold in Palestine only because the people displaced there happened to speak a Semitic language. The argument does not make the conflation any more valid in Palestine than it would have been elsewhere. It explains how the conflation spread.

The second argument concerns selective use. The broad, correct meaning of "Semitic" is often invoked to argue that criticism of Israeli policy cannot coherently be called antisemitic, since Palestinians are Semitic too. On the word's own terms, that argument is sound. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] The same breadth is rarely invoked when the subject is Palestinian displacement, such as the roughly 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinians displaced in 1948 whom later chapters document. This book does not argue that "antisemitism" should be stretched to cover harm to Arabs; the word has always meant hatred of Jews. Its point is that Palestinian displacement and casualties need no borrowed word. They are documented on their own terms, and they deserve the same seriousness whichever way the "Semitic" argument happens to point.
