Filastin: A Newspaper Named "Palestine"

While the Palestine Office was organizing land purchases from Jaffa, another institution in the same city was watching them. On 15 January 1911, two Arab Orthodox Christian cousins, Issa El-Issa and Yousef El-Issa, both educated in Beirut, founded a newspaper in Jaffa. They called it Filastin, "Palestine." [cite:wiki_falastin] [cite:nli_filastin_archive]

It is often said that "Palestinian" was an identity invented after 1948, or after 1967. The paper's masthead is a direct test of that claim. A newspaper named for the country, founded more than three decades before 1948, records a Palestinian public sphere, and a Palestinian name in print, long before either of those dates.

Filastin did not begin as a political organ against Zionism. It started as a twice-weekly paper concerned mainly with the Arab Orthodox community's dispute with the Greek-led hierarchy of its church. Between 1911 and 1914, its coverage of Zionism changed. A scholarly study of the paper traces a movement from cautious ambivalence to open hostility, as the paper reported on Zionist land purchases, on immigration, on violent incidents and on the Zionist Congresses. [cite:academia_filastin_zionism] By 1914, on one description, it had become the most consistent critic of Zionist land purchase in the country. The editors sent a copy of each issue to every village in the Jaffa district. [cite:wiki_falastin]

The paper outlasted the Ottoman Empire. Under the British Mandate it became one of the most influential Arabic dailies, known for its network of correspondents. Issa El-Issa helped found the Palestine Free Party in 1927. Filastin continued to appear in Jaffa until 7 April 1948, shortly before the city fell, and later moved to Jerusalem. [cite:palquest_issa_el_issa]

The record of the paper survives in a notable place. The Jrayed archive of the National Library of Israel holds 6,692 issues, spanning 1911 to 1948. [cite:nli_filastin_archive] A daily paper named "Palestine," read across the country and preserved in Israel's own national library, documents a Palestinian public identity and a sustained political argument decades before 1948.

The sources on the paper are specific in scope, and the claim made here stays within it. They establish when the paper was founded and by whom, how its coverage of Zionism changed before the First World War, how widely it was distributed, and how long it lasted. They establish, in short, that the name and the argument were in print by 1911. Set beside the Palestine Office, opened in the same city three years earlier, Filastin shows that the land pipeline described in the previous section was observed, reported and opposed in Arabic before the First World War.
