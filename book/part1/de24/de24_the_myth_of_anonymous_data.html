The Myth of Anonymous Data

Almost every company in [scene:de24_apps_and_ai_companies|the previous section] says that the data it shares is anonymous, aggregated, de-identified or hashed. The words mean different things. Data is anonymous only if it can never be linked back to a person. It is pseudonymous if the name has been swapped for a code, such as an advertising number or a customer ID, which still follows the same person from record to record. It is de-identified if obvious identifiers have been removed, which says nothing about whether the rest can be matched. A hash is the output of a one-way mathematical function that turns, say, an email address into a fixed string of characters; the same address always produces the same string. The Federal Trade Commission's technologists put the consequence plainly in July 2024: "hashes aren't 'anonymous' and can still be used to identify users," because a hash "still creates a unique signature that can track a person or device over time." [cite:de24_ftc_hashing] This section sets out what the research and the record show about how often a stripped record leads back to a name.

Three facts about a person

The modern study of the problem began with Latanya Sweeney, a computer scientist, who in 1997 matched supposedly anonymous hospital records to the Governor of Massachusetts, William Weld, using publicly available records. [cite:de24_wiki_sweeney] In a working paper of 2000 she estimated, from 1990 Census data, that 87 percent of Americans, 216 million of 248 million, were probably unique on just three facts: five-digit ZIP code, sex and date of birth; she noted that the voter list for Cambridge, Massachusetts, which carries those facts with names, could be bought for $20. [cite:de24_sweeney_2000] A recomputation by Philippe Golle on the 2000 Census put the share at 63 percent, lower but in his words in general agreement. [cite:de24_golle_2006] Both figures are estimates of likely uniqueness, not counts of people actually identified, and both stand. In 2019 a team at Imperial College London and the Université catholique de Louvain built a statistical model that estimated 99.98 percent of Americans would be correctly re-identified in any dataset with 15 demographic attributes, the kind of detail a marketing file routinely holds. [cite:de24_rocher_2019]

Four points in time and space

Movement and spending are more identifying still. In March 2013 Yves-Alexandre de Montjoye and colleagues studied fifteen months of phone-location data for one and a half million people in a European country and found that four points, each a place and an hour, were enough to single out 95 percent of them; making the data coarser helped little, because uniqueness fell only very slowly as the resolution dropped. [cite:de24_demontjoye_2013] In January 2015 the same lead author, with others, studied three months of credit-card records for 1.1 million people with names and card numbers removed, and found that four purchases, each a shop and a day, identified 90 percent; knowing the price of a purchase raised the risk by 22 percent on average, and women were easier to identify than men. [cite:de24_demontjoye_2015] Tastes work the same way. When Netflix released "anonymous" film ratings for about 500,000 subscribers for a public prize contest, Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov showed that eight ratings with dates, two of them allowed to be wrong, picked out 99 percent of the records, and two ratings with dates sufficed for 68 percent; matching a few users to their public reviews on another site revealed what appeared to be their political preferences. [cite:de24_netflix_2006]

From the laboratory to the newsroom

Journalists then did with commercial data what the researchers had predicted. In December 2018 The New York Times reported that at least 75 companies received precise, supposedly anonymous location data from phone apps, that several claimed to track up to 200 million American devices, and that one database logged a phone's position as often as every two seconds. The companies said the data was tied "not to someone's name" but to a unique code; the reporters followed one such code to a mathematics teacher in upstate New York, Lisa Magrin, from her home to a weight-loss meeting and a dermatologist's office. [cite:de24_nyt_2018] A year later the paper's opinion desk obtained a single file of more than 50 billion location points from over 12 million phones in 2016 and 2017, traced the movements of a senior Defense Department official, and concluded that "it's child's play to connect real names to the dots"; the law professor Paul Ohm told it that precise location over time is "absolutely impossible to anonymize." [cite:de24_nyt_2019] In July 2021 a Catholic newsletter, The Pillar, bought commercially available app location data from an unnamed vendor, matched a device to the general secretary of the US bishops' conference, Monsignor Jeffrey Burrill, and reported his use of a dating app; he resigned before publication, and the app's maker, Grindr, called the report a "witch hunt" and said it did not believe it was the source. [cite:de24_ap_pillar] He is named here only because the case became public; it shows that anyone with money, not only a state, could do it.

The industry has also linked codes to names on purpose. Researchers at IMDEA Networks in Madrid, Radboud University and KU Leuven reported on 3 June 2025 that from September 2024 Meta's Facebook and Instagram apps on Android listened on hidden local channels inside the phone, so that a Meta tracking code on an ordinary website could pass the browser's anonymous cookie to the app, which sent it to Meta with the logged-in account. It worked in private browsing and after cookies were cleared. Meta "paused" the feature the same day, citing "a potential miscommunication" with Google over its app-store rules. [cite:de24_reg_localhost] The FTC's hashing warning drew on similar cases, among them a counselling service, BetterHelp, which the commission said shared hashed email addresses with Facebook knowing they would be matched back to people. [cite:de24_ftc_hashing]

How a fusion platform would join it

What remains is to describe, as a mechanism and not as a documented use, how a data-integration platform of the kind Palantir sells could turn a stripped trail into a named person. Each step below rests on a documented capability; the chain as a whole has not been documented in any specific case. A location trail tied to an advertising number, of the kind brokers sell (see [scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Five]), rests most nights at one address. An address is a key in the investigative databases ICE already licenses: LexisNexis's Accurint lets users search by name, address, Social Security number and licence plate, and its 2026 work statement requires a direct API connection to "the Palantir platform." [cite:de24_bu_lexis] An ICE legal memorandum obtained by the American Civil Liberties Union argued that location data tied to advertising numbers rather than names falls outside the Supreme Court's warrant rule for phone records, which is the legal premise for buying it. [cite:ow9_aclu_adid] And an ICE tool reported in January 2026 already gives each target's address a "confidence score." [cite:ow9_fortune_elite]

The platform does not collect; it joins what its customer loads, and Palantir says the customer decides what goes in. Whether that is a limit or only a description is argued in [scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Seven]. What matters here is that the anonymity on which the commercial trade depends does not survive contact with a well-stocked identity database, and that agencies hold such databases already.

The worst-case reading, tested

Documented: that records on most American adults can be bought or borrowed, and that immigration enforcement buys them. ICE paid for Accurint from 2021 and ran more than 1.2 million searches in seven months; it bought CLEAR, which carried utility records on more than 170 million people until October 2021 and was renewed without competition in 2026; and in 2026 it required its LexisNexis bridge to connect directly to "the Palantir platform." [cite:de24_bu_lexis] [cite:de24_globe_utility] [cite:de24_lawnext_tr] Documented, too, that the government's own files were opened to enforcement from 2025: Social Security data inside SAVE, tax addresses and Medicaid contact data. [cite:de24_npr_save] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de24_wt_medicaid] And documented that "anonymous" commercial data can be named by anyone with money and a second database. [cite:de24_nyt_2019] [cite:de24_ap_pillar]

Probable: that the pieces described in the flow above can be joined for an individual target, since each is in place and the software is built to join. What would settle it is a record of one case run that way. None has been published, and the ICE address score itself exists because addresses decay and are often wrong. [cite:ow9_fortune_elite] Probable, also, that reuse of civil records will continue to widen while the courts are divided: one appeals panel declined to stop the IRS sharing in February 2026 and another upheld a block in September. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep]

Not supported: that the data layer has been used to find critics for their speech; the documented attempts to unmask critics ran through administrative subpoenas to platforms, set out in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Twenty-Eight]. Not supported: that agencies buy shopping baskets or loyalty-card histories. Not supported: that any of these tools was designed from, or ordered because of, the Dark Enlightenment texts. Fusion centres date from 2003, ICE's plate-data access from 2018, the LexisNexis contract from 2021; they were built and bought under presidents of both parties, and a later policy that rhymes with a theorist's wish is not thereby his (see [scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One]). [cite:de24_psi_fusion] [cite:de24_upi_vigilant] [cite:de24_bu_lexis]

The record also runs against the worst case in specific places. A Democratic administration cancelled ICE's first plate-database request in 2014; utility data stopped flowing in 2021 after press reports and a senator's pressure; a court found 42,695 unlawful IRS disclosures and limited Medicaid sharing to five fields; Flock paused its federal pilots after a state audit, and an Oregon city tore its cameras down; California now lets residents send one deletion request to every registered broker. [cite:de24_physorg_2014] [cite:de24_globe_utility] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de24_wt_medicaid] [cite:de24_ap_flock] [cite:de24_katu_woodburn] [cite:de24_ca_drop] What none of them touches is the purchase itself: the House passed a bill to require warrants for bought data in April 2024, and it did not become law. [cite:ow9_cs_4thact]
