Ten Days in August: The Overflight Crisis of 1958

The British paratroopers who flew to Amman in July 1958, and the American airlift to Jordan, flew over Israel to get there. For about three weeks Israel allowed it. Then, on 2 August, it ordered the flights stopped. Eight days later they resumed, and the American Secretary of State declared the matter closed. In between, the Secretary of State rebuked Israel's ambassador to his face, and Israel set down in writing what it wanted from the United States and had not been given. Why Ben-Gurion stopped the flights is still argued. This section tells the ten days in order and then sets out the argument.

Before the crisis. The flights followed from the events told in [scene:cw4_1958|the year the region turned]: the coup in Baghdad on 14 July and the British decision to send paratroopers to support King Hussein in Amman. According to a report of the Jewish Telegraphic Agency published on 8 August 1958, Israel had first permitted the British paratroop overflights about three weeks earlier, hoping for firmer Western action against Nasser. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1958] The decision was a choice, not a formality. It put Israel's territory in the service of a British operation in an Arab state, at a moment when Israel's government was also asking Washington to back a pact against Nasser, as told in [scene:cw4_periphery|the periphery and the Trident].

1 August: two letters on one day. On 1 August 1958 the Soviet Union sent Israel a note calling it "an immediate associate" in aggressive acts against Arab countries. Ben-Gurion wrote in his diary that the note was "paved with threats to the 'national interests' of Israel." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] On the same day, in Washington, Dulles dated his reply to Ben-Gurion's periphery letter of 24 July. It said that "we are prepared to do what we can to be of assistance," and that "we believe that Israel should be in a position to deter an attempt at aggression by indigenous forces." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] The second sentence may have mattered more to Israel: it was an American statement, in writing, that Israel should be able to deter attack by its neighbours. But the letter reached the American embassy only on 2 August and was delivered to the Israelis on 4 August. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] When it gave its stop order, Israel's government had the Soviet note in hand and not the American letter.

2 August: the stop order. On 2 August Israel's Assistant Director General, Comay, told the American ambassador, Lawson, that overflights "by US planes must stop immediately," with "no flights even tonight." Ben-Gurion made the same demand of the British. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d34] Lawson's telegram home gave his reading of the cause. Ben-Gurion, he judged, "does not feel he has firm basis to resist strong Cabinet and potential public opposition to overflights." Comay called the Soviet note "firm and threatening." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d34] The ambassador's report thus pointed to two pressures at once: a Soviet note that the Israeli official himself described as threatening, and a cabinet and public that Ben-Gurion could not, in Lawson's view, carry with him.

3 August: the rebuke. The next day Dulles received Israel's ambassador, Abba Eban, and Minister Yaacov Herzog at his residence. The American record of the conversation, titled "Israeli Decision to Request U.S. to Cease Overflights of Israel," shows him angry. He called it "particularly shocking that Israel would do this without any consultation with the US." He reminded them that "the Eisenhower Doctrine made clear" that the United States would support Israel if it were attacked by a Communist power, and he asked whether Israel felt "so menaced by the USSR that it would do whatever the Soviet Union requested." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d35] Schonmann, drawing on the same archives, quotes him calling the step "a show of fear for the Russians to behold." [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017]

Eban's answer that day did not dwell on Moscow. He explained that the British request "involved the sending of additional troops into Jordan," which were "an added convenience but not crucial." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d35] The argument was that Israel had not abandoned the West at a moment of need: the troops already in Amman were there, and what Israel had refused was more of them.

5 August: the letter and the list. Two days later the Israelis came back with a letter. On 5 August Eban, Herzog and Shimon Peres gave the Acting Secretary of State, Herter, Ben-Gurion's letter consenting to the resumption of American airlift flights. Eban told Herter that Israel's doubts had not stemmed from the Soviet note, which Israel did not plan to answer hastily. He proposed technical talks with Colonel Harkabi to end the airlift as soon as American needs allowed, and suggested the Aqaba route as an alternative. And he said that the most urgent issue was "the elimination of U.S. doubts about Israel's steadfastness." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d36]

Ben-Gurion's letter went further than consent. It complained that "we have not been successful in receiving arms assistance from the United States" and that "we have never been granted a guarantee of our integrity," and it noted that the American assurance of help under the Eisenhower Doctrine had never been put in writing. It proposed closer ties among the countries of the periphery. Peres then set out Israel's arms needs. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d36] The letter turned a crisis over flights into a statement of grievance. Israel had lent its airspace to two Western powers; in return it had a verbal assurance, a presidential expression of "interest" in its integrity, and a letter from Dulles that had arrived after the crisis began. It had no arms from the United States and no written guarantee.

The last row of that comparison was never said to the Israelis. The Policy Planning Staff's judgment of 11 August, that "the cornerstone of the suggested entente is not firm," and Dulles's approval on 18 August of "extreme caution in giving Ben-Gurion further encouragement or support," were internal American papers found by Schonmann. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] Together with Eisenhower's letter of 25 July, which offered Israel confidence in "United States interest in the integrity and independence of Israel," they show the distance between what Israel asked for that summer and what Washington was prepared to give. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d31]

6–10 August: the resumption. The American airlift resumed on 6 August. On 10 August Dulles wrote that "we can consider the situation which arose out of the interruption of our flights a closed matter," and Israel renewed the overflights. Ben-Gurion wrote that he had gained "a free hand" in drafting the reply to Moscow. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] The phrase suggests what he thought he had won: not the arms or the guarantee he had asked for, but room to answer the Soviet Union in his own way without American objection.

The press account at the time was blunter. The Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported that Israel "apparently yielded to pressure from the United States." It also reported that, according to Israeli sources, the ban had been prompted not by the Soviet note but "by British attempts to set Israel up as a bogey in the present crisis." The same paper reported a new Soviet map that placed Jerusalem and central Palestine in Jordan. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1958]

Why did Ben-Gurion stop the flights? Two explanations were given at the time, and both have evidence behind them.

The first is that Israel gave way to the Soviet note and to pressure at home. This was Dulles's reading on 3 August, when he asked whether Israel would do "whatever the Soviet Union requested," and it is close to Lawson's of 2 August, which placed the cause in strong cabinet and potential public opposition that Ben-Gurion did not feel able to resist. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d35] [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d34] Its strongest evidence is the timing: the stop order came the day after the Soviet note arrived, and the Israeli official who delivered it described that note to the American ambassador as "firm and threatening." [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d34] Ben-Gurion's own diary entry, that the note was "paved with threats," shows that he took it seriously. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017]

The second is that the Soviet note was not the cause, and that the decision turned on domestic and British politics. This was Eban's account to Dulles and to Herter, and it was the account Israeli sources gave the Jewish Telegraphic Agency. [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d35] [cite:rs_cw4_frus58_d36] [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1958] Its strongest evidence is what Israel actually refused and when it relented. It refused additional British troops, which Eban called "not crucial," and it resumed the American flights within four days, before it had answered Moscow at all. The Israeli sources' charge that Britain was setting Israel up "as a bogey" points to a fear that Israel would be made to carry the cost of an operation that was not its own. [cite:rs_cw4_djn_1958] On this reading, the stop order was a protest against being used without being supported, and Ben-Gurion's letter of 5 August, with its list of what Israel had not received, is the evidence of what the protest was about.

The two readings are not wholly exclusive. Lawson's telegram itself joined the Soviet note to the cabinet's opposition, and a cabinet's opposition to overflights may have rested on fear of Moscow, on resentment of London, or on both. What the sources show is that the explanation given changed between 2 and 5 August: on the first day an Israeli official stressed the Soviet threat, and three days later Israel's ambassador said the Soviet note had not been the reason.

An accidental crisis? Schonmann offers a third way of reading the episode, which she calls "the accidental crisis in American–Israeli relations." Her point turns on dates. Dulles's letter, with its promise of help and its statement that Israel should be able to deter attack, and the Soviet note were dated the same day, 1 August; and the Israeli stop order was given on 2 August, before the American letter was delivered on the 4th. [cite:rs_cw4_schonmann_2017] Had the letter arrived first, the implication runs, Ben-Gurion might have weighed the Soviet threat against an American assurance in hand. This is her interpretation, and it is labelled as such here. It explains the timing well. It explains less well why Ben-Gurion's letter of 5 August, written after the Dulles letter had been delivered, still complained that Israel had no arms and no written guarantee; Dulles's letter, generous as it was in tone, gave neither.

What it showed. Whatever its cause, the crisis exposed the terms of the relationship. Israel's airspace mattered to Britain and to the United States in July 1958, and Israel used that fact, for four days, to make its case. The Secretary of State's response was to question Israel's nerve. The case Israel made, arms and a written guarantee and support for its periphery, was not met that summer; within days of the crisis's end the American planners were advising caution on the periphery, and the arms question returned in the Hawk decision of 1962, told at the end of this chapter.

The record leaves several things unsettled. Ben-Gurion's own explanation of the stop order is not among the sources gathered here beyond his diary's description of the Soviet note and his later line about a "free hand." The cabinet discussion that Lawson referred to is reported only through the ambassador's reading of it. Whether British flights resumed on the same terms as the American ones is not stated. And Israel's reply to the Soviet note, which Ben-Gurion felt free to draft as he wished, is not described in these sources.
