Three Families

A national score describes a system; a family lives a budget. This section follows three households in each country, a lower-income family, a middle family and an upper-middle family, and asks what each takes in, what the state takes, what the essentials cost and what is left over, using each country's own survey of real household budgets rather than invented examples. It then asks what would change for each American family under Israel's rules. [scene:us22_delta|What the gap means in a life]

Where the figures come from

The American families are the lowest, middle and fourth fifths of households in the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Expenditure Survey for 2024, ranked by income before taxes. [cite:us22_bls_ce24_q] The Israeli families are the same fifths in the Central Bureau of Statistics' Household Expenditure Survey for 2021, the latest year for which the bureau's full table of income, taxes and spending by income group could be obtained for this book; each fifth is the average of two of the table's tenths, which each hold a tenth of households. [cite:us22_cbs_hes21_t2] The bureau published headline results for 2024 in October 2026; on those figures the middle Israeli fifth kept about 23 per cent of its net income after spending, almost exactly the 2021 share, and this section will be updated when the full 2024 tables are released. [cite:us22_davar_hes24]

Four differences in how the surveys count are adjusted for or stated. The American survey reports income tax only up to 2023, so the 2024 American income tax is estimated by applying each fifth's 2023 ratio of tax to income, and American payroll taxes are computed at the legal 7.65 per cent of wages and 15.3 per cent of self-employment income; Israel's survey reports all compulsory payments, including income tax, National Insurance and the health tax. [cite:us22_bls_cesan24_nr] Pension and insurance contributions are left out of spending in both countries and counted as part of what is left over. Israel reports mortgage payments separately, as a form of saving, while the American survey counts mortgage interest as housing spending; to avoid flattering Israel, its mortgage payments, principal and interest, are subtracted in full. And the American fifths are ranked by household income, the Israeli ones by income per person, so the American middle family is a little smaller, 2.5 people against 3.2. Amounts are converted at the World Bank's purchasing-power rate for household consumption, 4.28 shekels to the dollar in 2020, the latest found, which already allows for Israel's higher prices. [cite:us22_wb_prvtpp]

The middle and upper-middle families

The middle families show the gap most clearly. The American family takes home about twice as much per person, in dollars that buy the same goods, and spends almost all of it: about 81 cents of every dollar of income goes on everyday spending and about 13 cents in taxes, leaving about 6 cents. The Israeli family takes home less, pays slightly less in tax and compulsory contributions even with its health tax included, spends about 69 cents of each shekel, puts about 5 into its mortgage, and has about 16 left. Even in dollars per person, after converting for prices, the Israeli middle family has more left over at the end of the year, about $2,290 against $1,760, though the years differ. [cite:us22_bls_ce24_q] [cite:us22_cbs_hes21_t2]

The upper-middle families divide differently. Both keep a meaningful share, about 18 per cent of income in America and 22 per cent in Israel, but the American family's larger income leaves it more in absolute terms, about $7,690 a person against $4,690. At this level the American system's higher pay outweighs its costs in dollars, while the Israeli family still keeps the larger share and holds more guarantees. Where the money goes also differs: the American families spend more on health care and transport, the Israeli families more on food and education, the last reflecting private lessons, after-school programmes and fees on top of free schooling.

The lower-income families

The bottom fifth cannot be compared the same way. In both surveys these households spend more than the income they report, about twice their income in the American survey and about 1.4 times in the Israeli one, because they draw down savings, receive help in kind, borrow or under-report income, and because the two groups are different people: the American bottom fifth averages 1.6 people with half an earner and a head of household aged 57, many of them retirees living alone; the Israeli bottom fifth averages 3.5 people, and in its poorest tenth 43 per cent of households are Arab. [cite:us22_bls_ce24_q] [cite:us22_cbs_hes21_t2] What can be compared for a low-income family is what the law guarantees, and there the systems differ most.

If each American family lived under Israel's rules

No survey can move a family across the ocean, and wages, prices and jobs would not travel with it. What can be said is which of each family's costs the Israeli system would carry and which it would add. The lower-income family would gain the most: health cover without a premium, more of a lost paycheck replaced, a child allowance, state-paid child support and lower tax on its wages, against dearer food, rent and cars. The middle family would stop paying the $3,842 a year in health insurance premiums its American counterpart pays, face university fees of about NIS 12,000 a year rather than American tuition and debt, and gain paid leave, sick pay and a compulsory pension; on the surveys above, families at that rung in Israel keep a larger share of their income. The upper-middle family would lose most: Israel's income tax rises to 50 per cent at the top, its unemployment benefit is capped, and its cars and homes cost more, so American pay at that level buys more, though the family would keep the same guarantees as everyone else. [cite:us22_pwc_il_pit] [cite:us22_nii_uiamount] [cite:us14_huji_tuition]

Are they happier?

Each country measures this, but not in a way that can be laid side by side by income. In January 2025, 38 per cent of Americans in households earning under $40,000 said they were very satisfied with their personal lives, against 55 per cent of those earning over $100,000, a gap of 17 points that Gallup has found for years. [cite:us22_gallup_pl25] Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics found 92.2 per cent of adults satisfied with their lives in 2026, Arab citizens 90.6 per cent, but this book found no breakdown by income. [cite:us22_ynet_cbs5787] The World Happiness Report, which asks both populations the same question, ranks Israel eighth and the United States twenty-third. What the American figures show is that satisfaction there rises steeply with income; whether Israel's rises less steeply, as a system that guarantees more at the bottom might suggest, is not established by the data found.

What the record shows and does not. It shows real household budgets at three rungs of each country's income ladder, from each national survey, with every adjustment stated. It does not show a matched year: the Israeli table is from 2021 and will be replaced when the 2024 tables appear. It does not fully capture prices that differ item by item, quality, or the value of guarantees a family never draws on. And it cannot say how any particular family would fare if it moved.
