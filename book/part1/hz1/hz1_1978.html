The Good Fence

Israel's first allies inside Lebanon were in the border villages. In 1976, as the civil war spread south, Israel opened its northern border to residents of Lebanese villages along the frontier under a policy it called the Good Fence, offering medical care and economic help; from 1977 southern Lebanese Christians were allowed to work in Israel and to export goods through Haifa, crossing at the Fatima Gate near Metula. [cite:hz1_wiki_freeleb] [cite:hz1_alhurra_fence] Alhurra, describing the policy in 2026, gives its aim as winning the villages' loyalty and turning the strip into a security belt against the Palestinian factions. [cite:hz1_alhurra_fence]

The policy had a military side from the start. In August 1977 Israel's foreign minister, Moshe Dayan, told the United States that Israel wanted to help the Christians of the south "in a military way", and the US embassy reported that Prime Minister Menachem Begin had admitted publicly, for the first time, that Israel had been supporting them with artillery fire from inside Israel. [cite:hz1_frus_1977] On 24 September 1977 President Jimmy Carter sent Begin a message stating that "current Israeli military actions in Lebanon are a violation of our agreements", which limited the use of American weapons to self-defence, and warning that Congress would be informed and further deliveries ended if they did not stop. [cite:hz1_frus_carter]

The local commander of this alliance was Saad Haddad, a Greek Catholic major in the Lebanese army who commanded a battalion of about 700 men at Marjayoun and broke with the army command in 1976. [cite:hz1_olj_sla] [cite:hz1_wiki_haddad]

Operation Litani

On 11 March 1978 a squad of Palestinian fighters that had sailed from Lebanon landed on the Israeli coast near Ma'agan Michael and killed 38 Israeli civilians, 13 of them children; the PLO claimed the attack. Counts of the dead and wounded differ slightly between sources. [cite:hz1_wiki_coastal] On the night of 14–15 March Israel invaded Lebanon, and within a few days it held the whole south except the city of Tyre and its surroundings. [cite:hz1_unifil_bg] [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] About 25,000 Israeli troops took part. [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] On 15 March Lebanon protested to the Security Council, stating that it had no connection with the Palestinian operation. [cite:hz1_unifil_bg]

The toll fell mostly on people who were not fighters. Estimates of Lebanese and Palestinian dead run from about 1,100, the figure given by the American scholar Augustus Richard Norton, to about 2,000 in other accounts; Norton's estimate describes most of the dead as Palestinian and Lebanese civilians. Between 18 and 20 Israeli soldiers were killed. Estimates of the displaced run from 100,000 to 250,000. [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] [cite:hz1_wiki_coastal] Israel used American cluster bombs in the operation. In August 1979 Secretary of State Cyrus Vance said Israel "may have violated its agreement" with the United States through its use of American weapons in the south; Israeli military sources acknowledged that the use of cluster bombs in the Litani operation had breached the agreement, while Israeli defence officials denied any other violation and said their operations were meant to deter attacks on Israeli targets. [cite:hz1_jta_cluster]

Resolution 425 and UNIFIL

On 19 March 1978 the Security Council adopted Resolutions 425 and 426. They called on Israel to cease its military action immediately and withdraw its forces from all Lebanese territory, called for strict respect for Lebanon's sovereignty within its internationally recognised boundaries, and set up the United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL) to confirm Israel's withdrawal, restore international peace and security, and help the Lebanese government restore its authority in the south. The first UNIFIL troops arrived on 23 March. [cite:hz1_unifil_bg] The force was meant to be interim. It stayed for decades, and its record, and the argument over its future, run through the later parts of this book. [scene:hz8_terms|The 2024 terms]

When Israel withdrew later in 1978, it did not hand its positions to UNIFIL or to the Lebanese army. It handed them to Haddad's militia. [cite:hz1_wiki_litani] The resolution asked Israel to leave all Lebanese territory; what it left behind was a strip along the border held by a Lebanese force that Israel supplied.

Free Lebanon

On 18 April 1979 Haddad, speaking from Metulla in Israel, proclaimed the area his men held to be "Free Lebanon". The next day the Lebanese government branded him a traitor and dismissed him from the army. [cite:hz1_olj_sla] [cite:hz1_wiki_freeleb] His force was mainly Christian, but it also recruited Shia and Druze men from the south; L'Orient-Le Jour reports that its salaries drew recruits in hard times and that Shia later became the majority of its ranks. [cite:hz1_olj_sla] After Haddad died of cancer in January 1984 a former Lebanese army general, Antoine Lahad, took command, and the militia became known as the South Lebanon Army. It collapsed when Israel withdrew in May 2000, and most of its members fled to Israel. [cite:hz1_olj_sla] Part Four covers its role in the occupied zone. [scene:hz4_zone|The security zone]

That Israel armed and financed this militia is documented, not inferred. In July 1982 Haddad himself told the Christian Science Monitor that Israel had contributed about $10 million toward his men's salaries since 1978, paying $100 a month per man, and had provided weapons and civilian services such as roads and hospitals. [cite:hz1_csm_haddad] In April 1981 senior Israeli military sources acknowledged for the first time that Israel was also supplying equipment to Christian forces in northern Lebanon, and the same report described Israel as giving full backing to Haddad's militia on the border. [cite:hz1_csm_1981] A 2026 Alhurra account puts Israel's support for Haddad's forces at about $35 million a year, without specifying the years; that figure is single-sourced, and Haddad's own figure for salaries is far lower. [cite:hz1_alhurra_fence]

The two governments described the arrangement in opposite terms. Begin spoke of a "moral commitment" to Lebanon's Christians, and Israeli officers argued that if Christian forces were destroyed the PLO and Syria would have free movement up to Israel's border. [cite:hz1_csm_1981] The Lebanese government, which never recognised Free Lebanon, treated its founder as a traitor. [cite:hz1_wiki_freeleb] For the question Part Eleven tests, whether armed groups in this region have served Israel as tools as well as threats, this is the first documented case: a Lebanese force that Israel paid, armed and used to hold ground it had been asked by the Security Council to leave. [scene:hz11_proxies|Groups Israel armed] Whether that served Israel's security or prolonged the conflict is an argument, taken up there.

Iran, 1979

Seven months after Musa al-Sadr disappeared, a revolution in the country of his birth brought clerics to power. The new government broke with Israel at once. On 17 February 1979 Yasser Arafat became the first official visitor to the new Islamic Republic, and Iran handed the PLO the building that had housed Israel's mission in Tehran. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini urged Arafat to call the Palestinian revolution an Islamic one; Arafat replied that it was a national movement of Muslims and Christians. [cite:hz1_aawsat_arafat]

The new state declared that its revolution was not meant to stop at its borders. The constitution adopted in October 1979 says in its preamble that it "provides the necessary basis for ensuring the continuation of the Revolution at home and abroad", and gives the armed forces, including the Revolutionary Guards, "the ideological mission of jihad in God's way", described as extending the sovereignty of God's law throughout the world. [cite:hz1_iran_const] In his New Year message broadcast on 21 March 1980, Khomeini said: "We should try hard to export our revolution to the world." The same message named the enemy as the powers "led by America, Israel and Zionism" and pledged support for "beloved Palestine and beloved Lebanon". [cite:hz1_merip_khomeini]

By the spring of 1980, then, two of the forces that would produce Hezbollah were in place: a Lebanese Shia community that had organised and armed under al-Sadr, and a government in Tehran committed in its constitution to exporting its revolution. The third, an Israeli army occupying much of Lebanon, arrived in June 1982. [cite:hz1_iran_const] [cite:hz1_wiki_sadr] [scene:hz2_invasion|The 1982 invasion]
