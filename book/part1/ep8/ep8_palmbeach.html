Palm Beach, 2005–2007

The case that finally reached Epstein began with a parent. In March 2005 the mother of a fourteen-year-old girl told the Palm Beach police that her daughter had been paid for a sexual encounter at a mansion in the town. Detectives identified more than thirty underage girls with matching accounts. Their chief, Michael Reiter, later called the outcome one of the worst failures of the criminal justice system he had seen. [cite:ep2_nbc_reiter2019]

The state side

Half of what went wrong in Florida went wrong in the state system, and the record now shows how. The Palm Beach County State Attorney, Barry Krischer, took the case to a grand jury, which in July 2006 indicted Epstein on a far lesser charge than the police had sought. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] [cite:ep2_nbc_reiter2019] The grand jury's transcripts stayed secret for eighteen years, until a law signed by Governor Ron DeSantis in February 2024 allowed a judge to release them. On 1 July 2024 Circuit Judge Luis Delgado did, calling Epstein "the most infamous pedophile in American history". Bradley Edwards, a lawyer for many of the victims, said the transcripts showed the prosecutor's office had gone in with an agenda of minimal charges, presented only part of the evidence and portrayed the girls as criminals. [cite:ep8_wusf_grandjury2024] Krischer did not respond to requests for comment at the time.

The federal case

The FBI opened its own investigation on 23 May 2006, nearly ten years after it had logged Maria Farmer's complaint. [cite:factually_farmer] The line prosecutor assigned to it in the US Attorney's office for the Southern District of Florida was Marie Villafaña, a sex-crimes specialist. She briefed the US Attorney, Alexander Acosta, and his deputy in July 2006; both later told investigators they did not remember the meeting. On 1 May 2007 she finished an 82-page prosecution memo supporting a 60-count indictment of Epstein and some of his assistants. In mid-May she asked to file the charges the next day, because Epstein was about to fly to New Jersey. Her supervisor urged caution. [cite:ep8_herald_villafana] [cite:ep8_bloomberg_villafana2026] The Justice Department's own later review confirms the draft 60-count indictment and the memo, and records that on 31 July 2007 the office offered Epstein a way out through a state plea instead. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020]

The defence

What Villafaña faced was the best legal team money could assemble. It included Jay Lefkowitz and others from Kirkland & Ellis, the former independent counsel Kenneth Starr, Alan Dershowitz, Roy Black, Jack Goldberger, and Lilly Ann Sanchez, a former member of the same US Attorney's office who negotiated directly with Villafaña's supervisor. [cite:ep8_herald_villafana] The team argued that this was "a quintessentially state matter" with no federal interest, attacked the girls' credibility, and when the local office would not give way, went over its head to the Criminal Division in Washington and then to the office of the Deputy Attorney General. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] [cite:ep8_herald_villafana] None of that was unlawful. It is what a defendant with unlimited money is entitled to do, and it is the first half of the two-track structure this part describes: the same rules, worked by people who can afford to work every one of them.

The agreement

The non-prosecution agreement was signed on 24 September 2007. In return for a guilty plea in state court, the United States agreed not to bring federal charges against Epstein, against four named women who worked for him, or against "any potential co-conspirators", a category left unnamed and unlimited. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] The victims were not told of it, or consulted, before it was signed. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] On 12 October 2007 Acosta met Lefkowitz for breakfast at a West Palm Beach hotel; Lefkowitz wrote afterwards to thank him for promising "genuine finality". [cite:ep8_herald_villafana] The internal review found no evidence that the breakfast produced the agreement, which had been signed weeks before, but noted that the meeting discussed a key addendum. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] [cite:ep8_herald_villafana] The defence's appeal to Washington failed when the Deputy Attorney General declined to intervene on 23 June 2008, and Epstein pleaded guilty a week later, on 30 June. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020]

He was sentenced to eighteen months and served thirteen, on work release that let him leave the jail daily for an office registered to a foundation he had set up, at the address of his own lawyer's firm. When Villafaña learned of it in November 2008 she called it a "material breach" and asked her superiors, "Can I indict him now?" The answer was no. [cite:ep8_herald_villafana]

The strongest case for the deal

The book owes the people who made the agreement their best argument, stated before any criticism. Acosta has said that the Palm Beach State Attorney was ready to let Epstein "walk free with no jail time, nothing", and that the federal office stepped in as a backstop to make sure he served time and registered as a sex offender. Managers in the office worried about unsettled legal questions, the credibility of witnesses and what a trial would do to the girls. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] The internal review found no evidence that Epstein's wealth or connections drove the decision. [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] Those are real considerations, and prosecutors weigh them every day.

The same review weighed them and still concluded that Acosta's decision "constitutes poor judgment": his view of the federal interest was "too narrow", his deference to the state "too expansive", and the agreement contained "several unusual and problematic terms" adopted "without the consideration required". [cite:ep8_doj_opr2020] It found no professional misconduct. That is the official verdict, and it has two halves: no rule was broken, and the outcome was wrong. The rest of this part explains how both can be true at once.
