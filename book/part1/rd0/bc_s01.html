The Square Mile: Privilege Without Sovereignty

At the heart of London lies a financial district of roughly one square mile, run by the oldest municipal corporation in Britain. The City of London Corporation governs it through a Lord Mayor, a Court of Common Council and its own police force. Its privileges predate Magna Carta, and later Acts of Parliament have routinely preserved them by express exception. [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation]

A popular account, widely repeated online, holds that the City is a sovereign city-state beyond the reach of Parliament and the Crown, and that the monarch must ask the Lord Mayor's permission to enter it. The documents do not bear this out. They tell a different story and, in some respects, a more interesting one. The City's real privileges are unusual enough without inflation, and three of them stand out.

The first is the vote. In Corporation elections, businesses are allocated votes alongside residents. At the City elections of April 2013, the roughly 9,000 resident votes were outnumbered by a business vote some 32,000 strong. Those figures come from the Green Party, which campaigns to end the arrangement. The same Green Party statement cited a Bloomberg survey that questioned how far the business vote represented the workers it was meant to speak for. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:wiki_city_of_london_corporation] Nowhere else in the United Kingdom do businesses vote in this way.

The second is the City Remembrancer, an official who watches Parliament on the Corporation's behalf. The Remembrancer sits in the House of Commons, facing the Speaker's chair, the only person who is not a member permitted on the floor. The office reviews legislation to protect the Corporation's interests. It has a budget reported at about £6 million and a team of in-house lawyers. The author Nicholas Shaxson has called it "the world's oldest institutional lobbyist." In 2013 the Green MP Caroline Lucas formally asked the Speaker to remove the Remembrancer from the floor. [cite:greenparty_remembrancer_2013] [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation] [cite:wiki_city_remembrancer]

The third is ceremonial. When the monarch enters the City on state occasions, the Lord Mayor meets the sovereign at Temple Bar and presents the City's sword. An account from 1869 described this as the Mayor "requiring" the sovereign to ask leave to enter. Yet the ceremony is a courtesy, not a legal requirement. The sovereign's carriage has passed Temple Bar without stopping, as the BBC's Huw Edwards noted on air during a Jubilee. Parliament's authority over the City rests on statute, not on the Lord Mayor's leave. [cite:metabunk_city_sovereign_debunk] [cite:wiki_temple_bar]

The legend stops at the sword. The statute book does not. What the record does not support is sovereignty. The Corporation is a local authority, not a state. It has less autonomy than the Crown Dependencies of Jersey, Guernsey and the Isle of Man, each of which has its own legislature, and Parliament can and does legislate for the City. [cite:metabunk_city_sovereign_debunk] [cite:ohuiginn_city_corporation] The Corporation's own self-description is more revealing than the legend: it has said that it acts "rather like a trade body" for finance and business. That is the documented finding. It is also the more serious one, because it is true.

The City matters to this history not as a hidden sovereign but as something older and better documented: an arrangement that gave finance a formal place inside the legislature of the state that would later issue the Balfour Declaration. Its firms could vote. Its interests were read into bills before they passed. The banking houses that follow, beginning with [scene:bc_s02|the London house of N M Rothschild & Sons], did their business inside this structure.
