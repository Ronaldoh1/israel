Where Governments Said No: Switzerland, Germany, Britain and Denmark

Palantir's platforms run in American agencies with little public argument about whether to buy them; the arguments are about how they are used. In Europe the argument has often come earlier, at the point of purchase, and several governments and one constitutional court have said no, or not yet, or only on conditions. Their reasons are worth reading closely, because they are the clearest statements on record of what a careful buyer thinks the risks of such a platform are. Israel's contract with the company is treated separately, in [scene:de37_palantir_and_israel|Part Forty] and in the library's [scene:ow9_israel|One-Way Mirror].

Switzerland: seven years of refusals

In December 2025 the Swiss online magazine Republik and the investigative collective WAV published the results of 59 freedom-of-information requests to 41 federal offices. They documented at least nine approaches by Palantir to Swiss authorities over seven years, all unsuccessful, and an internal assessment of about twenty pages by the Swiss army, prompted by a meeting between Palantir's European head, Louis Mosley, and the army's chief, which advised against working with the company. Its central concern was the risk that data could flow to United States government agencies; it also weighed dependence on a single foreign supplier and named open-source alternatives, though its authors had not tested them in depth. [cite:de27_site36_swiss] [cite:de27_np_republik] Palantir's answer was that it is "not a surveillance company," that no data is passed to American authorities and that it would resist any order to do so, and that its systems are self-contained, with customers keeping control. [cite:de27_site36_swiss]

Palantir then went to court, demanding that Republik publish a counter-statement on 23 points it said were inaccurate. The Zurich Commercial Court ruled on 4 June 2026 for the magazine on 22 of the 23, ordering one minor correction about the development history of a Palantir product. [cite:de27_np_republik] On 29 June Palantir said it accepted the ruling and would not appeal. [cite:de27_srf_accepts] The Swiss record is therefore a well-documented refusal for stated reasons of data sovereignty, not a finding that Palantir had misused anything.

Germany: a constitutional threshold, and a split between states and army

Germany's Federal Constitutional Court ruled on 16 February 2023 that the police laws of Hesse and Hamburg allowing "automated data analysis" were unconstitutional. The court described what such analysis does, establishing "relationships or connections between persons, groups of persons, institutions, organisations, objects or matters," including through data mining and self-learning systems, and held that for so intrusive a power the law must require a "sufficiently identifiable danger," a threshold the two laws fell "far short" of. Hamburg's provision was void at once; Hesse's could run, with restrictions, until 30 September 2023. [cite:de27_bverfg_2023] The court named Hesse's platform, hessenDATA, but not its supplier; Hesse had used Palantir's software since 2017, and a Palantir executive responded that the company "merely provides the software for processing data, not the data itself." [cite:de27_reuters_bverfg] [cite:de27_wiki_pltr]

The ruling did not end German police use; it set conditions for it. Bavaria, North Rhine-Westphalia and Hesse use Palantir's software, and in November 2025 Baden-Württemberg's parliament voted 113 to 22 to let its police use Gotham from the second quarter of 2026, while passing a resolution calling for a "European Palantir alternative by 2030 at the latest"; the civil-liberties group GFF has suits pending against two states' laws. [cite:de27_posteo_bw] The armed forces went the other way. In April 2026 Germany ruled out Palantir's Maven Smart System for the Bundeswehr, and in September the head of its cyber command said it was evaluating 30 AI tools, most of them German, with a prototype due in 2027. [cite:de27_tnw_bundeswehr]

Britain: a health contract under review

In 2023 NHS England awarded Palantir a £330 million, seven-year contract to build a Federated Data Platform linking health-service data. [cite:de27_reuters_nhs] [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] In June 2026 the technology secretary, Liz Kendall, said the government was "reviewing every single aspect" of the deal, citing patient confidentiality, public trust and reliance on an American supplier; the initial term ends in early 2027, when it must decide whether to continue. [cite:de27_reuters_nhs] The government's infrastructure authority had meanwhile raised the platform's whole-life cost to £1.1 billion and cut its forecast benefits to £808 million, while still rating the project green; two parliamentary committees urged ministers to consider the break clause, and NHS England apologised in August for a privacy assessment that wrongly said only NHS staff could see identifiable data. [cite:de27_computing_nista] Palantir's British head, Louis Mosley, called a review clause "good and normal practice" and warned against "ideologically motivated campaigners." [cite:de27_guardian_mosley] In May 2026 London's mayor blocked a £50 million police contract with the company. [cite:de27_reuters_nhs] As of October 2026 no decision on the health contract had been announced.

Denmark: a system in use, a supplier unnamed

Denmark's national police have run a system called POL-INTEL since 2017, and it is widely reported to be built on Palantir's Gotham. [cite:de27_wiki_pltr] The government's own answers to parliament describe it as integrating internal and external data sources to strengthen "intelligence-led" policing, but do not name a supplier. [cite:de27_ftdk_polintel] The Danish case is documented as a system and reported as Palantir's.

Read together, Europe has not said no to Palantir; it has said no in some places and set terms in others. The refusals rest on two documented arguments. One is legal: a constitution can require a concrete danger before police may run joined data through an analytic engine, which American law does not require for data an agency already holds or buys. The other is sovereignty: a buyer outside the United States worries that an American company could be compelled by American law to hand over what it holds, which Palantir denies would happen. Neither argument is a finding that the company has misused data, and the record in this part contains none outside the Medicaid incident, where the data was the American government's own. [cite:de27_bverfg_2023] [cite:de27_site36_swiss] [cite:de27_npr_medicaid]

The worst-case reading, tested

The book's working hypothesis, the Sophia platform's and never a finding, reads immigration enforcement as the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination. Palantir bears on it as the joining layer: the tool that would turn the state's scattered records into one view of a person.

Documented: Palantir's platforms sit at the centre of the Pentagon's targeting and planning, the Army's data, and ICE's enforcement, with US government revenue of $809 million in a single quarter, up 90 percent. [cite:de27_8k_q2_2026] Three men control just under half of all shareholder votes through a voting trust. [cite:de27_10q_q2_2026] Its chief executive speaks openly of lethal use against enemies abroad. [cite:de27_giz_karp2025] And in January 2026 Medicaid data reached the company through ICE outside a court's limits, with copies later found. [cite:de27_npr_medicaid]

Probable: that Palantir is becoming the hub through which ICE's other data passes, because contracts and work statements require it, and that this lock-in would carry any future change of policy through the same platform. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:de28_bu_lexis] [cite:de27_hg_ero]

Not supported: that Palantir has used, or been directed to use, its software against political critics in the United States, or that its federal growth was arranged by its chairman's political allies; no document shows either. The record runs against two parts of the worst case. The ICE work began in 2014 under the Obama administration, so the tool predates the occasion. [cite:ow9_mj_icm2019] And the strongest checks on platforms of this kind, a constitutional court's threshold, a buyer's refusal on sovereignty grounds, a judge's pause on Medicaid sharing, are documented as working, in Europe and in one American courtroom. [cite:de27_bverfg_2023] [cite:de27_npr_medicaid] Whether the American law around the platform supplies such checks is the subject of [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty].
