Shaykh 'Izz al-Din al-Qassam: From Jabla to Ya'bad, 1882–1935

On 20 November 1935, in the hills near Ya'bad, close to Jenin, a British police force fought a small armed band for six hours. When it was over the band's leader, a preacher from Haifa in his fifties, was dead [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. Shaykh 'Izz al-Din al-Qassam had spent fifteen years in Palestine as a teacher, imam and official of the Muslim religious courts, and the last several of them, by the accounts that survive, organizing in secret for armed struggle. His life in the field lasted eight days. His death drew the largest funeral Haifa had held, and the argument over what to call him has not ended.

He was not born in Palestine. Al-Qassam was born in 1882 in Jabla, on the Syrian coast south of Latakia, and studied at al-Azhar in Cairo [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. After the First World War, when France took control of the Syrian coast, he joined the revolt of 1919–20 against the French there, and the French sentenced him to death [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. He reached Haifa in late 1920, or in December 1920 in another account, taught at the Burj school, and became imam and preacher at the Istiqlal Mosque [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam] [cite:rs_rd3_majalla_qassam]. (The mosque's name, "independence," is a coincidence of vocabulary; it is not the Istiqlal party founded in 1932, though the two would later cross paths through one of that party's founders.) The preacher who settled in Haifa already carried a death sentence from one European mandatory power for fighting it.

Through the 1920s his career ran through the ordinary institutions of Muslim public life under the Mandate. In July 1928 he was elected president of the Haifa branch of the Young Men's Muslim Association, and in 1930 the Haifa Shari'a court appointed him a ma'dhun, an official post conferred by the religious court [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. These were positions of standing, held in the open. They gave him a pulpit and an association of young men.

Alongside that public life, according to the accounts of his movement, he built a clandestine one. Those accounts describe cells of no more than five men, organized into units for preaching, for intelligence and for military training, and guerrilla attacks on Jewish settlements beginning in 1931 that soon paused after leaks reached the British [cite:rs_rd3_globalsecurity_qassam]. This part of the record rests on reporting rather than on documents, and it is thin. The research behind this book holds no verified list of those attacks, no verified count of their victims and no verified account of their dates and places; figures for them that circulate elsewhere rest on a single unconfirmed source and are not used here. What is reported is the structure: a small, compartmented organization that kept religious teaching, intelligence and arms in separate hands, and that chose Jewish settlements as well as the British as targets.

The decision to go to the hills came in the autumn of 1935, and the leadership did not support it. Al-Qassam wrote to Rashid al-Haj Ibrahim of his plan to take to the hills [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_hajibrahim_h]. Al-Haj Ibrahim was one of the eleven founders of the Istiqlal party, which had named British imperialism as the main enemy [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_hajibrahim]. According to Al Majalla, the Mufti of Jerusalem, Hajj Amin al-Husseini, declined to back a revolt, saying the time was not right [cite:rs_rd3_majalla_qassam]. That account is reported, from one outlet; the research does not hold the Mufti's own words. If it is accurate, al-Qassam went to the hills without the backing of the Mufti, whose place among the rival leaderships and parties of these years is described in [scene:rd3_arab_politics|Palestinian politics, 1928–1935].

The autumn was tense. In October or November 1935 (the sources disagree on the date) the British seized 537 barrels at Jaffa port that had been shipped from Belgium with rifles and ammunition hidden in cement, and the discovery caused significant unrest among Palestinian Arabs; one account dates a protest general strike to 26 October 1935 [cite:rs_rd3_ej_haganah] [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_cement]. That shipment and the armed bodies of the Yishuv belong to [scene:s07e2|the armed line]. The sources used here do not say whether the seizure bore on al-Qassam's decision, and this book does not assume it did.

On 12 November 1935 al-Qassam declared jihad in Haifa and left for the forests of Ya'bad, near Jenin, with about eleven companions [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. Eight days later, on 20 November, he was killed in a six-hour fight with a larger British police force near Ya'bad [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. The nearest thing to a contemporary official account in the research is an American one. Leland Morris, the US Consul General, reporting to Washington in April 1936, wrote that al-Qassam's bands had caused the death of one British constable, and that four of his men died and five were captured [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512_h].

How large his following was, and how many died with him, are disputed. Al Majalla, drawing on British archives, puts his fighters at between 50 and 200 and calls a claim of 800 "grossly exaggerated"; it says he died with two colleagues [cite:rs_rd3_majalla_qassam]. The Interactive Encyclopedia of the Palestine Question says al-Qassam and four of his men were killed, and the US consul's despatch likewise reports four of his men killed and five captured [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam] [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512_h]. The consul's figure is the only one here written at the time by an outside official; it agrees with the Palestinian encyclopedia and not with Al Majalla on the dead. None of the sources reconciles the counts.

The response to his death showed a reach that his small band had not. Haifa declared a general strike on 21 November 1935 and held the largest funeral procession the city had seen; he was buried at Balad al-Shaykh [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam]. It came at a moment of unusual Palestinian unity. By late 1935 the five Arab parties had formed a united front, which the same American consul described as a response to a "national emergency" [cite:rs_rd3_frus1936_d512]. The established leaders had not followed al-Qassam into the hills; a crowd of Haifa's people followed his coffin.

What to call him is the oldest dispute about him, and both readings rest on documented ground. Palestinian accounts present al-Qassam as a leader of resistance to British occupation whose death helped ignite the revolt of 1936–39 [cite:rs_rd3_palquest_qassam] [cite:rs_rd3_globalsecurity_qassam]. In that reading he was a man who had already fought one European mandatory power in Syria and chose arms against a second; the fight in which he died was a fight with British police. The other reading starts from the targets. His group's attacks were directed at Jewish settlers as well as at the British, and he called for confronting Jewish settlers "as enemies and not as immigrants or guests" [cite:rs_rd3_globalsecurity_qassam]. In that reading, a movement that attacked civilian settlements was not only an anti-colonial insurgency, whatever its leader's death later came to symbolize. The two descriptions are not mutually exclusive. The documented record supports both halves: an armed campaign against the Mandate power, and attacks on Jewish settlements, with civilians among the targets. This book does not choose between the labels; it sets out what each rests on.

His name outlived him. In 1991 Hamas named its military wing the 'Izz al-Din al-Qassam Brigades, according to Al Majalla [cite:rs_rd3_majalla_qassam]. That later use has shaped how he is remembered on every side, and it is one reason the disputes over his following and his targets are argued with such heat. It belongs to a different era from the one in which he lived.

Much about al-Qassam remains unsettled by the sources available here. The size of his organization rests on estimates that run from 50 fighters to a claimed 800. How many died with him on 20 November is given as two or four. The attacks of 1931 onward are reported in outline but not documented case by case, so their number and their victims cannot be stated. The Mufti's refusal rests on a single reported account. What is solid is the arc: a Syrian-born cleric condemned by the French, a respected Haifa preacher and court official for a decade, a clandestine organizer, and a man killed by British police eight days after declaring an armed revolt that the Palestinian leadership had declined to call. Within months the country would be in the general strike and revolt told in [scene:s07a5|the Arab Revolt].
