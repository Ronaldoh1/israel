Palestinian Christians: the Theology from the Other Side of the Wall

The Christians who live in the land the theology is about are almost absent from its literature. They are few and growing fewer. Christians made up about 12 per cent of the population of Palestine early in the twentieth century; by the Palestinian census of 2017 there were roughly 47,000 to 50,000 in the West Bank and East Jerusalem, about 1 per cent of the population, and about 1,000 in Gaza before the war that began in October 2023. [cite:ts11_aje_christians2025] Many belong to churches older than any European denomination, Greek Orthodox, Latin Catholic, Melkite, Armenian, Syriac, Anglican and Lutheran, and a small evangelical community. From the late 1980s some of their clergy and scholars built a theology that answers Christian Zionism directly. This section follows four of its landmarks: Naim Ateek and Sabeel, the Kairos Palestine document, the Christ at the Checkpoint conferences, and Munther Isaac's preaching during the Gaza war, each with the criticism it drew.

Naim Ateek and Sabeel

Naim Ateek was born in Beisan, now Beit She'an, in 1937; in 1948 his family was displaced to Nazareth, and he has said that Israeli forces destroyed the town. He became an Anglican priest in 1967 and later a canon of St George's Cathedral in Jerusalem. His book Justice, and Only Justice (1989), based on his doctoral dissertation, is described as the first statement of a Palestinian theology of liberation; it argues against Zionist readings of the Hebrew Bible. He founded the Sabeel Ecumenical Liberation Theology Center in Jerusalem (its own histories give 1991 and 1994) and wrote a 2006 paper calling for "morally responsible investment," which became a reference point for the church divestment campaigns described in the previous section. [cite:ts11_wiki_ateek] [scene:ts11_churches|The historic churches]

Ateek's language has been contested. His Easter message of 2001 described Palestine as "one huge golgotha," using the crucifixion as an image of Palestinian suffering. [cite:ts11_wiki_ateek] Critics have argued that Sabeel's use of crucifixion imagery about Israel revives the old Christian charge against the Jews that Nostra Aetate repudiated; the reference work consulted for this book lists a 2011 article making that case but does not summarise it or record a reply, and the argument is therefore noted here as a criticism made, not a finding. [cite:ts11_wiki_ateek] [cite:ts11_va_nostra4]

Kairos Palestine, 2009

On 11 December 2009, in Bethlehem, a group of Palestinian Christians launched the Kairos Palestine document at an event chaired by the former Latin Patriarch Michel Sabbah. Its authors included Sabbah, Archbishop Atallah Hanna and the Lutheran Bishop Munib Younan, with Rifat Kassis as coordinator; Younan later asked for his name to be removed. [cite:ts11_jcr_kairos] [cite:ts11_transcend_kairos] The document calls the Israeli occupation "a sin against God and humanity" and states that "any theology that legitimizes the occupation is far from Christian teachings." It asks churches abroad "to revisit theologies that justify crimes perpetrated against our people and the dispossession of the land," declares that "non-violent resistance to this injustice is a right and duty for all Palestinians including Christians," and calls for divestment and "an economic and commercial boycott of everything produced by the occupation." [cite:ts11_transcend_kairos] [cite:ts11_jcr_kairos] Sabeel endorsed it. [cite:ts11_wiki_ateek] In the passages checked, the document does not call Christian Zionism a "heresy," as some library material states; its target is "Biblical fundamentalism" and any theology used to justify the occupation. [cite:ts11_jcr_kairos]

The document's critics read it differently. Malcolm Lowe, writing for the Jewish-Christian Relations site, noted that its section 4.3 says that "the roots of 'terrorism' are in the human injustice committed and in the evil of the occupation," argued that the scare quotes and the language of "resistance" served as euphemisms, and observed that the excerpts he examined contained no outright rejection of terrorism. [cite:ts11_jcr_kairos] The document's own text, as published at its launch, commits its authors to non-violent resistance. [cite:ts11_transcend_kairos] Both readings rest on the same text and are given here side by side.

Christ at the Checkpoint

The most direct challenge to Christian Zionism from within evangelicalism itself has come from Bethlehem Bible College, an evangelical school, which first held its Christ at the Checkpoint conference in 2010 and has held it every two years since. One of its stated objectives is to "create a platform for serious engagement with Christian Zionism"; its 2012 manifesto stated that "Any exclusive claim to land of the Bible in the name of God is not in line with the teaching of Scripture." [cite:ts11_bbc_catc] [cite:ts11_jta_catc2014] Its speakers have included American evangelical figures: Joel Hunter, a board member of the National Association of Evangelicals, in 2012, and Geoff Tunnicliffe of the World Evangelical Alliance in 2014. Hunter said he heard no talk of Israel as an enemy and that the conference's aim was to "hear from Arab Christians." [cite:ts11_jta_catc2014]

Christian Zionist organisations answered in their own terms. The International Christian Embassy Jerusalem issued a briefing on 4 March 2012 describing the conference's message as "one-sided" and "based on a deeply flawed historical narrative," warning of "Cloaked versions of Replacement theology," and stating that some speakers had gone "into outright anti-Semitism." [cite:ts11_icej_catc] CUFI's David Brog said in 2014 that the "anti-Israel" message was "resonating" and that "This generation is in play." [cite:ts11_jta_catc2014] The exchange is a theological dispute between named evangelical institutions, each stating its own position.

Munther Isaac and the Gaza war

Munther Isaac, ordained in 2016 as a Lutheran pastor in Bethlehem and director of Christ at the Checkpoint from 2012, wrote From Land to Lands (2015), which challenges a Zionist reading of the Bible and argues that the land should be shared, and The Other Side of the Wall (2020), which criticises Christian Zionists for overlooking abuses against Palestinians. [cite:ts11_wiki_isaac] In Advent 2023 his Christmas Lutheran Church set its nativity scene in rubble, and on Christmas Eve he preached "Christ in the Rubble," which circulated widely; his book of that title followed in 2025. In April 2024 he told Tucker Carlson that Israel was committing genocide in Gaza. Reviewers have said he describes Palestinian suffering accurately but disregards Israeli security concerns, and his permit to travel between Bethlehem and Jerusalem was cancelled, which he links to his activism. [cite:ts11_wiki_isaac]

The war reached the churches of Gaza themselves. On 19 October 2023 an Israeli airstrike destroyed a building in the compound of the Greek Orthodox Church of Saint Porphyrius, where displaced people were sheltering, killing 18 civilians; the Israeli military said it had struck a Hamas "command and control centre," and Amnesty International found no sign of a military objective and called the strike reckless, amounting to a war crime. [cite:ts11_amnesty_porph] On 17 July 2025 a tank shell struck the Holy Family Catholic Church in Gaza City, killing three people and wounding the parish priest, Gabriel Romanelli, among others. Israel's Foreign Ministry said that "Israel never targets churches," Prime Minister Netanyahu expressed regret that "stray ammunition" had hit the church, the Latin Patriarch Pierbattista Pizzaballa said "they hit the church directly," and the Vatican called the attack "unjustifiable." [cite:ts11_ncr_holyfamily] In the West Bank, arsonists targeted St George's Church in Taybeh in July 2025. [cite:ts11_aje_christians2025]

What the record shows is a body of Palestinian Christian theology, written by named clergy and institutions, that rejects Christian Zionism on scriptural grounds and calls on foreign churches to act. It also shows the counter-charges: that this theology echoes older anti-Jewish themes and is soft on violence, made by named critics and organisations. Neither side's case is settled by the other's assertion; the texts are cited so that each can be read.
