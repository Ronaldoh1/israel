What the Vatican's Authority Covered

The wartime record of the Catholic Church, taken up in the next section, is often argued over with a loose idea of what the Church could and could not do. A common assumption holds that the Vatican has, or claims, authority over Christian scripture and doctrine in general, Catholic and Protestant alike. It has not held such authority since the sixteenth century, and the limits of what it did hold are the frame for any fair reading of what it did between 1939 and 1945.

The Scofield Reference Bible shows the boundary plainly. Discussed elsewhere in this library as the document that did most to shape American Christian Zionism, it was never submitted to Rome for approval, and Rome was never in a position to demand its retraction. That was not an oversight on Rome's part. It follows from the structure of Church authority itself.

That authority is called the magisterium: the Church's teaching office, exercised through the Pope and the bishops. It binds Catholics, which in practice means Catholic doctrine, Catholic clergy and Catholic religious publications. It has never claimed, and has no mechanism to exercise, binding authority over people or institutions outside the Catholic Church. [cite:catholic_imprimatur_explained]

The instrument through which it governs what Catholics print is the imprimatur, Latin for "let it be printed." It is an approval process applied to Catholic religious publications specifically: catechisms, devotional works, and translations of scripture intended for Catholic use. Protestant publishers never sought it and never required it. Among them was Oxford University Press, which published the Scofield Bible.

The reason for the boundary lies in the Reformation. Its founding premise was the rejection of Rome's authority to bind the conscience of believers. The principle of sola scriptura held that scripture, not the Church hierarchy, was the final authority. Evangelical Protestants who read the Scofield Bible stood, by their own tradition's founding principle, entirely outside any claim Rome could make on their beliefs. To ask why the Vatican did not stop Scofield is a little like asking why one country's supreme court did not strike down another country's law. The jurisdiction was never there.

That answers the narrow question and sharpens a wider one. Authority to bind and the capacity to speak in public are two different things. The Vatican has routinely issued prominent public statements on subjects entirely outside its binding jurisdiction: climate policy, nuclear weapons, immigration law, and the birth-control policies of secular states. It exercises the second capacity constantly on matters where it has none of the first. [cite:wiki_pius_xii_holocaust]

So the fair question about Scofield is not why Rome failed to force a retraction; it never could have. It is why the Church, which by the account in this library's sources had already ruled against premillennial dispensationalism internally in the 1940s, did not use its considerable public voice to say so loudly, as it has on other questions equally outside its jurisdiction. The same distinction, between what the Church could command and what it could say, runs through the far graver wartime question of [scene:vat_s00b|Pius XII and the Holocaust].

This section is a frame, not a finding. It establishes the reach of the Church's formal authority and nothing more. It does not settle why the Church spoke on some subjects and kept quiet on others; the sources here state the distinction, not the motives behind any particular silence.
