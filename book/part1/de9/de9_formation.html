Frankfurt, Swakopmund, Stanford: The Making of an Investor

Peter Andreas Thiel was born on 11 October 1967 in Frankfurt am Main, in what was then West Germany. His father, Klaus Friedrich Thiel, was a chemical engineer who went on to work for mining companies; his mother was Susanne Thiel. The family moved to the United States when he was about one year old and lived first in Cleveland, Ohio. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] Most of what is known about the next decade comes from biographies and from his own later remarks, and the two need to be kept apart. Where a detail rests on his own account, this section says so; where a detail belongs to his parents rather than to him, it says that too, because a father's employer is part of a child's setting, not part of the child's record.

Southern Africa, 1971–1977

In 1971 the family moved to South West Africa, the territory that became Namibia, then governed by South Africa, and it also lived for a time in South Africa itself. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] Klaus Thiel worked as a project manager on a uranium mine near the coastal town of Swakopmund. The boy attended Pridwin, a whites-only preparatory school in Johannesburg, and then spent two years at the German-language school in Swakopmund. [cite:de9_dm_davis] He changed elementary schools seven times in all. He later linked the Swakopmund school, with its strict discipline, to his dislike of regimentation, a remark that rests on his own account and is usually cited as an early root of his libertarian views. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] Max Chafkin, whose 2021 biography The Contrarian is the fullest independent account of these years, wrote that Thiel "often spoke fondly of his childhood in South Africa." [cite:de9_dm_davis] Nothing in the published record documents what a child between four and ten thought about the apartheid system around him, and nothing here suggests otherwise.

The family settled in Foster City, a planned suburb south of San Francisco, in 1977. At Bowditch Middle School he placed first in a statewide mathematics competition, and in 1985 he graduated as valedictorian of San Mateo High School. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel]

Stanford and the Review, 1985–1992

Thiel studied philosophy at Stanford University and graduated in 1989. There he encountered the French literary theorist René Girard, whose "mimetic" theory, that human desire is learned by imitation and that imitation breeds rivalry and violence, strongly influenced him as an undergraduate and has remained central to his thinking. [cite:de9_fortune_antichrist] [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] Girard's place in his thinking, up to the lectures on the Antichrist he gave in 2025, is the subject of [scene:de10_girard_and_the_katechon|Part Ten].

In 1987 he co-founded The Stanford Review, a conservative and libertarian student paper, and served as its first editor-in-chief until he graduated. The paper was launched in the middle of a campus argument over Stanford's decision to replace its required course on Western culture with a broader programme. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] It became a durable node in his later network: David Sacks, who would co-write a book with him and in 2025 became the White House's adviser on artificial intelligence and cryptocurrency, wrote for it. [cite:de9_fortune_net24] Who else passed through it, and where they sit now, is mapped in [scene:de13_who_they_are|Part Thirteen].

The apartheid remarks: an allegation and a denial

Two accounts from Stanford concern apartheid, and both are disputed. In November 2016, as Thiel's support for Donald Trump drew attention, Julie Lythcott-Haims, a former Stanford dean who lived in his freshman dormitory in 1985–86, said he had told her that "moral issues were irrelevant" to the apartheid government's economy. A second classmate, Megan Maxwell, said he had told her "you shouldn't judge a government based on whether it fits your view of morality." [cite:de9_wnyc_apartheid] His spokesperson replied: "Peter has never supported apartheid, but he can easily see how a conversation might be misremembered 30 years later." [cite:de9_wnyc_apartheid] Chafkin's biography later reported that two former peers recalled Thiel saying of apartheid "It works," and that South Africa's denial of civil rights to Black people "was economically sound"; Thiel's spokesperson denied that those encounters took place. [cite:de9_dm_davis]

These are recollections of private conversations three decades old, given on the record by named people, and denied by the man they concern. They are not documents, and no statement in Thiel's own hand or voice expressing support for apartheid is on the record. Both accounts are recorded here, and the dispute is graded in [scene:de9_claims_vs_record|the claims against the record].

The Diversity Myth, 1995

After Stanford, Thiel and Sacks turned the Review's preoccupations into a book, The Diversity Myth: Multiculturalism and Political Intolerance on Campus, published by the Independent Institute, a libertarian research organisation in Oakland. [cite:de9_indep_dm] Its argument, in the publisher's summary, was that Stanford's turn to multiculturalism in the 1980s and 1990s had produced less learning, not more: a curriculum stripped of its Western civilisation requirement, speech codes, lowered standards and a conformist culture that viewed minority identity mainly through the lens of oppression, amounting to an anti-Western bias rather than real openness. [cite:de9_indep_dm]

The book also contained passages on sexual assault that were widely quoted when he entered politics, among them the suggestion that a woman might "realize" that she had been "raped" the next day or even days later. [cite:de9_cnn_apology] On 25 October 2016 Thiel told CNNMoney: "More than two decades ago, I co-wrote a book with several insensitive, crudely argued statements," and "I regret writing passages that have been taken to suggest otherwise." [cite:de9_cnn_apology] The apology is his own explanation and is recorded beside the original text, not in place of it. The book's broader case against campus multiculturalism he has not withdrawn.

Law and finance, 1989–1998

Thiel took a law degree at Stanford Law School in 1992. He clerked for Judge James Larry Edmondson of the US Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit in 1992–93, then joined the New York firm Sullivan & Cromwell as a securities lawyer and left in less than a year. In 1993 he became a derivatives trader in currency options at Credit Suisse, and in the same years worked as a speechwriter for William Bennett, the former education secretary. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] He then went home to California and raised about $1 million from friends and family to start an investment firm, Thiel Capital Management; reference works date its founding to 1996. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel] In 1998 he put $100,000 into a start-up called Fieldlink, the company that would become PayPal. [cite:de9_wiki_thiel]

What the formation shows

The documented formation is that of a gifted, mobile child of an engineering family, a philosophy student shaped by Girard, a campus polemicist and a lawyer who chose finance over the law within a year. Three features of it recur through the rest of this book. The first is the network: the Review and the book with Sacks were the start of a circle of collaborators that would follow him into PayPal and, decades later, into government. [cite:de9_fortune_net24] The second is the habit of contrarian argument in print, from the Review to The Diversity Myth to the 2009 essay in which he wrote that he no longer believed "freedom and democracy are compatible," discussed in [scene:de3_other_lineages|Part Three] and [scene:de10_education_of_a_libertarian|Part Ten]. [cite:de3_cato_thiel] The third is that the most damaging items in the early record, the apartheid remarks, are disputed recollections, while the most quoted written passages, in the 1995 book, are ones he has said he regrets. The rest of his life is a matter of companies, money and filings, and is far better documented, beginning in [scene:de9_paypal_palantir_facebook|the next section].
