The Baron Who Built the Colonies, and Turned Herzl Away

A persistent popular story holds that the Rothschilds funded Zionism from the beginning and that Herzl's movement was their project. The documents separate two things that story fuses. Rothschild philanthropy did build settlements in Ottoman Palestine, and it began doing so fifteen years before Herzl. But when Herzl asked for support for a political movement, the answer was no, and the movement that eventually produced a state was launched over that refusal.

The philanthropist in question was Baron Edmond de Rothschild of the Paris house. After the pogroms of 1881–82 in the Russian Empire, he began financing Jewish agricultural colonies in Ottoman Palestine. Between 1882 and 1895 he financed and administered roughly thirty of them, including Rishon LeZion, Zikhron Ya'akov and Rosh Pinna. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] The work earned him the title "the known benefactor," HaNadiv HaYadua. In 1896 he instructed the Jewish Colonization Association to begin lending to colonists. [cite:betweenmyths_rothschild_israel]

These colonies were run on Edmond's own terms, through an administrative system of his own, and the colonists complained of his administrators. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] The model was philanthropic settlement, administered from Paris. It built settlements; it did not ask for a state.

Herzl's proposal was a different kind of thing. In July 1896 he sought Edmond's backing for political Zionism, a state secured by charter rather than colonies secured by purchase. Edmond declined. Herzl's own diary for 22–26 July 1896 records his "rude telegram to Edmond Rothschild" and his resolve that "from Rothschild's negative attitude I must salvage everything." [cite:herzl_diaries_vol2_archive] He turned instead to organizing a congress. The First Zionist Congress met in Basel the following year without the baron's sponsorship.

The refusal matters because of what came out of Basel. The Congress, the Zionist Organization and, in time, the state itself grew from the political project Edmond would not fund. His colonization had predated Herzl by fifteen years; the political movement was launched after he refused it. [cite:wiki_edmond_james_de_rothschild] The two lines did converge, but only later: through Walter Rothschild as the addressee of the Balfour letter of 1917, and through land work carried out during the Mandate by the institution that inherited Edmond's colonies.

That institution was the Palestine Jewish Colonization Association, known as PICA. In 1924 Edmond transferred his colonies to it. After his death in 1934 it was presided over by his son James and continued land purchase and settlement through the Mandate period. Its successor, Yad Hanadiv, still acts in Israel for Rothschild family trusts, and James and Dorothy de Rothschild later funded the Knesset building. David Ben-Gurion's tribute called Edmond "the builder of the Jewish Yishuv." [cite:betweenmyths_rothschild_israel]

Both halves of this record belong in the history. Rothschild money did build much of the physical foundation of the Jewish community in Palestine, and that contribution was substantial and is well documented. What it did not do was launch or own the political movement. The popular version keeps the first fact and drops the second. Restoring the second does not diminish the first; it simply shows that philanthropy and political Zionism began as two projects with different aims, different methods and, in 1896, different answers to the question of whether to seek a state.
