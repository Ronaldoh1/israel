Five Patterns: How the Brakes Came Off

A brake, in this part, means a veto point: a person or rule other than the president that can say no and make it stick. A commissioner who cannot be fired for disagreeing is one. So is an inspector general, the internal auditor Congress placed inside each large agency to look for waste and wrongdoing and report to Congress as well as to the agency head. So is a career official with a right of appeal, a sum of money Congress has ordered spent, a court with power to halt an order, and a law that keeps tax or health records away from police. Between 2017 and 2026 orders, rulings and notices weakened each of these, and they fall into five patterns. This section sets out each one with one test for every president: where an earlier administration of either party used or widened the same power, it says so. The five are not one plan. No document shows them designed together, and the book's rule holds: convergence is not coordination ([scene:de1_spine_and_overlay|Part One]). Status is as of October 2026.

Pattern one: who the president can remove

Since 1935 the Supreme Court's decision in Humphrey's Executor had let Congress protect the members of multi-member commissions, such as the Federal Trade Commission, from removal except for cause. On 29 June 2026, in Trump v. Slaughter, the Court overruled it by six votes to three and held that the president may remove FTC commissioners at will. It left open offices traditionally outside the executive branch, naming the Federal Reserve, and the same day, in Trump v. Cook, it refused the government's request to remove a Fed governor while her case continued. [cite:de0_slaughter] The ruling gave constitutional footing to an order already in force. Executive Order 14215 of 18 February 2025 sent the significant rules of independent agencies, including the Federal Election Commission, to the White House's regulatory review office before publication, let the budget office adjust how much of their money they may spend, and declared that the president's and the attorney general's legal opinions "are controlling on all employees." Only the Fed's monetary policy was excepted. [cite:de31_fr_14215]

The same pattern ran through the career service. The rule of 6 February 2026 and Executive Order 14410 of 3 June 2026 moved about 8,000 senior officials into Schedule Policy/Career, where they can be removed by written notice without the usual procedures; [scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|Part Thirty-Two] sets that change clause by clause against Curtis Yarvin's 2012 proposal and finds rhyme, not provenance. [cite:de30_wh_eo14410] [cite:de30_npr_8000]

The removal power did not grow under one party alone. On 9 July 2021 President Biden removed the Commissioner of Social Security, Andrew Saul, although the statute allowed his removal only for cause. A Justice Department opinion issued the same day, relying on two Supreme Court decisions of 2020 and 2021, concluded that "the statutory restriction on removing the commissioner is unconstitutional." Saul replied, "I consider myself the term-protected Commissioner of Social Security." [cite:de31_govexec_saul] What is new in 2025 and 2026 is the reach: from one officer to the commissions as a class and to thousands of career posts.

Pattern two: the watchdogs

On the night of Friday 24 January 2025 17 inspectors general were dismissed by short emails, effective immediately. The law requires the president to give Congress 30 days' notice and reasons before an inspector general is removed, and Congress received neither. Senator Chuck Grassley, a Republican who had defended an inspector general against President Obama in 2009, wrote that "There may be good reason the IGs were fired. We need to know that if so." Senator Elizabeth Warren called it "a purge of independent watchdogs in the middle of the night." [cite:de31_npr_igs] Eight of those removed sued. At a hearing in March 2025 a Justice Department lawyer told the court, "we don't dispute that the president did not comply with the sentence in the statute," while arguing that the notice rule did not bind him. [cite:de31_gma_storch] On 24 September 2025 Judge Ana Reyes ruled: "President Trump violated the IGA. That much is obvious." She still declined to reinstate them, because they had not shown irreparable harm and the president could simply fire them again after giving notice. [cite:de31_wt_storch]

Removing inspectors general is not new, and both parties have done it. President Obama removed the AmeriCorps inspector general, Gerald Walpin, in June 2009, saying he had lost confidence in him; Grassley questioned then too whether the notice rule had been met. [cite:de31_govexec_walpin] In 2020 President Trump replaced several, including those at Defense and in the intelligence community. [cite:de31_npr_igs] In December 2022 Congress tightened the law to require "a substantive rationale, including detailed and case-specific reasons," and the first tests came from President Biden, who gave notice in 2024 that he was removing the Railroad Retirement Board's inspector general after an integrity inquiry. [cite:de31_crs_in12363] The 2025 removals differ in scale and in form: seventeen at once, with no stated reasons and no notice.

Pattern three: the purse

The Constitution gives Congress the power to spend, and the Impoundment Control Act of 1974, passed after President Nixon refused to spend money Congress had voted, sets the rules for withholding it. An impoundment is a withholding; a rescission is a cancellation, which Congress must approve within 45 days. The Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's own auditor, rules on whether a withholding is lawful. By 29 September 2025 it had found seven violations in that year alone, covering electric-vehicle charging, museums and libraries, Head Start, school energy upgrades, medical research at the National Institutes of Health and two sets of disaster funds. "The burden to justify withholdings rests with FEMA," it wrote of the last. [cite:de31_sac_fema]

On 28 August 2025 the White House sent Congress a "pocket rescission": a request to cancel nearly $5 billion in foreign aid, sent so late that the money would expire before the 45 days ran out, whatever Congress did. The budget office argued that the statute allowed it; GAO held that the provision "may not lawfully be used to effect a pocket recission." [cite:de31_crs_pocket] On 26 September 2025 the Supreme Court, in a short unsigned order, let about $4 billion of it lapse, saying the Act likely barred the aid groups' suit. Justice Kagan, for the three dissenters, wrote that the majority had "effectively altered the balance of power between Congress and the president over the power of the purse." [cite:de31_rc_scotus_aid]

GAO found on 16 January 2020 that the first Trump administration's hold on military aid to Ukraine broke the same law: "OMB violated the ICA when it withheld USAI funds for a policy reason." [cite:de31_gao_ukraine] On 15 June 2021 it found that President Biden's pause of more than $1 billion in border-wall money was a lawful "programmatic delay," and two Republican senators answered that this showed "two sets of rules." [cite:de31_rc_wall] Presidents of both parties have held back money they asked Congress to cancel. [cite:de31_crs_pocket] The new step was the pocket rescission upheld, for now, in a ruling the Court said was not final.

Pattern four: emergencies

The National Emergencies Act of 1976 lets a president declare a national emergency, which unlocks powers in other laws. The most used is the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which lets a president block transactions with foreign threats. By May 2022 presidents had declared 75 emergencies under the Act, 68 of them mainly to impose IEEPA sanctions, and 41 were still running; the oldest dated from 1979. [cite:us2_brennan_2022] In 2025 the emergencies were used for a new purpose: tariffs on most of the world's goods, declared over drug trafficking, migration and trade deficits. [cite:us2_cato_nea50] On 20 February 2026, in Learning Resources v. Trump, the Supreme Court held by six votes to three that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs. [cite:us11_ks_ieepa] The administration moved the policy to other statutes, as [scene:de31_what_was_blocked|the third section] traces. The library's comparison of emergency rule in two countries is in [scene:us2_emergency|its section on emergency powers].

The emergency as a route around Congress had been tried by the other party. In 2022 President Biden relied on the COVID-19 national emergency and a 2003 law, the HEROES Act, to cancel student debt covering more than $430 billion in principal. On 30 June 2023 the Court struck it down by six to three, saying the Secretary of Education could not "rewrite the statute from the ground up." [cite:de31_ag_nebraska]

Pattern five: the walls between datasets

The fifth pattern loosened the rules that keep records given for one purpose from being used for another. Tax records went to immigration enforcement under an agreement of April 2025; Medicaid records followed under a July 2025 agreement; a federal notice effective 30 September 2026 allows the records of families on cash welfare (Temporary Assistance for Needy Families, or TANF) to be disclosed to other agencies, including the Department of Homeland Security, to check immigration status. [cite:de30_ap_krause] [cite:de30_hipaa_chhabria] [cite:de24b_bl_tanf] On the consumer side, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau withdrew guidance that treated background dossiers on workers as regulated credit reports, and on 15 May 2025 withdrew a proposed rule on data brokers, citing "its changed policy objectives." [cite:de24b_cs_withdraw] [cite:de31_fr_broker] The fullest account of these walls and the courts' answers is in [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|Part Thirty-Two] and [scene:de24b_benefits|Part Twenty-Five].

This pattern also predates 2025. The first Trump administration agreed in 2018 to share data on the sponsors of migrant children with immigration enforcement; the Biden administration barred that use by a rule effective 30 April 2024; the bar was rescinded on 25 March 2025. [cite:de30_tracker_orr]

If it were connected

Each pattern removes a brake on its own; joined, they would remove the brakes on one another. Each join is graded documented, probable, possible or not supported. Removal and data: the acting head of the tax agency reportedly stepped down over the tax-sharing agreement in April 2025, and under the new schedule an official in her chain who objected to a data transfer could be removed by written notice. That join is possible; no removal for refusing a transfer is on record. [cite:de30_ap_krause] [cite:de30_wh_eo14410] Watchdogs and data: inspectors general are the offices that audit how agencies use personal records, and the Homeland Security inspector general opened such an audit in February 2026. Removing the auditor of a data join is possible; it has not happened at that office. [cite:de28_warner_audit] The purse and the watchdogs: an oversight office whose funds are withheld stops working without anyone being fired; possible, with no document. Removal and the commissions: after Slaughter a president can remove the members of a commission that enforces privacy or election law; that is documented as a power and not yet as a use against a sitting regulator of data. [cite:de0_slaughter] [cite:de31_fr_14215] What stands in the way of every join is the same short list: the notice and reasons rules Congress wrote, courts that will still rule on them, and the Fed's carve-out.
