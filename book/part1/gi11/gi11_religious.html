The Kook Lineage and Religious Zionism

"Religious Zionism" names two different things, and the difference matters for this book. It is the name of a broad current within Orthodox Judaism that treats the modern state as religiously significant, and it is also the name of a specific political party led by Bezalel Smotrich. Neither is a synonym for religious Jews. Many observant Jews and several of their best-known authorities rejected the territorial theology traced here, and this section sets their record beside it. The political scientist Nadav Shelef, who followed how religious nationalist groups defined the homeland from 1925 to 2005, found that their claims changed over time: a minority backed a Nile-to-Euphrates homeland in 1925, the mainstream later narrowed its claim to Israel, the Golan, Gaza and the West Bank, and a hardline wing later revived the larger claim. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] The lineage below is the one through which the post-1967 claim to the whole land acquired its religious form. [scene:gi1_versions|Part One: the versions]

Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook and Mercaz HaRav, 1904–1935

Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935) came from the Russian Empire to serve as rabbi of Jaffa in 1904–05, became the first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine in 1921 and founded the Mercaz HaRav yeshiva in Jerusalem in 1924. Unlike most Orthodox authorities of his day, he respected the secular Zionist pioneers, worked with them and, in theological terms, hailed them as playing a role in "hastening the messianic deliverance." He supported the settlement of the land and held that the Western Wall and the Temple Mount were holy sites belonging to the Jewish people. [cite:gi11_wiki_aikook] [cite:gi11a_wiki_aikook] The same rabbi also ruled that Jews must not enter the Temple Mount because the purity laws could not be observed, a ruling the Chief Rabbinate still cited in 2013 against the activists who now go up to the site. [cite:gi11_toi_rabbis2013] His legacy therefore pointed in more than one direction, and the next section returns to that ruling. [scene:gi11_temple|The Temple Mount]

The son's theology of the whole land, 1967–1982

His son, Zvi Yehuda Kook (1891–1982), ran Mercaz HaRav from 1952 until his death. [cite:gi11_wiki_zvikook] At the yeshiva's celebration of Israel's nineteenth Independence Day in May 1967, about three weeks before the war, he spoke last and, in the account of one student, Yitzhak Shilat, "with a rage and fury that I had never heard before": "Where is our Hebron? Are we forgetting it?" He repeated the verse "My land was divided." After the June war, in which Israel took Hebron, Nablus (Shechem) and the rest of the West Bank, his students treated the speech as prophecy and distributed tens of thousands of copies. [cite:gi11_ynet_hebron] [cite:gi11a_ynet_hebron] He hailed the victory as a sign of divine leadership and signed the September 1967 manifesto of the Movement for the Whole Land of Israel. [cite:gi11_wiki_zvikook] [cite:gi4_inss_einav] [scene:gi4_movement|Part Four: the Movement]

After the 1973 war he opposed every proposal to give up territory. In a 1974 lecture he warned that yielding the Golan and the West Bank would mean a war fought "over our bodies and limbs," and he stated the principle in one line: "No one, no prime minister, has the authority to renounce any part of the country." He opposed Menachem Begin's negotiations with Egypt because, he said, Sinai "formed part of Biblical Israel." [cite:gi11_wiki_zvikook] [cite:gi11a_wiki_zvikook] His students, together with religious war veterans and activists from the youth wing of the National Religious Party, founded Gush Emunim, the "Bloc of the Faithful," in early 1974; its campaign to settle the hills of Samaria, removed by the army seven times before the government compromised at Sebastia in 1975, is told in the West Bank volume. [cite:gi4_lustick_gush] [cite:wb_ej_gush] [scene:wb4_movement|The West Bank: the settler movement] He died on 9 March 1982, about six weeks before the last of Sinai was handed back to Egypt. [cite:gi11_ynet_hebron]

This is the documented core of the religious version of the whole-land claim: a named rabbi, a named yeshiva and a named movement, which held that the land was not the government's to give. It was not the position of the Israeli governments of the day, which signed the Sinai withdrawal over his objection.

From movement to party

The current reached government through parties. The Religious Zionism party, led by Smotrich, ran in 2022 on a joint list with Itamar Ben-Gvir's Otzma Yehudit that won 14 seats, and its coalition agreement with Likud of 1 December 2022 stated that "the prime minister will lead the formulation and advancement of policies within the framework of applying sovereignty in Judea and Samaria, while choosing the timing and weighing all of the State of Israel's national and international interests", and gave Smotrich civil powers there. [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026] [cite:gi5_cgtn_sov] [cite:gi6_wiki_37gov] [cite:gi5_ih_rzdeal] In a March 2025 profile, NPR quoted Smotrich: "My long-term desire is for the state of Israel to be governed according to the Torah." He objects to the word annexation, which, he said, "implies taking something that isn't yours," and says the West Bank, which he calls Judea and Samaria, "belongs to us." [cite:gi11_npr_smotrich] [cite:gi11a_npr_smotrich] His September 2025 proposal to apply sovereignty to 82 per cent of the West Bank is examined in Part Six. [cite:gi6_toi_82pct] [scene:gi6_votes|Part Six: the votes]

One statement goes beyond the West Bank. In the ARTE documentary Israel: Extremists in Power, reported in October 2024, Smotrich was quoted as saying: "It is written that the future of Jerusalem is to expand to Damascus." The outlet that reported it added a list of countries into which, it said, he wanted the state to extend; that list is the outlet's paraphrase, not his words, and this book found no clarification from Smotrich or the government. [cite:gi11_newarab_damascus] It is a documented statement by a named minister about scripture. It is not a government decision, and no plan, map or law to that effect was found.

Kahanism is a separate current, and the mainstream of the religious camp itself treated it as such. Rabbi Meir Kahane's Kach party called for the "immediate annexation of every part of Israel that is in our hands" and for every Arab in Israel to be offered "the right of residence as a non-citizen", with compensation for property for those who left. Israel's own institutions excluded it: the Central Elections Committee disqualified it in 1988 as "manifestly racist," and the cabinet outlawed it in 1994 after its supporters praised Baruch Goldstein's massacre of 29 Palestinian worshippers in Hebron. [cite:gi11_wiki_kach] [cite:gi11a_wiki_kach] Ben-Gvir was a Kach youth coordinator, was convicted of incitement to racism and support for a terrorist organisation, and kept Goldstein's portrait on his wall until about 2020; his party entered the Knesset in 2021 on a joint list arranged with Netanyahu's backing. [cite:hm_jvl_bengvir] [cite:hm_wiki_otzma] [scene:hm12_bengvir|The Hamas Architecture: Ben-Gvir]

As of October 2026, before the election of 27 October, the two parties were running separately. Religious Zionism held seven of the Knesset's 120 seats and, in polls reported between June and August, hovered around the 3.5 per cent threshold, below it in a June poll and just above it, at about five seats, in August; Smotrich rejected a renewed joint list in August, and Ben-Gvir had said in June that the two must run apart. The June poll gave Otzma Yehudit eight seats. [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026] [cite:gi11_jpost_rzp2026] [cite:gi11_toi_bg2026] Some former supporters told Reuters that Smotrich "no longer represents the values of Religious Zionism," over his handling of the ultra-Orthodox draft. [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026]

The religious counter-record

Within Religious Zionism itself, Oz VeShalom, founded about a year after Gush Emunim, and its successor Netivot Shalom argued that some in the movement had made the state an end in itself, above "justice, peace, and life itself," and in the 1990s supported the peace process including a Palestinian state. Rabbi Yehuda Amital, head of the Har Etzion yeshiva in a West Bank settlement bloc, founded Meimad in 1988; it held that putting the Land of Israel above all other values contradicts the Torah and was open to territorial compromise. On 9 May 2003, 170 religious Zionists published a manifesto urging the movement to give up control of the territories. [cite:gi11_mjl_moderate] [cite:gi11a_mjl_moderate] Meimad held a Knesset seat on Labor's list from 1999 until 2009. [cite:gi11_wiki_meimad]

The leading Sephardi authority of his generation, Rabbi Ovadia Yosef, spiritual leader of the Shas party, ruled in 1979, as peace with Egypt was debated, that "nothing is more important than the saving of life" and that it was permitted to return territory to achieve lasting peace; Shas's abstention helped the Oslo Accords pass in 1993, though he opposed the 2005 Gaza withdrawal. [cite:gi11_toi_ovadia] A letter published in 2013, undated and of unstated provenance, has him declaring land for peace void during the Second Intifada; it rests on one source and is treated here as unverified. [cite:gi11_jp_ovadia] Further from the state, the Satmar Hasidic movement, the largest Hasidic group, followed Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum in calling Zionism "a severe heresy"; after 1967 he held that the war was no miracle and forbade prayer at the Western Wall and the other captured holy sites, so as not to legitimise Israeli rule. [cite:gi11_wiki_satmar] The older argument from the Three Oaths is set out in the Road to 1948 volume. [scene:oaths_s01|Does scripture forbid a Jewish state?]
