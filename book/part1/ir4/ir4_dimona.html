Dimona: Israel's Undeclared Arsenal

Israel's nuclear programme is older than Iran's enrichment programme by more than three decades, and it is the only one in the Middle East that, by every independent estimate, has produced weapons. Israel's government has never confirmed that it has them. This section sets out what the documented record shows about that arsenal, how the United States came to accept it, and how Israel explains its position, so that the comparison with Iran running through the rest of this volume rests on both records and not one.

A reactor built with France

On 3 October 1957 France and Israel signed a revised agreement for a 24-megawatt reactor at Dimona in the Negev, with an unwritten protocol for a chemical plant to separate plutonium from spent fuel. Construction was secret and outside any IAEA inspection. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] How the deal was made at the time of the Suez war, and how Washington discovered the site in 1960, are told in earlier volumes. [scene:cw3_sevres|Sèvres] [scene:cw4_dimona_discovery|How Washington found Dimona] By January 1963 a US National Intelligence Estimate, NIE 30-63, judged that the reactor at full power could produce plutonium for one or two weapons a year. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

Inspections that were not inspections

President Kennedy pressed Ben-Gurion and then Levi Eshkol for regular American visits to Dimona, in what the editors of the declassified letters call "the toughest American-Israeli confrontation over the Israeli nuclear program." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [scene:s11b|The battle of the letters, 1963] The visits that followed were not IAEA inspections and not inspections in the treaty sense. US scientists visited Dimona seven times during the 1960s; Israel controlled the timing and agenda of each visit, and, according to later accounts summarised by the Federation of American Scientists, installed false control-room panels and bricked over lifts and corridors to hide parts of the plant. The visitors reported no weapons-related activity, such as a reprocessing plant. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] The Johnson administration ran the visits alongside the first sales of American combat aircraft to Israel. [scene:s11d|Skyhawks and Dimona, 1966] It is "widely reported," in the FAS summary, that Israel had two bombs by the June war of 1967. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] In November 1968, in the conditions attached to the sale of Phantom jets, Israel's ambassador Yitzhak Rabin repeated the formula Israel still uses: that it "will not be the first power in the Middle East to introduce nuclear weapons." [cite:ow1_jvl_phantomconds]

The 1969 understanding and the policy of opacity

The visits ended under President Nixon. At a White House meeting on 26 September 1969, according to the historian Avner Cohen's reconstruction, Nixon and Prime Minister Golda Meir reached an understanding: the United States would end its visits to Dimona and stop pressing Israel to sign the NPT, and Israel would not declare, publicise or test its arsenal. The written record of that understanding, reportedly kept by Kissinger and Rabin, has not been made public. [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] The Johnson administration's "protracted" efforts to bring Israel into the treaty had failed. [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] The Hebrew word for the resulting policy is amimut, opacity or ambiguity. Cohen describes it as a posture "credible enough to generate effects of deterrence" yet "opaque enough to maintain political distance": enough evidence to deter enemies and enough silence "to allow friends to look the other way." [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] What that silence means for American non-proliferation law, under which aid can be cut to states that traffic in enrichment technology outside safeguards, is examined in The One-Way Mirror. [scene:ow11_exceptions|Laws applied differently]

Vanunu

The fullest public evidence about Dimona came from a former technician there, Mordechai Vanunu. On 30 September 1986 agents of the Israeli government seized him in Italy and took him secretly to Israel; on 5 October the London Sunday Times published his account and photographs of the plant. [cite:ir4_amn_vanunu] His information indicated that Dimona was producing about 40 kg of plutonium a year, and analysts concluded from it that Israel had between 100 and 200 weapons; the FAS's own satellite-based analysis suggests a rate closer to 20 kg. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] [cite:ir4_aca_vanunu] He was tried behind closed doors, convicted of treason and aggravated espionage, and sentenced to 18 years. [cite:ir4_amn_vanunu] He served the full term, more than 11 years of it in solitary confinement; Amnesty International said in 1996 that holding him alone in a cell for ten years was "cruel, inhuman and degrading." [cite:ir4_aca_vanunu] [cite:ir4_amn_vanunu] On his release on 21 April 2004 he was barred from leaving Israel and his movements and foreign contacts were restricted. Israel's stated reason, given to Amnesty in 1996 and again in 2004, was that he might still reveal secrets; Vanunu said he had none left to reveal. [cite:ir4_amn_vanunu] [cite:ir4_aca_vanunu]

How many weapons

No official figure exists, so every number is an estimate. In the late 1990s the US intelligence community estimated 75 to 130 weapons; the FAS judges claims of up to 400 exaggerated and calculates that Israel's plutonium production would support at least 100 weapons but probably not many more than 200. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimated in June 2024 that Israel holds about 90 warheads: about 30 for aircraft and up to 50 for Jericho ballistic missiles, with its German-built Dolphin submarines possibly giving it a sea-based option, which Germany denies. SIPRI also reported that Israel appeared to be upgrading the plutonium facility at Dimona. [cite:ir4_ynet_sipri] [cite:hm_almon_idf] Israel neither confirms nor denies any of it. [cite:ir4_ynet_sipri]

Outside the treaty, and the resolutions

Israel has never signed the NPT, so it has made none of the commitments Iran made in 1968, and its reactor at Dimona has never been under IAEA safeguards. [cite:ir4_fas_israel] [cite:ow11_aca_opacity] [cite:ir4_unp_res8067] The UN General Assembly has repeatedly called on it to join. Resolution 80/67, adopted on 1 December 2025 by 145 votes to six with 30 abstentions, called on Israel to accede to the treaty "without further delay," renounce nuclear weapons and place its unsafeguarded facilities under full-scope IAEA safeguards; Israel and the United States were among the six states voting against. [cite:ir4_unp_res8067] On 18 September 2009 the IAEA General Conference, by 49 votes to 45, adopted its first resolution in nearly two decades on "Israeli nuclear capabilities," calling on Israel to accede to the NPT and place all its facilities under safeguards; a similar resolution failed in 2010 by 51 votes to 46 after the United States lobbied against it. [cite:ir4_iaea_gc53] [cite:ir4_aca_gc2010]

Israel's stated position is that joining the treaty must follow, not precede, a comprehensive regional peace. Shaul Chorev, director-general of the Israel Atomic Energy Commission, told the IAEA in 2010 that singling Israel out diverted attention from Iran and Syria, "the real cases of dangerous proliferation and non-compliance." [cite:ir4_aca_gc2010] In 2018 Israel rejected the General Assembly's annual resolution "in its entirety," noting that four states of the region that had joined the NPT, Iran, Iraq, Syria and Libya, had "violated their NPT obligations." [cite:ir4_unidir_eov2018] The US position in 2010, in the words of its ambassador to the IAEA, was that defeating the resolution "preserved the opportunity for progress" toward a regional conference. [cite:ir4_aca_gc2010]

The asymmetry, stated plainly

The two records differ in kind. Iran signed the treaty, accepted inspection, was found by the IAEA to have concealed activities and to have conducted coordinated weapons-relevant work until 2003, and in June 2025 was found in breach of its safeguards obligations; by the agency's count it held more highly enriched uranium than any state without weapons, and it has no weapon. [scene:ir4_programme|Iran's programme] Israel never signed, has never opened Dimona to IAEA inspection, and by independent estimates holds about 90 weapons. Israel's governments argue that the comparison is false: a state that broke a treaty it signed, tests ballistic missiles able to carry nuclear warheads and backs armed groups presents a different danger from a state that never signed and has never tested or declared. [cite:ir4_aca_gc2010] [cite:ir4_unidir_eov2018] [scene:ir9_record|Iran's own record] Most UN member states, voting year after year, have held that both must be inside the same rules. [cite:ir4_unp_res8067] Which argument is stronger is a judgement for the reader; that the asymmetry exists is not in dispute. Why Israel has acted to keep a nuclear monopoly in the region, from Iraq in 1981 to Iran in 2025, is examined in Part Thirteen. [scene:ir13_why|Why Iran?]
