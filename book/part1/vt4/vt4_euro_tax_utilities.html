Euros, Untaxed Pay and Borrowed Utilities

Day-to-day life in Vatican City depends on two sets of arrangements. Treaties with Italy and the European Union supply the currency, the water, the railway link, customs-free goods and the tax treatment of salaries. Then there is what the state runs itself: shops, a pharmacy, a petrol station, a post office, a health fund, a police force and a guard. This section takes each in turn from the documents that set it up and from reporting on how it works now.

The euro, by agreement

Vatican City State does not have its own currency. It uses the euro under a Monetary Agreement with the European Union signed in Brussels on 17 December 2009, in force from 1 January 2010, which replaced an earlier agreement of 29 December 2000. [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary] [cite:vt4_rv_monetary2009] The agreement lets the state issue its own euro coins within a ceiling. Article 3 sets that ceiling in two parts: a fixed part, €2,300,000 in 2010 and revisable each year for Italian inflation and changes in the collector market, plus a variable part equal to Italy's average coin issuance per head in the previous year multiplied by the Vatican's population. In a year when the papacy is vacant, the state may also issue a commemorative coin and collector coins, with any excess charged against the ceiling of the year before or after. [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary] The European Commission's summary of the microstates' agreements still gives the Vatican's annual ceiling as €1 million; the text of the agreement, published by the Governorate, gives the fixed part from 2010 as €2.3 million plus the variable part, and this volume follows the text. [cite:vt4_eurlex_summary] [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary]

Vatican coins are prized by collectors, and the agreement addresses that directly: under Article 6(4) at least 51 per cent of the coins issued each year must be put into circulation at face value. [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary] The microstates may not issue banknotes. [cite:vt4_eurlex_summary] The agreement also binds the state to more than coinage. Article 8 obliges it to adopt, by direct transposition or equivalent measures, the European Union acts listed in an annex on euro notes and coins, money laundering, fraud and counterfeiting of cash and non-cash means of payment, and statistical reporting, by deadlines the annex sets; the Commission updates the annex each year. A Joint Committee of Vatican and Union representatives (the Commission, Italy and the European Central Bank) meets at least once a year and decides unanimously. [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary] This clause is one reason the claim, repeated in popular writing on the Vatican, that the Vatican stands outside European anti-money-laundering rules is wrong; Part Five describes how the obligation is checked. [scene:vt5_moneyval_euro|Part Five: MONEYVAL and the euro]

No income tax, no VAT

The tax position of Vatican pay rests on Article 17 of the Lateran Treaty. Salaries and other payments made by the Holy See, by the central bodies of the Church and by bodies the Holy See manages, to dignitaries, employees and staff, are exempt in Italian territory from any tax from 1 January 1929. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The state itself levies no income tax on them. Il Post reported in May 2025 that Vatican salaries remain untaxed on the basis of the 1929 Pacts and are set broadly in line with Italian pay for comparable roles. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] The exemption is the treaty's, not a concession the state grants itself; Italy agreed to it in 1929, as part of the bargain Part Three described. [scene:vt3_what_each_got|Part Three: what each side got]

Goods are treated the same way. Article 20 of the treaty admits goods destined for Vatican City or Holy See institutions through any Italian border point or port, fully exempt from customs and local duties. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] No value-added tax applies to goods sold inside the state. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] That makes the state's shops cheaper than Italian ones, and access to them is controlled. When the Associated Press reported in November 2017 that the Vatican would stop selling cigarettes to employees at its duty-free shop and supermarket, it noted that shopping required a "commercial card" issued to employees, retirees, residents, accredited diplomats and members of religious congregations, and that purchases avoided Italy's 22 per cent VAT. The Holy See said it could not "contribute to an activity that clearly damages the health of people", giving up an estimated €10 million a year. An Ernst & Young audit in 2013 had found 41,000 cards in use against about 5,000 employees. [cite:vt4_cbs_cigarettes] The same report cited a 2015 book, Avarice, as saying that tax-free petrol was the largest source of such sales. [cite:vt4_cbs_cigarettes]

The perks have been under pressure. Il Post reported that the Annona supermarket, opened almost a century earlier for Holy See and Governorate staff, closed on 13 January 2025 for renovation and a five-year concession to an outside chain; that seniority increments were frozen from 2021 to 2023; that the staff contract had not been renewed since 2008; and that hiring was limited to one new employee for every three who retired. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] These measures followed budget cuts ordered as donations fell; Part Nine sets out the finances behind them. [scene:vt9_peters_pence|Part Nine: Peter's Pence]

Water, rail, post and power

The 1929 treaty made Italy responsible for the physical connections. Under Article 6, Italy undertook to secure an adequate supply of water owned by Vatican City, to build a railway station inside the city at a site marked on the treaty's map and let Vatican rolling stock run on Italian railways, and to link the city's telegraph, telephone, radio and postal services directly with other states, all at Italy's expense and within a year. [cite:vt4_lateran_text] The Vatican's post office is used today mainly by nunciatures sending parcels and vestments, and by visitors who buy its stamps. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers]

Electricity is the one service the Holy See has set out to supply itself. On 21 June 2024 Francis issued the motu proprio Fratello Sole, setting the goal of "the complete energy self-sufficiency of the Vatican City State" through an agrivoltaic plant, solar panels raised above working farmland, at Santa Maria di Galeria, the extraterritorial site of Vatican Radio's transmitters. [cite:vt4_vcs_fratellosole] Italy and the Holy See signed an agreement for the plant on 31 July 2025, which the Italian Senate approved for ratification on 7 January 2026; it takes effect when both sides have completed ratification. [cite:vt4_vcs_galeria] On 1 June 2026 Leo XIV created the Fratello Sole Foundation to build and run the plant, led for its first three years by the President of the Governorate and the President of APSA. As of June 2026 the plant was still a plan. [cite:vt4_vcs_fratellosole]

Health and the hospital outside the walls

There is no hospital inside Vatican City. Employees are covered by a health fund, the Fondo assistenza sanitaria (FAS), paid for by monthly contributions from salaries; its clinics stand near the Annona, and serious cases are given priority access to the Bambino Gesù and Gemelli hospitals in Rome. Il Post reported that the fund had dropped physiotherapy and raised some charges amid the cuts. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] The Vatican pharmacy, with about fifty staff, sells medicines on prescription, some not available in Italy, without VAT. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers]

The Bambino Gesù children's hospital is the Holy See's own. Founded in 1869 by Duchess Arabella Salviati and given to the Holy See in 1924, it stands on extraterritorial ground on the Janiculum, has been recognised as an Italian research hospital since 1985 and works within Rome's National Health Service. [cite:vt4_wiki_bambino] Its main site is too small. On 8 February 2024 Cardinal Pietro Parolin and Alfredo Mantovano, Under-Secretary to Italy's Council of Ministers, signed a non-binding declaration of intent to build a new hospital at the former Forlanini hospital in Rome: the Holy See would buy the site from the Lazio Region, INAIL, Italy's workplace-insurance institute, would build and lease the complex, and a further agreement would extend the immunities of Lateran Treaty Articles 15 and 16 to the new premises. [cite:vt4_press_bambino2024] On 3 December 2025 the budget committee of Italy's Chamber of Deputies approved an amendment providing the property exchange between the State and Lazio needed to free the Forlanini site for the Holy See. [cite:vt4_qs_forlanini] This volume did not find a report, as of October 2026, that the transfer or the immunities agreement had been completed.

Police and guard

Two bodies keep order. The Corps of Gendarmerie, founded by Pius VII in 1816, numbered 122 in 2022 and handles policing, border and traffic control and criminal investigation under the Governorate. [cite:dv1_wiki_gendarmerie] Its judicial police section works under the Promoter of Justice. [cite:vt4_cwr_judicial2020] The Pontifical Swiss Guard, founded in 1506, protects the Pope; it was enlarged from 110 to 135 members in 2019 and recruits unmarried Catholic Swiss men who have done Swiss military service. [cite:dv1_wiki_swissguard] The Governorate counts 120 Swiss Guards among the citizens living inside the walls. [cite:vt4_vcs_population] Outside the basilica steps, St Peter's Square is policed by Italy under Article 3 of the treaty, as section one described. [cite:vt4_lateran_text]

The picture that emerges is of a state that is sovereign in law and dependent in practice. Its currency is the European Union's, used under rules that oblige it to follow EU law on money laundering and fraud; its water, rail links and prisons were to be provided by Italy; its pay and goods are untaxed because Italy agreed in 1929 that they would be. [cite:vt4_vcs_monetary] [cite:vt4_lateran_text] What the state runs for itself is small: shops, a pharmacy, a post office, a health fund and two small forces, with the Governorate controlling the commercial activities. [cite:vt4_ilpost_workers] [cite:dv1_wiki_gendarmerie]
