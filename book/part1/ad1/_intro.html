This part sets out the American Dream as Americans first meet it, before any of it is tested. It begins with the phrase itself: what the historian James Truslow Adams meant when he named it in 1931, which was more than cars and high wages, and the older ideas he drew on, from the Declaration of Independence to Lincoln's ladder of free labour and Horatio Alger's lucky clerk. It then shows how the Dream is taught today, quoting the national economics standards, a state's required course, a widely used textbook, the history curriculum, the citizenship test and the songs of Saturday-morning television, and how far Americans still believe it. Its last section turns to the sellers, presidents and candidates of both parties, builders and lenders, follows one national campaign to sell the Dream as a house from the podium to the outcome, and states the question the rest of the book sets out to answer.
