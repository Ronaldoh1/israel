Who Recognizes a Nation?

American recognition of Israel is sometimes remembered as an act of Congress. It was not. The decision belonged to one office, and on May 14, 1948 it was exercised by one man, by press release, in the space of eleven minutes.

The constitutional basis is a short clause. Article II, Section 3 provides that the President "shall receive Ambassadors and other public Ministers." [cite:const_art2s3] In Zivotofsky v. Kerry (2015), the Supreme Court held that it gives the President the exclusive power to recognize foreign sovereigns. [cite:scotus_zivotofsky] Read through that holding, the power Truman used in 1948 was the President's alone.

Truman used that power on the day of the declaration. Eleven minutes after Israel's proclamation, he announced de facto recognition in a short press release. [cite:truman_recognition] It was not yet full legal recognition. De jure recognition followed on January 31, 1949. In the interval, as an earlier section noted, the Soviet Union became the first state to extend full legal recognition, on 17 May 1948 ([scene:so_s01|Moscow's recognition]).

Congress had no vote on the question. Its role was indirect: resolutions of support, appropriations and the Senate's confirmation of ambassadors, including the first American ambassador to the new state. These were real powers, but none of them decided whether the United States would recognize Israel. That choice was made in the White House.

It was also made against the written advice of the government's own senior officials. The Secretary of State, the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary and the CIA had all advised against it ([scene:ore_s01|the CIA's warning]). The warnings, from the State Department, the Pentagon and the newly created intelligence agency, are traced earlier in this stage. Truman overruled them.

The constitutional point gives the story of 1948 its shape. Because recognition rested with the President alone, the arguments of the preceding months, over partition, over trusteeship and over oil and strategy, came down in the end to a single decision-maker. The advisers could warn, Congress could pass resolutions and the advocates could press their case, but only the President could decide.
