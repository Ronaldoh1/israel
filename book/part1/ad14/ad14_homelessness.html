Homelessness: The Count, the Court and the Fight Over Housing First

A home is the first asset of the taught Dream and the floor beneath every other part of it: an address to put on a job application, a place for children to sleep before school, somewhere to keep medicine and papers. Homelessness is the point at which that floor gives way. This section gives the latest national count and what changed in it, the evidence on why homelessness is higher in some places than others, the 2024 Supreme Court ruling that allowed cities to punish sleeping outside, the 2025 executive order and funding changes that turned federal policy away from "Housing First", and what the research shows about that approach and its critics, with the status of each as of October 2026.

The count and what changed in 2025

The Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) measures homelessness with a point-in-time count: on one night in January, local agencies count people in shelters and transitional housing and people sleeping outside or in places not meant for living. The count was 647,258 in 2007, fell to 549,928 in 2016, rose to 653,104 in 2023 and jumped to a record 771,480 in 2024, of whom 274,224 were unsheltered. [cite:us15_crs_homeless] The January 2025 count, published on 29 May 2026, was 745,652, a fall of 3 per cent, with 266,320 unsheltered. HUD noted that the total was still 27 per cent above 2013, unsheltered homelessness 36 per cent higher, and chronic (long-term) homelessness 81 per cent higher. [cite:ad14_hud_pit2025] [cite:us15_hud_pit2025] A widely repeated claim says homelessness keeps setting records above 770,000 a year; on the latest count it fell.

The 2025 release also changed how the numbers were framed. HUD attributed the decline to "decreases in Sanctuary Cities", its term for cities that limit cooperation with immigration enforcement, and added a broader figure, 1,456,923, that combines the homeless count with people living in housing paid for by homelessness programmes. That second figure is not a count of homeless people; formerly homeless people in permanent housing are by definition housed, and the point-in-time count has always excluded them. [cite:ad14_hud_pit2025] Both framings are HUD's own and support its stated case, set out below, that spending on permanent housing has not paid off.

Why some places have more

Public debate often explains homelessness through addiction and mental illness. Those conditions are common among people living on the street, but they are also common in places with little homelessness. Gregg Colburn, a housing researcher at the University of Washington, and Clayton Page Aldern, a data scientist, tested the usual explanations against the wide differences between American cities in their 2022 book Homelessness Is a Housing Problem. Mental illness, drug use, poverty, weather and the generosity of local benefits did not explain why some regions have far higher rates; the cost and availability of rental housing did. [cite:ad14_ucp_colburn] That points back to the rent and supply record in Part Six. [scene:ad6_price_and_rent|Part Six: price and rent] [scene:ad6_eviction_and_traps|Part Six: eviction]

A widely shared comparison sets the roughly 15 million vacant homes in the country against the number of homeless people and concludes that there is no shortage, only a refusal to house people. The vacancy count includes homes listed for rent or for sale, homes between tenants and seasonal homes; some vacancy is what allows people to move at all, and the rental vacancy rate was 7.3 per cent in the second quarter of 2026. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] The comparison does not show that housing is withheld by design, and the record does not support that claim.

The court: punishing sleeping outside

Grants Pass, a small city in Oregon, enforced its ban on camping on public property with civil fines, then with bans from public spaces and short jail terms for repeat violators. Lower courts, relying on an earlier Ninth Circuit decision, Martin v. Boise, held that punishing homeless people in this way was cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. On 28 June 2024 the Supreme Court reversed, 6–3, in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson. Writing for the majority, Justice Neil Gorsuch held that the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment does not apply to general laws against camping on public property, leaving the policy to elected officials: "Homelessness is complex. Its causes are many." Justice Sonia Sotomayor, for the three dissenters, called the enforcement "unconscionable and unconstitutional". [cite:ad14_ncc_grantspass] The ruling did not require any city to ban camping; it removed a constitutional barrier for those that chose to.

The 2025 policy turn

Housing First is the approach of placing homeless people into permanent housing without first requiring sobriety or treatment, and offering services once they are housed. On 24 July 2025 President Trump signed an executive order, "Ending Crime and Disorder on America's Streets", that ended federal support for Housing First, directed HUD to make treatment a condition of its programmes, promoted civil commitment, the court-ordered treatment of people with severe mental illness, barred federal addiction grants from funding harm-reduction programmes such as syringe distribution, and told agencies to favour places that enforce bans on camping, loitering and open drug use. [cite:ad14_hma_eo] HUD Secretary Scott Turner stated that "the status quo of 'housing first' has failed to meaningfully reduce homelessness", pointing to a 151 per cent rise in federally funded beds and a 111 per cent rise in Continuum of Care spending since 2013. [cite:ad14_hud_pit2025]

The money flows mostly through HUD's Continuum of Care programme, about $4 billion a year in grants to local networks of providers. In November 2025 HUD capped permanent supportive housing, which made up about 87 per cent of the programme's spending, at 30 per cent of funds, and limited the share of existing grants protected for renewal to 30 per cent, down from about 90 per cent. Twenty-one state attorneys general and a group of counties and nonprofits sued; HUD withdrew the notice on 8 December 2025, and Congress, in the appropriations law of 3 February 2026, ordered expiring grants renewed in the meantime. [cite:ad14_naco_coc] HUD's next notice, issued on 1 June 2026, protected 60 per cent of renewals, set aside $1.3 billion of $4.04 billion for new transitional housing and services-only projects, and awarded points for requiring participation in treatment. A federal district court vacated it on 7 August 2026 under the Administrative Procedure Act; on 16 September 2026 the First Circuit Court of Appeals stayed that ruling pending appeal, and HUD reinstated the notice with a 30 September deadline. [cite:ad14_crs_coc2026] [cite:ad14_naco_vacate] Status as of October 2026: in the latest reports, from late September, the competition was proceeding under the stay and the appeal was pending.

What the evidence shows

Housing First has been tested in randomised trials, in which people are assigned by lottery to the programme or to the usual services. In Canada's At Home/Chez Soi trial in five cities, participants were stably housed for 73 per cent of a two-year period, against 32 per cent for the comparison group. In the HUD-VASH programme for veterans, Housing First participants were housed in 35 days rather than 223. Effects on health were unclear, substance use was about the same as under treatment-first programmes, and most of the cost was offset by savings on shelters, jails and hospitals, although that evidence is limited. [cite:ad14_jpal_hf] The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine reached a similar verdict in 2018: permanent supportive housing "increases an individual's ability to remain housed", but the committee "found no substantial evidence" that it improves health outcomes, except limited data for people with HIV/AIDS. [cite:ad14_nasem_psh]

The critics' strongest point is HUD's: national homelessness rose while spending on permanent housing grew. The strongest reply is that a programme can house the people it serves without lowering a regional count driven by rents, which is what the trials and the Colburn–Aldern findings together suggest. [cite:ad14_jpal_hf] [cite:ad14_ucp_colburn] The record supports neither the claim that Housing First has failed at what it measures, keeping people housed, nor the claim that it can end homelessness by itself while housing costs rise.

Where it works as promised

The safety net has caught people before. The national count fell by about 15 per cent between 2007 and 2016, from 647,258 to 549,928, and fell again in 2025. [cite:us15_crs_homeless] [cite:ad14_hud_pit2025] For the individuals it reaches, Housing First keeps people in a home more than twice as much of the time as the alternatives tested against it, and veterans in HUD's targeted programme were housed in about a month instead of more than seven. [cite:ad14_jpal_hf] What the record does not show is a national policy that has kept the floor of the Dream in place while rents climbed; that question returns in Part Fifteen, which asks who writes the rules.
