The Wall and August 1929: Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed

A common account of Mandate Palestine dates organized violence between Arabs and Jews to the Arab Revolt that began in 1936. The record puts a turning point seven years earlier. In the last week of August 1929, violence that began in Jerusalem over the Western Wall, which Muslims call al-Buraq, spread to Hebron and Safed. It left a precise death toll, ended one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the country for nearly four decades, led to a British commission of inquiry, and, in the judgment of historians, made the political reputation of the Mufti of Jerusalem, Hajj Amin al-Husseini [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929] [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini].

The dispute had a specific starting point. In September 1928, on Yom Kippur, Jewish worshippers set up a portable screen at the Wall to separate men from women, and its removal opened the conflict [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots]. The ground mattered because of who owned it. The Wall belonged to Muslim religious authorities, and Jews prayed there. Through 1928 the tension built on both sides. A documented Arab campaign asserted Muslim rights over the site, and documented Jewish activity pressed for prayer arrangements there; each side treated the dispute as central to its larger claim [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. Hajj Amin al-Husseini is documented as leading the 1928 campaign asserting Muslim rights over the Wall [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. The record shows real escalation from both directions in the year before the riots. It does not settle which particular acts bear more of the blame.

The final days before the outbreak are dated precisely. On 15 August 1929 a few hundred Jewish youths, Betar members among them, marched to the Wall and raised a flag. On 16 August a Muslim demonstration went to the Wall, beat Jews who were there and burned prayer notes [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom] [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_buraq]. In the days that followed, according to the historians cited here, rumor and organized propaganda inflamed the city, and the Mufti's activists distributed inflammatory material [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929] [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini].

The outbreak came on 23 August in Jerusalem. The Shaw Commission, which investigated it the following year, found that it began there as an attack by Arabs on Jews, that it "was not premeditated", and that it was not a revolt against British authority [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d]. The police force was small. Only about 150 British police and 1,500 local police served the whole of Palestine [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom]. The British command at the time is part of the story of the High Commissioners in [scene:s07a2|the High Commissioners].

The worst of the killing was in Hebron. There, on 24 August 1929, it began at about 9 a.m. Eight American students of the Slobodka Yeshiva were among the dead [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom]. Sixty-seven Jews were killed in the city [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The sources differ on how the count fell across the two days. Human Rights Watch gives 67 Jews killed and dates the massacre to 23 August; the EBSCO reference account records one yeshiva student killed on 23 August and 64 killed at the Heichal residence on 24 August [cite:rs_rd3_hrw_hebron] [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots]. Those two EBSCO figures add to 65, not 67, and the sources used here do not reconcile the difference.

Hebron also produced a documented account of rescue. According to Human Rights Watch, the Zionist Archives list 435 Hebron Jews who found refuge in 28 Arab homes. Norman Bentwich wrote that many Jews were saved by their Arab neighbours [cite:rs_rd3_hrw_hebron] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom]. The figure of 435 rests on that one archival list as reported by Human Rights Watch; it is not, in the sources here, independently counted. It belongs in the record beside the death toll.

After the killing the British authorities evacuated Hebron's surviving Jews. A community present in the city for centuries was removed within days, and organized Jewish life there did not resume until 1968, thirty-nine years later [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. Unlike many episodes of violence in these years, this one produced an immediate, total and lasting change. The circumstances of the 1968 return, and what came after it, belong to a much later part of this book.

The violence reached Safed on 29 August 1929. Between 18 and 20 Jews were killed there and about 80 wounded, and some 200 Jewish homes were burned [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom] [cite:rs_rd3_jewishcurrents_safed].

By the end of the week the dead numbered 133 Jews and 116 Arabs, according to the EBSCO reference account and the Jewish Virtual Library. Most of the Arab deaths were caused by British police and military fire during the suppression [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots] [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom]. The Jewish toll is itself disputed. The Jerusalem Story lexicon of the Institute for Palestine Studies gives 113 Jews and 116 Arabs, and adds that the Mandate probably under-reported Palestinian casualties [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_buraq]. The two accounts agree on the Arab figure and differ by twenty on the Jewish one; neither source, as used here, explains the gap.

The reckoning took several forms, and Britain's own report to the League of Nations for 1929 records the first of them. Collective punishments were imposed on towns and villages that took part in attacks on Jews at Hebron, Safad, Motza, Artuf and Beer Tuvia, with fines totalling £P17,840. The courts moved quickly. By the end of December 1929, 791 Arabs and 66 Jews had been tried summarily; 17 Arabs and 1 Jew had received capital sentences, and 7 death sentences had been confirmed on appeal [cite:rs_rd3_lon_report_1929]. These were measures of the Mandate administration, and Britain reported them to the League as the mandatory power answerable to it.

On 17 June 1930 three Arab men were hanged in Acre for their part in the riots. Twenty-five other death sentences, two of them on Jews, were commuted. This account rests on a single general reference, and the identities of the three men are not established in the research for this book, so they are not named here. Palestinians remember the day as "Red Tuesday" [cite:rs_rd3_wiki_1930].

The Shaw Commission reported in March 1930. On the question that has divided accounts ever since, the Mufti's role, it was precise. It found no evidence that Hajj Amin al-Husseini had incited the outbreak, but held that he "must accept a share in the responsibility". It found premeditation not proved against the Arab Executive [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d]. On causes, Shaw distinguished two levels. The fundamental cause was Arab hostility rooted in disappointed political aspirations and fear for their economic future. The immediate causes included the incidents at the Wall from September 1928 to 16 August 1929, the Jewish demonstration of 15 August, an inflammatory press, and the enlargement of the Jewish Agency in 1929 [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d], whose making is told in [scene:us_s03|the enlarged Jewish Agency]. What London did with Shaw's findings, and with the land inquiry that followed, is the subject of [scene:pf_s01|the Passfield White Paper and its reversal].

The ownership question at the Wall itself went to an international body. A commission of three members, none of them British, sat in Jerusalem from 19 June to 19 July 1930 under the approval of the League Council: Eliel Löfgren of Sweden, Charles Barde of Geneva and J. van Kempen of the Netherlands. It reported in December 1930 [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_wall_1930]. It found that the Wall and the pavement before it are Muslim property, and that the Wall is "sacred to both Parties". Jews were to have free access for their devotions, but could bring only the appurtenances permitted under the Turkish regime, and screens separating men and women were barred [cite:rs_rd3_unispal_wall_1930]. The commission thus ruled on the very object, a screen, that had opened the dispute in September 1928. It confirmed Muslim ownership and Jewish access together, and held Jewish worship there to the arrangements of the Ottoman years.

The three main readings of August 1929 rest on different evidence, and each has a strong form. In the Palestinian account, preserved in the Jerusalem Story lexicon under the name "al-Buraq Uprising", the trigger was a provocation: Betar-led demonstrators raised the Zionist flag at a site under Muslim authority, and the response grew from a real fear that the holy places would be taken over [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_buraq]. The march of 15 August appears in each of the accounts cited here, and Shaw named it among the immediate causes, so this reading draws support from the inquiry's findings as well as from Palestinian memory. In the Jewish account, the cause was a Muslim campaign over the Wall led by the Mufti, and incitement that ended in massacres of unarmed communities [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_prayer_pogrom] [cite:rs_rd3_jewishcurrents_safed]. Its evidence is equally concrete: the Mufti's documented 1928 campaign, the inflammatory material his activists distributed, and the fact that the dead of Hebron, eight American yeshiva students among them, were killed in another town, a day after the outbreak in Jerusalem. The British inquiry stood between the two. It found no premeditation, a share of responsibility on the Mufti, and the 15 August demonstration among the immediate causes, while naming an Arab attack on Jews as the form the outbreak took [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d].

Behind the question of the Mufti's role in the riots lies a larger one about what the riots did for him. Historians cited in this research, notably Oren Kessler in a 2024 account, describe 1929 as the event that made al-Husseini's political reputation among Palestinians, the platform on which his leadership of the 1936–39 revolt was later built [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. That reading places the start of his rise seven years before the revolt. It does not make 1936 less important to his career. It says what came before it and made it possible.

Several things the record does not settle. The first is the Mufti's direct part. That his activists distributed inflammatory material before the riots is documented. Whether he organized the violence itself, or capitalized on tensions that moved beyond his control, is a question historians still debate; Shaw's "share in the responsibility" was a judgment, not a finding of organization [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini] [cite:rs_rd3_shaw_palquest_d]. The second is the count. The Jewish dead of the week are given as 133 or 113, the Hebron dead as 67 or, by one day-by-day account, 65, and the Arab dead, most of them killed by the forces of order, may have been under-recorded [cite:rs_rd3_jerusalemstory_buraq] [cite:rs_rd3_hrw_hebron] [cite:rs_rd3_ebsco_wwriots]. The third is the rescue: the 435 sheltered in Hebron rest on one archival list as reported by one organization. What is not in doubt is the sequence, from a screen at the Wall in September 1928 to a commission's ruling on screens in December 1930, and the dead and displaced in between.
