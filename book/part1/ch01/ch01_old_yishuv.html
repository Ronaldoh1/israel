Before Zionism: The Old Yishuv and the Count of Jerusalem

Political Zionism did not bring the first Jews to the land. When the movement's first settlers arrived from 1881, they found a Jewish community already there, long settled and organized on entirely different lines. Historians call it the Old Yishuv, to distinguish it from the New Yishuv that began with the First Aliyah, the wave of immigration from 1881 onward.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv] It grew in the eighteenth century mainly through Orthodox groups, among them Hasidim and the disciples of the Vilna Gaon.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv]

Its life centered on four cities that Jewish tradition held holy: Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias. In the nineteenth century most of its members lived in Jerusalem.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv] [cite:rs_ch01_posen_ottoman] The previous section described the Ottoman province around them, a land most of whose people were Arabic-speaking Muslims and Christians; the Jewish communities of these four cities were long established within it, as Ottoman subjects or as foreign nationals living under Ottoman rule.

The community lived largely on halukka, a Hebrew word meaning "distribution." Jews abroad sent donations, and the money was handed out in the land by kollels, bodies organized by country of origin or by Hasidic affiliation. Over time the community became almost completely dependent on these funds.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv]

Politically, the community had a recognized place inside the Ottoman order. The Sephardi leadership, headed by a chief rabbi, the Hakham Bashi, a title later rendered Rishon LeZion, was officially recognized by the Ottoman authorities and represented the Jews before them. The arrangement resembled the millet order described in the previous section, under which recognized religious communities were led by their own heads. The Ashkenazi kollels, which had grown up separately, united at the end of the nineteenth century under a General Committee of Knesset Israel.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv]

How many people was this? The figures come with ranges, and the ranges matter. By the National Library of Israel's count, the Jewish population of the land was about 7,000 in 1800 and about 27,000 in 1880. Most researchers put the 1880 figure somewhat lower, at 23,000 to 26,000, and agree that about half of them lived in Jerusalem.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv] [cite:rs_ch01_posen_ottoman] The community roughly tripled or quadrupled between 1800 and 1880, before political Zionism existed.

The growth that followed was faster, and it changed the community's character. Roughly 60,000 Jews arrived between 1881 and 1914. The First World War then reversed much of it. By the summer of 1918, after wartime expulsions, hunger and disease, 56,000 Jews remained in the land, little more than half the number of 1914.[cite:rs_ch01_posen_ottoman]

A second set of numbers looks very different, and the reason is instructive. The historian Justin McCarthy reconstructed Palestine's population from the Ottoman registers. His counts of Ottoman-citizen Jews are about 13,000 of 369,000 people in 1860, 13,942 of 440,850 in 1878, 17,991 of 516,131 in 1890, and 38,754 of 722,143 in 1914.[cite:rs_ch01_mccarthy_pop] Set beside the figure of 23,000 to 27,000 for 1880, his count of 13,942 for 1878 is strikingly low. The explanation lies in what the registers counted. They recorded Ottoman citizens, and they did not include foreign nationals, among whom were many Jewish immigrants.[cite:rs_ch01_mccarthy_pop] Jews who lived in the land as foreign nationals do not appear in them. Neither set of figures is simply wrong. They answer different questions: how many Jews lived in the land, and how many Jews the empire counted as its own citizens.

The same caution applies to the total population. McCarthy's register totals, 516,131 in 1890 and 722,143 in 1914, are counts of Ottoman citizens; the approximate figure of 600,000 for the late nineteenth century given in the previous section comes from a different source. Comparing them requires knowing who each one left out.

In the second half of the nineteenth century Jerusalem's Jews began to build beyond the Old City. The first neighborhoods outside the city walls are associated with Moses Montefiore, Yoel Moshe Salomon and Yehoshua Yellin.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv]

The Old Yishuv did not last as the land's leading Jewish community. The decline of the Ottoman Empire and the successive Zionist immigration waves cost it that place.[cite:rs_ch01_nli_oldyishuv] The story of the newcomers begins with [scene:ideo_s01|the First Aliyah].

The most argued-over question about this community concerns Jerusalem itself. Did Jews form the majority of the city before Zionism? The question became a matter of state in 1971. On 22 February of that year, Jordan and the United Arab Republic wrote to the UN Secretary-General in a letter circulated as document S/10130. On 1 March 1971, Israel's ambassador to the United Nations, Yosef Tekoah, replied in a letter to the Secretary-General, document S/10138.[cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] The Israeli letter set out a series of figures for the city of Jerusalem: 28,000 Jews among 43,000 people in 1887, citing Baedeker's guide of 1894; 40,000 of 60,000 in 1905, citing the thirteenth edition of the Encyclopaedia Britannica; 33,971 of 62,578 at the British census of 1922; 51,222 of 90,503 in 1931; and 97,000 of 157,080 in 1944, citing the Survey of Palestine.[cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] Its argument was that Jews had been the majority of the city itself in 1922, 1931 and 1944 and for decades before the Mandate, and that the Arab figures in S/10130 described the whole Jerusalem sub-district, a wider area in which Arabs were the majority, rather than the city.[cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] The figures that Jordan and the United Arab Republic used are not reproduced in the sources available for this book, so their side of the exchange is known here only through Israel's description of it.

The same Israeli letter states that in 1948 Jordan destroyed the Jewish Quarter of the Old City, including 39 synagogues and places of learning, expelled its Jews, and barred Jewish access to the holy places from 1948 to 1967.[cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] These are statements in an official Israeli document addressed to the United Nations, made in the course of a diplomatic dispute, and they are reported here as such.

A second compilation broadly supports the trend. Figures gathered by the Jewish Virtual Library, drawing on Oesterreicher and Sinai (1974), give Jerusalem 7,120 Jews of 15,510 people in 1844, 12,000 of 25,030 in 1876, and 28,112 of 45,420 in 1896, and 33,971 Jews at the British census of 1922.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_jerpop] By these figures Jews were a little under half of the city's people in 1844 and 1876 and a majority by 1896.

The two compilations agree on the Jewish figure for 1922, 33,971, but not on the city's total. Israel's letter gives the 1922 census total as 62,578; the Jewish Virtual Library table gives 52,081.[cite:rs_ch01_un_s10138] [cite:rs_ch01_jvl_jerpop] The sources do not explain the difference, and it changes the Jewish share of the city in that year considerably. Which total is right cannot be settled from these documents alone.

Writers today draw opposite conclusions from these numbers. Making the case for a long Jewish claim to the city, the columnist Stephen Flatow, writing in the Jerusalem Post in 2023, cited visitors' calculations showing a Jewish plurality from the mid-1800s and the 1922 census showing Jews as the majority of the city.[cite:rs_ch01_jpost_flatow] The strongest form of this case rests on the series of figures Israel put before the United Nations, which a separate compilation broadly matches: by both, Jews were the city's largest group by the late nineteenth century.

The opposing caution rests on the quality of the evidence and on what it is taken to mean. The nineteenth-century figures come from visitors' estimates and secondary compilations, not from a systematic census, and many Jewish residents were foreign subjects outside the Ottoman registers, so the Ottoman state's own counts cannot confirm them.[cite:rs_ch01_mccarthy_pop] The analyst Seth Frantzman, writing in 2016, stressed who those Jews were: the Jews of nineteenth-century Jerusalem were largely Sephardi and religious, and the city was a Middle Eastern one.[cite:rs_ch01_frantzman_1888] His point bears less on the count than on what is read into it.

Both readings can be held against the same table. A Jewish plurality, and later a majority, in the city of Jerusalem is supported by several compilations, though the earliest of them rest on estimates. An Arab majority in the land as a whole is supported by every count here; an Arab majority in the wider sub-district is stated in Israel's own letter, though the figures behind it are not in these sources. Which unit, city or sub-district, should decide a political claim is not a question the numbers can answer.

Under British rule the Jewish share of the whole country rose quickly from a low base. Britain's census of 1922 counted 757,182 people in Palestine, of whom about 11% were Jews. The census of 1931 put the Jewish share at about 17%, and the estimate of the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine for late 1946, a population of about 1.85 million, put it at about 33%. The growth that the Old Yishuv's 7,000 of 1800 began, and the First Aliyah accelerated, continued at a far greater pace under the Mandate.

Several things the record here does not settle. The nineteenth-century figures are estimates, and their range, 23,000 to 27,000 for 1880, is the honest form of the answer. How many Jews lived in the land as foreign nationals, outside the Ottoman registers, at any given date is not established by these sources. The difference between the two 1922 totals for Jerusalem is unresolved. And the Arab side of the 1971 exchange at the United Nations is known here only from Israel's reply. What the record does establish is that a Jewish community lived in the land, chiefly in its four holy cities, before Zionism, that it was sustained from abroad and represented to the Ottomans through its Sephardi chief rabbi, and that by the figures available it was the largest group in the city of Jerusalem by the late nineteenth century while remaining a small minority in the land as a whole.
