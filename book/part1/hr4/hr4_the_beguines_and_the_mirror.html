The Beguines and the Mirror

The Beguines were women in the cities of northern Europe who, from the Middle Ages, "led lives of religious devotion without joining an approved religious order". [cite:hr_brit_beguines] They usually lived together in communities called beguinages and supported themselves, often by nursing or by cloth- or lace-making; they promised chastity only while they stayed, and could leave to marry. [cite:hr_brit_beguines] Their independence, their links with Dominican and Franciscan friars and the intense mysticism some of them practised led many to suspect them of heresy. [cite:hr_brit_beguines]

Marguerite Porete was a Beguine who wrote a book. The Mirror of Simple Souls describes the "Annihilated Soul", which has "given up everything except God through Love" and becomes united with God. [cite:hr_wiki_porete] Some time between 1296 and 1306 the book was judged heretical, and the bishop Gui de Collemedio ordered it "publicly burned in her presence at Valenciennes". She kept circulating it. [cite:hr_wiki_porete]

She was handed to the Dominican inquisitor William of Paris. She refused to speak to any inquisitor, refused to recant or to withdraw her book, and refused to take the required oath, so the tribunal condemned her as a relapsed heretic. She was "burnt alive on 1 June 1310 in Paris", at the Place de Grève. [cite:hr_wiki_porete] The next year the Council of Vienne drew up decrees ordering the dissolution of beguine communities, and its decree Ad nostrum, condemning a movement called the Brethren of the Free Spirit, cited extracts from her book. [cite:hr_brit_beguines] [cite:hr_wiki_porete]

The book outlived the fire. The Mirror circulated anonymously and was translated into Latin, Italian and Middle English, read by people who did not know that its author had been burned for it. Her authorship was recognised again only in 1946, when the scholar Romana Guarnieri identified Latin manuscripts of the text in the Vatican. [cite:hr_wiki_porete] The Church that burned the author preserved, without knowing it, copies of the book.

Her case adds a dimension to this shelf's pattern. The other heretics in this book belonged to movements with leaders and followers; Porete was a single laywoman with a book, and the charges against her were about what she had written and her refusal to unsay it. [cite:hr_wiki_porete] The Beguines as a whole were suspect for the same reason: they lived religious lives under no approved rule, which meant under no office that answered for them. [cite:hr_brit_beguines] Official policy toward them varied until the fifteenth century, when a consistent toleration took hold, and some communities still exist, mainly in Belgium. [cite:hr_brit_beguines]
