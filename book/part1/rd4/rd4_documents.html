The Perpetrators' Own Paper

The estimates set out in [scene:hol_s05|How the Number Is Known] were reached by historians after the war. The evidence beneath them is older. Much of it was written by the people who carried out the killing, in the routine forms of a modern state: situation reports, running tallies, minutes, telegrams, statistical summaries, a bound photograph album and the notes for a speech. These records were not composed for posterity. They were written to inform superiors, to coordinate ministries and to account for work done. They were captured by the Allies in 1945, entered into evidence at Nuremberg, and in some cases surfaced only decades later. This section takes the principal documents one at a time: what each is, who wrote it, when, what it says in its own figures and words, where the original is held, and how it came to light. Where the sources consulted here disagree on a date, a figure or a discovery, both versions are given.

The documents did not work alone. The killing depended on infrastructure that left its own paper trail. According to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, the European rail network played a crucial role in the Final Solution: the Transport Ministry organized the train schedules, and millions of people were transported by rail to killing centers between autumn 1941 and autumn 1944 [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_railways]. The reports below record the far end of that process.

The Einsatzgruppen reports, 1941–1942. The earliest large body of evidence comes from the Einsatzgruppen. Their reports survive in a series called the Operational Situation Reports USSR, the Ereignismeldungen. A set of 195 of them, covering June 1941 to 1 May 1942, was circulated across branches of the German government, the Wehrmacht among them [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_ezg_auth]. On 3 September 1945 the 6889th Berlin Document Group found the set on the fourth floor of Gestapo headquarters in Berlin [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_ezg_auth].

The reports count. At the Einsatzgruppen trial, the prosecution read from Operational Situation Report No. 128, which stated that "approximately 80,000 persons were liquidated until now by the Kommandos," and from Report No. 106, which stated that approximately 35,000 Jews of Kiev were killed on 29–30 September 1941. Another report gave the figure for Sonderkommando 4a at Kiev as 33,771 [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_nmt9_transcript]. The two Kiev numbers come from different reports, and the trial record preserves both.

The Jäger Report is a local tally of the same kind. It was signed at Kovno on 1 December 1941 by SS-Standartenführer Karl Jäger, commander of Einsatzkommando 3. It states that there are no more Jews in Lithuania apart from working Jews and their families. Yad Vashem holds it under the reference YVA O-18/245 [cite:rs_rd4_yv_jaeger]. According to the English translation published by the University of South Florida's Florida Center for Instructional Technology, and to Holocaust Denial on Trial, the report's running tally ends with the line "Total 137,346," covering 21 June to 1 December 1941 across 113 operations in 71 locations, and the original is held in the Central Lithuanian Archives in Vilnius [cite:rs_rd4_fcit_jaeger] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_ezg_auth].

These reports were the basis of the prosecution in the ninth of the Subsequent Nuremberg Proceedings, the Einsatzgruppen Case. Twenty-four defendants were indicted, led by Otto Ohlendorf; 22 were tried. The trial ran from 29 September 1947 to 12 February 1948, and the prosecutor, Benjamin Ferencz, relied on the wartime SS reports rather than on witnesses [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_case9] [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_nmt9_intro]. On 10 April 1948 the tribunal sentenced 14 defendants to death, 2 to life imprisonment and 5 to terms of 10 to 20 years. Only four of the death sentences were carried out, those of Ohlendorf, Naumann, Blobel and Braune, on 7 June 1951 [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_case9] [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_nmt9_intro]. The sources give two figures for the number of victims attributed to the defendants. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives the indictment's figure as between 723,661 and one million [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_case9]. Harvard Law School's Nuremberg Trials Project gives the prosecution's estimate as more than one million [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_nmt9_intro].

The Wannsee Protocol, January 1942. The meeting of 20 January 1942 is told in [scene:hol_s04|Wannsee]: fifteen officials, about ninety minutes, Reinhard Heydrich in the chair, nearly six months after Hermann Göring's documented order of 31 July 1941 [cite:yadvashem_wannsee_final_solution] [cite:ushmm_wannsee_conference]. What matters here is the document itself. The minutes were compiled and heavily edited by Adolf Eichmann from notes taken at the meeting; they are not a word-for-word transcript [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_wannsee_protocol]. Thirty copies were made. The stamp on the surviving copy reads "30 copies" and "16th copy," and copy no. 16 belonged to Undersecretary Martin Luther of the German Foreign Office [cite:rs_rd4_wannsee_text_ee] [cite:rs_rd4_jhi_wannsee] [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_wannsee_protocol]. The protocol's country table totals "over 11,000,000" Jews. Among its entries it lists the General Government at 2,284,000, Bialystok at 400,000, the USSR at 5,000,000 and England at 330,000, and it lists Estonia as "free of Jews" [cite:rs_rd4_wannsee_text_ee]. As Heydrich presented it, the figure took in Jewish populations in countries not yet under Axis control [cite:ushmm_wannsee_conference]. The surviving protocol, item R 100857, is reproduced courtesy of the Political Archive of the German Foreign Office, the Politisches Archiv des Auswärtigen Amts [cite:rs_rd4_cts_wannsee].

Only one of the thirty copies has been found, according to the House of the Wannsee Conference [cite:rs_rd4_ghwk_files_seized]. How and when it was found is told three ways. The House of the Wannsee Conference states that American soldiers discovered it in a file together with 300 other pieces of evidence, among Foreign Office files seized from external storage; it illustrates the seizure with a photograph of United States troops at Schloss Meisdorf on 19 April 1945 [cite:rs_rd4_ghwk_files_seized]. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum gives a later and more specific account: the copy came to light among the Foreign Office files in late 1946, when Kenneth Duke, a member of the American microfilming staff, found it and alerted Dr. Robert Kempner in March 1947 [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_wannsee_protocol]. The Jewish Historical Institute in Warsaw credits Kempner, the prosecutor, with finding it in 1947 [cite:rs_rd4_jhi_wannsee]. The accounts name different finders, and the sources consulted here do not reconcile them. The Holocaust Memorial Museum states that the protocol was first submitted as evidence in 1947, in the Ministries Trial, Case 11 of the Subsequent Nuremberg Proceedings [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_wannsee_protocol] [cite:rs_rd4_ghwk_files_seized]. It was cited again at Eichmann's trial in Jerusalem in 1961 [cite:ushmm_wannsee_protocol].

The Höfle telegram, January 1943. The second document is a radio message. It was sent by SS-Sturmbannführer Höfle of the staff of the SS and police leader in Lublin, the adjutant to Globocnik, and it was intercepted on 11 January 1943. It reported arrivals at four camps as of 31 December 1942, under single-letter codes: L (Majdanek) 24,733; B (Belzec) 434,508; S (Sobibor) 101,370; and T (Treblinka) 713,555. The total was 1,274,166 [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hoefle_blog] [cite:rs_rd4_sobibor_hoefle] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers]. The decrypt text held by The National Archives in Britain shows the Treblinka figure as 71,355. The archive notes that this figure is missing a digit; with 713,555, the four camp figures sum to the stated total of 1,274,166 [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hoefle_blog] [cite:rs_rd4_sobibor_hoefle]. According to Sobibor.org and Holocaust Denial on Trial, the telegram also gave figures for the last two weeks of 1942: L 12,761, B 0, S 515 and T 10,335, a total of 23,611 [cite:rs_rd4_sobibor_hoefle] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers].

The message was one of many German police messages decoded at Bletchley Park. The decrypt is held at The National Archives, Kew, among the GC&CS records, under catalogue reference HW 16/23, dated January 1943 [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hoefle_blog] [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hw16_23]. The sources differ on two points. The National Archives and Holocaust Denial on Trial state that the decrypt was discovered in 2000 among newly declassified records. Sobibor.org dates the discovery to 2001, citing its publication that year by the historians P. Witte and S. Tyas in Holocaust and Genocide Studies, volume 14-3, pages 468–486 [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hoefle_blog] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers] [cite:rs_rd4_sobibor_hoefle]. The difference may be one of discovery against publication; the sources do not settle it. They also differ on the addressee. The decrypt at Kew is addressed to SS-Obersturmbannführer Heim, Commander of the Security Police in Cracow [cite:rs_rd4_tna_hoefle_blog]. Holocaust Denial on Trial says the figures were sent to Eichmann in Berlin, which would imply a parallel message [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers]. No such second message is described in the sources consulted here.

The Korherr Report, 1943. The same figure appears again in a report that Heinrich Himmler commissioned from the SS statistician Richard Korherr under the title "The Final Solution of the European Jewish Question." According to the Holocaust Memorial Museum, Korherr finished it in January 1943 and gave it to Dr. Rudolf Brandt in March, and a summary was later passed to Hitler [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_korherr_nachlass] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers]. Yad Vashem's translation, dated 23 March 1943, gives total evacuations "including Theresienstadt and Special Treatment" of 1,873,549, or 1,786,356 without Theresienstadt [cite:rs_rd4_yv_korherr_text]. Holocaust Denial on Trial reports that the Korherr Report repeats Höfle's exact figure of 1,274,166 for the four camps of Operation Reinhard, and that its figures for the Jews of France (41,911) and Belgium (16,886) closely match the transport lists (41,941 and 16,861) [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers]. On that account, a radio intercept held in British archives and a statistical report written for Himmler give the same number.

The report also shows how the regime managed its own vocabulary. Himmler ordered that the term Sonderbehandlung, "special treatment," of the Jews not be used. Korherr was told to substitute wording meaning "processed through the camps in the Government-General," durchgeschleust. One instance of "Special Treatment" nevertheless remained in the final version [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_korherr_nachlass] [cite:rs_rd4_hdot_numbers]. The Holocaust Memorial Museum has acquired Korherr's personal papers, Accession 2021.201.1, in 6,142 digital images. They contain two copies of the report [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_korherr_nachlass].

The Stroop Report, May 1943. The suppression of the Warsaw Ghetto uprising, from 19 April to 16 May 1943, was recorded in an album prepared by SS Major General Jürgen Stroop. Three albums were made, for Himmler, for Krüger and for Stroop himself [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_stroop_photo] [cite:rs_rd4_barch_stroop]. Copies are held today at the United States National Archives, at the Bundesarchiv in Germany and at Poland's Institute of National Remembrance. The copy used at Nuremberg is in the United States National Archives. The Bundesarchiv states that its own copy was held shortly after the war by SHAEF and reached the Bundesarchiv only in 2005, from the British Foreign Office Research Department's "German Collection" [cite:rs_rd4_barch_stroop]. In the English translation published by the Holocaust History Project, Stroop's teletype of 16 May 1943 reports: "Total number of Jews dealt with 56,065, including both Jews caught and Jews whose extermination can be proved" [cite:rs_rd4_stroop_teletype]. The Holocaust Memorial Museum summarizes the total differently in two places: as "some 56,000" killed or captured, and as "more than 55,000" captured, of whom at least 7,000 were killed [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_stroop_photo] [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_denial_nuremberg]. The sources consulted here do not reconcile the teletype's figure with the two summaries.

Himmler at Posen, October 1943. On 4 October 1943 Himmler addressed SS generals at Posen. The United States National Archives holds his handwritten notes for the speech. They use the euphemism Judenevakuierung, "evacuation of the Jews," and the archive states that in sound recordings of the speech Himmler defined "evacuation" as "extermination" [cite:rs_rd4_nara_himmler_notes]. In the speech, Nuremberg document 1919-PS, he spoke of "the extermination (Ausrottung) of the Jewish people" as a page of glory that was never to be written. A transcript runs to 116 pages [cite:rs_rd4_nww2m_posen] [cite:rs_rd4_ghdi_posen] [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_evidence]. On the National Archives' account, the recordings show what "evacuation" meant in the speech of the man who had ordered that "special treatment" not be used in Korherr's report.

Capture and authentication, 1945. The documents reached the courtroom through a deliberate process of seizure, selection and certification. On 22 November 1945, before the International Military Tribunal, Colonel Robert Storey testified that "literally hundreds of tons of enemy documents and records were screened and examined," and that more than 2,500 documents had been selected and filed at the Nuremberg courthouse, with the originals kept in safes in the Document Room [cite:rs_rd4_avalon_storey]. Each American document received a trial identification number in one of five letter series that indicated how it had been acquired, among them L, R, C and EC; documents such as 2903-PS carry the PS suffix. On 19 November 1945 Major Coogan signed an affidavit, document 001-A-PS, entered as Exhibit USA-1, certifying the photostats as true copies of the originals [cite:rs_rd4_avalon_storey] [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_coogan]. The Holocaust Memorial Museum states that the Allies presented roughly 3,000 tons of documents and records at Nuremberg, drawn from millions of Nazi documents seized in 1945, and that a 1958 National Archives catalog of the captured records ran to 62 volumes [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_denial_nuremberg].

The records were preserved in copies before the originals went home. The Nuremberg records are in the United States National Archives as Record Group 238. The captured-document series were microfilmed, among them NG (publication T1139, 70 rolls), NI (T301, 164 rolls) and NOKW (T1119, 47 rolls), and the Tribunal's proceedings were published in 42 volumes [cite:rs_rd4_nara_imt_records]. In the early 1950s the Department of the Army, working with the American Historical Association, microfilmed captured records before the originals were returned; by March 1968 the Army had returned 35 shipments to Germany [cite:rs_rd4_nara_return].

The documentary record was matched by a visual one. On 12 April 1945 General Eisenhower visited the camp at Ohrdruf with Generals Patton and Bradley. That day he cabled General George C. Marshall: "The things I saw beggar description." On 19 April he asked Marshall to bring members of Congress and editors to the camps, so that the evidence could not be dismissed as propaganda [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_ohrdruf]. On 29 November 1945, introduced by the American prosecutor Thomas Dodd, the prosecution at the International Military Tribunal showed an hour-long film, The Nazi Concentration Camps, compiled from footage shot as Allied troops liberated the camps, with clips credited to George Stevens [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_film] [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_film_article].

Höss's affidavits and the Auschwitz figure. Not every number in the trial record has stood. The commandant of Auschwitz, Rudolf Höss, signed affidavit 3868-PS at Nuremberg on 5 April 1946. In it he estimated that "at least 2,500,000 victims were executed and exterminated" at Auschwitz by gassing and burning, and that "at least another half million succumbed to starvation and disease making a total dead of about 3,000,000" [cite:rs_rd4_harvard_hoess_3868] [cite:rs_rd4_avalon_hoess]. Testifying before the Tribunal on 15 April 1946, he confirmed the affidavit's contents as true and agreed that Eichmann had told him more than 2 million Jews had been destroyed at Auschwitz. He stated that he had commanded Auschwitz from May 1940 until December 1943 [cite:rs_rd4_avalon_hoess]. In a further affidavit, dated 14 May 1946, he gave 2 million Jews gassed and 500,000 killed by other means [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_hoess_affidavit].

The Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum states that Höss's figures were "highly overinflated," as Höss himself later said while in Polish custody. The Museum likewise treats as overinflated the Soviet figure of 4 million. The research of Franciszek Piper, published in 1990, gave 1.1 million as the most probable number of victims, and the Museum's present figures are that about 1.3 million people were deported to Auschwitz, of whom about 1.1 million were murdered [cite:rs_rd4_auschwitz_victims]. Höss's total of about 3,000,000 is nearly three times the Museum's figure of about 1.1 million.

Jerusalem, 1961. Adolf Eichmann had edited the Wannsee minutes. His trial of Adolf Eichmann, criminal case 40/61 of the Jerusalem District Court, opened on 11 April 1961. Some 1,600 documents were selected as evidence, most of them bearing Eichmann's signature, and a list of 108 survivor witnesses was prepared [cite:rs_rd4_yv_eichmann_trial]. The sources differ on the date of the verdict. The Holocaust Memorial Museum gives the guilty finding as 11–12 December 1961 and the death sentence as 15 December [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_eichmann_guilty]. Yad Vashem states that the trial ended on 15 December 1961 with the reading of the verdict, and elsewhere gives 13 December for the guilty finding [cite:rs_rd4_yv_eichmann_trial]. The sources agree that he was sentenced to death on 15 December 1961 and hanged at Ramla prison on 1 June 1962 [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_eichmann_guilty] [cite:rs_rd4_yv_eichmann_trial].

The Arolsen Archives. The Arolsen Archives hold records concerning the victims rather than the perpetrators. The Allies founded the International Tracing Service in 1948; it is now the Arolsen Archives. It holds information on about 17.5 million people and is part of UNESCO's Memory of the World [cite:rs_rd4_arolsen_profile]. According to a report of the Congressional Research Service, the eleven-nation International Commission agreed in May 2006 to amend the 1955 Bonn Accords, and the archives, then described as some 50 million documents, opened to the public on 28 November 2007 [cite:rs_rd4_crs_its].

What the documents do not show. The perpetrators' records count in their own categories. The Höfle telegram records arrivals at camps, not deaths. The Korherr Report counts "evacuations," and its author was ordered to replace "special treatment" with a phrase about being processed through camps. Himmler's notes say "evacuation," and only the recordings of his speech supply the meaning. The Wannsee Protocol is an edited summary, not a transcript: it records the substance Eichmann chose to set down and, as the account of the meeting stresses, no recorded objection, which is not the same as proof that no one in the room had doubts [cite:rs_rd4_ushmm_wannsee_protocol] [cite:ushmm_wannsee_protocol]. The Einsatzgruppen reports give different figures for the same massacre at Kiev, and the sources give the victims attributed to the Case 9 defendants as between 723,661 and one million (the indictment, per the Holocaust Memorial Museum) or more than one million (the prosecution's estimate, per Harvard's Nuremberg Trials Project).

Much of the record is also simply missing. Of thirty copies of the Wannsee Protocol, one has been found. The Höfle decrypt surfaced among newly declassified British records only in 2000 or 2001, and the Bundesarchiv's copy of the Stroop Report reached it only in 2005. The sources consulted here do not document what other records were destroyed, by whom, or when; that question lies outside this section and is left open. Nor does any single document hold the whole number. The documents described here cover particular units, particular camps, particular periods and particular cities. The total of six million is built, as [scene:hol_s05|How the Number Is Known] shows, by joining them to demographic reconstruction and to the naming of victims one by one. According to Holocaust Denial on Trial, a radio intercept found at Kew in 2000 or 2001 and a statistical report written for Himmler in 1943 give the same figure, 1,274,166. Neither was written for the courts. Himmler had ordered that the term "special treatment" not be used in the second.
