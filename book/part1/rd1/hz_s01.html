Any Power That Would Sign: Herzl's Search for a Charter, 1898–1904

When the delegates left Basel in 1897, the program they had adopted named an aim but no patron. Theodor Herzl's answer to that gap was a charter. By a charter he meant legal backing for Jewish settlement, granted by a great power that could make it stick. The familiar telling of Zionist history has the movement looking to Britain from the start, as though 1917 had been the plan all along. The diplomatic record of Herzl's own years shows something looser and more opportunistic. Between 1898 and 1904 he approached the German Kaiser, the Ottoman Sultan, the British Colonial Secretary, the Russian Interior Minister and the Pope, and to each he brought whatever that power seemed most likely to want.[cite:wiki_kaiser_levant_1898] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl]

The reason for this method lay in the structure of the problem. Herzl led an organization with a Congress and a program, but no territory, no army and no standing in international law. Palestine belonged to the Ottoman Empire. Only a sovereign, or a power that could lean on one, could turn a settlement scheme into a legal right. Herzl therefore needed someone else's signature, and he had no fixed view about whose it should be.

He tried Germany first. The introduction came through William Hechler, a Christian restorationist, a believer in the return of the Jews to their ancient land. Through him Herzl met Kaiser Wilhelm II three times in 1898: in Istanbul, at Mikveh Israel, an agricultural colony funded by the Rothschilds, and in Jerusalem itself.[cite:wiki_kaiser_levant_1898] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] He did raise the matter with the Sultan. The request failed, and the Kaiser's interest faded after it.[cite:wiki_kaiser_levant_1898]

Herzl then went directly to the ruler of Palestine. He had been pursuing a charter from Sultan Abdul Hamid II since 1896, and his offer had a consistent shape: help in consolidating the Ottoman public debt.[cite:w_herzl] The empire's debts were chronic, and Herzl proposed to put Jewish finance to work on them in exchange for the right to settle. On 17 May 1901 the Sultan finally received him. Herzl made the offer in person. No charter was granted.[cite:avnery_herzl_sultan] [cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] [cite:w_abdulhamid]

The clearest statement of the Ottoman position comes from Herzl's own diary. It records a message relayed to him from Abdul Hamid II: the Sultan could not sell even a foot of the land, because it belonged not to him but to his people.[cite:w_herzl] The message carries weight precisely because of where it is preserved. It was not written by an opponent of Zionism to discredit Herzl; it was written down by Herzl himself. It answers a claim still heard today, that the Ottoman Empire welcomed the Zionist movement or sold it Palestine. At the level of the throne, the answer was no.

That refusal was matched by policy. From the 1880s the Ottoman government imposed restrictions on Jewish immigration to Palestine and on Jewish land purchase there. The restrictions were real, but they were enforced unevenly. Corruption weakened them, and so did foreign consular protection. A second Ottoman instrument worked in the opposite direction. The Land Code of 1858 required land titles to be registered. Many peasants avoided registering, and large tracts were registered instead to urban notables and absentee owners, whose later sales to the Jewish National Fund led to the eviction of the tenant farmers who worked the land.[cite:w_ottoman_land_code] The Ottoman state thus played two roles at once: the ruler who refused a charter, and the legal order under which land could change hands regardless. How the absentee estates were bought, and why their tenants had no title to defend, is told in [scene:s04j|the land purchase mechanism].

With the Sultan's door closed, Herzl changed both his patron and his offer. In October 1902 he proposed to the British Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, a Jewish settlement at El-Arish in the Sinai. He framed it as a buffer protecting British interests at the Suez Canal. Britain tentatively agreed.[cite:unu_water_elarish] [cite:wiki_leopold_kessler] The scheme then ran into the authority that actually governed the ground. In 1903 Lord Cromer, Britain's man in Cairo, vetoed it. The Nile water needed to irrigate a settlement in the Sinai, he said, could not be spared.[cite:unu_water_elarish] The inducement this time was strategic rather than financial, and the ground Cromer gave for refusing it was water.

The most controversial approach came next. In April 1903 came the pogrom at Kishinev, in the Russian Empire, told in [scene:s04e|Kishinev and the East Africa offer]. In August 1903, months afterwards, Herzl twice met the Interior Minister, Vyacheslav von Plehve, a man widely regarded as responsible for the atmosphere that had produced the pogrom. He did so without consulting Russia's own Zionists. The meetings produced a letter in which the Tsar's government agreed to press the Sultan for a charter and to allow Zionist societies to operate in Russia.[cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl]

Russian Zionists criticized the meetings sharply.[cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] The two sides of that quarrel can be stated from the record. On one side stood what the meetings produced: a written commitment to press the Sultan, and legal room for Zionist societies inside Russia. On the other stood the manner of them: the movement's leader had met the minister widely held responsible for the climate of Kishinev, without consulting Russia's Zionists. The record shows the letter and the anger; it does not show that the letter ever moved the Sultan. Weeks later Britain's offer of territory in East Africa split the Sixth Zionist Congress.[cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress]

The last of these approaches went to Rome. On 26 January 1904 Herzl had a private audience with Pope Pius X, Giuseppe Sarto, to ask for the Vatican's support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. The refusal was direct and on the record.[cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] Herzl's diary records the Pope's words: "We cannot give this movement our blessing... We cannot prevent the Jews from going to Jerusalem — but we could never sanction it. The ground of Jerusalem, if it were not always sacred, has been sanctified by the life of Jesus Christ. As the head of the Church, I cannot tell you anything different." Herzl also met the Vatican Secretary of State, Cardinal Rafael Merry del Val, who is described as similarly unreceptive.

Two readings of the Pope's grounds appear in the sources, and they are not identical. One stresses doctrine: the Church could not support Jewish restoration to a land central to its own theology while the Jews denied Christ, a position later accounts call supersessionist.[cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] The other stresses place: the Vatican's specific concern was Jerusalem and the status of the holy sites, which would be complicated by a non-Christian sovereign, rather than Jewish settlement as such. The Pope's own words, as Herzl recorded them, contain both: he would not prevent Jews going to Jerusalem, but he would not sanction the movement, and he grounded that in the sanctity of the city. Either way, the founder of the movement went to Rome and was refused. The Vatican did not recognize the State of Israel until 30 December 1993, forty-five years after its independence; that longer story is told in [scene:vat_s01|the Vatican's long refusal].[cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition]

Set side by side, the approaches show a pattern. Herzl did not carry one proposal from capital to capital. He shaped each offer to its listener: finance for an indebted empire, a strategic buffer for a Britain anxious about Suez, and, in the Russian case, a movement that would draw Jews out of the empire. Germany, the Ottoman Empire, Britain and Russia were all approached within five years. On this reading, Britain's sponsorship in 1917 was the strategy finally succeeding with one power after failing with the others, not its point of departure.

The long arc of the Ottoman state's dealings with the Zionist movement, and later with Israel, runs from this period of imperial rule through the Treaty of Lausanne, decades of alliance and a later rivalry. On their own terms these approaches produced almost nothing. The Kaiser lost interest, the Sultan granted nothing, Cairo vetoed El-Arish, the Russian letter yielded no charter and sharp criticism from Russian Zionists, and the Pope said no. Herzl died in 1904. What outlasted him was the method itself, the search for a sovereign's signature, and an organization able to keep searching.

Several things the record does not settle. It does not say what, if anything, Herzl offered the Kaiser in return for his intercession, nor exactly what the Kaiser asked of the Sultan. The Sultan's refusal of land is known through Herzl's own diary, as a message relayed to him; the sources here do not give its date or its intermediary, or an Ottoman record of it. The precise terms of the debt offer, how much and from whom, are not documented in this material. Plehve's responsibility for Kishinev is a matter of how he was widely regarded, not a finding of a court. And while the Pope's words survive in Herzl's account, the weight Pius X himself gave to doctrine and to the holy sites cannot be separated from that one record.
