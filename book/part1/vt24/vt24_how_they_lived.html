How They Lived: Rites, Women, Money and Rules

The practice half of the question is whether these Christians asked for a tithe, imposed commandments, or demanded attendance on Sundays and holy days. The evidence here is thinner than for their teaching, and most of it is hostile: apart from a handful of their own texts, what we know about how Valentinians, Sethians or Basilideans actually lived comes from bishops writing to discredit them. Each answer below says which kind of evidence it rests on. The answers also need a fair comparison. The proto-orthodox churches of the second and third centuries did not have a compulsory tithe or a Sunday law either; those came later, and the measure of the Gnostic groups has to be taken against the church of their own time, not the church of the Middle Ages.

Rites

These were not groups without ritual. The Gospel of Philip, a Valentinian anthology, names five mysteries: "a baptism and a chrism and a eucharist and a redemption and a bridal chamber". [cite:vt24_gphil] The Apocryphon of John ends with the saviour sealing the awakened "in the light of the water with five seals", a phrase the scholar Birger Pearson reads as a rite of repeated immersion. [cite:vt24_apjohn] [cite:vt24_wiki_sethianism] Irenaeus describes Valentinian "redemption" rites that varied from group to group: a "spiritual marriage", baptism in water with invocations of the unknown Father, an anointing with oil and water and balsam, and a final anointing of the dying. In the same passage he reports that some rejected all such rites as unnecessary. [cite:vt24_ah1_21] The "bridal chamber" gave opponents material for accusations of sexual ritual; the text of Philip supports a sacramental reading at least as well as a literal one, and no independent evidence for such rituals exists.

Women

Women held roles in several of these groups that the bishops denied them. Marcellina led the Carpocratians in Rome. [cite:vt24_ah1_25] Irenaeus says the Valentinian teacher Marcus had women consecrate the eucharistic cup and urged them to prophesy, telling one, "Open your mouth, speak whatsoever occurs to you, and you shall prophesy". [cite:vt24_ah1_13] Tertullian complained that among heretics women were "bold enough to teach, to dispute, to enact exorcisms, to undertake cures—it may be even to baptize". [cite:vt24_praescr41] Both writers present this as proof of disorder; read against the grain, it is evidence that women taught and presided. The Gospel of Mary, with Mary Magdalene as the disciple who receives the Saviour's private teaching and Peter asking "Did He prefer her to us?", is the best-known text on the question and is treated in [scene:vt26_gospel_of_mary|Part Twenty-Six]. [cite:vt24_mary_pbs] The record is not uniform: the last saying of the Gospel of Thomas promises the kingdom to "every woman who will make herself male", a line read either as symbolic or as reflecting the low view of women common to the period. [cite:vt24_gthom]

Leadership

Tertullian's complaint about the heretics' order is itself informative. Among them, he wrote, "it is doubtful who is a catechumen, and who a believer"; "to-day one man is their bishop, to-morrow another"; "on laymen do they impose the functions of priesthood". [cite:vt24_praescr41] He meant it as ridicule. It describes groups with rotating, looser offices and little separation between clergy and laity. At the same time the Valentinians were often members of the ordinary congregations rather than a rival church. Irenaeus says they called ordinary church members "ecclesiastics", used "our phraseology", and asked why, "when they say the same things, and hold the same doctrine", the bishops called them heretics. [cite:vt24_ah3_15] Ptolemy claimed an apostolic tradition "received by succession" of his own. [cite:vt24_ect_flora] Constantine's undated edict against heretics, preserved by Eusebius, ordered Valentinians and others to stop meeting in their "houses of prayer" and to hand them over, which shows that some groups had regular assemblies and buildings by then. [cite:vt24_vc365]

Answer one: tithe and money

No tithe system is documented for any second- or third-century Gnostic group. That is an absence of evidence, not proof that money never changed hands, but there is one direct statement on the point in their own writings: the Apocryphon of John closes with "Cursed be everyone who will exchange these things for a gift or for food or for drink or for clothing". [cite:vt24_apjohn] The teaching was not to be sold. Against this stands a single hostile allegation: Irenaeus says Marcus targeted women "of great wealth", that they gave him their possessions, and that he "collected a very large fortune". [cite:vt24_ah1_13] It comes with accusations of seduction from a writer whose purpose was to discredit Marcus, and no independent source confirms it.

The comparison matters. The proto-orthodox churches did not collect a tithe either. Justin Martyr, describing a Christian assembly in Rome about 155, says that "they who are well to do, and willing, give what each thinks fit", and that the president uses it to support "the orphans and widows". [cite:vt24_justin67] The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1912 states that the clergy were at first supported by "the spontaneous offerings of the faithful", and that the earliest church legislation on tithes is a letter of bishops at Tours in 567 and the canons of the Council of Mâcon in 585, with civil enforcement first under Charlemagne in the late eighth century. [cite:vt24_ce_tithes] The compulsory tithe came about four centuries after these groups flourished; it belongs to [scene:vt31_tithe|Part Thirty-One]. The closest thing to a fixed religious economy in this family is the Manichaeans, a separate religion, whose lay Hearers supplied food to the Elect in return for their prayers. [cite:vt24_wiki_manich]

Answer two: commandments

No Gnostic group is documented as issuing a code of commandments of its own, and some texts reject the idea of one. In the Gospel of Mary the Saviour tells the disciples, "Don't lay down any rules beyond what I've given you, nor make a law like the lawgiver, lest you be bound by it." [cite:vt26_mattison_gmary] When the disciples in Thomas ask whether to fast, pray and give alms, Jesus answers "Do not tell lies, and do not do what you hate", and in saying 14 warns, "If you fast, you will give rise to sin for yourselves". [cite:vt24_gthom2] But "no rules" overstates the record. Ptolemy kept the Ten Commandments as God's "pure law", fulfilled rather than abolished by the Saviour, and reinterpreted fasting as "abstinence from everything evil". [cite:vt24_ect_flora] Irenaeus says Valentinians taught that ordinary believers needed faith and good works, while the spiritual were exempt "because they are spiritual by nature", and he and Plotinus both accused some groups of moral indifference. [cite:vt24_ah1_06] [cite:vt24_bmcr_plotinus] The fair summary is that these teachers rejected external law as the means of salvation, not ethics as such, and that their ethics ranged from rigorous to (according to their enemies) lax.

Answer three: Sunday and holy-day duty

No document shows a Gnostic group requiring attendance on Sundays or feast days, and their texts treat the calendar spiritually. Thomas saying 27 says that if you do not keep the Sabbath as a Sabbath you will not see the Father, and Ptolemy counts the sabbath, fasting and Passover among the laws whose "names remained the same but the content was changed". [cite:vt24_gthom2] [cite:vt24_ect_flora] They did meet: Constantine's edict, and Sozomen's account of it, forbade Valentinians and others to hold church "in private houses, or in public places". [cite:vt24_vc365] [cite:vt24_sozomen232] Here too the comparison cuts both ways. Justin describes the proto-orthodox Christians gathering "on the day called Sunday" in the 150s, but as a custom, not a legal duty. [cite:vt24_justin67] Constantine's civil law of 7 March 321 making the "venerable day of the Sun" a day of rest came long after these groups formed, and church rules on Sunday attendance followed; both are covered in [scene:vt31_sunday_and_law|Part Thirty-One]. [cite:vt21_wiki_sol]
