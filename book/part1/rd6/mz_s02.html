The Yemenite Children Affair

Of the charges that came out of the immigrant camps, the gravest concerns infants. The founding generation of Israel is often remembered as having received every Jewish immigrant on equal terms. For many of the Jews who came from Arab and Muslim countries after 1948, the record is different, and its most painful chapter is the disappearance of children from the camps. The affair runs from the first years of the state to a cabinet decision of 2021, and its central question has still not been answered to the satisfaction of the families who raised it.

The setting was the transit camps, the ma'abarot described in [scene:rd6_ingathering|the mass immigration of 1948–1952]. Among those placed there were the Yemeni Jews flown in by Operation Magic Carpet between June 1949 and September 1950, about 49,000 people, many of whom had walked for weeks to reach the airlift at Aden; their journey is told in [scene:rd6_exodus|the Jews of the Arab world after 1948]. [cite:w_magic_carpet] In those camps hundreds of children were reported dead or vanished. [cite:w_magic_carpet]

The families' account is this. Between 1948 and 1954, they say, their infants were taken from the camps and from clinics and hospitals. Doctors told the parents that the children had died, but refused to give them the bodies or death certificates. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] Most of the families were Yemenite, but others came from the Balkans, North Africa and elsewhere. They allege that the babies were given to Ashkenazi families, in Israel and abroad. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] The children who disappeared are counted in the hundreds. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] [cite:w_magic_carpet]

The state's own answer came through inquiries. Three official inquiries, the first of them in 1967, concluded that most of the children had died of disease. [cite:arabnews_stolen_babies] That finding did not end the matter. The newspaper Israel Hayom later published documents that, it said, indicated the possible organized involvement of state bodies. [cite:israelhayom_yemenite] The sources cited here do not give the dates of the second and third inquiries or of the newspaper's publication.

In February 2021 the Israeli cabinet approved NIS 162 million, about $50 million, in compensation for the families. About 1,050 families were eligible. The government stated that it "regrets" the events and recognizes the suffering of the families. [cite:tps_yemenite_2021] [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu called the affair one of the most painful in the state's history and asked that it be taught in schools. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] The decision expressed regret but did not accept responsibility. The family organizations said that it lacked the essential element: an official apology and an acceptance of responsibility by the state. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021]

The affair matters to the history of the founding years for a reason beyond its own facts. It can be read as evidence of discrimination among Jews inside the Jewish state. According to the Times of Israel, the immigrants from Arab and Muslim countries housed in the ma'abarot, which it describes as shantytowns, were largely sidelined by the European-born leadership of the ruling Labor party. [cite:toi_yemenite_2021] It is part of the record that many readers raised in Israel come to late.

What the record does not settle is the central question. The inquiries' finding, that most of the children died of disease, and the families' allegation, that children were taken and given away, are both on the record. The documents Israel Hayom published point toward possible state involvement without, on the evidence cited here, establishing its extent. The 2021 decision paid compensation and expressed regret without resolving which account is true, and without the admission of responsibility that the families asked for. How many children died, how many, if any, were given to other families, and who decided are questions this record leaves open.
