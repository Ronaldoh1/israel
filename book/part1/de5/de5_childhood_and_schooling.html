A Diplomat's Son, Three Grades Ahead

Curtis Guy Yarvin was born in 1973 into what his biographers describe as a liberal, secular family. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] The place and exact date of his birth are not in the published record used here, and much of what is known about his childhood comes from two kinds of source that need to be kept apart: his own accounts, given in interviews and on his blogs, and the reporting of journalists who spoke to him and, in some cases, to his relatives. Where the two agree the account below says so; where a detail rests on his word alone, it says that too.

His parents were both, in his own description to the New York Times in January 2025, career federal civil servants. [cite:de5_lgc_nyt] His father, Herbert Yarvin, worked for the United States government abroad. In an interview with the writer Max Raskin, Yarvin described him as a diplomat in Nicosia, the capital of Cyprus. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] Jacob Siegel, in a long profile for Tablet in 2022, reported that the family was posted to Portugal, Cyprus and the Dominican Republic, after earlier years in Fairfax County, Virginia. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] Ava Kofman, whose profile for The New Yorker appeared in June 2025 after months of access, reported that Herbert Yarvin held a doctorate in philosophy before he became a diplomat, and that the family lived in the Dominican Republic and Cyprus. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] The broad outline, a foreign-service family that moved between overseas postings, is therefore carried by two independent profiles as well as by Yarvin himself; the full list of his father's postings is not documented beyond those reports. The father's career is his own record and is not examined here.

His mother was a Protestant from Westchester County, New York, who later also joined the civil service; according to Siegel, his parents had met at Brown University in the mid-1960s. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] On his father's side, his grandparents were Jewish American communists, a detail Siegel reported and Yarvin has discussed in his own writing. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] It is a family fact, stated here once and plainly, and nothing in this book draws an inference from it.

A child moved ahead

The most consistent theme in every account of his early life is speed. Kofman reported that he was partly schooled at home by his mother, skipped three grades, and started high school at twelve after the family settled in Columbia, Maryland, a planned town between Baltimore and Washington. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] Siegel's figures are close: he was eleven in ninth grade, a twelve-year-old sophomore at a public high school in Columbia, after an admissions test moved him up two further grades. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] An older brother, according to biographical summaries, had skipped four. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] The school is identified as Wilde Lake High School, from which he graduated in 1988; the source for the school's name is one of his own later blog posts, not a school record. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin]

The Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth, a programme run from Johns Hopkins University since the 1970s, identified children who scored exceptionally well on college entrance tests at a young age and offered them accelerated courses. Accounts of Yarvin's place in it differ in date. Some biographical summaries say he entered it in 1985, at about twelve, a line that carries no cited source in the reference works that repeat it. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] Siegel reported that at fifteen he went to Johns Hopkins through the programme and, a year later, transferred to Brown University as a legacy admission, his parents having met there. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] The two accounts may describe two stages of the same relationship with the programme, a test at twelve and enrolment at fifteen; the record reviewed here does not settle it, and nothing turns on the difference beyond the plain fact, agreed by all sources, that he reached university years ahead of his age.

He graduated from Brown in 1992, at eighteen according to Kofman. [cite:de5_kofman_nuso] [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] He then began a doctorate in computer science at the University of California, Berkeley, and left after about a year and a half to take a job in the technology industry; the historian Joshua Tait, in a 2019 chapter on Yarvin, and Siegel both give that duration. [cite:de5_wiki_yarvin] [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] He never completed the degree.

What the early record does and does not show

Three points from this part of the record matter for the rest of the book.

The first is that the man who would later argue that the American civil service should be retired wholesale was himself the child of two federal civil servants, and of a stepfather who, Siegel reported, also joined the civil service. [cite:de5_tablet_siegel] [cite:de5_lgc_nyt] Yarvin raised the point himself in his 2025 New York Times interview, when he was asked how his proposal to empty the federal workforce would treat the people in it; his answer, that it should be handled "like a company that goes out of business," is examined in [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]. [cite:de6_nyt_transcript] The biography does not explain the ideas, and the book does not treat it as if it did: the record contains no statement by Yarvin tracing his politics to his parents' work, beyond his mention of it in answer to that question.

The second is that his formation was mathematical and technical, not historical or legal. Nothing in the documented record of his schooling involves political theory, history or law; he came to those, by his own account and by every biographer's, later and on his own, as an adult reading in San Francisco. The next section follows that turn. When later critics, from the political theorist Danielle Allen to the economist Garett Jones, debated him in 2025 on questions of government, they were debating a self-taught writer, and Yarvin has never presented himself otherwise. [cite:de5_crimson] [cite:de5_ylsfed]

The third is a matter of method. A good deal of what circulates about Yarvin's youth in online summaries, from the size of the family's moves to his supposed early reading, cannot be traced to a document or to a profile in which he or his relatives were interviewed. Those details are not repeated here. What can be said with confidence is short: a precocious child of a foreign-service family, moved through school years early, a graduate of Brown at eighteen and a Berkeley doctoral student who left to write software. The rest of his life is far better documented, much of it by himself.
