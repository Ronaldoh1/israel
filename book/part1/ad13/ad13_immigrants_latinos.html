Immigrants and Latino Americans: The Second Generation and the Tax Ledger

For immigrants the American Dream is not a civic lesson but a decision: a family moves because it expects its children to do better than they would have at home. The record on that expectation is one of the strongest in this book for the taught version. It is also a record with a hard edge, because millions of the workers who make the move have no legal status, pay taxes into programmes they cannot draw on, and since 2025 have faced new limits on benefits and new uses of their tax records. This section takes the long mobility record first, then Latino Americans as the largest group with immigrant roots, then the tax ledger of undocumented workers and the laws that changed in 2025 and 2026.

The scale is large. The Pew Research Center counted 51.9 million immigrants in the United States in June 2025, 15.4 per cent of the population, down from a record 53.3 million in January 2025; the unauthorised population reached a record 14 million in 2023, and 46 per cent of immigrants were naturalised citizens. [cite:us6_pew_immig2025] About 8.3 million unauthorised immigrants were in the labour force in 2022, just under 5 per cent of all workers. [cite:us13_epi_unauth]

The long record: children of immigrants climb

Ran Abramitzky, Leah Boustan, Elisa Jácome and Santiago Pérez linked millions of fathers and sons across more than a century of census and tax records. They found that the children of immigrants from nearly every sending country have higher upward mobility than the children of US-born parents who started at the same income, both a century ago and today. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer] Sons of immigrant fathers at the 25th percentile of the income distribution reached ranks about 3 to 4 percentiles higher than sons of US-born fathers between 1880 and 1910, and about 5 percentiles higher in modern data. The exceptions in the modern data include children of immigrants from Haiti and Jamaica, majority-Black countries whose children face the gaps described in [scene:ad13_black_americans|the previous section]. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_nber] Part of the advantage comes from where immigrant families settle: they tend to move to places with better opportunities, which the place research in [scene:ad3_relative_and_place|Part Three] shows matters causally. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer]

A percentile rank here means position in the national income distribution, from 1 at the bottom to 100 at the top; a gain of 5 ranks for a child starting at the 25th percentile is a move from roughly the bottom quarter toward the bottom third. The finding is a comparison of relative mobility (defined in [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two]) and holds for families starting at the same income, so it is not explained by immigrants being richer or poorer to begin with.

Latino Americans: convergence measured

Hispanic or Latino Americans (the Census Bureau's term covers people of any race with roots in Spanish-speaking countries) are both the largest group with recent immigrant roots and a group whose mobility can be measured over generations. The Chetty, Hendren, Jones and Porter study found that Hispanic children's upward mobility was slightly below that of white children and that Hispanic Americans are "moving up significantly in the income distribution across generations". [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:ad2_oi_race_hisp]

The current figures fit that path. The official poverty rate for Hispanic Americans was 13.9 per cent in 2025, the lowest on record, against 10.2 per cent for the whole population. [cite:us8_census_2025] Median Hispanic family net worth rose 18 per cent in real terms between 2022 and 2025, from $67,400 to $79,600 in 2025 dollars, still about a quarter of the white median ($79,600 ÷ $332,100 = 0.24). [cite:us8_scf2025] Hispanic homeownership rose from 41.2 per cent in 1994 to 49.5 per cent in 2005 in the Census Bureau's housing survey, and stood at about 48 per cent in 2021. [cite:ad13_census_hvs05] [cite:ad13_epi_hvs_race] The share of Latino adults with a bachelor's degree rose from 14.0 per cent in 2013 to 20.8 per cent in 2023, against a national 36.2 per cent. [cite:ad13_pnpi_latino] Life expectancy at birth for Hispanic Americans was 81.8 years in 2024, nearly three years above non-Hispanic white Americans (78.9), despite lower incomes. [cite:ad13_nchs_lt2024]

The tax ledger of undocumented workers

Undocumented immigrants are, for most of the Dream's safety net, contributors without claims. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP), a research group whose work generally favours progressive taxation, estimated that they paid $96.7 billion in federal, state and local taxes in 2022: $59.4 billion federal and $37.3 billion state and local. [cite:ad13_itep_state] [cite:ad13_itep_sub] Of the federal total, $25.7 billion was Social Security tax, $6.4 billion Medicare tax and $1.8 billion unemployment-insurance tax, funding programmes these workers are barred from using; ITEP found that in 40 states they pay a higher share of income in state and local taxes than the top 1 per cent of households there, and that they often cannot claim the Child Tax Credit or the Earned Income Tax Credit. [cite:ad13_itep_sub] [cite:ad13_itep_pr] Adding the payroll figures shows the size of the one-way flow: 25.7 + 6.4 + 1.8 = $33.9 billion a year, about 35 per cent of the total paid ($33.9bn ÷ $96.7bn).

The ledger has another side. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine reviewed the evidence in 2016 and found that first-generation immigrants cost governments more than the native-born, mainly at the state and local level; it found the second generation to be among the strongest fiscal contributors of any group, contributing the most of any generation to state budgets. Where it found negative wage effects, they fell on native-born workers without a high-school diploma and on earlier immigrants. [cite:ad13_nasem] The two findings measure different things: ITEP counts taxes paid by one group in one year, and the National Academies count taxes against the cost of services across generations. Both are needed to describe the ledger fairly.

What changed in 2025 and 2026 (status as of October 2026)

The budget reconciliation law signed on 4 July 2025 (Public Law 119-21, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) changed several of these terms. From 1 January 2026 it imposed a 1 per cent tax on international money transfers made in cash, by money order or by cashier's cheque, while transfers from bank accounts and cards were exempt. It required the adult filing for the Child Tax Credit, or one spouse on a joint return, to have a Social Security number, where before only the child needed one. It ended food-assistance (SNAP) eligibility for refugees, asylees and several other lawfully present groups; it limited Medicaid and CHIP to green-card holders, Cuban and Haitian entrants and migrants from the Pacific Compact states from 1 October 2026; and it limits ACA premium credits in the same way from 1 January 2027. [cite:ad13_usccb_obbba] Most of these changes fall on lawfully present immigrants, since undocumented immigrants were already excluded from the benefit programmes; the Child Tax Credit rule also reaches families in which a child has a Social Security number but the parents file with a taxpayer identification number instead.

Tax records also took on a new use. The Treasury and the Department of Homeland Security signed a data-sharing agreement in April 2025 under which Immigration and Customs Enforcement requested about 1.28 million names and the IRS verified about 47,000; on 24 February 2026 a federal appeals court panel in Washington declined to block the sharing. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb]

Where it works as promised

The second-generation record is the clearest evidence in this volume that the Dream as taught describes something real. For more than a century and in modern data, the children of immigrants who start poor climb further than the children of US-born parents who start at the same place, and Latino Americans, many of them children and grandchildren of immigrants, are closing gaps in poverty, wealth, homeownership and degrees. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer] [cite:ad13_oi_race_sum] [cite:us8_census_2025] [cite:ad13_pnpi_latino] [scene:ad2_what_holds|Part Two] places this finding among the parts of the promise that hold. The exceptions are documented too: the children of Black immigrants, and the parents themselves when they lack legal status, who pay into the safety net without the right to use it.
