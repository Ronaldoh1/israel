The Case That It Would Have Formed Anyway

The opposite argument holds that Hezbollah, or something like it, would have formed without the invasion, because the forces that built it were already moving before June 1982. This too is an argument about a history that did not happen. It rests on three bodies of documented fact: Shia political mobilisation in the 1960s and 1970s, a network of Islamist clerics trained in Iraq, and revolutionary Iran's declared aim of exporting its revolution.

Mobilisation before 1982. Lebanon's Shia community did not enter politics in 1982. The cleric Musa al-Sadr moved from Najaf in Iraq to Tyre in late 1959, became the first head of the Supreme Islamic Shia Council in 1969, and in 1974 founded the Movement of the Deprived with Hussein el-Husseini. [cite:hz3_wiki_sadr] Its armed wing, formed in January 1975, became known as Amal. [cite:hz3_wiki_amal] Amal drew on a community that the historical record describes as the poorest in Lebanon and the most neglected by the state, and its support grew after three events: al-Sadr's disappearance on a visit to Libya at the end of August 1978, Israel's invasion of the south that year, and Iran's revolution in 1979. [cite:hz3_wiki_amal] [cite:hz3_wiki_sadr] [scene:hz1_1978|Operation Litani] An organised, armed Shia party therefore existed before 1982. But Amal was founded as a secular movement to reform the Lebanese system on behalf of the deprived, not a religious revolutionary one; [cite:hz3_wiki_amal] the Israeli researcher Shimon Shapira notes that it rejected Khomeini's doctrine of rule by the senior jurist and stayed loyal to the Lebanese government, which is why Tehran looked elsewhere. [cite:hz3_itic_shapira]

The clerical network. The men who led Hezbollah came largely out of a different tradition, formed in the seminaries of Najaf. Mohammad Hussein Fadlallah, often called Hezbollah's spiritual mentor, a title he and the group both denied, studied in Najaf for twenty-one years before settling in Beirut in 1966. [cite:hz3_wiki_fadlallah] The Islamic Dawa Party, an Iraqi Shia Islamist party founded around Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr between the late 1950s and late 1960s, had a Lebanese branch that, by Wikipedia's account, later became Hezbollah; among its members it lists the Hezbollah co-founders Abbas al-Musawi and Mohammad Yazbek. [cite:hz3_wiki_dawa] Norton, in his history, stresses Iraq rather than Iran as the place "where many of Hezbollah's leading intellectuals originated", and describes a directive from Iraq for these clerics to work inside Amal to give it a more Islamic character. [cite:hz3_aljadid_norton] Naim Qassem has said that Hezbollah began as factions of Islamic movements inspired by Fadlallah. [cite:hz3_wiki_fadlallah] In the summer of 1982, Amal's deputy leader Husayn al-Musawi broke away to form Islamic Amal, one of the groups later folded into Hezbollah. [cite:hz3_wiki_amal]

Iran's stated aim. The third force is the one this side of the argument treats as decisive. After 1979, Ayatollah Khomeini's government declared that it would export its revolution, and, according to Shapira, a network built under a cleric named Montazeri supported Shia Islamist movements in the Hijaz in Saudi Arabia, in Bahrain, in Kuwait and in Lebanon. [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] Shapira, a retired Israeli brigadier general writing for the Meir Amit Intelligence and Terrorism Information Center, an Israeli research centre, puts the thesis directly: "The decision to establish Hezbollah was made in Tehran three years before Israel invaded Lebanon." [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] He names Ali Akbar Mohtashami, Iran's ambassador in Damascus, as the organisation's architect, and dates his posting to August 1981, a date this book could not confirm independently. [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] For him the invasion was a catalyst that gave Iran's plan its opening, not the plan itself. Hezbollah's own manifesto of 1985, with its pledge to Iran's supreme leader, and Qassem's statement that "the Israeli invasion of Lebanon was not the reason Hezbollah was founded", are the strongest evidence this side has, because they come from the organisation itself. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] Norton, on the other side, does not deny Iran's role; his own formula is that Hezbollah was "created by the Israeli occupation of Lebanon and by Iran". [cite:hz3_npr_norton]

Where Israel did not occupy. One way to test the argument is to look at what the same networks did in countries Israel never entered. On 12 December 1983, bombs struck the American and French embassies, the airport and the main oil refinery in Kuwait; seventeen suspects were arrested, most of them members of the Iraqi Dawa party, and the attacks were described as punishment for Kuwait's, America's and France's support of Iraq in its war with Iran. [cite:hz3_wiki_dawa] The network Shapira describes reached Saudi Arabia, Bahrain and Kuwait as well. [cite:hz3_itic_shapira] These facts show that Iran's revolutionary network sought armed allies, and found some, where there was no Israeli occupation; their stated enemies were the governments that backed Iraq. They do not show that any of those allies would have become what Hezbollah became, a political party with its own army, and the sources gathered for this book do not settle that question. The groups Iran later backed in Iraq and Yemen grew out of other wars, and Part Ten follows them. [scene:hz10_iraq|Iraq's militias] [scene:hz10_houthis|The Houthis]

The test of 2000. A second test is what happened when the occupation ended. If occupation alone explained Hezbollah, its end should have weakened the case for its arms. Instead Hezbollah kept its weapons after the Taif agreement required militias to disarm, on the ground, which the agreement's text does not state, that it was resisting Israel's occupation of the south, and kept them again after Israel's withdrawal in May 2000, citing the Shebaa Farms and Lebanese prisoners. [cite:hz4_lbc_arms] [cite:hz4a_olj_taif] [cite:hz4_merip_2000] One side reads that as evidence that resistance was the means and Iran's project the end; the other reads it as the ordinary persistence of an armed movement whose grievances were not all settled, since Lebanon still claims the farms and Israel still held Lebanese prisoners, some until an exchange in 2004 and others, among them Samir Kuntar, until July 2008; Israel says the farms are Syrian land, and the UN placed them on the Syrian side of its line. [cite:hz4_crs_shebaa] [cite:hz4_wiki_raid2000] [cite:hz5b_aj_swap] [scene:hz4_withdrawal|The withdrawal] Both readings are arguments about motive. The documented fact is the persistence.

Compared with Hamas. Part Three of the Hamas volume reached a two-part answer: the Islamist movement in Gaza predated Israel's occupation, but the armed organisation called Hamas was founded in the uprising against it. [cite:hm_pbs_baconi] [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] [scene:hm3_question|Would Hamas exist?] Hezbollah's case is similar in its first half and different in its second. Shia political mobilisation and the Najaf clerical network both predated 1982. But Hezbollah was born with a foreign state's Revolutionary Guards training it and a pledge of loyalty to that state's leader in its founding text, while Hamas grew out of a movement, the Muslim Brotherhood, rather than out of a state. [cite:hz3_wiki_hezbollah] [cite:hm_pbs_baconi] The occupation explains more of Hamas's armed turn than of Hezbollah's; Iran explains more of Hezbollah's shape than of Hamas's.

What cannot be known. No source can say whether Iran's ambassador and the Revolutionary Guards would have found a Lebanese foothold without an Israeli army in the south, whether the Najaf-trained clerics would have taken Amal over or split from it anyway, or whether a small armed Islamist party would ever have grown into the force described in later parts of this book. What the record does support is narrower. Every one of the forces in the figure above is documented; the invasion brought them together in the same place and time; and among the people with most reason to play down the occupation's part, an Israeli prime minister said it created Hezbollah and an Israeli defence minister warned in 1985 of what it was producing, while the organisation itself has described its founding both as a response to the occupation and as something the occupation did not cause. This book's reading, offered as argument, is that Hezbollah had more than one parent, and that removing any one of them changes what would have been born.
