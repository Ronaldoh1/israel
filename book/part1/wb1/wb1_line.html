The Green Line

Rhodes, 3 April 1949. Israel and Jordan signed their armistice at Rhodes. Its Article II states that no provision would prejudice the rights, claims and positions of either side in a final settlement. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text] The line it drew left Israel holding about 78 percent of the Mandate's area, as Israel's own Foreign Ministry describes it. The agreements were meant to be interim, until peace treaties replaced them and the line became known as the Green Line. [cite:rs_rd6_mfa_armistice_map]

Wadi Ara. The armistice moved the line eastward in one place. Under Article VI, Jordanian forces replaced the Iraqi army on the northern front, and the Wadi Ara area, villages in the hills of the "Triangle," passed to Israel. The agreement said their people would keep "full rights" of residence, property and freedom. [cite:rs_rd6_arm_jordan_text]

Jerusalem divided. The plan had made Jerusalem and Bethlehem an international zone. The war left Israel holding West Jerusalem and Jordan holding East Jerusalem, including the Old City, the Temple Mount and the Western Wall, and Jews were denied access to their holy places there. [cite:rs_rd6_bpb_jerusalem]

The refugees in the hills. The war's refugees did not stay inside the line that had been drawn around their homes. British officials in February 1949 estimated that 320,000 of them were in the West Bank, more than in any other single place, out of an estimated 810,000 in all; the UN later registered 726,000. [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix] The Library of Congress study of Jordan gives the West Bank's own people at about 500,000, and says about 500,000 refugees took refuge in the West Bank or across the river. [cite:rs_rd6_loc_jordan] The hill country that the plan had set aside for an Arab state now held most of what was left of Arab Palestine. Within a year it would be annexed by a neighbouring king.
