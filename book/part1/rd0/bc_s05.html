Restoration Before Zionism: The Protestant Lobby

A common assumption holds that Christian support for a Jewish state arose after 1948, as a response to the Holocaust and to Israel's founding. The record places its political origins more than a century earlier. Protestant "restorationism," the belief that a Jewish return to Palestine would fulfil biblical prophecy, was an organized political current in Britain and the United States decades before Theodor Herzl wrote a word.

In Britain its leading advocate was Anthony Ashley-Cooper, Lord Shaftesbury, the evangelical peer and reformer. He was also family to the man who ran British foreign policy: Lord Palmerston, the Foreign Secretary, was his wife's stepfather. In 1839 Shaftesbury pressed Palmerston to sponsor Jewish settlement in Palestine under British protection. In 1840 Palmerston instructed his ambassador at Constantinople to urge the Ottoman sultan to encourage Jewish settlement, and Britain's new consulate in Jerusalem was given a brief to protect Jews. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] [cite:wiki_shaftesbury] Shaftesbury's phrase for Palestine, a country without a nation for a nation without a country, predates the Zionist slogan it resembles.

The motives were mixed, and the sources are frank about it. Restorationism was theological and imperial at once. Shaftesbury and Palmerston saw a protected Jewish presence as a British foothold in Ottoman Syria. American evangelicals read the same prospect as prophecy. Most Jewish communal bodies of the period opposed the idea or ignored it. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] The project was being advanced on behalf of Jews, for reasons that had little to do with Jewish opinion.

The American counterpart came half a century later. In March 1891 the Chicago evangelist William E. Blackstone presented President Benjamin Harrison with a petition urging American support for a Jewish homeland in Palestine. Known as the Blackstone Memorial, it carried the signatures of more than four hundred prominent Americans, among them John D. Rockefeller, J. P. Morgan and the Chief Justice. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] It preceded Herzl's Der Judenstaat by five years and the first Zionist Congress at Basel by six.

The sequence these documents establish is the reverse of the one most people assume. Christian political support for a Jewish return came first; Jewish political Zionism came second. By the time a Zionist movement existed, a Christian political base for a Jewish state had already been built in Britain and the United States, at a time when most Jewish communal bodies opposed or ignored the idea. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism]

That base did not disappear. It was given a theology for the mass Protestant public in the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, which made dispensationalist support for a Jewish state a widespread doctrine. It found political expression in Balfour, himself a Christian restorationist, whose declaration of 1917 met a ready audience in Britain's governing class. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] Later organizations of Christian supporters of Israel, such as Christians United for Israel in the Hagee era, stand at the far end of a lineage that begins here, not in 1948.

The finding is a structural one. Before the Zionist Congress, and before Rothschild money built colonies in Palestine, Protestant restorationism had already made a Jewish return to Palestine a live question in British and American politics, for reasons of prophecy and empire. That constituency, not any single family, was a pre-existing structure into which the Zionist project would later connect.
