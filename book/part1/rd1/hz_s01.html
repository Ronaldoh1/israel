Herzl in Search of a Patron, 1898–1903

In the five years after Basel, Theodor Herzl went from one court to the next in search of one thing: a charter, meaning legal backing for settlement from a great power. The familiar version has the movement looking to Britain from the start. The diplomatic record does not. Herzl approached Germany, the Ottoman Empire, Britain and Russia in turn, offering each whatever it seemed to value.

He tried Germany first. Through William Hechler, a Christian restorationist, Herzl met Kaiser Wilhelm II three times in 1898: in Istanbul, at the Rothschild-funded colony of Mikveh Israel, and in Jerusalem.[cite:wiki_kaiser_levant_1898] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] The Kaiser raised the matter with the Sultan. The request failed, and the Kaiser's interest faded.

Herzl then went straight to the ruler of Palestine. On 17 May 1901 Sultan Abdul Hamid II received him. Herzl offered the help of Jewish finance with the Ottoman Empire's chronic debts in exchange for a charter. No charter was granted.[cite:avnery_herzl_sultan] [cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] The offer reveals the method: the Empire was chronically in debt, so Herzl proposed finance.

With Britain the offer changed shape. In October 1902 Herzl proposed to the Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, a Jewish settlement at El-Arish in the Sinai, presented as a buffer protecting British interests at the Suez Canal. Britain tentatively agreed. In 1903, however, Lord Cromer, the British authority in Cairo, vetoed the scheme. The Nile water needed for irrigation, he said, could not be spared.[cite:unu_water_elarish] [cite:wiki_leopold_kessler] This time the inducement was strategic rather than financial, and it foundered on a practical question of water.

The most controversial approach came in August 1903. Only months after the Kishinev pogrom, and without consulting Russia's own Zionists, Herzl twice met the Russian Interior Minister, Vyacheslav von Plehve. Plehve was widely regarded as responsible for the atmosphere that had produced Kishinev. The meetings yielded a letter in which the Tsar's government agreed to press the Sultan for a charter and to let Zionist societies operate in Russia.[cite:wiki_sixth_zionist_congress] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_herzl] Russian Zionists condemned the meetings sharply.

Weeks later, Britain's offer of territory in East Africa split the Sixth Zionist Congress, a crisis taken up in [scene:s04e|the East Africa offer].

Taken together, these episodes reveal the founding strategy of political Zionism. It was tied to no single power. It sought a charter from whichever power would grant one, and it tailored the offer to each: finance for the indebted Ottomans, a strategic buffer for Britain at Suez, emigration in the Russian case. Germany, the Ottoman Empire, Britain and Russia were all approached within five years. Britain's sponsorship in 1917 was the strategy finally succeeding, not its starting point.

On their own terms, these early efforts produced little. The Kaiser lost interest, the Sultan granted nothing, Cairo vetoed El-Arish, and the Russian letter provoked a backlash inside the movement. What lasted was the method itself, and an organization able to carry it forward after Herzl's death in 1904.
