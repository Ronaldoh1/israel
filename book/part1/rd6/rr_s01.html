The Right of Return: What Resolution 194 Says, and How Each Side Reads It

The "right of return" is read in sharply different ways. Two simple versions are common. One holds that international law guarantees every refugee's descendant a return. The other holds that the resolution behind the claim means nothing. The text itself is short, and both sides cite it. The argument is over what it obliges.

Resolution 194 (III) was adopted by the UN General Assembly on 11 December 1948. Its paragraph 11 resolved that

the refugees wishing to return to their homes and live at peace with their neighbours should be permitted to do so at the earliest practicable date

and that compensation should be paid for the property of those choosing not to return. [cite:unispal_194_survey] [cite:wiki_res_194] The General Assembly has reaffirmed the resolution every year since 1949. [cite:wiki_res_194]

The history of its reception is less straightforward than either side's present position suggests. Arab states and Palestinian representatives at first rejected the resolution, because accepting it implied acceptance of Israel. Palestinians now cite it as the foundation of the right of return.

Israel's reading was stated early. Ben-Gurion told the UN conciliators that the clause "live at peace with their neighbours" made return contingent on peace with the Arab states. [cite:unispal_194_survey] Critics of the Palestinian reading add three further points: that General Assembly resolutions are recommendations rather than binding law, that the text says refugees "should be permitted" to return rather than that they must be, and that the return of several million descendants would end Israel's Jewish majority. [cite:unwatch_194]

Supporters of the Palestinian reading answer that the right does not rest on Resolution 194 alone but is grounded in broader international law. They also point to the circumstances of Israel's own admission to the United Nations in 1949, which came with an assurance from Abba Eban that Israel would honor resolutions 181 and 194. [cite:wiki_res_194]

Both readings are documented, and this book does not resolve them. What can be said is where each one places the weight. The Israeli reading rests on the conditional clause and on the legal status of a General Assembly resolution. The Palestinian reading rests on annual reaffirmation, on wider legal principle, and on Israel's own assurance at admission. Each side quotes the same sentence and stresses a different part of it.

The practical consequence has been continuity of deadlock. The talks at [scene:lau_s01|Lausanne] in 1949 broke down on this question, with Israel offering return only within a final peace and the Arab states demanding return first. More than fifty years later, at Camp David in 2000, it remained unresolved. Every negotiation in between has run into the same paragraph. It is a reminder that the most durable disputes of 1948 were not only about land and armies, but about how a few lines of text adopted that December would be read.
