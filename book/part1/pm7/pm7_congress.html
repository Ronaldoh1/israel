The Congressional Trips

Of all the channels described in Part One, the trips are the one that reaches the most members of Congress, and the one most members have used. Since 2012 about half of the House members serving in 2024 had traveled to Israel on a trip paid for by AIPAC's charitable foundation. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] This section sets out how the trips are allowed, how many there have been, what they cost, who goes and what they see, from a study of congressional travel records by the Howard Center for Investigative Journalism, published by Capital News Service, and from reporting on the foundation's own filings.

How the trips are allowed. After the lobbying scandals around Jack Abramoff, the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act of 2007 restricted travel paid for by lobbyists and the organizations that employ them. AIPAC, which employs lobbyists, could no longer sponsor week-long trips itself. The law left an exception for charitable organizations, and according to The Intercept AIPAC campaigned for it; it has since paid for the trips through the American Israel Education Foundation, a 501(c)(3) charity it founded, which shares its offices, board members and part of its logo. Craig Holman of Public Citizen, who helped draft the 2007 law, put it plainly: "These trips would be illegal otherwise." [cite:intercept_aief_donors] AIPAC's own account is that it uses the foundation to work within the law; critics call the arrangement the "AIPAC loophole." [cite:cnsmd_trips_study]

The scale. House members and their staff reported about 4,100 privately sponsored foreign trips from 2012 to 2023. More than a quarter of them went to Israel, more than to the whole of the Western Hemisphere and Africa combined, and about three-quarters of the Israel trips were paid for by AIPAC's foundation. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] The Howard Center's database lists 868 trips sponsored by the foundation for 463 congressional offices; the offices that traveled most were those of Steny Hoyer, the long-serving Democratic leader, with 22 trips, Kevin McCarthy, the former Republican speaker, with 19, and Hakeem Jeffries, the current Democratic leader, with 10. [cite:cnsmd_aief_explorer] The Senate's travel reports, more than 2,600 in the same period, could not readily be analyzed by sponsor or destination, so the Senate's share is not known. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study]

The cost. The trips are not cheap, and they are not the largest of the foundation's expenses. By LegiStorm's count, AIPAC-related travel cost at least $10 million from 2012 to 2023. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] In 2019, the busiest recent year, donors financed 129 trips at a total cost of $2.32 million, taking 64 Democrats and 65 Republicans on fourteen separate departures; from 2019 to late 2023 the foundation spent $6.1 million on 309 trips, 144 for Republicans and 165 for Democrats. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] A single trip can cost more than $10,000 a person, and members may bring senior staff, spouses or children. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] The foundation spent $68,000 on two trips for Representative Glenn Ivey of Maryland, including luxury accommodation. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] Set against the foundation's annual revenue of about $60 million, the trips are a modest share of what it spends. [cite:pm_pp_aief990]

What they see. The itinerary of a June 2024 trip, reconstructed by the Howard Center, gives a picture of the program. The delegation spent four days in Israel and stayed at the Kempinski hotel in Tel Aviv. It met Prime Minister Netanyahu, the defense minister, Yoav Gallant, and the National Unity leader Benny Gantz, heard briefings on the attacks of 7 October, visited the site of the Nova music festival and met families of hostages. It did not visit Gaza or the West Bank, and its members met fewer Palestinians than travelers on J Street's trips. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] The foundation describes its trips as education about the US–Israel relationship that includes meetings with Israeli leaders across the political spectrum. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study] J Street, the second-largest sponsor, runs smaller trips that include Palestinian communities; the foundation's volume was nearly seven times J Street's. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study]

Who pays. The foundation's donors are redacted from the copies of its tax returns made public. One unredacted filing, for 2019, obtained by reporters, named eight donors, among them the Koret Foundation, the Charles and Lynn Schusterman Family Foundation and the Paul E. Singer Foundation. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] Because gifts to a charity are tax-deductible, critics of the arrangement argue that the public shares the cost of the trips through the donors' deductions; its defenders answer that the same is true of every charity.

What the trips do. The trips are the orientation layer of the network: the place where members meet Israel's leaders, see the country's security problems on the ground, and form relationships with the organization that later endorses them and steers its members' donations to them. The record shows the sequence; it does not show what any member took away. Supporters regard the trips as legitimate education about an ally. Critics regard them as a one-sided introduction, paid for by the same network that funds members' campaigns. Both descriptions fit the itinerary. The trips resumed about five months after 7 October 2023, and between March and early September 2024 eight of them took 71 House members and staff to Israel. [cite:cnsmd_trips_study]
