From the CIA's Venture Fund to the Army's Ten-Year Deal

Palantir Technologies is an American company, and the first thing to establish about it is whose customer it is. It was founded in 2003 by a group of investors that included Peter Thiel, and it took early seed money for its government counterintelligence work from In-Q-Tel, the venture-capital arm of the Central Intelligence Agency. [cite:ow9_ap_s1] BuzzFeed News, reporting from inside the company, put In-Q-Tel's investment at about $2 million and said the money helped the start-up shape its data-analysis software to the CIA's work. [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015] The sources used here do not agree on the year the In-Q-Tel money arrived; they agree that it came early and that it was small beside what followed.

What followed was a business built mainly on the American state. When Palantir filed to list its shares in August 2020, the Associated Press reported from the filing that nearly half of its 2019 revenue of $746 million came from government agencies, that it had lost $580 million that year, and that the US Army alone had paid it $135 million since a court ruling in September 2018. [cite:ow9_ap_s1]

The intelligence agencies. Palantir's early work with American and allied intelligence is documented mainly through the Snowden archive. In February 2017 The Intercept published documents showing that Palantir had built a tool called "XKEYSCORE Helper," which let analysts move data from the National Security Agency's XKEYSCORE system into Palantir to sort and visualise it; by about 2010, the documents indicated, at least three of the Five Eyes countries were using Palantir to process intelligence. [cite:ow9_int_xks] After the 2013 disclosures Palantir denied any link to the NSA's PRISM programme; it did not answer The Intercept's questions about XKEYSCORE. [cite:ow9_int_xks] The NSA relationship did not last. BuzzFeed reported that by the summer of 2015 it had ended, that pilot work had never become a full contract, and that the chief executive, Alex Karp, told staff the two sides had "walked away" from each other. The same report described the company's software as deeply embedded at the CIA, which had considered cancelling its contract after a 2013 magazine profile and decided Palantir would be too hard to replace. [cite:ow9_bf_karp2015]

The Pentagon. The scale of Palantir's military business grew sharply after 2024. In May 2024 the Defense Department awarded it a five-year, $480 million contract for the Maven Smart System, the Pentagon's main platform for fusing sensor and intelligence data into a picture commanders can act on; in May 2025 the ceiling was raised by $795 million, to nearly $1.3 billion through 2029. The director of the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency said then that Maven had more than 20,000 active users across the services and combatant commands. [cite:ow9_ds_maven] In August 2025 the Army went further, folding 75 separate contracts, 15 with Palantir as prime contractor and 60 as subcontractor, into a single enterprise agreement worth up to $10 billion over ten years; the Army is bound only to a minimum, which was not disclosed. [cite:ow9_bd_army] In February 2026 the Department of Homeland Security signed a five-year blanket purchase agreement worth up to $1 billion, which, the next section shows, put Palantir's platforms within reach of every major DHS component. [cite:ow9_sa_dhsbpa]

What this means for the question of this book. The record above describes a company whose money, origins and largest customers are American. That matters because a common account of Palantir, repeated in material reviewed for this volume, treats it as a conduit through which American intelligence passes to Israel, often by pointing to the company's hiring of former Israeli intelligence soldiers or to Thiel's politics. Shared employers and shared opinions are not a documented channel; the NSA's actual channel to Israel, a 2009 memorandum, is described in [scene:ow4_memorandum|Part Four]. The documents used in this part show Palantir selling to US agencies and, from January 2024, to Israel's Ministry of Defense; they do not show data belonging to an American agency being passed by Palantir to Israel. Karp himself has described the Israeli business as small: "Israel has historically bought our product, it's not a commercially relevant part of our business," he said in March 2024. [cite:ow9_fortune_karp] The Israeli contract is examined in the third section, with what is documented, what is reported and what the company denies.

The company's stated purpose for its government work is consistent across these years: that its software lets agencies analyse data they already hold lawfully, faster and in one place. In its May 2025 letter to the United Nations Special Rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territory, it put the point plainly: "we do not conduct surveillance," the customer does, using data it has lawful access to. [cite:ow9_pal_unletter] That description is accurate as far as it goes, and it is also the reason the next section begins with the data rather than the software. Where the data comes from, and what law allows an agency to hold it, decides what a platform like Palantir's can be used to do.
