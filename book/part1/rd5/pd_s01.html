Plan Dalet: The Document and the Argument

On 10 March 1948 the general staff of the Haganah issued the operational plan that would govern its conduct in the weeks before and after the British withdrawal. In Hebrew it was Tochnit Dalet; in English it is usually called Plan D or Plan Dalet. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] A long-standing popular account holds that Palestinians left their homes in 1948 of their own accord, at the urging of Arab leaders, before Jewish forces did anything to them. That account has to reckon with this document. Its text is a primary source and is not in dispute. What historians dispute, working from the same archives, is what the plan was for.

The plan did not come from nowhere. It was the fourth in a series. Walid Khalidi, the Palestinian historian whose 1961 essay set out the argument that it was a master plan, dates the earlier Plan B to September 1945 and Plan C, or Gimmel, to May 1946. Plan C, he writes, was put into practice from 1 December 1947, the first day of the fighting that followed the United Nations vote. Plan D was, in his words, "finalized on 10 March 1948," and its operational objectives were put into practice from 1 April 1948. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The dates matter. Plan D was issued nine days before the United States told the Security Council that partition could not be carried out peacefully ([scene:rd5_trusteeship|Washington's retreat]), and its operations began three weeks later.

Khalidi first made his case in a 1961 essay titled "Plan Dalet: Master Plan for the Conquest of Palestine." When the Journal of Palestine Studies republished it in its Autumn 1988 issue (volume 18, number 1), it printed the text of Plan D, dated 10 March 1948, as an appendix. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The quotations below are from that translation.

The plan opens with its purpose: "The objective of this plan is to gain control of the areas of the Hebrew state and defend its borders." Its scope reached past those borders, to "areas of Jewish settlement and concentration which are located outside the borders." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The plan's provisions on villages are the passage at the center of the argument. They applied to villages inside and near the territory to be held. Brigades were to encircle and search them, and to garrison or demolish them according to their strategic position. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] Section 4(b) sets out the alternative to holding a village:

Destruction of villages (setting fire to, blowing up, and planting mines in the debris)

Where resistance was met, the same section provides, "the population must be expelled outside the borders of the state." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] In practical terms, the text gave brigade commanders two courses for a village in or near their area: hold it if it submitted, or, if it resisted, destroy it and expel its people. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet]

The mechanism is worth stating plainly. The plan was issued from the top, by the general staff. It was carried out by brigades. And it left the judgment at the point of contact, whether a village had resisted, to the commanders on the spot. That last feature is the hinge on which the scholarly argument turns. A reader who sees the text as a design for removing a population reads the discretion as a formality. A reader who sees it as a military plan reads the same discretion as the reason outcomes differed from place to place.

Khalidi also listed the operations that, in his account, carried the plan out between 1 April and 14 May 1948. The names, dates and objectives below are as he gives them. They are his translations and his characterizations, not an Israeli official list. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988]

The list covers the weeks in which the mixed towns fell: Haifa, Jaffa, Safed and others, with the depopulation of several hundred villages. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] What happened in Tiberias and Haifa, and the separate argument over why Haifa's Arab population left, are told in [scene:rd5_cities|the fall of the mixed towns]. One discrepancy belongs here. In this list Khalidi assigns Haifa to Misparayim and the Jaffa villages to Chametz. In his separate study of Haifa he writes that the operation planned as Misparayim was renamed Chametz. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] The two accounts by the same historian do not line up, and the material gathered for this book does not resolve which name the Haifa operation finally carried.

Khalidi's own text contains a correction that is easy to miss. He wrote that "Up to 1 March not one single Arab village had been vacated by its inhabitants." A footnote amends the sentence: at least ten villages had been captured by 1 March 1948. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The correction cuts in a specific direction. It weakens the cleanest form of his chronology, in which nothing happened to villages before Plan D. It does not support the older account of a voluntary departure, since the villages in question were captured, not abandoned on instructions.

On what the plan meant, three positions stand side by side.

The first treats Plan Dalet as a master plan. Khalidi's 1961 essay and Ilan Pappé's 2006 book The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine read it as the blueprint for deliberately removing the Arab population from the territory of the Jewish state. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] In his 1988 restatement Khalidi argued that Plan D created "the military fait accompli upon which the state of Israel was to be based," and that the exodus was its product. He called the claim that Arab leaders had ordered an evacuation "a piece of mythmaking," citing the radio monitoring records of the BBC and of FBIS. And he faulted Benny Morris for treating the exodus in "an historical vacuum." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Pappé went further on the question of terms, arguing that the plan meets the United Nations definition of ethnic cleansing. [cite:pappe_ethnic_cleansing] The strongest form of this case rests on the document's own words: destruction and expulsion were written into an order issued before the Arab states invaded, and the operations Khalidi lists began within three weeks of it.

The second reading is Benny Morris's. His archival work documented the expulsions of 1948 in detail. He reads Plan Dalet as a military plan to secure territory and lines of communication before the Arab states' invasion. Its village provisions, in his account, gave local commanders discretion, which they exercised in some places and not others. Expulsions were decided at brigade level rather than ordered from the center, and the outcome was produced by war and by those local decisions, not by a single master plan. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] The strongest form of this case rests on the same text read differently: the plan states a military objective, makes expulsion conditional on resistance, and, in Morris's reading, the record of 1948 shows villages treated in different ways under the same orders.

Between these stand other historians, and a third position that this book takes as its own working rule. The text of Plan Dalet is settled, and so is the depopulation that followed. Whether the text caused the depopulation by design, or supplied the frame within which local commanders brought it about, is a genuine dispute among serious historians working from the same archives. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet]

The disagreement is narrower than it first appears, and it does not run where the popular account would place it. None of the three positions supports the claim that Palestinians left before any Jewish forces acted. Khalidi's own footnote records villages captured before March. Morris, whose reading is the less sweeping, is also the historian whose research documented the expulsions. What remains in dispute is design and cause, not whether expulsions took place. How large a part Arab leaders' orders played is a separate question, and an estimate from an Israeli army intelligence report of June 1948 is taken up in [scene:rd5_refugees|the refugee section]. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref]

Several things the record gathered here does not settle. It does not show what written instructions reached each brigade, or how each commander read the village clauses on the day. The operation list rests on Khalidi's translations and characterizations, and his two accounts of the Haifa operation's name do not agree. And the central question, whether the plan was written to produce the exodus or produced it through the choices of men in the field, remains open among historians who have read the same files. This book records the document and the readings, and leaves the reader to set them against the events that follow.
