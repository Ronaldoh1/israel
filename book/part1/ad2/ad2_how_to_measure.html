How to Measure the Dream

Most arguments about the American Dream are arguments about measurement. Two honest people can look at the same economy and report that the typical family has never been richer and that half of all children now fall short of their parents, and both can be right, because they are measuring different things. This section sets out the six choices that decide what a number about the Dream means. The rest of the book names its choice each time it gives a figure, and states the trade-off once, here.

Absolute and relative mobility

Mobility means movement between the circumstances a person is born into and the circumstances they reach as an adult. It is measured in two different ways. Absolute mobility asks whether a child ends up with a higher income than their parents had at the same age, after adjusting for inflation. It answers the most common form of the promise, "my children will do better than I did." Relative mobility asks whether a child ends up higher or lower in the national ranking than their parents were. A family at the 20th percentile, the point below which a fifth of families fall, whose child reaches the 60th percentile has moved up in relative terms, whatever happened to incomes overall.

The two measures moved differently over the past eighty years. Absolute mobility fell from about 90 per cent of children born in 1940 to about 50 per cent of those born in the 1980s. [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] Relative mobility barely moved: the chance that a child from the bottom fifth would reach the top fifth was 8.4 per cent for children born in 1971 and 9.0 per cent for those born in 1986, and the authors call the rank-based measures "extremely stable" for the 1971 to 1993 cohorts. [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] The economists who produced both findings, Raj Chetty and his colleagues, explain the difference with a ladder. The chance of climbing from one rung to another has not changed, but "the rungs of the ladder have grown further apart," so the rung a child is born on matters more than it did. [cite:ad2_chetty_trends] Absolute mobility fell mainly because growth reached the bottom and middle less than it once did: the same researchers estimate that today's economy, if its growth were shared as it was for the 1940 cohort, would restore absolute mobility to about 80 per cent, while faster growth with today's sharing would reach only about 62 per cent. [cite:ad2_oi_fading]

Income and wealth

Income is what comes in over a period, usually a year: wages, salaries, business profits, interest, rent, pensions and government cash benefits. Wealth, also called net worth, is what a family owns at a moment, its house, savings, retirement accounts, business stakes and other assets, minus what it owes. A family can have a good income and little wealth, as many young professionals with student loans do, or modest income and great wealth, as many retirees with paid-off homes do. Wealth matters to the Dream because it is what pays for a down payment, survives a lost job and passes to the next generation.

The gaps between groups are much wider in wealth than in income. In the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances for 2025, the median white non-Hispanic family had an income of $95,300 and the median Black family $49,400, a ratio of about 1.9 to 1 ($95,300 ÷ $49,400 = 1.93). The median white family's net worth was $332,100 and the median Black family's $36,800, a ratio of about 9 to 1 ($332,100 ÷ $36,800 = 9.0). [cite:ad2_scf_t1] Part Twelve examines how that difference built up. [scene:ad12_wealth_gap|The wealth gap] The Federal Reserve publishes wealth figures from two sources built in different ways, the survey, taken every three years, and its quarterly Distributional Financial Accounts; this book does not join figures from the two.

Median and mean

The mean is the ordinary average: add up everyone's income and divide by the number of people. The median is the middle value: half of families have more, half have less. When a few people have very large amounts, the mean is pulled far above what a typical family has, and the median is the better guide to the typical case. In the 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, mean family income was $145,200 and median income $82,200, so the mean was about 1.8 times the median. For net worth the gap is far wider: a mean of $1,241,500 against a median of $215,900, nearly 5.8 times ($1,241,500 ÷ $215,900 = 5.75). [cite:ad2_scf_t1] A claim that "the average family" has a million dollars is true of the mean and false of the typical family. This book reports medians for typical outcomes and names the mean wherever it uses one.

Real and nominal dollars

A nominal figure is the number of dollars as they were counted at the time. A real figure adjusts for changes in prices, so that a dollar in one year buys the same amount as a dollar in another. Comparisons across years are meaningful only in real terms. The Census Bureau reported that median household income in 2023 was $80,610 in the dollars of that year, which it restated as $82,690 in 2024 dollars after prices rose 2.6 per cent. [cite:ad2_census_ccpiu] In 2025 dollars, the median rose from $61,910 in 1984 to $87,460 in 2025, a real gain of about 41 per cent ($87,460 ÷ $61,910 = 1.41). The series is not a smooth climb: it fell after each recession, and in 2025 dollars the 2012 median, $69,070, was below the 1999 median, $73,760. [cite:ad2_fred_mhi]

Which price index

Turning nominal into real dollars requires a price index, a measure of how much a fixed basket of purchases costs from one year to the next, and the choice of index changes the answer. The best-known index, the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), fixes the weights of its spending categories for a year at a time and assumes that households do not switch between categories when prices change. The chained CPI-U (C-CPI-U), published since 2002, updates its weights every month to allow for that switching; from 2001 to 2023 it rose on average about 0.2 percentage points a year more slowly than the CPI-U, though not in every year. [cite:ad2_bls_ccpiu] The Commerce Department's Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index uses another formula and a wider scope: it covers spending "by and on behalf of" households, where the CPI covers their out-of-pocket spending. [cite:ad2_bea_cpipce] Small yearly differences compound over decades. An index that rises more slowly makes past dollars worth less in today's money, and so shows more real growth.

The choice matters most for the mobility record. Chetty and his colleagues used the CPI-U-RS, a research version of the CPI-U, and report that their decline holds under other deflators. [cite:ad2_chetty_fading] The economist Scott Winship, re-estimating the figure for children born in the early 1980s, found that adjusting for smaller family sizes raised the share who out-earned their parents from 50 to 60 per cent, that switching to the PCE index and survey data raised it to 67 per cent, and that counting government benefits raised it to 68 per cent. [cite:ad2_reeves_winship] Both sides find a fall from about 90 per cent; they disagree on how far.

This book's rule is simple. Where a source already publishes real figures, they are reported in that source's own dollars and index, and labelled. Where the book converts dollars itself, it follows the Census Bureau's practice for household income: the chained CPI-U from 2000 onward and the CPI-U-RS before 2000. [cite:ad2_census_ccpiu] Where the choice of index would change a conclusion, as in the wage and productivity record of Part Four, both readings are shown. [scene:ad4_wage_split|The wage split]

Households and individuals

A household is everyone living in one home; a family is a household of related people; an individual is one person. Household income can rise because each earner earns more, or because more members work, and it can fall because households have grown smaller even when each person is better off. American households have shrunk: one-person households were 20 per cent of all households in 1975 and 29 per cent, 39.7 million, in 2025. [cite:ad2_census_hh2025] A household median therefore understates what the same income means per person today compared with fifty years ago, and a per-person or size-adjusted measure, which divides income by a factor that grows with household size, corrects for it. Wages, by contrast, are measured per worker or per hour, and say nothing about how many earners a family needs. Part Five takes up that question. [scene:ad5_hours_two_jobs|Hours and second jobs]

Before and after taxes and benefits

A last choice cuts across all the others: whether income is counted before or after taxes and government benefits. Market income, from work and investment alone, shows what the economy pays; income after taxes and transfers shows what families can spend. The Congressional Budget Office finds that the top 1 per cent's share of income before taxes and transfers doubled from 9 per cent in 1979 to 18 per cent in 2022, and after taxes and transfers rose from 7 to 14 per cent. [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] Both figures are accurate, and they answer different questions. The book states which one each figure uses.

These choices are not tricks for reaching a desired answer; each measures something real. Absolute mobility tracks whether living standards rise across generations, relative mobility whether the starting line decides the finish, wealth whether families can weather shocks and pass on security, and the median what a typical household experiences. The book uses each where it answers the question at hand and names it there, beginning with the full mobility record in Part Three.
