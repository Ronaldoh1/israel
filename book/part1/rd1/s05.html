The Scofield Reference Bible, 1909

In 1909 Oxford University Press published a King James Bible with one man's notes printed beneath the text on every page. A common view holds that Christian support for Israel comes straight from ancient scripture, read plainly. The verses involved are ancient. The framework that links them to a modern political return of Jews to Palestine, and that made such a return a matter of prophecy for millions of American Protestants, has a more recent and more traceable origin. Its main vehicle was this annotated Bible. The research this book draws on argues that before Scofield the framework was "substantially absent" from American Protestant tradition, and that afterward it became a mainstream evangelical framework. Over the following century, on this argument, it produced a political constituency of roughly 30 to 40 million Christian Zionists, which supported the Zionist project in America before American Jewish support consolidated. [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] That is a strong claim. The record supports parts of it more firmly than others, and this section sets out which parts are which.

The ideas did not begin with Scofield. The earlier volume of this library traces [scene:bc_s05|the Christian restorationists] who came before him. The more immediate source was John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), an Anglo-Irish minister who founded the Plymouth Brethren and developed dispensationalist premillennialism in the 1830s. Darby made at least five journeys to North America between 1862 and 1877 and spread the framework among American Protestants decades before Scofield's Bible existed. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] That qualifies the claim of absence, and the record goes further: the framework was not absent from America before 1909. In 1878 the Niagara Bible Conference's creed looked for Christ's personal return "when Israel shall be restored to their own land", the first of a series of national prophetic conferences met in New York the same year, and the Bible institutes founded from the 1880s taught the new premillennialism almost without exception. [cite:ts3_ak_niagara] [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] What it lacked was a place in the mainstream. According to the research, standard nineteenth-century American Protestantism was largely amillennialist or postmillennialist, and did not treat the Church as a separate dispensation from Israel; the premillennial Bible-conference movement was a growing minority within it. Nor did Darby himself make a Jewish return a precondition of Christ's return: his rapture could come at any moment, and the restoration of Israel belonged to the events that followed it. [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999] [cite:ts3_darby_hopes10]

The man who carried Darby's framework into the American mainstream had an unusual biography for the purpose. Cyrus Scofield was born in Michigan in 1843 and was staying with relatives in Tennessee when the Civil War began. He enlisted in the Confederate Army in 1861 and was discharged in September 1862 as a Michigan-born alien; ordered back to camp, he crossed to Union lines and took the federal oath. In 1873 President Ulysses S. Grant appointed him United States Attorney for Kansas. He held the post for about six months and, in the Justice Department's words, "was forced to resign" over "questionable financial transactions". In October 1878 he was jailed in St. Louis on forgery charges and held awaiting trial for much of a year; on 6 November 1879 the prosecution dropped the charges by nolle prosequi, and he was never convicted or sentenced. In these years he abandoned his first wife and their daughters, and she divorced him for desertion in 1883; it was his only divorce. He began pastoral work in Dallas in 1882 and was ordained a Congregationalist minister in October 1883, without formal seminary training. [cite:ts4_rushing] [cite:ts4_doj_ks] [cite:ts4_wiki_scofield] He was not a scholar of Hebrew or Greek. Between 1901 and 1907 he wrote the annotations. He died in 1921, and the book stayed in print after him. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] The full record, and the rumours that have grown around it, are in [scene:ts4_record|The Theology Shield: Scofield's record].

The most cited critical biography is Joseph M. Canfield's The Incredible Scofield and His Book (1988), a hostile account; the legal record above rests on later work with court and newspaper sources, which shows no conviction. [cite:ts4_rushing] Canfield is also the origin of a claim that the research this book draws on repeats: that Samuel Untermyer, a New York lawyer who later became a prominent Zionist, stood behind Scofield. It is not a documented association. Scofield and Untermyer were both members of New York's Lotos Club, but Scofield's proposers were other men, no record shows the two ever met, and Canfield introduced Untermyer with the words "we assume". [cite:ts4_sfiles] The backers Scofield's contemporaries named were businessmen, among them Alwyn Ball Jr. and John T. Pirie, and a 2025 fact-check rated the later claim that the Rothschilds funded the Bible false. [cite:ts4_cyf] The story's shape, Jewish money steering Christian belief, is an old antisemitic one, and it is examined, with its chain of sources, in [scene:ts4_rumours|The Theology Shield: the rumours tested]. Even the documented backers are no evidence of who caused the book's success or wrote its theology. The theology came largely from Darby, and Scofield's work would have found some readers in any case. The research's own account of what produced the book's scale points elsewhere: Oxford's distribution, and what it calls the funding architecture that put the Bible "into every seminary, Sunday school, and pulpit in the American South and Midwest." The material does not say how much the backers gave or how their support was arranged. The biographical record is also no argument against the theology. Scofield's personal history and the framework's later influence are separate facts, each to be checked on its own terms.

The book's form did as much work as its content. Each page carried the King James Version, the standard English Protestant translation of 1611, at the top. Scofield's notes filled a substantial part of the bottom. A reader did not open a Bible and then consult a separate commentary. The commentary was already in place on the page, printed under Oxford's imprint and bound in the same covers as the text. According to the research, the notes did three things. First, they divided biblical history into seven "dispensations," periods in which God deals with humanity in different ways. Second, they introduced a pre-tribulation rapture, the belief that the faithful will be taken up before a time of catastrophe. The research calls this a modern doctrine that is not found in the biblical text. It dates its origin once to the eighteenth century and elsewhere to Darby in the 1830s. Third, on the research's account, they presented the political return of Jews to Palestine as a prerequisite for the Second Coming of Christ. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] The notes themselves do not put it that way. Scofield's note on Deuteronomy 30:3 sets out Israel's future in order, and "The return of the Lord" comes before "Restoration to the land" and "National conversion". [cite:ts5_sac_deut30] What the notes did was write a future national restoration to the land into the prophetic scheme that ordinary readers met on the page. Scofield had systematized Darby's framework and put it into the vocabulary of American Protestants. The decisive step was placing it in the margins of the Bible ordinary readers already used.

The research describes the effect of that placement as cumulative. Over years of daily reading, text and note merged in the reader's memory, until Scofield's reading became simply "what the Bible says." A claim is hard to examine if the reader never registers it as a separate claim. The label on the book stayed the same, the Bible, while what the reader received under that label now included one man's interpretation. The Reference Bible sold millions of copies through the twentieth century. On the research's account, a mid-century American evangelical who studied the Bible seriously was likely to meet Scofield's notes as the frame for reading it. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] A revised edition followed in 1917. It is often said to have added references to the Balfour Declaration, issued that November, but no source for this has been found, and the 1917 notes checked for this library contain no such reference; they still describe Israel as "now in the third dispersion". [cite:ts5_wiki_srb] [cite:ts1_sac_gen15]

Oxford's part is the step most easily misread. Oxford University Press is one of the two oldest and most prestigious academic publishers in the English-speaking world; Cambridge University Press is the other. [cite:wiki_oxford_university_press] Its name gave a marginal framework an authority it could not have earned on its content alone. A self-published set of the same notes would probably have reached a small readership. Under Oxford's imprint, bound into the scriptural text, it reached millions. The research calls this a transfer of legitimacy, not a conspiracy, and the Press's governance supports that reading. Oxford University Press has never been privately owned. It is a department of the University of Oxford. Since the seventeenth century it has been governed by the Delegates of the Press, 15 sitting academics appointed by the university's Vice-Chancellor, who met fortnightly to approve every title. There was no owner or publishing family whose interests could explain the decision, and no single person positioned to ask why a new theological framework should be bound into scripture. The book went through the same committee process as any other title. Because the underlying text was technically just the King James Version with added footnotes, no special theological review was triggered. [cite:wiki_oxford_university_press] The record shows a gap in review. It does not show that anyone used that gap deliberately, and it does not show any promotional strategy by the Press. The Press did not set out to shape American policy in the Middle East, but its imprint gave the notes an authority its editors had not intended to confer. The Press has never withdrawn the book. Later editions, the New Scofield of 1967 and the Scofield Study Bible III of 2002, were revised but kept the core dispensationalist framework. The research counts 117 years of continuous publication.

The research then sets out what the doctrines meant politically, as later Christian Zionist teaching drew them out. Two of the verses are ancient. Genesis 12:3 reads "I will bless those who bless you, and whoever curses you I will curse," and Genesis 15:18 gives the land promise. The research's argument concerns how Scofield's framework applied them to a modern state.

On the research's reading, none of these doctrines had been part of mainstream American Protestant theology before Scofield placed them there. The research summarizes the change this way: the framework was standard by 1950 and taken for granted by 2000. It also argues that the framework leaves no theological role for the people already living in Palestine. Palestinian Christians, members of a community older than most European churches, become an anomaly. Palestinian Muslims become obstacles to the fulfillment of prophecy. These are arguments about the logic of a doctrine, and they should be read as arguments. They are not a census of what each believer holds.

The research also makes a theological critique, and it is presented here as one side of a dispute. Evangelical Protestantism teaches salvation through Christ alone. Scofield's scheme runs a separate track for national Israel, in which an ethnic nation and a literal grant of land carry weight of their own in the history of salvation, alongside the work of Christ. The sequence the research reads in the notes runs as follows. Israel is dispersed. Israel is regathered, in unbelief. The regathered nation passes through the Tribulation. Only then does Israel turn to Christ. The Second Coming follows, Christ establishes a millennial kingdom, and Israel stands at its center. Scofield's own summary at Deuteronomy 30:3 orders the end differently, placing the Lord's return before the restoration to the land and the national conversion. [cite:ts5_sac_deut30] The critique fixes on the fourth step. In a tradition of salvation by grace, it asks, why must a people be regathered in unbelief and pass through catastrophe before conversion? On this reading, the notes never reconcile the two tracks; they simply print them on the same page. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] [scene:s04fa|The Uganda counterfactual] tests the same sequence against a different geography. The material does not give the replies of dispensationalist theologians to this critique. A reader should treat it as a contested theological argument, not a settled finding.

A separate passage is not history at all, and is labeled here as interpretation. One symbolic reading, put forward in this library's opening volume, holds that the confusion dissolves if "Israel" is read not as land but as IS-RA-EL, a "consciousness architecture." In that reading IS stands for Isis, the receptive or lunar principle, RA for the solar or radiant principle, and EL for the unifying divine force. Of the three, only EL is real Hebrew etymology. On this reading "Israel" names a spiritual condition rather than a nation, and Scofield's notes performed a literalizing move, turning a symbol into a flag, a border and an ethnic deed. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] This is offered as a key for thinking, not a documented claim. The documentary case stands without it.

What followed belongs to later volumes and is summarized here only in outline. Dallas Theological Seminary, founded in 1924, became an institutional home for training pastors in the framework. Between the 1920s and the 1960s, according to the research, Scofield's theology became standard in Southern Baptist and other evangelical denominations and in Bible colleges of the South and Midwest. Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth (1970), the best-selling nonfiction book of the 1970s, carried it to a mass readership. In 1979 Jerry Falwell founded the Moral Majority. Tim LaHaye and Jerry Jenkins's Left Behind novels, published from 1995 to 2007, sold more than 65 million copies. In 2006 John Hagee, pastor of Cornerstone Church in San Antonio, published Jerusalem Countdown and founded Christians United for Israel (CUFI). [cite:hagee_jerusalem_countdown] CUFI claims more than 10 million members, a self-reported figure. It holds an annual Washington Summit. [cite:cufi_org] Membership claims of this kind cover a wide range of engagement and do not mean that every member holds the same views. The alliance between these organizations and Israeli leaders is told in [scene:bwm_src_s15|the Christian Zionist network].

The size of the constituency rests on survey figures, and they should be stated exactly. The research cites "Pew 2019" figures (42 percent of white evangelicals against 27 percent of American Jews, and 75 to 80 percent of white evangelicals backing Israel as a biblical requirement) for which no 2019 survey has been traced; they are not used here. The documented figures are these. In a Pew Research Center survey published on 3 October 2013, 82 percent of white evangelical Protestants said God gave the land of Israel to the Jewish people, against 40 percent of American Jews. [cite:ts1_pew_2013] In a LifeWay Research poll of 2,002 Americans with evangelical beliefs in September 2017, 80 percent said Israel's founding in 1948 fulfilled biblical prophecy, but only 23 percent favoured a treaty giving the Palestinians a state, 31 percent opposed one and 46 percent were unsure. [cite:gi11_ct_lifeway] In Pew's survey of May 2026, white evangelical Protestants were the only religious group in which a majority, 57 percent, held a favourable view of the Israeli government. [cite:ts11_pew_2026] The figure of 30 to 40 million Christian Zionist voters is an estimate in the research, not derived from any of these surveys. [cite:wiki_dispensation_theology] It is not a count, and the range is wide.

Two limits keep the account honest. First, nothing in it was hidden. Scofield published openly, Oxford's decisions were public, and Christian Zionist organizations argue their positions in public. To read the chain as a conspiracy would be a category error. Scofield published in 1909, the Balfour Declaration came in 1917, and the state was declared in 1948. He was one factor among many, and nothing here claims that he or Oxford brought the State of Israel about. The research does use the word "manufactured" for the constituency. What the documents support is narrower: a publishing event put a specific framework into wide circulation, and institutions carried it forward for decades.

Second, this is a dispute within Christianity, not a case against Christianity. Mainline Protestant denominations, including the United Methodist, Episcopal, Presbyterian (PC-USA), Evangelical Lutheran and United Church of Christ churches, largely do not embrace Christian Zionism. Roman Catholic tradition does not endorse it. The research describes Orthodox tradition as rejecting it explicitly in one passage, and speaks of "most Orthodox tradition" in another. In 2006 the heads of four Jerusalem churches signed the Jerusalem Declaration on Christian Zionism, which calls it a "false teaching that corrupts the biblical message of love, justice, and reconciliation", and in 2009 Palestinian Christian leaders from across denominations issued the Kairos Palestine document. [cite:ts1_wiki_jerdecl] The research names the Palestinian Christian Sami Awad, the mainline Protestant scholar Gary Burge and the evangelical Shane Claiborne as public figures whose positions show that the framework can be contested inside the tradition. They show that holding it is not required of Christians. The Christians who reject Scofield's framework do so on grounds within their own traditions.

The record establishes the book, its publisher, its author's history, its notes and their place on the page, and the later institutions that carried the framework. It leaves open how far the Reference Bible itself, as distinct from Darby's earlier preaching, the Bible colleges and later popular writers, caused American Christian support for Zionism. It does not show the size or terms of the money behind the annotations. It also does not show what Oxford's Delegates discussed when they approved the book. The constituency figures are survey estimates. The research's account of later American policy, which it partly traces to this constituency, is examined in later volumes against the full record of who decided what.
