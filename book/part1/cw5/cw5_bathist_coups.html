Baghdad and Damascus, 1963: Two Coups and a Unity Charter

In the spring of 1963 Israel's Prime Minister answered an American request about the Dimona reactor with a letter about something else: a new Arab federation. To understand why, the reader needs the events of the preceding three months in Baghdad and Damascus. In February and March 1963 officers and party members of the Ba'ath Party seized power first in Iraq and then in Syria. In April the two new governments and Nasser's Egypt announced in Cairo that they would unite. By late July the union was dead, broken by a failed coup in Damascus and by Nasser's public denunciation of the Syrian regime. This section tells that sequence, naming the parties and officers concerned, and sets out the dispute over whether the United States had a hand in the first of the two coups.

Iraq, 8–9 February 1963. On 8 February 1963 the Iraqi Ba'ath Party overthrew the Prime Minister, Abd al-Karim Qasim, after about two days of fighting. Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr became prime minister and Abdul Salam Arif president, while the party's secretary-general, Ali Salih al-Sa'di, controlled the National Guard, the party's militia. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] Qasim was executed on 9 February 1963 after a summary proceeding broadcast on Baghdad radio. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] Qasim had been one of the six Arab heads of state to whom Kennedy wrote on 11 May 1961, in the letter told in [scene:cw5_nasser_outreach|Kennedy and Nasser]. [cite:rs_cw5_jvl_jfk_arab_letter]

How many people died in the coup and its aftermath is uncertain, and the estimates differ widely. Ariel Ahram estimates that about 80 Ba'athists and "between 300 and 5,000" communist sympathizers were killed in the fighting. Bryan Gibson cites 1,500 to 5,000 suspected communists killed by the Ba'ath militia. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] [cite:rs_cw5_gibson_majalla] The two ranges overlap at their upper end but differ fivefold at their lower. They also count somewhat different things: Ahram the dead of the fighting, Gibson those killed by the militia. No single figure can be given. The militia in Gibson's count was the National Guard under al-Sa'di, which grew to 34,000 by August 1963. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan]

The Ba'ath did not keep Iraq long. About nine months later, in November 1963, Arif, the president it had installed, ended Ba'athist rule in a counter-coup. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan]

Was the United States behind the Iraqi coup? This is the most disputed question in the episode, and scholars who have worked on it reach opposite conclusions. Both cases deserve to be set out in their strongest form.

The case for an American role is made by several historians. Wolfe-Hunnicutt calls American involvement "an open secret." Jacobsen writes that there is "ample evidence" of it. Citino is more guarded: he concludes that the United States "at best ... condoned and at worst ... contributed" to the coup, and that the full extent of its role "cannot be fully established." [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] Those who argue for a role also point to two statements by participants or neighbours. Al-Sa'di, the party secretary-general who commanded the National Guard, is reported to have said that the Ba'athists came to power on a "CIA train." And King Hussein of Jordan made a claim in 1963 about CIA lists. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] On this reading, an American government whose intelligence service was working against Qasim helped bring him down, and figures in the region said as much.

The case against a direct role is made by other scholars, from the declassified American record. Gibson, writing in Al Majalla in 2012, concludes on "the preponderance of evidence" that the CIA, though it had been plotting against Qasim, "was not behind" the coup. Hahn finds that the declassified documents "offer no evidence" of direct American involvement, and Barrett and West reach the same view. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] [cite:rs_cw5_gibson_majalla] Former CIA officials describe their role as watching the plotters, not supporting them. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_ramadan] On this reading, American officials knew a great deal about the plot, but knowing of a coup is not the same as organizing it, and the documents show only the first.

A single document sits at the centre of the dispute. Robert Komer wrote a memorandum to Kennedy about the coup; Wikipedia's citation dates it 8 February 1963, while Gibson describes it as written "the day after the coup." Gibson reads it as showing that the CIA had "excellent reporting on the plotting" without having arranged it. Other scholars cite the same memorandum as evidence of complicity. [cite:rs_cw5_gibson_majalla] The disagreement is therefore not only over what evidence exists but over what the same sentence means: whether excellent reporting on a plot is the mark of an agency with sources inside it, or of one that had helped to make it.

This book does not resolve the question, because the sources reached for it do not. Two points can be stated plainly. First, Gibson, who rejects a direct role, accepts that the CIA had been plotting against Qasim. Second, even the most cautious of those who argue for a role, Citino, says that its extent cannot be fully established. The open ground between those two positions is what remains in dispute.

Syria, 8 March 1963. A month after Baghdad, Damascus followed. On 8 March 1963 the Ba'athist Military Committee and allied officers seized power in Syria, with little violence, and declared a state of emergency. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] That emergency was not lifted until 2011. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] A National Council for the Revolutionary Command was formed to hold power, though the sources date it differently: Wikipedia's account gives 9 March, while the Library of Congress country study dates to 24 March the provisional constitution that confirmed the Council's power. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria] The two dates need not conflict, since one may date the Council's formation and the other the confirmation of its authority, but the sources reached here do not say so, and both are given.

The Cairo agreement, 17 April 1963. With Ba'athist governments in Baghdad and Damascus, the three states announced in Cairo on 17 April 1963 an agreement for a federal union of Egypt, Syria and Iraq. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria] The proclamation of that day called for a military union to bring about "the liberation of Palestine." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Ben-Gurion took it up. His letter to Kennedy of 26 April 1963 did not answer the American request on Dimona; it raised the Arab Federation instead, and proposed a joint US–Soviet guarantee of the territorial integrity of the states of the region. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That exchange is told in [scene:s11b|the battle of the letters].

The union unravels, April–July 1963. The federation began to come apart within days of its announcement, and it came apart in Damascus. Between 28 April and 2 May the Syrian Military Committee purged more than 50 Nasserist officers, and Nasserist ministers resigned from the government. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria] The Ba'athist officers who held power in Syria were removing the officers loyal to the leader with whom they had just agreed to unite.

The Nasserists answered by force. On 18 July 1963 Colonel Jassem Alwan led a Nasserist coup attempt in Damascus. Amin al-Hafiz crushed it. Twenty-seven conspirators were executed. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_july1963] [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria] Estimates of the casualties range from 40–70 to "hundreds," and the sources reached here do not settle the number. [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_july1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria] Four days later, on 22 July 1963, Nasser announced that he was abandoning federation with Syria and called the Ba'athist regime "fascists." The Library of Congress study says that the union effort had "collapsed by July 22." [cite:rs_cw5_wiki_syria_july1963] [cite:rs_cw5_countrystudy_syria]

What the sequence meant. The proclamation of 17 April and its collapse by 22 July frame the spring of 1963 from two sides. In Israel the proclamation was the document Ben-Gurion set before Kennedy. In Damascus the same union was undermined within days by the purge of Nasser's officers, and Nasser's own break in July ended it as a working project. Both facts are true at once: the union was proclaimed in terms that called for "the liberation of Palestine," and the three governments that proclaimed it could not hold it together for a hundred days. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] Which of the two weighed more heavily on Israel's security that year is a judgment the sources reached here do not make.

What the record leaves open. The number of people killed in Iraq in February 1963 is known only as a range, from Ahram's 300 to Gibson's 5,000 among communists and suspected communists, besides about 80 Ba'athists. Whether the United States helped to bring about the Iraqi coup remains disputed among named scholars, and the declassified record reached here does not decide it. The date of the National Council for the Revolutionary Command differs between two sources. The casualties of the July coup attempt in Damascus run from 40–70 to "hundreds." The rule of the Ba'ath in Syria did not end with the failure of the union; what followed for the regime of Amin al-Hafiz and the officers around him is told in [scene:cw6_syria|Stage Six].
