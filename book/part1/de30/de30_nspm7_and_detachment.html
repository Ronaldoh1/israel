NSPM-7 and Detachment 201

Two documents of 2025 complete the legal architecture this book has followed. The first, a presidential memorandum signed in September, tells federal investigators to pursue domestic political violence and the people and money behind it, and describes the ideas it calls warning signs. The second is a military appointment: four senior technology executives sworn in as Army officers in June. Neither is a surveillance tool in itself. One sets who the tools are pointed at; the other places the builders of the tools inside the force that buys them. This section sets out what each says and what has been documented under each, and then tests the book's worst-case reading against Book Five as a whole.

What NSPM-7 says

A National Security Presidential Memorandum (NSPM) is a directive from the president to the agencies, published but not passed by Congress. NSPM-7, "Countering Domestic Terrorism and Organized Political Violence," was signed on 25 September 2025. It cites the assassination of Charlie Kirk that month, alongside the killing of a health-insurance executive in 2024, the 2022 attempt on Justice Kavanaugh and attacks on ICE officers. Section 1 names the "common threads animating this violent conduct": "anti-Americanism, anti-capitalism, and anti-Christianity," "support for the overthrow of the United States Government," and "extremism on migration, race, and gender." [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] Section 2 directs the FBI's Joint Terrorism Task Forces, which pair federal agents with local police, to investigate "institutional and individual funders, and officers and employees of organizations"; the Treasury to "identify and disrupt financial networks" and guide banks on filing Suspicious Activity Reports, the confidential reports banks send to the Treasury; and the IRS to ensure that no tax-exempt body is "directly or indirectly financing political violence or domestic terrorism." Section 3 lets the Attorney General recommend groups for designation as a "domestic terrorist organization," citing the definition of domestic terrorism in federal criminal law, 18 U.S.C. 2331(5). The memorandum does not mention the First Amendment; it says it "shall be implemented consistent with applicable law." [cite:de30_wh_nspm7]

No federal statute provides for designating a domestic group as terrorist. [cite:de30_dla_nspm7] The criminal code defines domestic terrorism but attaches no list to it, unlike the foreign-terrorist list kept by the State Department, membership of or support for which carries criminal penalties and frozen assets. A law firm's analysis for its clients concluded that NSPM-7 "creates no new legal authority" and that a domestic label carries reputational and practical, not formal legal, consequences. [cite:de30_dla_nspm7] Three days before the memorandum, on 22 September, the president had signed an order stating, "I hereby designate Antifa as a 'domestic terrorist organization,'" calling it "a militarist, anarchist enterprise" and directing agencies to "investigate, disrupt, and dismantle" its operations and their funders; the order cites no statute. [cite:de30_govinfo_antifa] On 13 November the State Department used the foreign-list power it does have to designate four European groups, Antifa Ost in Germany, an Italian anarchist federation and two Greek groups. [cite:de30_state_fto]

The Attorney General's implementing memorandum of 4 December 2025, addressed to all federal prosecutors, made the program operational. It ordered the FBI to "compile a list of groups or entities engaged in acts that may constitute domestic terrorism," updated every 30 days; set up a cash reward system and the recruitment of cooperators; reached back five years; gave agencies 14 days to send their "Antifa and Antifa-related intelligence" to the task forces; and told prosecutors to consider tax crimes. Its list of indicators includes "opposition to law and immigration enforcement," "radical gender ideology," and "anti-Christianity." A footnote states: "No investigation may be opened based solely on activities protected by the First Amendment." [cite:de30_bondi_memo]

What has been done under it

The documented prosecutions concern violence. On 4 July 2025 a police officer was shot, and survived, outside the Prairieland ICE detention centre in Alvarado, Texas. Prosecutors called the defendants the "North Texas Antifa Cell," and by April 2026 a federal jury in Fort Worth had convicted eight of nine of providing material support to terrorists, built on attempted murder of a federal officer and destruction of government property. The prosecutor told jurors the defendants were on trial for their conduct, not their beliefs; the defence argued antifa is not an organisation, and the prosecution did not have to prove that it was. [cite:de30_kera_prairieland]

The use against organisations is reported, not adjudicated. On 2 September 2026 Senators Ron Wyden and Raphael Warnock wrote to the Treasury citing press reports that officials were reviewing the tax-exempt status of the Open Society Foundations, the Southern Poverty Law Center and the Council on American-Islamic Relations under a "blueprint," and that IRS criminal investigators were working with the FBI on nonprofits; the letter asked whether any group had been recommended for examination or revocation and reports none completed. [cite:de30_wyden_letter] An amicus brief filed on 25 August 2026 for FIRE, the Society for the Rule of Law and the libertarian Cato Institute argues that DHS has used NSPM-7 to infiltrate meetings, monitor encrypted chats and seize years of financial records from unions and nonprofits, and that the memorandum "treats left-leaning political viewpoints as indicia of domestic terrorism." These are the brief's allegations; no court has ruled on them. [cite:de30_pd_amicus] Ideological deportation cases and the unmasking subpoenas are in [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Twenty-Eight]; the best-known earlier federal programme aimed at domestic political groups, the FBI's COINTELPRO, which ended in April 1971, is in [scene:de29_the_precedent|Part Twenty-Nine]. [cite:de29_wiki_cointelpro]

Detachment 201

On 13 June 2025 the Army swore in four executives as lieutenant colonels in the Army Reserve: Shyam Sankar, chief technology officer of Palantir; Andrew Bosworth, chief technology officer of Meta; Kevin Weil, chief product officer of OpenAI; and Bob McGrew, an adviser at Thinking Machines Lab and formerly OpenAI's chief research officer. As "Detachment 201" they serve part-time as senior advisers on technology for the Army's reorganisation. [cite:de30_army_det201] An Army spokesman said in June 2025 that "It's not in our interest to show any favoritism to a company" and "They're not making acquisition decisions," and that the conflict rules were those for any reservist. [cite:de30_d1_det201] A year later, when three more were commissioned on 10 June 2026, Dane Knecht of Cloudflare, Sam Pullara of Sutter Hill Ventures and Serkan Piantino, the Army described stricter terms: "mandatory" recusal under the Joint Ethics Regulation, confidential financial disclosure, legal review of each task and a legal bar from vendor selection. It credited the first cohort with advice on munitions supply chains, counter-drone work and autonomy. [cite:de30_ds_det201]

Sankar's company has been an Army software contractor since it won a contract worth up to $876 million in 2019, and its later awards are set out in [scene:de27_money_and_war|Part Twenty-Seven]. [cite:de10_defnews] That is a documented conflict of position, which the Army's 2026 rules exist to manage. No document shows any member steering a contract; that claim is not supported on the current record.

The worst-case reading, tested

The hypothesis is the Sophia platform's, not a finding: immigration enforcement as the occasion, a sovereign executive without a veto as the destination. Across Book Five the capability is documented. Phones can be followed through commercial location data bought without a warrant, as with ICE's Webloc licence, which an internal ICE analysis said could be queried without a warrant ([scene:de25_location_and_mobile|Part Twenty-Five], [scene:de28_brokers_and_doctrine|Part Twenty-Eight]). [cite:de25_404_webloc] "Anonymous" data can be traced back to the person ([scene:de24_the_myth_of_anonymous_data|Part Twenty-Four]). ICE buys an integration system, ImmigrationOS, from Palantir, whose own account is that it processes customers' data rather than collecting it ([scene:de27_capability_not_intent|Part Twenty-Seven]). [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:de19_palantir_record] The walls between civil records and enforcement were breached in 2025–26, and a federal directive now names ideas, including opposition to immigration enforcement, as indicators of domestic terrorism. [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep] [cite:de30_bondi_memo] Immigration was the stated occasion for nearly every data transfer, and at least one tool built for immigrants, SAVE, was turned on citizens' voter rolls. [cite:de19_npr_save]

Probable: that the indicator lists steer investigative attention toward critics of immigration policy and the left, because the texts name them and the reported reviews of nonprofits follow them; but this rests on text and reports, not on a finding about any person. [cite:de30_wh_nspm7] [cite:de30_wyden_letter] Not supported: that the toolkit has been used as a programme against political opponents; no court has found it, no nonprofit has lost its status, the documented terrorism convictions follow a shooting, and capability is not intent. Not supported either: that any of it came from the Dark Enlightenment's texts; the line from RAGE to the new civil-service schedule is rhyme, not provenance ([scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|the first section]).
