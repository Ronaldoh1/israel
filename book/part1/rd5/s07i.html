UNSCOP and the Empty Chair

The plan that the General Assembly voted on in November 1947 began as the report of an eleven-nation committee that spent the summer of 1947 between Lake Success, Jerusalem, Beirut, Amman, Geneva and the displaced-persons camps of Munich, Salzburg, Vienna, Berlin, Hamburg and Hanover. The United Nations Special Committee on Palestine, known as UNSCOP, is usually remembered as a neutral fact-finding body that heard both sides equally and reached its own conclusion. Its own report tells a more specific story. The committee was genuinely international, and it did reach its conclusions by its own votes. But it did not weigh two prepared Palestinian cases against each other. One party, the Jewish Agency, came with a map, witnesses and a proposed itinerary. The other, the Arab Higher Committee, chose to stay away. The Arab case was put to the committee instead by the governments of six Arab states and by Transjordan's king, outside Palestine.

The committee was Britain's doing. On 2 April 1947 Sir Alexander Cadogan, for the United Kingdom, asked the United Nations to put Palestine on the agenda of the next regular Assembly and to summon a special session as soon as possible to constitute a special committee [cite:rs_rd5_un_a286]. (How London reached that point is told in [scene:rd5_britain_exits|Britain hands Palestine to the United Nations].) The special session convened at Flushing Meadow on 28 April 1947. On 15 May it adopted Resolution 106 (S-1), which created the Special Committee on Palestine with "the widest powers to ascertain and record facts" and told it to report by 1 September 1947 [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop] [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report].

Who would sit on it was itself contested. Australia proposed eleven members, none of them permanent members of the Security Council. The Assembly adopted that proposal by 13 votes to 11, with 29 abstentions [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report]. The members chosen were Australia, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Guatemala, India, Iran, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden, Uruguay and Yugoslavia [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:unscop_ecf]. On its face this was a multi-continent body, not a proxy for Washington or London. It held its first meeting at Lake Success on 26 May 1947, and on 2 June elected Justice Emil Sandström of Sweden as chairman and Alberto Ulloa of Peru as vice-chairman [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop]. Its members arrived in Palestine on 14 and 15 June and first met in Jerusalem on 16 June [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report].

The day before the members landed, the Arab Higher Committee had informed the Secretary-General that it would abstain from cooperating with the committee. On 10 July 1947 its vice-chairman, Jamal Husseini, wrote again to say that it would not reverse that decision [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report]. The boycott was a deliberate political choice with stated reasons. The Arab Higher Committee regarded the whole framework as illegitimate: in its view any United Nations body that set out to decide Palestine's future without first affirming the self-determination rights of the country's existing majority was biased before it began [cite:unscop_ecf]. The Jewish Agency took the opposite course. It engaged fully and supplied detailed logistical help, including its own proposed itinerary for the committee's visits around the country [cite:unscop_ecf].

The consequence inside Palestine was a hearing room in which only one of the two Palestinian communities' organized leaderships made a formal case. For the Jewish Agency the committee heard Moshe Shertok and David Horowitz, and it heard Chaim Weizmann in a personal capacity [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report]. David Ben-Gurion, testifying as head of the Jewish Agency, told the committee plainly that an independent Jewish state was the only acceptable outcome. The map presented on the Zionist side was substantially the Peel Commission's map of 1937, with the Galilee, the Negev and West Jerusalem added [cite:unscop_testimony]. One historical account describes the Agency's presentation as forthright and exhaustive, overwhelming the committee "with data, logic, and cogent analyses of every central and tangential issue" [cite:unscop_testimony]. That account is a judgment about the presentation's weight, and it is worth reading carefully. The imbalance it describes did not come from the Arab argument being weak. It came from the Arab Higher Committee's argument never being formally put before the committee in Palestine at all. Nor was the Agency improvising. By 1946 partition had already become its avowed policy, and in August 1946 it had drawn up its own partition proposal, more than a year before UNSCOP existed [cite:unscop_testimony] [cite:jewishagency_1946plan]. That earlier preparation is the subject of [scene:s07h|the Jewish Agency's 1946 proposal].

It is here that the familiar charge against UNSCOP, and the committee's own record, have to be read side by side. The charge, as it is often put, is that the committee heard testimony "almost exclusively" from the Zionist side [cite:unscop_ecf]. The committee's report shows something narrower and more exact. No Palestinian Arab body testified, because the Arab Higher Committee boycotted the proceedings [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report]. But the Arab states did put their case. After an informal visit to Damascus on 21 July 1947, the committee heard the governments of Egypt, Iraq, Lebanon, Saudi Arabia, Syria and Yemen in Beirut on 22 July, with the Lebanese Foreign Minister, Hamid Frangié, among them. Transjordan declined to send a representative outside the country, so on 25 July the chairman and seven members went to Amman to see King Abdullah [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop]. The committee also travelled through the region and could observe conditions directly; it did not hear literally nothing of Arab objections [cite:unscop_ecf].

Both readings therefore hold, each within its limits. "One side only" is accurate for the Palestinian parties: the Jewish Agency was heard at length and the Arab Higher Committee by its own decision not at all. It is not accurate for the Arab case as a whole, which six governments presented in Beirut and a seventh, Transjordan's king, gave in Amman. What the record establishes is that no prepared Palestinian Arab case, with its own witnesses and its own maps, stood in the committee's record beside the Agency's. That is a more specific claim than "the process was rigged", and a more defensible one.

The committee also looked beyond Palestine. After a vote of six to four, with one abstention, a subcommittee visited displaced-persons camps in Munich, Salzburg, Vienna, Berlin, Hamburg and Hanover between 8 and 14 August 1947 [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop]. The vote on whether to go at all was close, and the record used here does not say which members voted which way or why.

The drafting began in Geneva on 28 July. On 29 August the committee approved eleven recommendations unanimously and a twelfth with two dissents, and it signed its report on 31 August 1947; the report's cover carries the date 3 September 1947 [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop]. On the central question, the committee split. A majority of seven, Canada, Czechoslovakia, Guatemala, the Netherlands, Peru, Sweden and Uruguay, recommended partition into a Jewish state and an Arab state joined in an economic union, with an international regime for Jerusalem. A minority of three, India, Iran and Yugoslavia, recommended a single federal state with Jerusalem as its capital. Australia abstained on both votes, which were recorded as 7 to 3 and 3 to 7 [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report] [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_unscop] [cite:unscop_wiki].

The precise count matters, because it is sometimes given wrongly. The Center for Israel Education states that eight of the eleven members endorsed partition, and that the Jewish Agency accepted the plan while the Arab Higher Committee rejected it [cite:rs_rd5_cie_unscop]. The committee's own report records seven, with Australia abstaining [cite:rs_rd5_unscop_report]. The report is the primary document, and on this point the summary is in error. The split also corrects a second simplification: the recommendation behind the partition vote was not a unanimous international finding but a majority view, with three members preferring a single federal state.

The majority plan became the working text. The Assembly's Ad Hoc Committee revised it in the autumn, and the revised plan went to the General Assembly vote of 29 November 1947 [cite:unscop_wiki]. That vote, its lobbying and its tally are told in [scene:s09|Resolution 181].

Several questions remain open on this record. The documents used here do not say what the Arab governments argued in Beirut, beyond the fact that they were heard, nor what King Abdullah told the members in Amman. They do not record how individual members weighed the Jewish Agency's testimony against the Arab governments' statements, or whether the Arab Higher Committee's absence changed any member's vote; the account of the Agency "overwhelming" the committee is one historian's characterization, not a record of members' private responses. And while the boycott is documented as a choice, with its reasons, whether a Palestinian presence would have altered the outcome is a counterfactual that no document can settle. What the record does show is how the recommendation was built: by a committee chosen in a close vote, that heard one Palestinian party in full, heard the Arab case only through Arab governments, visited the displaced-persons camps after a divided vote, and ended seven to three.
