What Was Congress Doing? Thirty Years of Resolutions

A common belief holds that Congress debated Palestine thoroughly and, in the end, voted the Jewish state into being. It did neither. Congress never voted to create Israel, and it never voted to recognize it. What it did, repeatedly over three decades, was pass or press resolutions of support, while the decisive acts of partition policy and recognition remained with the executive. Laid out in one place, the congressional record from 1918 to 1948 shows both how persistent that support was and how often it was checked.

The story begins with the executive. After President Wilson endorsed the Balfour Declaration in 1918, Congress followed in 1922 by passing the Lodge–Fish resolution unanimously. It was an endorsement, not an act of policy, and it set the pattern for what followed.

The next major effort came in 1944. Resolutions were introduced in the House by Representatives James Wright, a Pennsylvania Democrat, and Ranulf Compton, a Connecticut Republican, and in the Senate by Robert Wagner, a New York Democrat, and Robert Taft, an Ohio Republican. They called for unrestricted Jewish immigration to Palestine and for a "free and democratic Jewish commonwealth." The sponsorship was bipartisan in both chambers. The House hearings were not one-sided. Among the opponents who testified was Rabbi Morris Lazaron, who argued that Zionism was an internal Jewish matter and not a question for Congress [cite:djn_1944_hearings]. His testimony shows that not every witness Congress heard supported the resolutions.

The military then intervened. In March 1944 the Army Chief of Staff, General George Marshall, and Secretary of War Henry Stimson asked that action be postponed for military reasons, and the committee deferred the resolutions [cite:djn_1944_marshall]. In October Stimson told Senator Taft that political considerations now outweighed the military ones [cite:aja_silver_1944]. Even so, in December the resolutions were deferred again at the administration's request. Twice in one year, then, the resolutions were held back by the executive branch, once for reasons the military gave openly and once at the request of the administration as a whole.

The resolution that finally passed was not the one first introduced. On 17 and 19 December 1945 the two houses adopted a concurrent resolution. A State Department memorandum noted a significant change in its language. It called not for a "Jewish commonwealth" but for "a democratic commonwealth in which all men, regardless of race or creed, shall have equal rights" [cite:frus1945_concres]. The difference is easy to miss in a summary of the period, and the State Department did not miss it. The text Congress actually adopted spoke of equal rights regardless of race or creed, not of a commonwealth described as Jewish.

The champions of these efforts were named members of both parties. Among the leading supporters in Congress were Representative Emanuel Celler of Brooklyn [cite:w_celler] and Representative Sol Bloom, chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee [cite:w_sol_bloom], alongside the Senate sponsors Wagner and Taft. Their work shaped the climate in which policy was made.

The limits, however, are part of the record. Congress held no vote on partition and none on recognition. Those were executive acts, taken by the president. For thirty years Congress expressed support, sometimes unanimously, sometimes after long delay and in altered language. The decisions themselves were made elsewhere.
