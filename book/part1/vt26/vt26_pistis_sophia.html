Pistis Sophia: Wisdom's Fall, Her Repentance, and Mary's Questions

Pistis Sophia is the longest of the texts in this part and the least read. It survives in a single parchment book that has been in London since 1785, so it was never lost in the way the Nag Hammadi books were; it was simply ignored by almost everyone except specialists and, from the 1890s, Theosophists. Its name joins two Greek words, pistis (faith) and sophia (wisdom), and its central figure is a divine being of that name who falls, cries out in a series of repentances and is restored. Its human protagonist is Mary Magdalene, who asks most of the questions, and its recurring complaint comes from Peter, who says she will not let the men speak. This section sets out the manuscript, the outline, the teaching, the Peter–Mary conflict, and the companion Books of Jeu, then gives one labelled esoteric reading. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] [cite:vt26_bge_pistis]

What it is

The text is preserved in the Askew Codex, now British Library Additional MS 5114. Anthony Askew bought it from a bookseller in 1772, and the British Museum bought it from his heirs in 1785. Where it was found and how it reached England are unknown; Egypt is most probable. It is a parchment book of 178 leaves (356 pages), with two leaves missing, written by two scribes of the same period. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] Some scholars date the manuscript to the late fourth century, and the work itself to the third or fourth; it is a Coptic translation of a Greek original. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] [cite:vt26_bge_pistis] Moritz Gotthilf Schwartze's Coptic text and Latin translation was published after his death by J. H. Petermann in 1851, under a title that assigned it to Valentinus. Carl Schmidt's German translation followed in 1905, and G. R. S. Mead's English translation in 1896, revised in 1921. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] [cite:vt26_wiki_mead] Mead's 1921 translation is in the public domain and is quoted here by chapter. Before 1945, the Askew, Bruce and Berlin codices were the only three manuscripts holding complete Gnostic works in Coptic. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew]

The ascription to Valentinus has not held. Carl Schmidt and Adolf von Harnack assigned the text to the Ophites; the Encyclopedia of the Bible notes possible Valentinian influence but calls it uncertain, and the text names no sect. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] [cite:vt26_bge_pistis] It is safest described as a late Gnostic revelation dialogue whose group cannot be identified.

Outline

The codex holds four books, though how many works they represent is debated. Book One (chapters 1 to 62) opens: "when Jesus had risen from the dead, ... he passed eleven years discoursing with his disciples", teaching them only the outer mysteries. [cite:vt26_mead_ch1] (One encyclopedia summary gives twelve years. [cite:vt26_bge_pistis]) Jesus then ascends in light through the aeons, defeats their rulers, returns and begins to tell the story of Pistis Sophia. Book Two (chapters 63 to 101) completes her fall and restoration and turns to long cosmological explanation. Book Three (chapters 102 to 135) gives an ethical code, rules for handing on the mysteries, teaching on repentance and on the make-up of the human being. Book Four, after a gap, is probably a separate and older work on the fate of souls, the punishment of sinners, the zodiac and a baptism called "the first offering". Books Two and Three refer the reader to "the two Books of Jeu" for mysteries necessary to all. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] [cite:vt26_bge_pistis] The crucifixion is not narrated. [cite:vt26_bge_pistis]

What it teaches: Sophia's fall and return

Pistis Sophia dwells in the thirteenth aeon, above the twelve aeons ruled by hostile powers. She sees a light-power below her and takes it for the true Light; she does not know it belongs to "the triple-powered Self-willed", a ruler resentful that she has turned toward the light. She leaves "the thirteenth æon", goes down through the twelve and into the chaos, where a lion-faced power "devoured all the light-powers in Sophia". [cite:vt26_mead_ch31] [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] From the depths she sings repentances to the Light. After each, Jesus invites a disciple to interpret it, and the disciple matches it to a Psalm of David or an Ode of Solomon: Peter reads the second through Psalm 70; Martha reads the thirteenth through Psalm 50. [cite:vt26_mead_ch36] [cite:vt26_mead_ch57] [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] She is rescued and brought back, but only to the thirteenth aeon, not to the highest realm. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis]

The text's purpose is less the myth than the mysteries. Book Three, which sets the rules for handing them on, opens with a demanding moral standard. Jesus tells the disciples to say: "Renounce the whole world and the whole matter therein and all its care and all its sins", and then lists more than thirty things to renounce, among them false witness, avarice, wrath, cursing, theft, adultery, murder, magic potions and "the doctrines of error". [cite:vt26_mead_ch102] This matters for the popular picture of the Gnostic Christians as people without commandments. Some texts, like the Gospel of Mary, warn against adding rules; Pistis Sophia lays down a long code and attaches the mysteries of salvation to it. The difference between it and the institution is not that one had rules and the other did not, but who held the mysteries and on what terms. Part Thirty-One compares the two systems row by row. [scene:vt31_two_economies|Two economies of the sacred]

Mary and Peter

Mary Magdalene is the most prominent disciple in Pistis Sophia, asking many of the questions and giving many of the interpretations; John is second. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] Other women speak too: it is Martha who interprets Sophia's thirteenth repentance and is praised for it. [cite:vt26_mead_ch57]

Peter objects twice. In chapter 36, after Mary has interpreted at length, he breaks in: "My Lord, we will not endure this woman, for she taketh the opportunity from us", and she has let none of them speak. Jesus answers by inviting whoever is moved by the spirit to speak, and gives Peter his turn. [cite:vt26_mead_ch36] In chapter 72 Mary says she is ready to interpret but hesitates: "I am afraid of Peter, because he threatened me and hateth our sex." The reply is that anyone "filled with the spirit of light to come forward" may speak, and "no one shall be able to prevent him". [cite:vt26_mead_ch72] The pattern is the same as in the Gospel of Mary and in the last saying of the Gospel of Thomas: Peter resists a woman's spiritual authority, and the text sides with her. Esther de Boer uses this recurrence to argue that the Mary of the Gospel of Mary is the Magdalene. [cite:vt26_wiki_gmary] The Gospel of Thomas is treated in [scene:vt25_sayings_gospels|the sayings gospels].

The Books of Jeu and James Bruce

The two Books of Jeu that Pistis Sophia cites are probably the texts of that name in the Bruce Codex, now Bodleian Library MS Bruce 96. The Scottish traveller James Bruce bought it in about 1769 in Upper Egypt, near Medinet Habu, on the journey that also took him to Ethiopia; the Bodleian has held it since 1848. It contains the Books of Jeu and an untitled text, both in very poor condition from mildew and fading. Carl Schmidt edited it in 1892 and Violet MacDermot translated it into English in 1978. [cite:vt26_wiki_bruce] [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] In them Jeu is the figure who orders the cosmos, and the books are tied to astrology. [cite:vt26_wiki_pistis] Bruce's journey reappears in [scene:vt27_how_the_west_lost_enoch|the next part], because the same traveller brought the Book of Enoch to Europe.

What the institution said

No ancient council, pope, or heresy catalogue names Pistis Sophia. It is absent from the Decretum Gelasianum's list of rejected books. [cite:vt26_tert_decretum] Epiphanius in the 370s attacked a lost "Greater Questions of Mary", which the NASSCAL catalogue lists alongside Pistis Sophia as related literature, but no source checked for this book shows they are the same text. [cite:vt26_nasscal_qmary] The record shows a text that dropped out of use and was preserved in one copy; it does not show a condemnation. Its modern history ran through the British Museum, Schwartze's posthumous Latin edition and Mead, who had joined the Theosophical Society in 1884, served as Helena Blavatsky's private secretary from 1889 and in 1909 founded the Quest Society for the comparative study of religion. Mead helped make "gnosis" a central idea in the modern understanding of Gnosticism, and Gilles Quispel proposed that he influenced Carl Jung. [cite:vt26_wiki_mead] [cite:vt26_wiki_askew]

Interpretation, not finding: Sophia's fall as a map of ascent

Modern esoteric readers, from the Theosophical circle in which Mead worked to the Sophia platform's own Reignite material, read the Pistis Sophia myth as a map of the soul rather than a story about the cosmos. In this reading, Sophia is the human soul: she mistakes a reflected light for the true one, descends into matter, loses her light to the powers of appetite and self-will, and returns only through repentance and knowledge, stage by stage, through the same aeons she fell through. The disciples' interpretations of her repentances through the Psalms are read, in this lens, as instruction in how a person turns from the false light to the true. Read through this lens, the text offers the same unmediated ascent as the Gospel of Mary's soul passing the powers, while also, unlike that gospel, attaching it to mysteries held by Jesus and passed to his disciples. This is a reading held by those named here; the text itself presents the myth as an account of the heavens, and its third-century readers' own understanding is not documented.

What the canon kept

The canonical gospels did not keep any of these texts, but they kept Mary Magdalene at the centre of the resurrection. All four name her at the empty tomb, and in John she is the first to see the risen Yeshua and is sent to tell the others. [cite:vt26_wiki_magdalene] The texts outside the canon took that role and made it a teaching office: in the Gospel of Mary she instructs the apostles, in Pistis Sophia she out-questions them. In both, Peter objects, and in both he is answered. The institution that traced its authority to Peter kept her as witness and, in 2016, raised her feast to the apostles' rank; it did not keep the texts in which she teaches. [cite:vt26_va_feast2016]
