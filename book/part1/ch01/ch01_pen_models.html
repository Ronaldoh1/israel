Three Ways a Country Holds the Pen

The American system scatters the decision. Elsewhere it is concentrated, and the way it is concentrated shapes what can change and how fast. Three cases central to this history show three different models: a ministry that decides directly, a federation that decides by consensus, and a people whose curriculum is paid for, and can be stopped, by others.

Israel: the ministry decides

Israel's Ministry of Education sets the national curriculum and approves the textbooks its schools may use, including those for the separate Arab-language school system attended by Palestinian citizens of Israel. Because one office holds the power, the account of 1948 taught to Arab pupils has changed with the minister. In 2007 Yuli Edelstein's ministry approved a sixth-grade history book for Arab schools that used the word Nakba, the Arabic for "catastrophe" that Palestinians use for 1948. In July 2009 his successor, Gideon Sa'ar, moved to remove it, telling the Knesset that no country would treat its own founding as a catastrophe in its official curriculum. [cite:pen_csm_nakba2009]

The ministry's reach has since extended from the page to the teacher. On 4 November 2024 the Knesset passed, by 55 votes to 45, a law that lets the ministry's director-general dismiss a public-school teacher found to have expressed support for an act of terror or a terrorist group, and lets the minister cut state funding to a school where such expressions occurred and its managers knew or should have known. [cite:pen_ynet_teachers2024] Adalah, the legal centre for Arab citizens of Israel, petitioned the Supreme Court to strike it down, arguing that its terms are vague, that it bypasses criminal courts and that it chills what teachers say. On 31 December 2025 the court ordered the state to explain why the law should not be annulled; as of 14 November 2026 the case is pending. [cite:pen_adalah_teachers] Supporters present the law as protecting pupils from incitement. Either way, it shows how much of what is said in an Israeli classroom about the conflict now rests with one ministry.

Germany: sixteen states agree

Germany reaches a strong, shared account by the opposite route. Education belongs to its sixteen states, not the federal government. The states coordinate through a standing conference of their education ministers, which traces its start to a meeting in Stuttgart in February 1948 and became permanent that July. Since 1949 its decisions have required unanimity. [cite:pen_kmk_history] The conference has set common guidance on teaching the Holocaust and National Socialism. [cite:rs_ch01_kmk_holo] Because every state must agree, no single election can quickly change what German schools teach about that history. Centralization by consensus is slow in both directions, which is why Germany's memory culture has proved so stable.

The Palestinians: whoever pays

The Palestinian Authority wrote its first full curriculum around 2000, after decades in which Palestinian pupils were taught from Jordanian and Egyptian books. From the start, much of the money came from foreign donors, and the donors' conditions came with it. In late 2000 a monitoring group, the Center for Monitoring the Impact of Peace, published a study of the new first- and sixth-grade books alleging incitement. That December, during an election campaign, the Italian government cited the study and told the Palestinians it could no longer pay for the new curriculum, and the World Bank told the Palestinian education ministry that money for textbooks and teacher training would go to other projects. Other donors followed. [cite:pen_nation_books2001] Other researchers disputed the study: a team at the Hebrew University's Truman Institute found the new books freer of negative stereotypes of Jews and Israelis than the Jordanian and Egyptian books they replaced. [cite:pen_nation_books2001]

The lever has stayed in place. In May 2025 the European Parliament passed a resolution calling for European Union funding to the Palestinian Authority to be frozen until the textbooks were reformed, and pressed the European Commission to enforce a condition on that funding agreed the year before. [cite:pen_jc_ep2025] The Union also commissioned its own review of the books, published in 2021 and examined in the next part of this volume, whose findings each side reads as proof of its case. [cite:gei_faq]

The one study that looked at both sides

Most monitoring studies examine one side's books. One examined both with the same method. Commissioned by the Council of Religious Institutions of the Holy Land and paid for with $590,000 from the US State Department, it was designed by Bruce Wexler, a Yale psychiatrist, and led with Sami Adwan of Bethlehem University and Daniel Bar-Tal of Tel Aviv University. It analysed 94 Palestinian and 74 Israeli books and reported in February 2013. [cite:pen_forward_wexler2013]

Its findings pleased neither side's advocates. Dehumanizing portrayals of the other side were rare in both systems. The researchers counted 20 extreme negative characterizations of the other side in Israeli state secular books, 7 in Israeli ultra-Orthodox books and 6 in Palestinian books, numbers too small to be statistically meaningful. Both systems cast the other side as the enemy and their own almost wholly positively; one-sided portrayals were strongest in the Palestinian and ultra-Orthodox books, and the Israeli secular books were the most self-critical. Both erased the other on the map: 58 percent of Palestinian maps from after 1967 showed "Palestine" covering Israel, and 76 percent of Israeli maps showed no Green Line and no Palestinian Authority. [cite:pen_forward_wexler2013]

The Israeli government had refused to cooperate. Its education ministry called the findings "predetermined" and said any comparison between the two school systems was "completely unfounded." [cite:pen_forward_wexler2013] A study that existed only because a foreign government paid for it, and whose findings one of the two governments dismissed as decided in advance, shows the last point of this section: in every model, whoever funds the checking decides which questions get asked.
