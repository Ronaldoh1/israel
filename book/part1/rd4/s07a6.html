The Peel Commission: Partition's First Draft

Britain's first answer to the Arab Revolt was an inquiry. The Palestine Royal Commission, known everywhere as the Peel Commission, was formed explicitly to investigate the causes of the 1936 uprising. Its report, published in July 1937, went far beyond diagnosis. It recommended dividing Palestine into three parts: a small Jewish state; a larger Arab state, to be merged with Transjordan; and a British-administered corridor including Jerusalem. It was the first official proposal to partition the territory. [cite:wiki_peel_commission]

Partition is often taught as a product of the United Nations in 1947, reached through a neutral international process. The documents place its origin a decade earlier, in the report of a British royal commission. The sources used here establish the structure of that recommendation and its starting point. They do not reproduce every line the commission drew on its map, and the details of its proposed boundaries are not the subject of this section.

The two sides answered in very different forms. The Arab Higher Committee, led by al-Husseini, rejected the plan outright, with no conditions of any kind. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] That was the formal position of the committee itself. It does not tell us what every Arab inhabitant of Palestine thought about partition, and it should not be read as if it did.

The Zionist response was harder to summarize, and historians have been careful not to force it into a single word. The Twentieth Zionist Congress, meeting that year and deeply divided, is described by historians as having either "accepted" or "not rejected outright" the principle of partition. At the same time it specifically rejected the borders the commission proposed and authorized continued negotiation with Britain. [cite:jvp_us_admin_partition_timeline] The position was deliberately ambiguous. It was neither a clean acceptance nor a refusal, but a way of keeping the principle alive while contesting its terms. An honest account preserves that ambiguity rather than forcing on it a clarity the sources do not show.

Not every Zionist voice shared even that conditional openness. Jabotinsky, the leader of the Revisionist movement, testified before the commission and spoke forcefully against any partition at all. He argued for Jewish rights to the whole of Mandate Palestine, including Transjordan, a position his movement held throughout this period. [cite:wiki_jabotinsky] His testimony fit the maximalist stance that defined his career. It was not the mainstream position of the Congress, which was considerably more equivocal than his flat refusal.

What makes Peel important for the rest of this book is its afterlife. Ten years later, the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine held hearings and produced its own proposed map, and that map bore a documented resemblance to earlier partition thinking. Peel is where that thinking began. [cite:peel_report_1937] The claim here is one of conceptual and structural lineage, not line-for-line duplication; the two maps were not identical in every technical detail. But the lineage changes how the 1947 vote should be understood. It was not a fresh proposal arriving from nowhere. It was a second attempt at an idea Britain had already put forward, and that both sides had already answered once: one with outright rejection, the other with calculated ambiguity.

The Peel report also contained a second recommendation. It drew less attention at the time than the map, and a great deal since. It concerned not where the borders should run, but who should live on either side of them. That recommendation is the subject of the next section, on [scene:pl_s01|the word "transfer"].
