The Consequence Engine

The second rung of the ladder, being denied things, is where the worst case would do most of its work, because denial rarely needs a judge. This section goes domain by domain through what American law already lets public bodies, or private firms acting on public signals, withhold from a person: money and credit, purchases, education, work, travel, time off, housing, benefits and a presence online. For each it asks what triggers the denial today, who decides, what notice and appeal the person gets, how often it happens, and what the worst-case extension would be. The finding to keep in view is that each of these levers exists, and each was built for something else: a debt, a conviction, a lapsed status, a foreign designation. No theorist text examined here prescribes any of them; the denied rung is supplied entirely by existing law. [cite:de35_land_ebook]

Money: credit, banking and purchases

The federal government collects debts it is owed without suing. The Treasury Offset Program takes tax refunds, part of Social Security and federal salaries for overdue federal debts and child support, after at least 60 days' notice and a chance to dispute. [cite:de24b_crs_top] Defaulted student loans, owed by about 9.3 million borrowers in mid-2026, can be collected by garnishing up to 15 per cent of disposable pay without a court order; the Department of Education paused these collections in January 2026 and planned to resume them in the autumn. [cite:us17_ci_defaults] [cite:ad7_natp_pause] [cite:ad7_ci_garnish]

The sharpest money lever is a sanctions designation. Once a person is placed on the Treasury's list of blocked persons, American banks, card networks and technology firms may not deal with them, and foreign banks tend to follow for fear of sanctions of their own. The 2025 order against the International Criminal Court showed what that means for an individual. Nine court officials were designated; the prosecutor's bank accounts were closed and his visa revoked, and Judge Kimberly Prost lost her credit cards "immediately," while Amazon's Alexa stopped answering her. "Your whole world is restricted," she said. [cite:de35_ap_icc] The UN special rapporteur Francesca Albanese, designated in July 2025, said that "no bank can open a current account for me" and that she could no longer rent a car without a credit card. [cite:de35_jc_albanese] A federal judge enjoined her designation on 13 May 2026 on free-speech grounds in a suit by her husband and daughter; an appeals court paused that ruling, Treasury re-listed her on 27 May, and on 15 June the appeals court let the sanctions stand while the appeal continued. [cite:de34_audit_albanese_dn] [cite:de35_reuters_albanese] [cite:de35_jc_stay] No American is known to have been designated for speech ([scene:de34_the_repurposing_test|Part Thirty-Six]).

Private debanking runs both ways. Under President Obama, a Justice Department initiative put pressure on banks over gun sellers and payday lenders until it was declared ended in 2017; between 2020 and 2023 the largest banks restricted lawful sectors including firearms, private prisons and political action committees, the Comptroller of the Currency found; and an August 2025 order told regulators to end "politicized" debanking. [cite:de26_cfslm_chokepoint] [cite:de26_occ_debank] [cite:de26_fr_14331] Purchases can also be restricted. Federal law bars gun sales to defined categories of people, and the FBI's check system issued 110,505 denials in 2024, of which 28.6 per cent of those appealed were overturned. [cite:de35_fbi_nics] Twenty-three states won federal waivers to bar food-stamp purchases of candy and sugary drinks, until a federal judge vacated five of them in June 2026, holding that the department "cannot act with the force of law" without Congress. [cite:de35_bd_aragon] Stablecoin issuers must by law be able to comply with lawful orders to "seize, freeze, burn, or prevent the transfer" of their tokens, and Tether says it has frozen about $4.2 billion linked to illicit activity ([scene:de26_money_rails|Part Twenty-Eight]). [cite:de26_crec_genius] [cite:de26_forklog_tether] Outside the United States, Canada's government in 2022 used emergency powers to have banks freeze at least 257 accounts of protesters and donors without a court order; two Canadian courts later found the invocation unreasonable, and the Supreme Court of Canada agreed on 22 October 2026 to hear the government's appeal. [cite:de35_ps_rcmp] [cite:de35_cp24_scc]

Learning and working

Federal student aid once carried conduct conditions: men who had not registered for the draft and students with certain drug convictions were barred. Congress removed both in December 2020. [cite:de35_uc_fafsa] For international students the lever is status. More than 1,800 had records terminated or visas revoked by April 2025, many restored after a court hearing, and the speech-based deportation policy behind some of the revocations was later found unconstitutional. [cite:de24b_pie_sevis] [cite:de28_ch_aaup] Universities have been denied as institutions: the government halted more than $2 billion in Harvard's research grants in April 2025, and a federal judge held in September that the action violated the First Amendment and called its stated rationale "a smokescreen"; the government's appeal argued in April 2026 that grants are contracts it may end. [cite:de35_hm_harvard]

Work can be withheld in four ways. E-Verify checks a new hire against Social Security and DHS records, and a mismatch, which "does not necessarily mean" a person cannot work, must be contested within days. [cite:de24b_ev_manual] [cite:de24b_ev_tnc] Security clearances can be pulled with little review: the Director of National Intelligence revoked those of 37 current and former officials in August 2025, citing "politicization or weaponization of intelligence." [cite:de24b_kvia_gabbard] Orders against four law firms barred their lawyers from federal buildings and clearances until four district judges struck them down; the appeal was argued on 14 May 2026. [cite:de24b_gma_lawfirms] And licences can be suspended for debt: in 2018, by the count of the National Conference of State Legislatures, nineteen states could suspend a professional licence for student-loan default, though at least nine repealed or changed those laws in 2018 and 2019. [cite:de35_ncsl_licence]

Moving, resting and living

The United States has no exit permit, but a citizen may not leave without a valid passport, and passports are denied for tax debt over $66,000 in 2026, for child-support arrears over $2,500 and for certain drug-trafficking convictions. [cite:de25b_usc_1185] [cite:de25b_irs_passport] [cite:de25b_crs_childsupport] [cite:de25b_usc_2714] The terrorist watchlist held about two million names at the end of 2023, mostly foreigners; trusted-traveller status can be revoked without a conviction, as in the Minnesota case of January 2026 described in [scene:de25b_who_can_stop_you|Part Twenty-Seven]. [cite:de25b_cbs_watchlist] [cite:de25b_bu_cleland] Russia's 2023 law bars a man from leaving the country once an electronic draft summons is deemed served, and China's courts block flights and trains for judgment debtors. [cite:de25b_ng_russia] [cite:de35_tr_china] Time off is not a public right at all: federal law requires no paid sick leave, and the Family and Medical Leave Act gives eligible workers up to twelve weeks of unpaid leave, so rest is largely the employer's to grant. [cite:us22_dol_sick] [cite:us18_crs_leave] In housing, federally assisted landlords must refuse any household with a member on a lifetime sex-offender registry, and private landlords rely on screening reports whose errors led to a $15 million federal settlement with one screening firm in 2023. [cite:de35_lii_13663] [cite:ad6_ftc_turss]

Benefits carry the newest conditions. From 1 January 2027 adults aged 19 to 64 covered by the Medicaid expansion must show 80 hours a month of work, study or service, and the 2025 law raised food-stamp work requirements to age 64. [cite:ad8_ccf_ifr] [cite:ad11_mn_snap] Arkansas's 2018 experiment with a Medicaid work rule cost about 18,000 people their coverage in under a year. [cite:de24b_warren_equifax] Online, the denial can come from a private platform under pressure: Apple removed the ICEBlock app in October 2025 at the Justice Department's request, and a federal judge in Illinois found in April 2026 that injuries to two other ICE-tracking platforms were "likely traceable to government-coerced enforcement." [cite:de26_audacy_iceblock] [cite:de26_reason_rosado]

If it were connected

Every row of the table runs on its own list, kept by its own office. The worst case is not a new lever; it is a shared trigger. If a single flag, "critic" for example, were written once and read by all of these gatekeepers, a person could lose their passport, their clearance, their benefits, their bank and their platform in the same week. The pattern for that already exists in two places. In the United States, the child-support and tax rules carry one certified debt from a state or the IRS to the State Department's passport system, a debt flag enforced by a different agency. [cite:de25b_crs_childsupport] [cite:de25b_irs_passport] In China, a court list of judgment debtors is enforced by airlines and railways that blocked tickets 17.5 million and 5.5 million times in 2018. [cite:de35_tr_china] Graded as a join, the shared trigger is possible: the data paths partly exist, as [scene:de28_the_ecosystem_map|the ecosystem map] shows, but no document shows a common flag for speech. A sanctions designation is the one existing tool that already behaves like a shared trigger, because every bank and firm must obey it; its use against foreigners is documented, against Americans for speech it is not supported. What stands in the way is law: each lever's statute names its own trigger, and adding "critic" to any of them would take a new statute or rule, which courts have so far tested against the First Amendment, as in the Harvard and Albanese cases.
