The Ottoman Land Code of 1858: How the Deeds Became Sellable

A familiar account of the early Zionist land purchases holds that Jewish buyers acquired empty or unused land in Palestine from willing local owners. That account begins with the sale. The legal history begins much earlier, and it complicates the picture considerably. To understand how land changed hands in the twentieth century, one has to start with an Ottoman tax reform of the mid-nineteenth.

In 1858, as part of the reforms known as the Tanzimat, the Ottoman state issued a new Land Code, the Arazi Kanunnamesi. It sorted land into categories and required titles to be registered individually. The purpose was fiscal and military: registration would secure tax revenue and conscription rolls. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] The code was not aimed at Palestine in particular, and it had nothing to do with Zionism, which did not yet exist.

In Palestine, however, the code opened a gap between who owned land on paper and who worked it. Many cultivators, fearing both taxation and conscription, did not register the land they farmed. Title was frequently recorded instead in the names of village heads, urban merchants or notables, or absentee families living in Beirut and Damascus. The fellahin, the peasant farmers, went on working the same fields, in practice as tenants. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] The registry described one reality; the villages lived another.

That gap came to matter a great deal once there were buyers. Under the British Mandate, it was the registered titles that could be sold. The largest single sale to Zionist purchasing companies came in the early 1920s, when the Sursock family's holdings in the Jezreel Valley were transferred. The Sursocks were based in Beirut. The land they sold was populated and cultivated. After the sale its tenant cultivators were evicted, with compensation on terms set by the purchaser. [cite:wiki_ottoman_land_code_1858] The mechanics of those later purchases belong to a later part of this book. What matters here is where the deeds came from.

The finding should be stated at its proper size. The 1858 code did not cause the Zionist project. What it created was a documentary form: a registered deed, held in the name of someone who did not farm the land. Through such a deed, populated land could be lawfully transferred over the heads of the people living on it. A sale could be entirely legal, a titled owner selling to a willing buyer, and still remove families who cultivated the ground without holding the paper.

That is why the phrase "willing local owners" is both accurate and incomplete. The owners on the register might be willing. The people on the land were not on the register at all. An Ottoman revenue reform opened the gap between those two groups sixty years before the first large purchases, and later purchases, and later dispossessions, rested on it.
