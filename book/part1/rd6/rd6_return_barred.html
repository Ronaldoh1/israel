Barring Return and Hardening the Line, 1948–1956

The question most often asked about the Palestinian refugees of 1948 is why they did not go home when the fighting stopped. The answer has two parts, and both were in place before the armistices of 1949 were signed. The first was a decision taken by Israel's government in the middle of the war, and the facts that grew up around it. The second was a frontier regime that, in the years after the war, treated those who tried to cross back as intruders. This section follows both, from June 1948 to the eve of the 1956 war.

The decision of 16 June 1948. The decision is known through the research of the historian Benny Morris. As Arthur Hertzberg summarized Morris's research in 1988, Israel's cabinet decided on 16 June 1948 that "there could be no return during the war," leaving the question to be reconsidered later. [cite:rs_rd6_nyrb_hertzberg_1988] The decision was framed as temporary. It barred return for the duration of the fighting and deferred a final answer.

The date matters. It fell during the first truce, twelve days before the UN mediator, Count Bernadotte, proposed at Rhodes that displaced people be allowed "to return to their homes without restriction and to regain possession of their property." [cite:rs_rd6_frus_bernadotte_june] Israel's government had decided against return during the war before the international proposal for return was put on the table.

The facts on the ground. A deferred decision did not leave the question open in practice. Hertzberg, summarizing Morris, wrote that from the summer of 1948 abandoned houses and property were quickly occupied by Jews, and that by the time of the 1949 armistices Israel was both unwilling and unable to allow substantial return. [cite:rs_rd6_nyrb_hertzberg_1988] "Unable" is the important word. Once houses and property had been taken over, a return would have meant undoing that occupation. The legal machinery that converted this occupation into state ownership, from the Custodian of Abandoned Property to the Absentees' Property Law of 1950, is set out in [scene:ap_s01|the section on absentee property].

Who decided the flight itself. Morris's research also bore on the older and more charged question of how the refugees came to leave. According to Hertzberg's account, Morris found no formal decision to expel taken by the highest leaders, while the authority of commanders in the field shaped what happened. [cite:rs_rd6_nyrb_hertzberg_1988] Both halves of that finding are used in argument, often one without the other. Those who reject the charge of a planned expulsion cite the absence of a top-level order. Those who see the outcome as intended point to what commanders did with the authority they held. Morris's own finding contains both: no order from the top, and a war in which those on the ground had wide latitude. Whatever had caused the flight, the June 1948 decision barred return while the war lasted.

The "infiltration" years. Once the armistice lines of 1949 were drawn, people crossed them. Israel called them infiltrators. Who they were and why they came is the central dispute of this period, and the word itself carries one answer to it.

The most detailed study is Morris's Israel's Border Wars. As Avi Shlaim reported in his review of it in 1994, Morris estimated that "90 per cent or more" of the infiltrations of 1949–56 were driven by economic and social concerns rather than political aims: people returning to their homes, their fields, their property or their relatives. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994] Morris estimated that between 2,700 and 5,000 infiltrators were killed in 1949–56, "the great majority of them unarmed." [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994] Israel's army, border guard and police adopted a policy of shooting suspects on sight. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994]

The figures need to be read as what they are. The range of 2,700 to 5,000 is an estimate, and a wide one. The 90 per cent is Morris's judgement of motive across thousands of crossings, not a count. Both come to this book through Shlaim's review, not from Morris's text directly. And the word "infiltration" covered very different acts: a farmer going back for a harvest, a family visiting relatives across the line, and, increasingly in the mid-1950s, an armed raid. Seen from one side of the line, these were refugees going home to what was theirs. Seen from the other, they were illegal entries into a sovereign state.

Retaliation and Qibya. Israel answered cross-border attacks with retaliation across the line. One instance came in October 1953 at Qibya. Unit 101, under Ariel Sharon, blew up 45 houses and killed 69 Jordanian villagers, "the majority of them women and children." The raid provoked international protest. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994]

Israel's leadership was divided over this policy. Morris divided them into "activists," led by David Ben-Gurion and including Dayan and Lavon, and "moderates," led by Moshe Sharett. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994] The division ran through the same government. Shlaim, reviewing the book, faulted Morris for underplaying Sharett's secret contacts with Nasser. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994]

The fedayeen. The Israeli case rests above all on what the border became by 1955. The Jewish Virtual Library, presenting the Israeli account, records that on 31 August 1955 Nasser announced that Egypt would dispatch "her heroes" to Israel's borders. It describes the fedayeen as fighters trained and equipped by Egyptian intelligence to carry out attacks inside Israel, in breach of the armistice agreements' ban on paramilitary hostilities. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_fedayeen] On this account, whatever most crossings had been in 1949, by 1955 a hostile state was organizing armed attacks across the line, which Israel answered with retaliation. The armistice agreements whose terms the fedayeen breached are described in [scene:rd6_armistices|the section on the 1949 armistices].

Two readings of the same border. The dispute over what infiltration was is not a dispute about whether people crossed. Both readings accept that they did, in large numbers, and that many died. It is a dispute about proportion and about cause.

The first reading, Morris's, holds that the crossings were overwhelmingly economic and social, the movement of refugees toward homes, crops and relatives, and that most of those killed were unarmed. Its strongest evidence is the 90 per cent estimate and the death toll. [cite:rs_rd6_lrb_shlaim_1994]

The second reading, presented by the Jewish Virtual Library, holds that the border was a security threat that, by 1955, included fedayeen organized by a hostile government's intelligence service, which Israel answered with retaliation. Its strongest evidence is Nasser's own public announcement and the fact that the attacks breached signed agreements. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_fedayeen]

The two readings describe different parts of the same record and, to a large degree, different years. Morris's estimate covers the whole period of 1949–56; the fedayeen belong chiefly to its last two years. Neither cancels the other. A border can be crossed mostly by unarmed people seeking their property and also be the route of organized armed attacks, and a state can face real attacks while its forces kill thousands of people, most of them unarmed.

What came next, and what the record leaves open. The years of the border wars ended in a larger war. On 29 October 1956, the eve of the Sinai war, a Border Police unit enforcing a curfew killed 49 Arab citizens of Israel returning home to Kafr Qasim, a village about a kilometre from the Green Line. [cite:wiki_kafr_qasim_massacre] That killing, and the trial that followed it, are told in [scene:mg_s01|Kafr Qasim, 1956].

Several things this section cannot settle. The cabinet decision of 16 June 1948 is known here through Hertzberg's summary of Morris, not from the cabinet record itself, and it is described as deferring the question, not closing it; the sources here do not say when, or whether, the government formally reconsidered it. The number of infiltrators killed is an estimate with a range of nearly two to one. And the number of Israelis, civilian and military, killed in cross-border attacks between 1949 and 1956 is not established in the sources gathered for this book. Without that figure the human cost of the border can be counted on one side only, and the reader should know that the count is incomplete, not that the other side's losses were small.
