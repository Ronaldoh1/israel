Customers, Not Voters

If the ruler is chosen by owners, everyone who is not an owner needs a new name. The texts supply one. In "Good Government as Good Customer Service", posted on 25 May 2007, Moldbug imagined a state called Fnargland and wrote: "Fnargland is a business. Like any business, it has no reason at all to alienate its customers." [cite:de35b_le] Land's 2012 summary calls residents "clients," gives them a "customer service function" to complain to, and leaves them the choice to "take their custom elsewhere." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] This section asks what that change of name means in practice: what a customer can and cannot do that a citizen can, who the owners would be, and who could actually use the one remedy the design offers, which is leaving.

What changes when a citizen becomes a customer

A citizen of a democratic republic has a share of the choice of government that does not depend on wealth: one vote, the right to stand for office, the right to petition and assemble, and access to courts that can rule against the state. [scene:de35_the_rights_ledger|Part Thirty-Seven] sets those rights against the texts one by one. A customer has a different relationship. The firm owes the customer the service it chooses to offer, and the customer's leverage is the threat to take business elsewhere. The texts accept this exchange openly. Land wrote that residents no longer need to take "any interest in politics whatsoever," and that to do so "would be to exhibit semi-criminal proclivities." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] Moldbug described the small states of his patchwork as governed "without regard to the residents' opinions." [cite:de35b_le]

The strongest case for the exchange, which the texts make and which [scene:de7_what_it_gets_right|Part Seven] examines, is that a ruler who profits from a well-run territory has a reason to run it well, and that competition between territories would discipline bad rulers better than elections do. The case depends on two conditions: that the owners' interest and the residents' interest coincide, and that residents can in fact leave. The texts assert the first and assume the second.

Who the owners would be

The design's first shareholders are the people who already hold influence. Moldbug's formalism, set out in "A formalist manifesto" on 23 April 2007, proposed "a rule that tells us whose wallet is whose": "let's figure out exactly who has what, now, and give them a little fancy certificate." [cite:de6_le_contra] Land's summary applies that rule to the state itself. The landscape of "political bribery" is mapped, and the privileges it buys in administration, legislation, the courts, the media and the universities become shares. [cite:de35b_land_ebook] The owners, in other words, are the existing holders of political favour, converted from informal to formal. The ordinary voter enters the calculation only "insofar as voters are worth bribing." [cite:de35b_land_ebook] The texts do not describe any later issue of shares to residents.

The two places where people associated with these ideas have actually written a constitution show how ownership and residence divide. Próspera, the private charter city on the Honduran island of Roatán, is governed by a nine-member council. Under its charter of 23 August 2018, residents select four trustees and the company that organised the zone selects four, and important decisions need a two-thirds majority, which gives the company an effective veto; about 2,000 people lived there in 2024. [cite:de22_charter] [cite:de22_wiki_prospera] Urbit, Yarvin's computer network, gives votes on its governance only to holders of its 256 "galaxies"; ordinary users, who hold "planets," have none. As of 2019, 185 of the 256 galaxies were held by the company Tlon, its employees and the employees of the Urbit Foundation. [cite:de23_compact_2025] [scene:de22_prospera_v_honduras|Part Twenty-Two] and [scene:de23_address_space|Part Twenty-Three] tell both stories. Neither is a state run by Yarvin or Land, and neither proves how a neocameral state would behave; both show owners holding the deciding vote.

Exit: who can actually leave

Leaving a country is not like changing shops. It needs a document, money, and another country willing to admit you. In the United States, federal law makes it unlawful for a citizen to leave or enter the country without a valid passport. [cite:de25b_usc_1185] In fiscal year 2025 there were 183.2 million valid American passports in circulation, a count of documents rather than people, since some people hold both a passport book and a card. [cite:de35b_usafacts_pass] The government can already deny a passport for certified tax debt above $66,000 in 2026 and for child-support arrears above $2,500. [cite:de25b_irs_passport] [cite:de25b_crs_childsupport] Renouncing citizenship carried a fee of $2,350 from 2015 until a final rule of 13 March 2026 cut it to $450; at least 8,755 Americans paid the higher fee after the cut was first promised in 2023. [cite:de35b_pbs_renounce]

The states that most restricted exit show what happens when a ruler controls the door. Soviet citizens needed exit visas, and the so-called refuseniks were denied them "for months, years, and even decades"; in 1972 the Soviet Union briefly charged emigrants "education reimbursement fees" of up to tens of thousands of dollars, which fell mainly on Jews seeking to leave for Israel or the United States, and the United States answered with the Jackson-Vanik amendment tying trade to freedom to emigrate. [cite:de25b_enc_refuseniks] [cite:de25b_crs_jv] China's use of exit bans has grown sharply, according to the rights group Safeguard Defenders. [cite:de25b_aj_china] The United States has no exit permit for citizens. [scene:de25b_who_can_stop_you|Part Twenty-Seven] sets out what the government can already do to stop a person travelling.

For those with means, exit is a different matter, and the record of this book's own subjects illustrates it lawfully. Peter Thiel obtained New Zealand citizenship in 2011 after spending a total of 12 days in the country, under an "exceptional circumstances" exemption; the New York Times reported in October 2022 that he was acquiring a Maltese passport through that country's investment scheme; and in the summer of 2026 he moved with his family to Buenos Aires. [cite:de9_nbr_12days] [cite:de9_mt_malta] [cite:de9_fortune_ba] None of this is alleged to be improper. It shows that the remedy the texts offer every resident is, in practice, most available to those who already have several passports and the money to move.

When exit replaces voice

Albert Hirschman, whose 1970 distinction between exit and voice the texts borrow, warned that the presence of exit "can therefore tend to atrophy the development of the art of voice," because those who care most about quality tend to leave first. [cite:de8_hirschman] A design that offers only exit therefore leaves behind the residents least able to go, without the one remedy, voice, that does not require money. The texts say little about those residents. Where Moldbug did address people a sovereign corporation would not want, in his 2008 "Patchwork" posts, his answer was to sell their housing out from under them and, after a line about biodiesel that he followed with "just kidding," to hold them in "permanent solitary confinement" connected to a virtual-reality interface; [scene:de6_the_hard_posts|Part Six] sets those passages out in context. [cite:de6_le_contra] [cite:de6_tnr_duran]

The worst-case reading, tested

The book's working hypothesis, which is the Sophia platform's and not a finding, is that immigration enforcement and government cuts are the occasion and a sovereign executive without a veto is the destination. This part bears on the destination. It is documented that the texts' stated end state has no election and no voice: owners choose the ruler, shares go to existing holders of influence, residents' interest in politics is described as "semi-criminal," and the only remedy left to non-owners is to leave. [cite:de35b_land_ebook] [cite:de35b_le] It is documented that Yarvin, asked in January 2025 how a bad ruler would be removed, called it "the most important question of all" and gave no mechanism in the exchange, and that the one mechanism attributed to the design, board-held keys over the state's weapons, is known only from secondary accounts. [cite:de35b_nyt] [cite:de8_kossina_patchwork]

The record of real systems runs against the design's central promise. In every case examined, rulers were removed only by someone with independent power, a Parliament, an army, an elected chamber or a workforce, and the one long experiment with shareholder sovereignty, the East India Company, ended with Parliament curtailing the shareholders and then abolishing the Court of Directors. [cite:de35b_banglapedia_cod] That a chief executive without an election would be removable by owners alone is not supported.

That the United States is moving toward the texts' end state is not supported by this part's material. No statute, order or bill examined in this book proposes abolishing federal elections, converting political influence into shares, or restricting citizens' exit by permit, and the United States has no exit permit for citizens. [cite:de25b_usc_1185] The rhyme that does exist is narrower and is documented elsewhere: the growth of what the executive can deny a person, from passports to benefits, without an election being involved at all, which [scene:de35_the_consequence_engine|Part Thirty-Seven] examines. Exit, the texts' remedy, already depends on documents the state can withhold, which is the point at which the design and the present machinery meet. The next part asks whether anyone outside the United States reads these texts, and whether publics elsewhere would want what they propose.
