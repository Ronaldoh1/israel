Partition Dropped: Woodhead and St James's Palace, 1938–1939

The Peel plan did not survive its first two years. Its publication in July 1937 had been followed by the restart of the revolt that autumn and by refusals from both sides, told in [scene:s07a6|the Peel Commission]; a United Nations paper later summed up the outcome as rejection by both. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unpacked] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_jvl_riots] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] London's next step was not to impose the plan but to test whether it could be carried out at all.

For that purpose Britain sent a technical commission under Sir John Woodhead. It had four members: Woodhead, Sir Alison Russell, Percival Waterfield and Thomas Reid. It investigated in Palestine from April to early August 1938. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_woodhead] Those were the months in which the revolt was building toward its peak, told in [scene:s07a5|the Arab Revolt]. The difference from Peel was one of mandate. Peel had been asked to investigate the causes of the revolt. Woodhead's commission, formally the Palestine Partition Commission, was asked a narrower practical question: whether partition could be made to work.

Its report, dated 9 November 1938, set out three boundary plans, labelled A, B and C. The commission preferred Plan C. It proposed that the two states remain in a customs union with the Mandatory government, and it questioned whether partition was economically and politically viable at all. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_ecf_woodhead] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_woodhead] Its conclusion was that no practical partition plan could be devised, because both Jews and Arabs refused partition. The British government accepted its conclusion that partition was politically, administratively and financially impossible. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_unccp_1949] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_woodhead]

The political ground is the one the commission spelled out: both communities refused partition. The economic doubt can be read in the commission's own proposal: a customs union with the Mandatory government would have kept the two states tied together after separation. The sources consulted here record these grounds, the three plans and the preference for Plan C. They do not give the population each plan would have placed in each state, and this book does not supply those figures.

Having dropped partition, Britain tried negotiation. It invited the Palestinian Arabs, the neighbouring Arab states and the Jewish Agency to a conference in London in early 1939, known as the St James's Palace conference. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_london1939] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_cojs_text] The exact dates on which it opened and closed rest on a single reference source and are not given here.

The delegations are documented by name, though from a reference source rather than the conference records. The Palestinian delegation was led by Jamal al-Husseini, a founding member of the Arab Higher Committee in 1936, and included Awni Abd al-Hadi, Musa al-Alami and others, with George Antonius as a secretary. Raghib al-Nashashibi joined two days after the opening. Egypt sent a delegation, as did Iraq, under its Prime Minister Nuri al-Said, and Saudi Arabia, under Prince Faisal. The Jewish Agency's delegation was led by Chaim Weizmann, with Ben-Gurion dominant in its decisions. It included non-Zionists and Rabbi Stephen Wise. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_london1939] Two features of these lists stand out. The Arab side came as a regional coalition, with three states represented alongside the Palestinians, which suggests that Palestine had become a question for the surrounding governments as well. And the Palestinian delegation brought Husseini and Nashashibi figures together in one delegation, after the split described in the revolt section.

The conference never met as one. The Arab Higher Committee's delegation refused to sit with the Zionist delegation, so the British held separate meetings with each side. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_london1939] [cite:rs_rd4_pw_cojs_text] Britain was the only party that sat with both.

One strand of the talks went back to the First World War. An Anglo-Arab committee under the Lord Chancellor, Lord Maugham, examined the Hussein–McMahon correspondence of 1915–16. It found that the Arab case had been given too little weight. But the two sides on the committee could not agree on the central point: whether Palestine had been included in the British pledge of Arab independence. [cite:rs_rd4_pw_wiki_london1939] The finding carried weight because of who chaired it, Britain's own Lord Chancellor. It did not settle the question on which the Arab case rested.

The conference failed, and the British government said so in its own words. In the White Paper that followed, it recorded that after talks lasting several weeks, "Neither the Arab nor the Jewish delegation felt able to accept these proposals, and the conferences failed to reach an agreement." [cite:rs_rd4_pw_cojs_text] Britain then issued its own policy. That policy, the White Paper of May 1939, is the subject of [scene:rd4_white_paper|the White Paper].

How to read this sequence is a matter of emphasis more than of fact. One reading stresses British retreat: having proposed partition, London dropped it within sixteen months, once the revolt reached its height and both communities had refused the plan. Another stresses that the refusals were real and mutual, as Woodhead's own conclusion and the 1949 United Nations paper both record. The documents support both. Britain's stated reason for abandoning partition was the refusal of both peoples, and the timing places that decision at the peak of the Arab revolt.

Some questions this record does not answer. The sources here do not name the British ministers who ran the London talks, nor give the conference's exact dates or the populations of the Woodhead plans. They do not record what the separate meetings with the Arab states' delegations discussed. They also do not show whether any version of partition was ever put to the conference.
