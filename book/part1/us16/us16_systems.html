Who Is Covered and Who Pays

The two systems start from different laws. Israel has a single statute that makes health insurance a condition of residence; the United States has several programmes, each with its own rules, and no law that covers everyone. This section sets out how each is built and who falls outside it; the next two set out what each spends and what each achieves. [scene:us16_cost|What it costs] [scene:us16_outcomes|Outcomes over time]

Israel: one law, four funds

Before 1995 health insurance in Israel was voluntary and ran through the sick funds. About 95 per cent of the population was insured by the early 1990s, but the largest fund, Clalit, required membership of the Histadrut labour federation or dependency on a member. [cite:us16_wiki_ilhealth] The Knesset passed the National Health Insurance Law in 1994 and it took effect on 1 January 1995. It made membership of one of the four funds compulsory and obliged every fund to accept any applicant. [cite:us16_wiki_ilhealth] A 2021 review in the Israel Journal of Health Policy Research summarised what changed: eligibility was cut loose from party or union membership, the funds lost the power to select healthier patients, a single government-funded basket of services was defined, and an earmarked health tax was created to pay for it. The state pays each fund by a capitation formula weighted for age, sex, area of residence and a small number of severe diseases. [cite:us16_ijhpr_nhil]

The four funds are Clalit, Maccabi, Meuhedet and Leumit. In 2024 Clalit held 50.96 per cent of members, Maccabi 27.48, Meuhedet 13.99 and Leumit 7.57. [cite:us16_wiki_ilhealth] The health tax is 3.2 per cent of monthly income up to 60 per cent of the average wage and 5.2 per cent above it; the government budget adds the rest, and public sources together paid 65 per cent of national health spending in 2024. [cite:us16_cwf_israel] The basket is the same in every fund and is widened each year on the recommendation of a public committee; in February 2025 it recommended adding 117 medicines and technologies. Cost-sharing is small and capped: NIS 22–35 (about $6–10) for the first specialist visit in a quarter, and 10 per cent of the maximum price for generic medicines and 15 per cent for branded ones, with a minimum of NIS 18. [cite:us16_cwf_israel] Coverage does not depend on a job: it follows residence, so losing work does not mean losing insurance. [cite:us16_ijhpr_nhil] [cite:us16_cwf_israel]

The record also shows the limits. The law excludes asylum seekers, undocumented migrants, foreign workers and holders of B1 work visas, who must rely on employer-bought or private policies; soldiers and prisoners, about 2 per cent of the population, are covered by separate systems. [cite:us16_cwf_israel] Asylum policy is treated in Part Six, and Palestinians in the West Bank and Gaza, who are not Israeli residents and are not covered by the law, in Part Nine. [scene:us6_asylum|Part Six: asylum] [scene:us9_westbank|Part Nine: the West Bank] Inside the system, a large private layer has grown on top of the public one. Non-public sources paid about 36 per cent of national health spending by the time of the 2021 review; a Brookdale Institute study in 2016 found close to 85 per cent of households holding the funds' supplementary insurance and 57 per cent holding commercial insurance, and the Commonwealth Fund reports that 88 per cent of adults had both kinds in 2022. [cite:us16_ijhpr_nhil] [cite:us16_cwf_israel] Waiting is the most common complaint: in the first quarter of 2024 the national average wait to see an endocrinologist or a neurologist was 50 days. [cite:us16_cwf_israel]

The United States: several systems side by side

American coverage is a patchwork of employer insurance, the federal Medicare programme for people aged 65 and over, the joint federal-state Medicaid programme for people on low incomes, marketplace plans created by the Affordable Care Act of 2010, and military and veterans' care. A person can hold more than one kind in a year or none. In 2025, by the Census Bureau's count, 53.5 per cent of the population had employment-based coverage for some or all of the year, 35.8 per cent had public coverage, 17.1 per cent Medicaid and 20.1 per cent Medicare; 26.7 million people, 7.9 per cent, were uninsured for the whole year. [cite:us16_census_2025]

The uninsured count has moved with the law. More than 50 million Americans, nearly 17 per cent, were uninsured in 2009; about 42 million in 2013, the year before the Affordable Care Act's main coverage provisions began; and 28.1 million, a record low of 8.8 per cent, in 2016. [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] The Census Bureau changed its survey questions in this period, so early and late figures are not strictly comparable, but the direction is not in dispute. The rate was 8.0 per cent in 2024 and 7.9 per cent in 2025. [cite:us16_ib_subsidy] Two decisions point the other way from 2026. The enhanced marketplace premium tax credits expired at the end of 2025; by KFF's estimate the average premium paid by subsidised marketplace enrollees rose from $888 to $1,904 a year, and enrollment fell from about 24.2 million to about 23 million. [cite:us16_ib_subsidy] The budget reconciliation law signed on 4 July 2025 cuts Medicaid and marketplace spending; the Congressional Budget Office estimated that it will add about 10 million people to the uninsured by 2034, 7.5 million of them through the Medicaid changes, and about 14.2 million together with the expiry of the enhanced credits. [cite:us16_kff_obbba] These are projections, not outcomes, and the status of both is as of October 2026, ahead of the 3 November midterm elections.

For most working Americans coverage is tied to a job and paid partly from wages. In 2025 the average employer family premium was $26,993, of which the worker paid $6,850; family premiums rose 6 per cent that year against wage growth of 4 per cent and inflation of 2.7 per cent. The average deductible for single coverage was $1,886, and 34 per cent of covered workers faced a deductible of at least $2,000. [cite:us16_kff_ehbs2025] Part Fourteen sets these costs inside a family budget. [scene:us14_basket|Part Fourteen: the household basket]

The American military aid to Israel does not enter Israel's health budget: it is a grant for weapons, mostly bought from American firms, and has fallen to about half a per cent of Israel's GDP. [cite:gi16_inss_aid] [scene:gi16_aid|The aid] The comparison here is between two ways of organising coverage, each paid for by its own taxpayers.
