The Tests It Fails

A diagnosis can be right and a cure still wrong. The Dark Enlightenment's cure is neocameralism, Moldbug's name for a state organised as a joint-stock company. In the passage Land quotes in his 2012 serial, "a state is a business which owns a country." Its ownership is divided into shares, each with one vote; the shareholders elect a board, the board hires and fires the managers, and the residents are the company's customers. [cite:de8_land_de] Because the texts make claims about how such a state would perform, those claims can be checked against states that exist, against the record of one-person rule, against companies that failed, and against the companies that once actually ruled countries. This section runs those four tests.

The models the texts name: Singapore and Dubai

The texts point to living examples. The neocameralist passage Land quotes says that the full approach "has never been tried," and that its closest equivalents are the enlightened absolutism of Frederick the Great and "lost fragments of the British Empire such as Hong Kong, Singapore and Dubai." Those states, it continues, are "weak only in political freedom, and political freedom is unimportant by definition when government is stable and effective." [cite:de8_land_de] Later in the serial Land uses Singapore, Hong Kong, Taipei and Shanghai as his measure of cities where it is safe to walk at night. [cite:de8_land_de] Yarvin has described the end state in the same terms: in Ava Kofman's 2025 account in the New Yorker, he proposes breaking nations into a "patchwork" of statelets, "like Singapore or Dubai," each with its own sovereign ruler. [cite:de8_kofman_ny]

Neither example is what the texts say it is. Singapore is not a company with a chief executive answerable to shareholders. It is a party state. Freedom House rates it Partly Free, 48 out of 100 in its 2026 report, and describes a system "dominated by the ruling People's Action Party (PAP) and the powerful Lee family since 1959," which "allows for some political pluralism" but constrains opposition parties and limits freedoms of expression, assembly and association. [cite:de8_fh_sg2026] Its leaders change by party procedure: on 15 May 2024 Lawrence Wong, chosen as party leader with the unanimous backing of the PAP's members of parliament, became the country's fourth prime minister since 1965, succeeding Lee Hsien Loong. [cite:de8_cnbc_wong] The political scientist Netina Tan describes Singapore as a hegemonic party autocracy whose resilience rests on what one of its own prime ministers called "a planned and orderly system of political succession." [cite:de8_tan_pap] In the standard typology of authoritarian rule developed by Barbara Geddes, Joseph Wright and Erica Frantz, which sorts regimes into dominant-party, military, personalist, monarchical and other types, a ruling party with rules for choosing its leaders is a different kind of regime from rule by one person. [cite:de8_wiki_geddes]

Dubai is one of seven emirates in the United Arab Emirates, which Freedom House rates Not Free, 18 out of 100, with political parties banned and "all executive, legislative, and judicial authority" resting ultimately with the seven hereditary rulers. [cite:de8_fh_uae2026] That is a hereditary monarchy, not a joint-stock sovereign; there are no shareholders who can vote a ruler out. The texts' own models, in other words, are a party state and a set of hereditary monarchies. Each achieved order by means the texts reject or never describe: an institutionalised party in one case, dynastic succession in the other.

The evidence on one-person rule

The distinction between a party state and one-person rule matters because the comparative evidence turns on it. In a paper first issued by the National Bureau of Economic Research in August 2025, Christopher Blattman, Scott Gehlbach and Zeyang Yu compared the economic growth of democracies, institutionalised autocracies and personalist autocracies, those in which power sits with one individual or a small clique, across 179 countries from 1960 to 2010. They found that whenever an "autocratic penalty" on growth appears, it is concentrated in personalist regimes; institutionalised dictatorships grew at rates statistically similar to democracies. They traced the penalty to low private investment, weak provision of public goods and conflict. [cite:de8_nber_w34093] [cite:de8_harris_w34093] A 2025 study of 32 countries in Eastern Europe and Latin America by Tatiana Kostadinova and Milena Neshkova found that governments led by personalist leaders were associated with higher grand and petty corruption, and that "the time a regime stays in power intensifies its impact on malfeasance." [cite:de8_kn_corruption]

The record also contains a finding that favours part of the texts' argument, and it belongs here. Studying European monarchies from 1000 to 1800, Andrej Kokkonen and Anders Sundell found that the risk of a monarch being deposed was "several times higher" where the crown did not pass by primogeniture, inheritance by the eldest son, and that the spread of primogeniture largely explains why depositions became rarer. [cite:de8_ks_primogeniture] A settled rule of succession made monarchy more stable. But that is a finding about a rule binding the ruler, not about the ruler's freedom from rules, and neocameralism replaces inheritance with a board that hires and fires. Yarvin, asked by CNN in 2025 how his system would avoid a tyrant, argued that monarchies "don't generally see a Holocaust" and then conceded that it requires concentrating power "in a single individual" and hoping "that this is the right individual." [cite:de8_cnn_yarvin] On the comparative evidence, the design most like the one he proposes is the one that performs worst.

Companies fail too, and something outside them catches the failure

The texts' central analogy is the well-run company, and the analogy has a record of its own. Enron, the seventh-largest company in the United States, collapsed in December 2001 in what was then the largest bankruptcy in American history; on 25 May 2006 a jury in Houston convicted its former chiefs Kenneth Lay, on all six counts against him, and Jeffrey Skilling, on 19 of 28. [cite:de8_aj_enron] When the crypto exchange FTX filed for bankruptcy in November 2022, its new chief executive, John J. Ray III, who had worked on the Enron clean-up, told the court he had never seen "such a complete failure of corporate controls," with control concentrated in "a very small group." [cite:de8_axios_ray] Its founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted on seven counts, including fraud, in 2023 and sentenced on 28 March 2024 to 25 years in prison; at least $8 billion in customer funds had been taken. [cite:de8_npr_sbf]

Both companies had boards, shareholders and auditors, and in both the internal controls failed. What ended the damage came from outside the company: prosecutors, juries, bankruptcy courts and regulators, all part of a sovereign state that stood above the firm. A sovereign corporation has nothing above it. Its board would be the highest authority, and the courts, the police and the regulator would be its own departments. The corporate record does not show that boards reliably restrain chief executives; it shows that when they fail, the state catches what they miss.

When companies were sovereigns

The world has run the experiment the texts describe, though not under that name. The Dutch East India Company, the VOC, chartered on 20 March 1602 as one of the first joint-stock companies, held powers to wage war, imprison and execute, make treaties, mint coins and found colonies. Between 1609 and 1621 it conquered the Banda Islands to enforce its monopoly on nutmeg; of an estimated 15,000 inhabitants, about 1,000 remained. Its board was dismissed in 1796 and its charter expired on 31 December 1799, when its possessions and debts passed to the Dutch state. [cite:de8_wiki_voc] The English East India Company, chartered on 31 December 1600, came to govern and tax Bengal. [cite:dv2_wiki_eic] [cite:de8_hh_eic] A famine in 1769–70, made worse by the company's grain monopoly and taxation, killed a number commonly estimated at up to ten million people. [cite:de8_hh_eic]

What followed is the most direct test the texts could ask for. The company's shareholders did not correct its government of Bengal; Parliament did. The Regulating Act of 1773, prompted by "the company's misgovernment of its Bengal lands, brought to a crisis by the threat of bankruptcy" and its request for a government loan, placed the company under parliamentary supervision, created a governor-general and a supreme court, and reformed the shareholders' own voting, raising the qualification to vote from £500 to £1,000 to make it harder for factions to buy control. [cite:de8_brit_regact] After the rebellion of 1857, the Government of India Act of 1858 transferred the company's powers to the Crown, and an Act of Parliament of 1873 wound the company up; it was dissolved on 1 June 1874. [cite:de8_brit_goi] [cite:de8_hh_eic] [cite:dv2_wiki_eic] Each correction came from a sovereign outside the company. A neocameral state, by design, would have none.

Four tests, one pattern. The states the texts hold up as models are a party state and hereditary monarchies, not sovereign companies; the comparative evidence finds that rule by one person performs worst on growth and corruption; companies with boards and shareholders fail, and are caught by a power outside them; and the companies that once ruled territory were corrected, every time, by a state above them. None of this shows that the texts' authors want the failures. It shows that the design they propose removes the outside power that has caught such failures before.
