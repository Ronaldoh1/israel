Conflicts and Contracts

Musk came into government owning companies that depended on it. SpaceX launched the Pentagon's satellites and NASA's astronauts; Tesla answered to federal safety and securities regulators; Starlink sold to the armed forces and to civilian agencies; and xAI, the artificial-intelligence company that came to own X, was a new federal vendor. [cite:de11_wapo_38bn] While Musk served as a special government employee, from January to May 2025, the same government regulated, investigated and bought from those companies. This section sets out, matter by matter, what is documented about those regulators and contracts during his time in office and after it. It asks one question of each: what happened, and when. Sequence is not causation here either. A case dropped or a contract won while Musk was in government does not show that he caused it, and the section says where the record runs the other way.

The safeguards on paper

Questions about conflicts were raised at once. On 5 February 2025 the White House press secretary, Karoline Leavitt, said that if Musk found a conflict with contracts or funding DOGE oversaw, "Elon will excuse himself from those contracts." [cite:de21_ap_recuse] That was a promise of self-policing, and its fulfilment cannot be checked. When the Government Accountability Office later audited DOGE's personnel, it identified 206 people who had held positions in the Executive Office of the President, 27 of them special government employees, the category Musk belonged to; the Executive Office "did not respond to GAO's requests for access to training records and financial disclosures for DOGE personnel." [cite:de21_gao_personnel] No public record shows Musk recusing himself from any matter, and none shows him declining to. The White House's position in court, that he was a senior adviser with no formal authority over DOGE, is examined in [scene:de17_what_doge_was_legally|Part Seventeen]. [cite:de11_audit_fisher]

The most complete inventory of what was at stake for his companies came from the opposition. On 28 April 2025 Democratic staff of the Senate's Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, working for its ranking member, Senator Richard Blumenthal, counted 65 actual or potential enforcement actions involving Musk's companies across 11 federal agencies, and estimated potential liability of $2.37 billion for the 40 they could quantify, calling the figure "a credible, conservative estimate." Their examples included the Federal Aviation Administration's case over SpaceX launch requirements, safety investigations of Tesla's self-driving software, animal-welfare allegations against Neuralink, labour cases at SpaceX and Tesla, and a dropped discrimination suit. They said the companies had given "abjectly inadequate cooperation." [cite:de21_reg_psi] [cite:de11_psi_memo] The memo is a partisan staff estimate of potential penalties, not assessed ones, and its conclusion that Musk's DOGE decisions were marked by "self-enrichment" is the authors' characterisation. [cite:de21_reg_psi] It is useful as a list. What follows tests the items on it that later records resolved.

Regulators: what happened to the cases

The Justice Department's SpaceX suit. In 2023 the department sued SpaceX for discouraging refugees and asylees from applying for jobs; SpaceX denied wrongdoing, and the case had been paused since November 2023 by the company's challenge to the department's authority. On 20 February 2025, a month into Musk's service, the department asked the court to let it dismiss the case with prejudice, so that it could not be brought again. It gave no reason. [cite:de21_axios_doj] This is the clearest instance of an enforcement action against a Musk company ending while he was in government. No document shows that he asked for it.

The Federal Aviation Administration. In September 2024 the FAA proposed $633,009 in civil penalties against SpaceX for using an unapproved launch control room and an unapproved propellant farm in 2023. [cite:de21_faa_penalty] Musk called it "more lawfare" and said SpaceX would sue the agency; on 25 September 2024 he wrote of the FAA's administrator, Mike Whitaker, "He needs to resign." [cite:de21_axios_faa] [cite:de21_snopes_whitaker] Whitaker announced his resignation on 12 December 2024 and left on inauguration day, about fifteen months into a five-year term. A widely repeated line holds that Musk forced him out; a fact-check found that agency heads often leave as administrations change and found no evidence that Musk arranged it. [cite:de21_snopes_whitaker] No resolution of the proposed penalties had been made public as of October 2026.

The National Labor Relations Board. SpaceX did not wait for the executive branch to change. It sued the board, arguing that the job protections of its members and judges were unconstitutional, and on 19 August 2025 the Fifth Circuit agreed that its structure was likely unconstitutional and upheld injunctions barring the board from pursuing unfair-labour-practice cases against SpaceX and two other employers. [cite:de21_franczek_nlrb] [cite:de21_cwc_nlrb] That relief came from a court, on an argument other employers also made, and the Supreme Court's decision in Trump v. Slaughter on 29 June 2026, which overruled the 1935 precedent protecting independent commissioners from removal, was decided in a case about the Federal Trade Commission. [cite:de0_slaughter] Both rulings helped SpaceX; neither was a decision of DOGE.

The Securities and Exchange Commission. In January 2025 the commission sued Musk for disclosing his Twitter stake late in 2022, alleging that the delay cost other shareholders more than $150 million; in December 2024 its lawyers had asked him to pay more than $200 million. The case settled for $1.5 million, which the agency called a record for a late disclosure and Musk's lawyer called a "small fine." Approving the settlement in July 2026, Judge Sparkle Sooknanan said she did so "despite its significant misgivings," having said in May that it raised "red flags," including whether Musk had received "special treatment"; she held that the court "may not step in the shoes of the SEC." [cite:de21_bberg_sec] The judge's question is on the record. The answer to it is not.

Vehicle safety. One regulator kept going. On 7 October 2025 the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened an investigation covering 2,882,566 Teslas equipped with Full Self-Driving, after 58 reports of the cars breaking traffic laws, including running red lights; it had opened a separate inquiry into late crash reporting by Tesla that August, and earlier probes continued. [cite:de21_ap_nhtsa] An agency that had been listed among those Musk's position might neutralise opened its largest Tesla investigation after he left office.

Payments. On 28 January 2025 X announced a payments service, X Money, with Visa. [cite:de21_engadget_xmoney] On 9 May 2025 Trump signed a resolution of Congress, S.J.Res. 28, repealing a rule of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau that would have placed large digital-payment apps under federal supervision. [cite:de21_hk_cra] The repeal was passed by both houses of Congress under the Congressional Review Act and applied to the whole industry. That it benefited X is an inference from structure; no lobbying record tying the repeal to X has been published.

Contracts: what was won

The FAA episode is the one in which Musk's companies and his government role met most directly. In February 2025 the agency began testing Starlink terminals at sites in Alaska, Oklahoma City and Atlantic City, under an existing contract held by L3Harris; SpaceX said the kits were leased "free-of-charge," and three SpaceX engineers received ethics waivers to serve at the FAA as special government employees while remaining at the company. Musk wrote that the Verizon system for air-traffic communications was "breaking down very rapidly," then corrected himself to say that the failing system was L3Harris's and Verizon's was "not yet operational." SpaceX said there was "no effort or intent for Starlink to 'take over'" Verizon's $2.4 billion contract, and the Transportation Secretary, Sean Duffy, called Starlink "part of a solution." [cite:de21_ap_starlink] The contract that mattered went elsewhere: in December 2025 the department named Peraton as prime integrator for the air-traffic-control overhaul, with $12.5 billion appropriated by Congress. [cite:de21_ain_peraton]

The larger awards are already set out in [scene:de11_money_and_influence|Part Eleven] and are only summarised here. SpaceX won about $5.92 billion of national-security launches on 4 April 2025, in a competitive procurement begun before 2025 in which it shared the work with two rivals; an executive order of 13 August 2025, after the break with Trump, eased launch licensing in a way Reuters said would benefit SpaceX most; and in April 2026 SpaceX was one of twelve firms given Golden Dome interceptor agreements. [cite:de11_ussf_nssl] [cite:de11_reuters_spaceeo] [cite:de11_bd_sbi] On 25 September 2025 the General Services Administration made xAI's Grok models available to every federal agency for $0.42 per agency for eighteen months, describing it as the last frontier model added to a government-wide programme that already offered others. [cite:de21_gsa_xai] Palantir, the company Peter Thiel co-founded, won a $29.9 million ImmigrationOS contract from Immigration and Customs Enforcement in April 2025 and an Army enterprise agreement worth up to $10 billion over ten years that August. [cite:ow9_reg_immos] [cite:ow9_bd_army] These are sequence: they fell in the same months as DOGE, and no document links any of them to a DOGE decision. Palantir's government business is the subject of [scene:de27_origins_and_control|Part Twenty-Seven].

What the record shows, and what it does not

Documented: the overlap itself. Musk held a government role while companies he controlled faced dozens of federal matters, his promised recusal cannot be checked because the financial disclosures were not produced to the auditor, and several matters ended in his companies' favour during or after his service: a suit dropped without explanation, a settlement a federal judge approved with open misgivings, and labour cases halted by a court. [cite:de21_gao_personnel] [cite:de21_axios_doj] [cite:de21_bberg_sec] [cite:de21_franczek_nlrb] Open, and not graded probable: that the presence of the 2024 election's largest donor inside the government made enforcers more cautious toward his companies. Timing allows the inference, no document supports it, and the regulator with the most open cases against Tesla cuts against it. Not supported: that Musk directed any contract to his companies or ordered any case closed. The largest award was competitive, the air-traffic contract went to another firm, the most consequential relief came from courts, and the regulator with the most open cases against Tesla opened a bigger one after he left.
