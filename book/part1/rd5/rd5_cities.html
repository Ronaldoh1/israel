The Fall of the Mixed Towns, April–May 1948

For four months after the partition vote, the fighting in Palestine was a civil war, with attacks in both directions. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] In April 1948 it changed character. The Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi writes that the Zionist forces "did not begin to seize and hold on to Arab territory" until the offensive that began on 1 April 1948. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] That offensive opened under Plan Dalet, issued three weeks earlier ([scene:pd_s01|Plan Dalet]). Within six weeks the towns in which Arabs and Jews had lived side by side, and the Arab towns near them, had changed hands one after another.

Khalidi's phrasing is precise, and it should be read beside his own correction elsewhere: a footnote in his study of Plan Dalet records that at least ten Arab villages had been captured by 1 March 1948. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The two statements can stand together. Villages were taken before April; the systematic seizing and holding of Arab territory, in his account, began with the April offensive.

Tiberias was the first town to fall, on 18 April 1948. Its Arab population, about 4,500 to 5,000 people and swollen by refugees from nearby villages, was evacuated. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa]

Haifa followed within the week, and its fall is the best documented and the most argued over. Early on 21 April 1948 the Haganah's Carmeli Brigade, under Moshe Carmel, attacked as British forces withdrew from their dividing positions in the city. That same day Major-General Hugh Stockwell, the British commander, had told Arab representatives that his forces would redeploy. The city fell on 22 April. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa]

The cost is known from British figures, which Khalidi cites: about 100 Arabs killed and 200 wounded, and about 20 Jews killed and 40 wounded. On 22 April about 50,000 Arabs were crowded into the Old Town. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa]

On 22 April the Arab delegates met Stockwell at the Town Hall over the terms of a truce; Haifa's Jewish mayor was there too, according to one account. What was said there, and what it meant, is the heart of the dispute over why Haifa's Arabs left. Khalidi's account, which draws on an account by Koussa, runs as follows. Stockwell warned the Arab delegates that if they did not sign the truce he would not be responsible if "three or four hundred more" were killed. The delegates had asked Damascus for instructions and had no reply. Their request for transport out of the city, according to Koussa's account, was made "on the spur of the moment," not under orders from the Arab states. Khalidi concludes: "Within a week, some 50,000 Arab inhabitants had been expelled." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa]

Khalidi goes further on the British role. He argues that the Haganah had advance knowledge of the British redeployment, and he calls Stockwell "an accomplice in the fall of Haifa." [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] The strongest form of his case is that the timing of the attack, the redeployment and the ultimatum at the Town Hall fit together too closely to be chance, and that the evacuation request was the improvised act of men without instructions under military pressure, not a plan from outside.

A sharply different account appears in an opinion article in the Jewish Chronicle by Florit Shoihet, published on 12 August 2026. According to that article, Haifa's Jewish mayor, Shabtai Levy, pleaded with the Arab delegates at the same meeting: "Stay in the city and be equal citizens." Stockwell, it reports, told the delegates, "You have made a foolish decision." The Haganah, it says, broadcast assurances of safety and civil rights, and about 60,000 Arabs left. [cite:rs_rd5_jc_haifa] The strongest form of this case is that Haifa's Arabs were offered terms of equal citizenship by the Jewish mayor and urged to stay by the British commander, and that their own delegation chose to leave. The article is an opinion piece and cites no specific archive for these details, and it should be weighed as such.

The third position is the historian Benny Morris's, as Khalidi reports it, and it has shifted. Morris first acknowledged, in 1987, that there were no Arab evacuation orders. He later argued that such orders were issued after the fighting. Khalidi disputes the later position. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] The broader question of evacuation orders, and the June 1948 Israeli intelligence estimate that attributed no more than 5 per cent of departures to them, is set out in [scene:rd5_refugees|the refugee section]. [cite:rs_rd5_lockman_ref]

The two departure figures, 50,000 and about 60,000, come from opposite sides of the argument and are not far apart. The dispute is less about how many left than about why.

There is also an unresolved question about the operation's name. Khalidi writes in his Haifa study that the operation planned as Misparayim was renamed Chametz. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_haifa] In his list of Plan D operations, however, he dates Misparayim to 21 April and assigns it to Haifa, and dates Chametz to 27 April and assigns it to the villages around Jaffa. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] The research gathered here does not reconcile the two.

After Haifa the other towns followed in the last three weeks of the Mandate. Khalidi's operation list gives the frame: Chametz against the villages around Jaffa from 27 April; Yiftach in eastern Galilee from 28 April; Matateh in the Tiberias area from 3 May; Gideon at Beisan from 11 May; and Ben Ami at Acre on 14 May, the day the state was proclaimed. [cite:rs_rd5_khalidi_1988] Jaffa and Safed fell in these weeks. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] During the war an estimated 100,000 to 110,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were displaced into Lebanon, the large majority from northern Palestine, including the Acre area. [cite:lebanon_refugee_policy]

The record gathered for this book is uneven across these towns. Tiberias and Haifa are documented in detail; Jaffa, Safed, Beisan and Acre appear here only through Khalidi's list of operations, and the exact dates on which Jaffa, Safed and Acre fell were not established in this research. For Haifa, the British casualty figures and the timing of the redeployment are documented. Whether the Haganah knew of the redeployment in advance rests on Khalidi's argument. What was said at the Town Hall rests on two accounts that cannot both be complete: Khalidi's, from the Arab side, and an opinion article that cites no archive. And Morris's own changing view shows that the question of evacuation orders in Haifa has not been settled even by a historian whose archival work documented the expulsions in detail.
