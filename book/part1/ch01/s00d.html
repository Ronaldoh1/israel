The Star and the Menorah

Symbols carry meaning, and when a political movement selects one symbol over another, the choice says something about what the movement takes itself to be. The usual assumption is that the Star of David is the ancient symbol of the Jewish people and stands on Israel's flag as it always has. The dated record shows two symbols of very different ages, and a choice between them made first by a political congress in 1897 and then by a new state in 1948.

The older symbol is the seven-branched menorah. Exodus 25:31–40 describes it as the lampstand of the Tabernacle, and it later stood in the Temple in Jerusalem. [cite:exodus_25_bible] Its oldest visual record is the Arch of Titus in Rome, completed in 81 CE, which depicts the sack of the Second Temple. [cite:wiki_arch_titus] It is the oldest continuous Jewish symbol, and it is the emblem of the modern State of Israel, on the state seal rather than the flag. [cite:wiki_menorah]

The Magen David, the Shield of David, is medieval as a distinctively Jewish symbol. The six-pointed star as a shape is old and appears in many unrelated cultures. Its documented use as a Jewish communal emblem appears in fourteenth-century Prague; in 1354 the Holy Roman Emperor Charles IV granted Prague's Jewish community the right to its own flag, red with a yellow Star of David. Its adoption as the universal Jewish symbol came later still, driven by European Jewish communities between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries that wanted an emblem parallel to the Christian cross and the Islamic crescent. It carried Kabbalistic associations in some traditions but was not widely recognized as the Jewish symbol until the modern period. [cite:unpacked_star_of_david_history] [cite:wiki_star_david] Gershom Scholem's 1949 monograph, Magen David: The History of a Symbol, documents the modern and Zionist role in fixing the hexagram as the Jewish emblem; before the seventeenth century, on this account, the symbol of the people would have been the menorah. [cite:scholem_star_david] According to several historians, Herzl himself called the star a "meaningless symbol of Judaism" in this period. None of this makes the star less meaningful to those who hold it now. Symbols gain meaning whatever their age. The point concerns the claim of antiquity, which is often overstated.

The flag came out of the movement. Herzl's Der Judenstaat of 1896 proposed a white flag with seven gold stars standing for a seven-hour workday. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] It never caught on. At the First Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1897, a different design flew instead: two blue horizontal stripes on white with a blue Magen David between them. [cite:basel_program] It closely followed a design created in 1885 by David Wolffsohn for the Bnei Zion association in Boston, modeled on the colors of the tallit, the prayer shawl. Wolffsohn, who later succeeded Herzl as president of the World Zionist Organization, is credited with proposing it as the movement's banner. [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel]

Why the star rather than the menorah is a matter of interpretation. Scholem argued that the star was chosen precisely because it lacked the menorah's religious weight, which left it "available" for a secular political project that needed a Jewish symbol without tying itself to rabbinic authority. [cite:scholem_star_david] The documented record supports a plainer explanation as well: by the 1890s the Magen David was the closest thing to a widely recognized, non-sectarian Jewish emblem, legible across a movement that drew members of many religious and secular backgrounds.

The state then adopted the movement's banner. When Israel declared independence on May 14, 1948, the Provisional Council of State took the Basel flag as the state flag, formalized on October 28, 1948. [cite:declaration_israel_1948] [cite:wiki_flag_of_israel] The menorah went on the state seal in 1949. The decision was not made without debate. The Provisional Council first tried to create a distinct state flag, and its stated concern was that Diaspora Jews who already used the Zionist flag would face accusations of dual loyalty if their movement's banner became a foreign state's flag. An alternative seven-stars design proposed by Otto Wassisch was considered and rejected. In July 1948 Foreign Minister Moshe Sharett cabled Diaspora Jewish leaders for their views, and the answer came back overwhelmingly in favor of keeping the existing flag. The emblem's process was more contested still. A selection committee rejected 450 proposals from 164 applicants outright, a second public competition was held, and the winning design by the Shamir brothers was sent back twice: once to add the word "Israel," and once to replace their modern menorah with the historical version carved on the Arch of Titus. [cite:crwflags_israel_flag_1948] [cite:wjc_flag_unveiled_1948] [cite:nli_emblem_israel_blog]

Both facts hold at once. The choice was considered and consulted on, and it still placed the movement's symbol on the flag and the Temple's on the seal. The movement never hid that this was its 1897 banner; the history is public and uncontested. What it shows is that the flag's lineage runs through a nineteenth-century political movement rather than through the Temple period.

One reading goes further. It holds that a state emerging from a religious tradition might have led with that tradition's oldest symbol, and that leading with the movement's emblem signals which lineage the state understands itself to continue: the political project of Herzl and the Congress. [cite:basel_program] A related reading holds that displaying the flag in synagogues, schools and community centers teaches, below the level of argument, that the political project of 1897 and the religious tradition of millennia are one thing, the same conflation traced in [scene:s00c|the section on Zionism and Judaism]. These are interpretations, not documented findings. Many Israelis and Jews elsewhere experience the flag as encompassing both histories, and most who display it do so out of a genuine connection to Israel or to Jewish identity, not as part of any messaging campaign.
