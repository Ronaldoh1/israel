Who Coordinates What Gets Posted

A common assumption holds that pro-Israel content on social media is simply organic enthusiasm, and that any suggestion of coordination is conspiracy thinking. The record for the years 2018 to 2026 is more specific than either the assumption or its opposite. It names firms, contracts, sums of money, a leaked set of instructions, and a meeting attended by a head of government. It also documents influence operations running in the other direction. Influence work around this conflict is not the property of one side.

The most concrete case is STOIC, an Israeli political consulting firm. Israel's Ministry of Diaspora Affairs paid it $2 million to run covert, AI-generated content carrying pro-Israel messaging about the Gaza war, aimed at younger, progressive Americans. FakeReporter, an Israeli watchdog group that tracks misinformation, identified the operation and tied five specific websites to it. The New York Times verified the reporting. Meta said it deleted the associated Facebook and Instagram accounts before they gained traction with real users, and STOIC did not respond to interview requests. [cite:npr_stoic_ai_influence] The case shows a funder, a firm and a dollar figure, corroborated from several directions. It does not show that this was the largest such effort, and by Meta's account it was caught before it reached many people.

A second paper trail runs through the Foreign Agents Registration Act, the American law that requires those working for foreign governments to disclose their work. Bridges Partners, a Washington-based firm working with Israel's Ministry of Foreign Affairs through Havas Media Group Germany, filed documents showing roughly $900,000 invoiced for an "Influencer Campaign" running from June to November 2025. After administrative and production costs, roughly $552,946 remained for payments to influencers, covering an estimated 75 to 90 posts. [cite:honestreporting_bridges_partners_fara] This is a government-mandated disclosure, a stronger kind of evidence than a figure repeated online. It describes one disclosed contract, not the whole public-diplomacy apparatus of which it was a part.

Disclosure filings show that money moved. An investigation by Time Magazine showed what coordination looked like from the inside. It revealed private group chats in which influencers taking part in a pro-Israel campaign were told what specific language to use on X, Instagram and TikTok, and were paid up to $4,250 per post. In a separate case, a Jewish influencer reported receiving a confidential brief from a different campaign, offering $1,500 to promote specific talking points under a non-disclosure agreement. He declined to sign. [cite:meo_israel_influencer_backfire] The refusal belongs in the record as much as the offer: not everyone accepted the arrangement. Nor does the investigation show that everyone who posted pro-Israel content in this period was part of a paid campaign. Unpaid supporters existed alongside the paid ones.

The effort reached the top of the Israeli government. In September 2025, in New York, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu met personally with several American social media influencers to enlist them in amplifying pro-Israel messaging in the United States. [cite:meo_israel_influencer_backfire] The meeting is documented. What was said inside it, and whether every influencer present agreed to take part, is not part of the public record.

On campus, coordination has also taken the form of deception. The Israel on Campus Coalition, the main convening organization of pro-Israel campus groups, has been reported to gather information on pro-Palestinian students through informants inside Jewish and pro-Israel student organizations, and to pass it to Israel's Ministry of Strategic Affairs. That claim rests on reporting and is less independently verified. A second claim about the same organization is firmer. The coalition secretly ran misleading Facebook pages and ads targeting the Palestinian-American poet Remi Kanazi, and in 2018 Facebook itself removed the pages, citing its policies against misrepresentation. [cite:wiki_israel_campus_coalition] The two claims carry different weight and should be kept apart. Neither shows that such tactics are unique to this organization, or that every affiliated campus group practiced them.

The same record shows coordination aimed against Israel. An Iran-linked network operated fake profiles, bots and groups across WhatsApp, X, Telegram, Facebook, TikTok and Instagram. It spread disinformation, impersonated journalists and rabbis, and worked to stoke tensions among Israelis themselves, including content aimed at Israel's LGBTQ+ community and at ultra-Orthodox Jews. [cite:calcalist_iran_social_media_network] A 2025 report from an independent research organization, "Conflict Amplified: Disinformation and Hate in the Israel-Hamas War," documented disinformation and coordinated hate campaigns from multiple sides of the conflict. [cite:isd_conflict_amplified_2025] The sources do not establish that these operations matched the pro-Israel campaigns described above in size or reach, only that both exist.

Taken together, the evidence describes a real, funded, coordinated messaging apparatus: named firms, contracts disclosed under federal law, private instructions to paid posters, and recruitment by a head of government. It does not support the claim that every pro-Israel or pro-Palestinian post online is centrally directed. The documents settle that coordination exists. They do not settle what any single post means.
