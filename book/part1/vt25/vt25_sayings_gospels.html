The Sayings Gospels: Thomas, Truth, Philip and the Secret Dialogues

Several Nag Hammadi texts call themselves gospels or present Jesus teaching his disciples in private. They are the texts most often cited for the claim that early Christians were taught to find the kingdom within themselves rather than through a church. This section takes each in turn. It gives what the text is, what it says in order, its core teachings in plain words, a few short quotations from the standard English translations, what bishops and heresy writers said about it, and where scholars disagree. The quotations are short because the modern translations are under copyright. [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin]

The Gospel of Thomas (Codex II, tractate 2)

What it is. The Gospel of Thomas is a list of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, most introduced only by "Jesus said". There is no birth story, no miracles, no trial, no crucifixion and no resurrection narrative. It opens: "These are the secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down." [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] The only complete copy is the Coptic one in Codex II, written shortly before 350. Parts of the same work in Greek had already been found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt by Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt between 1897 and 1904, without anyone knowing which work they belonged to. Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 1, now in the Bodleian Library in Oxford, dates from shortly after 200, and Papyri 654 and 655 from the mid-third century. [cite:vt25_ecw_thomas]

Outline. The sayings are not arranged by plot. They are grouped loosely by catchword and theme. The prologue and saying 1 promise that whoever finds the interpretation of the sayings "will not experience death". Sayings 2 to 5 deal with seeking, finding and the kingdom. Sayings 6 and 14 take up fasting, prayer and alms. There follow parables (the sower, the mustard seed, the net, the pearl, the leaven, the lost sheep), many with close parallels in Matthew, Mark and Luke, and sayings about the light within, the world as a corpse, becoming "a single one", and Jesus's identity. The collection closes with saying 114, an exchange about Mary. [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin]

Core teachings. Salvation in Thomas comes through understanding, not through belief in a saving death. The reader is to seek, be troubled, marvel and finally "rule over the All" (saying 2). The kingdom is not a future place in the sky. Saying 3 mocks leaders who say the kingdom is in the sky, since then "the birds of the sky will precede you", and continues: "the Kingdom is inside of you, and it is outside of you." Saying 113 adds that it "will not come by waiting for it" and that the kingdom "is spread out upon the earth, and men do not see it". [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] The emphasis on what lies within also appears in saying 70: "If you bring forth what is within you, what you bring forth will save you." In saying 108 the speaker promises that whoever drinks from his mouth "will become like Me". [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] On religious practice the text is pointed. Asked whether the disciples should fast, pray and give alms, Jesus answers in saying 14: "If you fast, you will give rise to sin for yourselves; and if you pray, you will be condemned". [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] No church office, sacrament or payment appears anywhere in the 114 sayings.

The text does not support a simple egalitarian reading. In saying 114 Peter says "Let Mary leave us, for women are not worthy of Life", and Jesus answers that he will "make her male" so that she too may become a living spirit. [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] Scholars read this as metaphor for transcending the body and its divisions, but the saying assumes that the female has to be transformed.

What the institution said. The earliest writer who quotes Thomas by name is Hippolytus of Rome, writing between 222 and 235. He says the Naassenes, a group he classed as heretics, quoted a saying "according to Thomas" about finding the kingdom in a child of seven. He calls them "impious" and attributes the saying to the physician Hippocrates rather than to Christ. [cite:vt25_hipp_ref5] [cite:vt25_ecw_thomas] Around 350 Cyril, bishop of Jerusalem, told candidates for baptism that only four gospels were authentic and that "The Manichæans also wrote a Gospel according to Thomas" that "corrupts the souls of the simple sort". He added that books not read in church should not be read privately either. [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] Cyril's attribution cannot be right as stated: the Greek fragments of Thomas date from about 200, before Mani was born in 216. The Decretum Gelasianum, a sixth-century list issued under the names of Popes Damasus and Gelasius, classes "the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use" among apocryphal books, under a closing anathema. [cite:vt25_decretum] [cite:vt25_ecw_thomas] These are warnings and lists of books not to read. None of them orders copies destroyed.

What scholars dispute. The date of Thomas and its relationship to the other gospels are the main disputes. Stephen Patterson has argued for composition around 70 to 80 CE, and Ron Cameron for the late first century. On their view Thomas preserves an independent stream of Jesus tradition, perhaps as early as some material in the canonical gospels. [cite:vt25_ecw_thomas] Mark Goodacre and Simon Gathercole argue that Thomas knew and reworded Matthew, Mark and Luke. Goodacre points to word-for-word agreements and dates the final form to around 135. Critics reply that oral tradition explains such agreements as well. [cite:vt25_goodacre_thomas] Most scholars now accept that the collection grew in layers, which leaves open how early its earliest sayings are.

The Gospel of Truth (Codex I, tractate 3; Codex XII, tractate 2)

What it is. Despite its title, the Gospel of Truth is a meditative sermon, not an account of Jesus's life. It survives almost complete in the Jung Codex and in fragments in Codex XII. The Greek original is usually dated between 140 and 180. Several scholars have proposed Valentinus himself as the author; others assign it to his followers. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth]

Outline and teachings. The sermon opens with joy at knowing the Father, then tells how ignorance of the Father produced anguish and terror. Error took shape like a fog and built its own substitute world. Jesus came as a teacher who exposed that error, and Error persecuted him and nailed him to a tree. When he taught, "the living book of the living" appeared in the hearts of those who listened. The sermon closes with the return of those who know to the Father and their rest in him. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] Its central teaching is that life without knowledge of God is like a nightmare, and knowing God is like waking up. Salvation is the end of ignorance, and the believer's goal is rest. [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth]

What the institution said. Irenaeus of Lyon, writing around 180, complained that the Valentinians had "arrived at such a pitch of audacity, as to entitle their comparatively recent writing the Gospel of Truth", which "agrees in nothing with the Gospels of the Apostles". [cite:vt25_iren_311] The same chapter makes his case that there can be exactly four gospels, neither more nor fewer. [cite:vt25_iren_311] Whether the Nag Hammadi sermon is the work Irenaeus meant is probable but not proven, since he does not quote it.

The Gospel of Philip (Codex II, tractate 3)

What it is. The Gospel of Philip is a collection of short teachings, sayings and comments, composed in Greek and preserved only in Coptic. Bentley Layton described it as a Valentinian anthology, and scholars generally place it in the school of Valentinus. Most date it to the third century. [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] [cite:vt25_nasscal_gphil]

Teachings. Philip is a sacramental text. It is often described as hostile to ritual, and it is not. "The Lord did everything in a mystery," it says: "a baptism and a chrism and a eucharist and a redemption and a bridal chamber." [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] The bridal chamber, the highest of the five, is a rite that joins male and female to each other and to the divine. Scholars dispute whether it was a performed ritual, and whether it had any sexual element. [cite:vt25_nasscal_gphil] On resurrection, Philip teaches that it must be received in this life: "Those who say they will die first and then rise are in error." [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] Philip also calls Mary Magdalene Jesus's koinōnos, a Greek loanword that can mean companion, partner or spouse. The often-cited passage saying that Jesus "used to kiss her" breaks off at the decisive word because the papyrus is damaged. One translator restored "mouth"; the critical edition leaves the gap open, and the text cannot settle the question. [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil]

What the institution said. No surviving ancient condemnation names this Gospel of Philip. Its school, the Valentinians, was the main target of Irenaeus's Against Heresies and later heresy writers, as Part Twenty-Four sets out. [scene:vt24_the_heresy_hunters|Part Twenty-Four: The Heresy Hunters]

The dialogue gospels: James, Thomas the Contender and the Saviour

Three further texts present the risen Jesus teaching chosen disciples in private. The Apocryphon (Secret Book) of James (Codex I, 2), probably written in Egypt in the second century, is framed as a letter from James the Just about a revelation given to him and Peter. Jesus tells them to "be filled" with the spirit and not to fear suffering, and warns them not to let "the kingdom of heaven become a desert within them". [cite:vt25_wiki_apjas] The Book of Thomas the Contender (Codex II, 7) is the most ascetic text in this group. It teaches that whoever knows himself comes to know the depth of all things, and calls for renouncing visible, bodily pleasures and the burning fire of desire. [cite:vt25_wiki_thomcont] The Dialogue of the Saviour (Codex III, 5), probably complete by about 150, has Judas, Matthew and Mary questioning Jesus about rest, the place of life, and the dissolution of "the works of womanhood" at the end of bodily existence. Unusually for this library, it treats the Father as the good creator rather than blaming a lower god. [cite:vt25_wiki_dialsav]

A Valentinian letter belongs with these texts for its subject. The Treatise on the Resurrection (Codex I, 4), addressed to a student named Rheginos, holds that the resurrection is real, not a metaphor, but that believers should already count themselves risen in spirit. Most scholars date it to the second half of the second century. [cite:vt25_wiki_treatres]

What this group shows

These texts give the clearest documentary support for the "kingdom within" theme of this book. Thomas places the kingdom inside and outside the hearer and rejects the outward practices of fasting, prayer and alms. Truth makes knowledge itself the means of salvation. James warns against letting the kingdom become a desert within. [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] [cite:vt25_wiki_gtruth] [cite:vt25_wiki_apjas] They do not support the claim that every Gnostic text rejected ritual and teachers. Philip prizes five sacraments, Thomas the Contender is rigorously ascetic, and the Valentinian authors wrote as teachers instructing pupils. [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] [cite:vt25_wiki_thomcont] The record of what bishops said about these books is consistent. From Irenaeus in about 180 to the Decretum in the sixth century, they warned against them, excluded them from church reading and anathematised the books and their authors. No surviving document orders the books burned. [cite:vt25_iren_311] [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] [cite:vt25_decretum] The next section turns to the second group: texts that do not teach in short sayings but tell a whole hidden history of the cosmos.
