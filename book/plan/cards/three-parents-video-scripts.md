## LONDON: THE FINANCIAL PARENT

### London 1: The city where businesses vote

**On screen:** the Square Mile's boundary glowing inside Greater London, then a ballot box filling with company logos (generic, no real logos).

Inside London there's another London: the City of London, one square mile, about 8,600 residents. And it runs elections unlike anywhere else in Britain.

Businesses vote. Everywhere else in the UK, business votes in local elections were abolished in 1969. Here they were kept, and in 2002 they were widened. A firm gets one voter for every five staff, up to ten, then one more for every fifty. In 2013 one campaign group counted about 32,000 business votes against about 9,000 residents' votes.

The Lord Mayor isn't chosen by voters at all, but by the City's livery companies, the descendants of medieval guilds.

At the last election, in March 2025, 45 of the 100 council seats were filled without a single vote being cast.

It's not a country. It still needs Parliament's permission to change its own rules. But no other council in Britain works like this.

### London 2: The City's own officer in Parliament

**On screen:** the Houses of Parliament, then a single empty chair in the gallery.

Since 1571, the City of London has had an officer no other council in Britain has: the Remembrancer.

His job is to watch Parliament for the City. The office tracks every bill that touches the City's interests, briefs MPs, gives evidence to committees and drafts the City's own private bills. In 2011 it reportedly had a £6 million budget and six lawyers.

You may have heard he sits on the floor of the House of Commons. He doesn't. Parliament says he watches from the visitors' gallery, by permission, and can't speak or vote.

But the record shows what the office has done when the City's privileges were threatened. Between 1835 and 1875, by one account, its lobbying helped defeat reform at least twelve times. In 1884 a campaign it largely ran killed a government bill to reform London, and the Remembrancer later admitted paying a go-between to have men break up meetings.

### London 3: The money machine, and what London actually did

**On screen:** the 1694 Bank of England charter, then a currency trading screen, then the Balfour letter.

London's power is money. In 1694, 1,268 subscribers lent the Crown £1.2 million in eleven days, through a private company: the Bank of England. It stayed privately owned for two and a half centuries.

Today the UK handles 37.8 percent of all the currency trading on earth.

So was London Israel's financial parent? The record says yes, but not the London most people mean. It was the British government and London's legal and financial system. The Balfour promise in 1917. The Mandate. The movement's bank, land fund and fundraising arm, all set up as English companies in London. Palestine's currency, run from London for twenty-one years.

The City of London Corporation, the medieval council, appears in none of those decisions.

And Britain didn't stay a parent. From 1939 it capped immigration. In 1947 it abstained on partition. In 1948 it blocked Palestine's sterling.

## THE VATICAN: THE THEOLOGICAL PARENT

### Vatican 1: A country that leans on its neighbour for everything

**On screen:** St Peter's Square from above, then a map showing water pipes, rail and prisons crossing into Italy.

Vatican City is the smallest country on earth: about 882 residents. Its ruler, the pope, holds all lawmaking, executive and judicial power. He's elected for life by the cardinals.

But this sovereign state can barely run itself. Under the 1929 treaty, Italy supplies its water and its rail, post and phone links. St Peter's Square is patrolled by Italian police. The Vatican has no prison beyond a few holding cells, so anyone its courts convict serves time in an Italian prison, at the Vatican's expense. Even its euro coins are struck by Italy's state mint.

And it was paid to exist. In 1929 Mussolini's government handed the Holy See 750 million lire in cash and 1 billion lire in bonds, to settle the land it lost in 1870.

Full sovereignty, almost no territory, and dependent on its neighbour for everything except authority.

### Vatican 2: The pope's bankers

**On screen:** an 1831 loan contract, then the Vatican Bank's tower, then a London townhouse.

People imagine a Vatican rich enough to fund nations. The record shows the money mostly flowed in.

In 1831 Pope Gregory XVI borrowed £400,000 from the Rothschilds, with a clause giving them first notice and preference on any new loan. The lenders used that position to press for better treatment of the Jews living under the pope.

In 1942 the Vatican created its bank, the IOR. In 1982, after Banco Ambrosiano collapsed, the bank denied liability but paid creditors $224 million.

More recently, the Vatican lost about €140 million on a London building. In 2023 its own court convicted Cardinal Becciu of embezzlement; that case was reopened on appeal and is still unresolved.

And in 2026 the Vatican Bank got a new president: a Luxembourg banker who once ran the Edmond de Rothschild group, a different firm from the 1831 lenders.

### Vatican 3: Parent of the idea, opponent of the state

**On screen:** a 1904 papal audience, the 1947 UN vote board, then a 1909 study Bible.

Was the Vatican secretly behind Israel? We tested that claim against its own private papers.

In 1904 Pius X told Herzl the Church "could never sanction" a Jewish return. In 1922 the Vatican formally objected to the Mandate. In 1943 its Secretary of State wrote privately against a Palestine "under Jewish predominance". It refused to recognize Israel for forty-five years, until 1993.

During the 1947 vote it stayed silent, but for its own aim: the plan put Jerusalem under international rule, which the Vatican wanted. No document shows it steering the Latin American votes, and no money moved from it to the movement.

But here's the twist. The Christian theology that became Israel's most loyal support grew out of the Vatican's church, through the Protestant break. In 1944 the Vatican ruled internally that it "cannot safely be taught", and never said so publicly.

## WASHINGTON: THE MILITARY PARENT

### Washington 1: The capital whose people don't count

**On screen:** the Capitol dome, then the DC flag, then "Taxation without representation" licence plates (no real logos).

Washington, DC is the only American city that belongs to no state. About 694,000 people live there, and they have no vote in the body that rules them.

Congress can overrule their laws. Every act the city council passes must sit before Congress for 30 days, or 60 for criminal law. Congress has struck down four of them, the latest in 2023. Even the city's own tax money has to pass through the federal budget.

The President appoints DC's judges and its chief prosecutor, and commands its National Guard. In August 2025 he took federal control of the city police for the first time ever. That control expired after 30 days, but the troops stayed. As of October 2026 about 2,900 were still deployed, with the mission extended to January 2029.

The capital was born in 1790 from a deal over war debts.

### Washington 2: Eleven minutes, and an embargo

**On screen:** a clock reading 6:11, the recognition statement, then crates stamped "embargoed".

On 14 May 1948, Israel declared independence. Eleven minutes later, President Truman recognized it, on his own authority, over the written objection of his Secretary of State, George Marshall.

But here's what's usually left out. That same year, the United States had an arms embargo in force. Israel's weapons for its founding war came from Czechoslovakia. The first full recognition came from Moscow.

So at the birth, Washington gave recognition, not the means to survive.

That changed later. From 1962 came the first arms sales, from 1968 the US was Israel's main supplier, and after 1973 the aid became huge. About $175 billion through 2025. A legal guarantee of Israel's military edge since 2008. More than fifty Security Council vetoes.

The volume's verdict: Washington wasn't the parent at birth. It's the parent by adoption, the one who raised and armed the child.

## HOW ISRAEL'S GOVERNMENT WORKS

### Israel 1: A state without a constitution

**On screen:** the 1948 Declaration, a blank page marked "Constitution", then a ticking calendar.

Israel's Declaration of Independence promised a constitution by October 1948. It never came.

Instead, in 1950 the Knesset decided to pass "Basic Laws" one at a time. Most can be changed by a simple majority. So there's no constitutional floor a governing coalition can't lower.

Other states share pieces of this. Britain has no written constitution either. What's rare is the combination. A state of emergency in force every day since May 1948, renewable without limit. No borders named at independence; Ben-Gurion said they didn't need to set them. A Law of Return that gives citizenship by descent to Jews anywhere. And the 2018 Nation-State Law, passed 62 to 55, which says national self-determination in Israel is "unique to the Jewish people", in a country where about a fifth of citizens are Arab.

Israel's government says a small state facing enemies needed room to act. Critics say the same design keeps its limits open-ended.

### Israel 2: How small parties hold big power

**On screen:** a 120-seat Knesset chart, two small blocks lighting up red.

Israelis don't vote for a prime minister. They vote for party lists. No party has ever won a majority on its own, so every Israeli government is a coalition, and the smaller the margin, the more a small party can demand.

After 2022, Itamar Ben-Gvir's party, with six seats, got control of the police. Bezalel Smotrich's, with seven, got civilian control of the West Bank. Thirteen seats out of 120 held two of the most important levers in the country.

Meanwhile the prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, has been on trial for bribery, fraud and breach of trust since 2020. He denies it. Under Israeli law a prime minister only has to resign after a final conviction.

The October 2026 election left neither side with a majority. Gadi Eisenkot was asked to form a government. As of mid-November, talks were still going, with a deadline of 20 December.

### Israel 3: The people who can't vote for it

**On screen:** the West Bank map split into Areas A, B and C, then a decision chain with "no vote" at the bottom.

There's a part of Israel's government most people never see: the part that rules millions of Palestinians who can't vote for it.

West Bank decisions don't go through a Knesset vote. A coalition minister proposes, the security cabinet or a ministerial committee approves, the army commander signs a military order, and Israeli bodies carry it out. Palestinians have no vote at any stage.

In February 2026 the security cabinet approved its biggest West Bank package in years: easier land purchases, Israeli planning control in Hebron, and enforcement reaching into Area A, which Oslo gave to the Palestinian Authority. Smotrich's office said the aim was to "bury the idea of a Palestinian state".

Nineteen months earlier, the International Court of Justice had found the occupation unlawful. Israel rejected the opinion. And at the Security Council, the one UN body whose decisions bind, more than fifty American vetoes have shielded it.
