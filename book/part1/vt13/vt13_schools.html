Schools, Hospitals and Who Runs Them

Claims about the Vatican's reach usually begin with a number: so many schools, so many universities, so many hospitals, all said to answer to Rome. The numbers exist, and they are large. What they measure needs care. They come from the Church's own statistical yearbook, the Annuarium Statisticum Ecclesiae, which Agenzia Fides, the news agency of the Pontifical Mission Societies, summarises each October for World Mission Day. The edition released on 17 October 2025 gives Catholic population figures to 30 June 2023 and all other figures to 31 December 2023, and it describes the institutions as those the Church "runs". [cite:vt13_fides2025] This section sets out those figures, worldwide and by continent, and then asks what "Catholic" means in law for a school or university, because the answer decides whether these numbers describe a network the Holy See commands or something looser.

The worldwide count

At the end of 2023 the yearbook counted 74,550 Catholic kindergartens with 7,639,051 pupils, 102,455 primary schools with 36,199,844 pupils and 52,085 secondary schools with 20,724,361 pupils. [cite:vt13_pv_dossier] [cite:vt13_fides2025] Taken together that is 229,090 schools and about 64.6 million pupils. Beyond school age, the same summary counted 2,688,625 students in higher institutes and 4,468,875 students in Catholic universities. [cite:vt13_pv_dossier] [cite:vt13_cna_stats] The summary gives no count of universities themselves, only of their students, so any figure for the number of Catholic universities in the world has to come from elsewhere and will depend on the definition used.

The same yearbook counted 103,951 charitable and health institutions. Among them were 5,377 hospitals, 13,895 dispensaries, 504 leprosaria, 15,566 homes for the elderly, the chronically ill and the disabled, 8,593 orphanages, 10,858 creches, 10,827 marriage counselling centres, 3,147 centres for social education or rehabilitation and 35,184 institutions of other kinds. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf] [cite:vt13_fides2025] The figures do not travel cleanly between versions. The Holy See Press Office's Italian summary omits the orphanages, so its listed categories add up to 95,358 rather than the stated total, and the National Catholic Register's English report gives "other" institutions as 5,184 where Fides and the press office give 35,184. [cite:vt13_pv_dossier] [cite:vt13_cna_stats] The continental tables in the Fides dossier close the gap: with 8,593 orphanages the categories sum to 103,951. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf]

Where the institutions are

The continental breakdown changes the picture of a European or American network. Africa had 45,206 of the world's 102,455 Catholic primary schools and 21,125,043 of their 36,199,844 pupils, which is 58 per cent of all Catholic primary pupils; an average African Catholic primary school taught about 470 children against about 190 in Europe. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf] Africa also had the most Catholic secondary schools (18,392) and the most hospitals (1,634, or 30 per cent of the total). [cite:vt13_fides_pdf] University education points the other way: 2,543,486 of the 4,468,875 students in Catholic universities, 57 per cent, were in the Americas, and 1,032,715 in Europe. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf] Europe dominated one category of care: 8,515 of the 15,566 homes for the elderly, chronically ill and disabled, and 5,092 of the 10,827 marriage counselling centres. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf]

The distribution follows where Catholics live and where the state does or does not provide the service. Of the 1.405 billion Catholics counted at 30 June 2023, 671.9 million lived in the Americas, 286.3 million in Europe, 280.7 million in Africa, 155.2 million in Asia and 11.3 million in Oceania. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf] Asia is the clearest case of reach beyond the faithful: Catholics were 3.3 per cent of its population, yet the yearbook counted 16,273 Catholic primary schools with 5,687,465 pupils and 11,629 secondary schools with 5,974,049, more secondary pupils than in Europe. [cite:vt13_fides_pdf]

National counts are kept separately and on their own methods. In the United States the National Catholic Educational Association reported on 24 March 2026 that there were 5,829 Catholic schools in the 2025–26 school year, 4,644 elementary and middle schools and 1,185 secondary schools, with 1,674,907 pupils, of whom 22.2 per cent (372,244) were not Catholic. [cite:vt13_ncea2026] Its figures come from the education offices of 176 dioceses, which shows where the records, and the responsibility, sit. [cite:vt13_ncea2026]

What makes a school Catholic

The Code of Canon Law of 1983 defines the term. Under canon 803 a Catholic school is one that "a competent ecclesiastical authority or a public ecclesiastical juridic person directs", or one that ecclesiastical authority recognises as Catholic in a written document; no school may call itself Catholic without that authority's consent. [cite:vt13_cic_803] In practice the directing authority is a diocesan bishop, a parish or a religious institute: canon 806 gives the diocesan bishop the right "to watch over and visit the Catholic schools in his territory, even those which members of religious institutes have founded or direct." [cite:vt13_cic_803] For universities, canon 808 likewise reserves the title "Catholic university" to institutions with the consent of competent ecclesiastical authority, canon 810 makes the authority named in each university's statutes responsible for appointing teachers of sound doctrine, and canon 812 requires those who teach theological disciplines to hold a mandate from that authority. [cite:vt13_cic_803]

The Holy See's own role is narrower than the totals suggest. Canon 816 reserves to the Apostolic See the erection or approval of "ecclesiastical" universities and faculties, those that teach theology, canon law and related sacred disciplines, and gives it their "higher direction"; their statutes and plans of study must be approved in Rome. [cite:vt13_cic_803] Since the 2022 reform of the Curia that work belongs to the Dicastery for Culture and Education. [cite:vt6_va_pe] [scene:vt6_curia_2022|Part Six: the Curia since 2022] The tens of thousands of parish primary schools in Africa and Asia, the hospitals and the homes for the elderly are counted in the yearbook because dioceses and orders report them, not because the Holy See owns or manages them. Each is directed by its own bishop, parish or order under canon law and, in civil law, by whatever body the local state recognises. The figures therefore measure the reach of a church spread across many legal owners, bound by a common law and a common head, and should not be read as the property or payroll of the Vatican, a confusion that underlies several inflated wealth figures corrected in Part Nine. [scene:vt9_true_vs_public_wealth|Part Nine: true and public wealth]

Within that common law the Holy See's levers over a school are indirect but real: it appoints the bishop who may visit and supervise it, it sets the canons that define it, and through the bishop it controls the use of the name. [cite:vt13_cic_803] The last section of this part follows the most important of those levers, the appointment of bishops. [scene:vt13_appointments_nuncios|How bishops are appointed]
