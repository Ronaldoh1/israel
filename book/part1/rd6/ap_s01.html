The Absentees' Property Law: Citizens Declared "Absent"

The war of 1948 displaced hundreds of thousands of people. What turned that displacement into a permanent transfer of property was not the fighting itself but a body of law passed after it. The familiar account holds that Palestinians who remained inside Israel in 1948 kept their homes and land as full citizens. For a large number of them, the law said otherwise.

The Absentees' Property Law, 5710-1950, was passed in March 1950. It built on an emergency regulation of 1948 concerning absentee property. Its own text provides that all absentees' property is vested in a government-appointed Custodian of Absentees' Property, that every right an absentee had passes automatically to the Custodian, that anyone holding absentee property must hand it over, and that the fact an absentee's identity is unknown does not prevent the property from being vested. [cite:un_absentees_property_law_text] Anyone found occupying such property, including its original owner, was in violation of the law. [cite:wiki_present_absentee]

Everything turned on the definition of "absentee." It reached anyone who had left his usual place of residence during the war, whether for areas outside Israeli control or for anywhere else. That captured the refugees beyond the new borders. It also captured tens of thousands of Arabs who had moved from one village to another inside Israel and who were afterward registered as citizens, issued identity numbers, and allowed to vote and to stand for the Knesset. In law they were absent; in fact they were citizens living in the country. They came to be called "present absentees," and they could not reclaim their property. [cite:wiki_present_absentee]

The numbers were not small. By 2001 the present absentees were estimated at about 200,000 people, nearly 17 percent of Israel's Arab citizens. Half the populations of the two largest Arab towns in Israel are internally displaced. [cite:wiki_present_absentee] [cite:wiki_land_day] [cite:cornell_bilsky_absentee_law]

The international objection came early. In April 1949, before the permanent law was passed, the UN Conciliation Commission for Palestine proposed, as a preliminary measure, "suspension of the application of the Absentee law until the final peace settlement." Arab delegations argued that the law treated nationals of Palestine as enemy aliens. Israel did not suspend it, and the 1950 law made the arrangement permanent. [cite:un_ccp_absentee_1949]

The law did not work alone. From 1948 to 1966 Israel's Arab citizens lived under military administrative rule, which restricted their movement. Together, the two instruments kept internally displaced citizens from physically returning to reclaim their homes, a subject the next part of this book takes up in its own right. Salman Abu-Sitta estimates that more than 1,000 square kilometres were expropriated from Arab citizens between 1948 and 2003; that figure is his estimate. [cite:wiki_present_absentee] [cite:wiki_land_day]

The law remains in force and has been applied in East Jerusalem. Its mechanism is simple to state. The word "absentee" kept its ordinary meaning in public speech, while its legal reach was widened to include people who had never left the country. The flight of 1948 is documented elsewhere in this book; this law documents how that flight became permanent ownership by the state, and how it reached even those who stayed.
