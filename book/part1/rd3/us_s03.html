Bringing In the Non-Zionist Money: The Enlarged Jewish Agency of 1929

In August 1929, at Zurich, the Zionist movement brought some of the wealthiest American Jews inside its institutions. The result was an enlarged Jewish Agency in which non-Zionists held half the seats. It is easy to assume that Zionism was always the consensus view of American Jewish leadership. In the 1920s it was not. Much of the wealthiest American Jewish leadership was non-Zionist, and the movement's leaders spent years trying to bring that wealth inside. Zurich was the institutional move that made the Agency the body that would govern the pre-state Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine.

The division showed first in fundraising. The American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, a non-Zionist body known as the Joint and chaired by the banker Felix Warburg, ran its own campaigns. The Zionist Organization of America, the ZOA, saw them as rivals and clashed with the Joint over them. To protect Keren Hayesod, the movement's fundraising arm, the ZOA created a United Palestine Appeal under Rabbi Stephen Wise [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. Two rival streams of American Jewish money, one Zionist and one not, were competing for the same donors.

Chaim Weizmann set out to merge them. From December 1924 to February 1925 he visited the United States. He came to negotiate an expanded Jewish Agency with Louis Marshall, president of the American Jewish Committee, and to launch the annual Keren Hayesod campaign [cite:einstein_papers_vol15_doc625]. The negotiation took years. It concluded at Zurich in August 1929, when the Sixteenth Zionist Congress ratified the agreement and the Council of the enlarged Jewish Agency met. Einstein, Léon Blum and Herbert Samuel were among those present.

The terms were striking. Seats on the Council were split equally between Zionists and non-Zionists. Louis Marshall became chairman of the Council and Lord Melchett associate chairman. Felix Warburg, the same man who had chaired the rival Joint, became chairman of the Administrative Committee. Keren Hayesod was recognized as the Agency's main financial instrument [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14]. Weizmann's papers record Marshall and Warburg assuring him that "financial troubles are over" [cite:israeled_weizmann_vol14_pdf].

Weeks later, in September 1929, Marshall died. The source on the Agency's founding places his death in the same month as the riots in Palestine, which other records date to August 1929; that violence is described in [scene:s07a4|Hebron, 1929]. Either way, the enlarged Agency lost its chairman within weeks of its creation, in the same season as the 1929 violence.

What mattered about Zurich was less any single figure than its structure. By 1929 the body that would run the pre-state Yishuv had brought wealthy American non-Zionists into its own governance, widening its funding base far beyond committed Zionists. That happened nineteen years before 1948. When the Jewish Agency appears later in this book as the government-in-waiting, this is the arrangement on which it was built.
