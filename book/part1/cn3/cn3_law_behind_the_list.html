The Law Behind the List: Nicaea to Theodosius

The Council of Nicaea in 325 is remembered as the moment the church defined its faith. It is just as accurately remembered as the moment an emperor took charge of a church dispute. Constantine summoned the bishops, carried them to Nicaea by the imperial post, opened the council in person and, according to Eusebius of Caesarea, urged the insertion into the creed of the word homoousios, "of the same substance", to describe the Son's relation to the Father. [cite:vt22_vc3] The council did not discuss which books were scripture, as Part One showed. It did something that mattered more for the later enforcement of any list: it established that a definition agreed by bishops under the emperor's eye would be backed by the emperor's power. [cite:vt22_canons] [scene:vt22_what_it_decided|What Nicaea decided]

The backing was immediate. The bishops who refused to sign were exiled. Arius, whose teaching the creed condemned, was banished, and Constantine wrote that any treatise composed by him should be "consigned to the flames", adding that anyone caught concealing one would be put to death. [cite:vt21_socrates19] [cite:vt22_socrates1] This is the earliest documented order by a Christian emperor to burn a Christian's books, and the penalty was not for writing them but for keeping them. The target was a theological opponent, not a rival gospel. But the mechanism, the state destroying writings that the bishops in communion with it had condemned, was now in place, and it did not depend on which books were at issue.

The mechanism was not yet stable, because the emperor's own commitment was not. Constantine's letter of about 324 had called the dispute "this truly idle question", which suggests he wanted agreement more than any particular answer, and within a few years he recalled the exiles. The bishop who baptised him on his deathbed in 337, Eusebius of Nicomedia, was one of the men exiled after Nicaea. [cite:vt22_vc2] [cite:vt22_wiki_eusnic] For half a century afterwards the Nicene and non-Nicene parties took turns holding imperial favour, and bishops were exiled and restored as the emperors changed. Athanasius of Alexandria, the most stubborn defender of Nicaea, was banished repeatedly from 336 onwards. [cite:cn_brit_athanasius] The lesson the bishops drew from those decades was not that the state should stay out of doctrine. It was that the state's power should be secured permanently for the right side.

That came on 27 February 380. The emperors Theodosius I, Gratian and Valentinian II issued the edict known by its opening words, Cunctos populos. It required all the peoples under their rule to follow the faith "delivered to the Romans by the divine Apostle Peter", as professed by two named bishops, Damasus of Rome and Peter of Alexandria. Those who did not were declared heretics; they could not call their meeting places churches, and they faced divine punishment first and imperial punishment after. [cite:vt21_wiki_thessalonica] The Council of Constantinople in 381 then gave the Nicene creed its final form. [cite:vt21_wiki_thessalonica]

The edict deserves a second reading for what it names. The legal test of orthodoxy in the Roman Empire was now agreement with the bishop of Rome and the bishop of Alexandria. Irenaeus's line of bishops, two centuries earlier an argument made by a provincial bishop, was now the law. The two men named in it were, as it happens, the most powerful bishops of the period, and Part Four shows that the first of them, Damasus, had reached his office over a pile of corpses.

The laws that followed applied the same machinery to the next round of losers. A law of 4 March 398 ordered the books of the Eunomians and Montanists destroyed and made their concealment a capital offence; a law of 3 August 435 banned the books of Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, and ordered them burned. [cite:vt21_fc_laws] The surviving orders name these groups far more often than they name any Gnostic gospel. [cite:vt21_fc_laws] By the end of the fourth century, however, the question of which gospels were read in the churches no longer needed a burning order. The bishops who decided it held the buildings, the courts and the emperor's law, and a gospel left off their lists was simply not copied for the churches they controlled. How those lists were closed, and by whom, is the subject of Part Four.
