The Terms

Paid early, in one sum

Since FY1991 Congress has required that Israel's Foreign Military Financing be paid in a lump sum early in the fiscal year; recent appropriations acts require at least $3.3 billion within 30 days of enactment. The money goes into an interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and Israel has used the interest to pay down debts to American agencies. [cite:aa_crs_2023]

Spent in Israel, until 2028

Foreign Military Financing is generally spent on American goods. Israel was allowed an exception, known as off-shore procurement, to spend part of it with its own defence industry: up to 26.3 percent under the 2007 agreement. The 2016 agreement phases the exception out, ending it entirely in FY2028. [cite:aa_crs_2023] Israel may also use cash-flow financing, which lets it order weapons now and pay for them from future years' aid. [cite:aa_crs_2023]

The edge, in law

A 2008 law defined Israel's "qualitative military edge" and requires the United States to assess it every four years and to certify that arms sales to other countries in the region will not harm it. [cite:aa_crs_2023] The United States also keeps a stockpile of munitions in Israel, the War Reserve Stocks for Allies–Israel, and Congress has authorised additions of up to $500 million a year through FY2027. [cite:dv4_crs_aid_2025]

The aid pays for about 16 percent of Israel's defence budget. [cite:aa_crs_2023] Israel's own spending has risen sharply since 2023: SIPRI put it at $48.3 billion in 2025, 7.8 percent of the country's GDP. [cite:us19_sipri_fs2025]
