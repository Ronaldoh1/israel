Where Palestinians Went: A Diaspora Older Than the Flight of 1948

Two Palestinian migrations are often run together. One began in the 1870s and carried families to Latin America under Ottoman rule. The other was the flight of 1948, which carried refugees to neighbouring Arab lands. A common assumption holds that Latin America's Palestinian communities are made up of 1948 refugees who were welcomed with open doors. Neither half of that assumption fits the record.

Palestinian emigration to the Americas began in the 1870s. Most early migrants were Christian families from Bethlehem, Beit Jala and Beit Sahour. Because they travelled on Ottoman papers, they were long called "Turcos." [cite:palquest_sv]

Chile holds the largest Palestinian community outside the Arab world, with estimates commonly running into the hundreds of thousands. In Santiago the football club Palestino was founded in 1920; as Club Deportivo Palestino it plays in the national first division. [cite:w_palchile] [cite:w_palestino]

El Salvador's community is estimated at roughly 70,000 to 100,000 people, overwhelmingly of Bethlehem origin, and is described as the third-largest in Latin America after Chile and Honduras. El Salvador's Bethlehem-descended population is described as several times larger than Bethlehem's own population today. [cite:palquest_sv]

The doors were not open. In 1921 El Salvador declared Arabs and Chinese to be "pernicious" races, a documented act of exclusion. Elites looked down on the "Turcos" as peddlers. The community built its standing in commerce despite that discrimination. [cite:palquest_sv]

It went on to political prominence. El Salvador has had two presidents of Palestinian descent. Antonio Saca won in 2004, against another candidate of Palestinian descent, Schafik Handal. Nayib Bukele won in 2019; his paternal grandparents emigrated from Jerusalem and Bethlehem in the early twentieth century, and his father became a well-known imam. [cite:palquest_sv] [cite:jweekly_bukele] Bukele's own politics on Israel are pro-Israel, a reminder that descent does not determine position. [cite:jweekly_bukele]

The refugees of 1948 were a different population, with a different date of departure and a different legal status. Roughly 700,000 people displaced that year went mainly to Jordan, including the West Bank, to Lebanon, to Syria and to the Gaza Strip. There, from 1950, they were registered by UNRWA, the agency described in [scene:ud_s01|the human-rights section].

The distinction matters for both histories. Treating the Ottoman-era emigrants as refugees of 1948 erases what they actually did: they left under an empire, met formal discrimination, and built communities that eventually produced heads of state. Treating the refugees of 1948 as part of an older, settled diaspora erases what happened to them: a displacement that remains unresolved. The two migrations share an origin in the same land. They do not share a cause.
