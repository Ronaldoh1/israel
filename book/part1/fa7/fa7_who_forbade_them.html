Who Forbade Them

The arts in this book were forbidden for at least four different reasons, and the documents name each one. Alchemy's one papal act, around 1317, was a law against fraud and counterfeit coin. [cite:vt30_wiki_spondent] Urban VIII's decree of 1631 was a security law, protecting a reigning pope from prophecy. [cite:vt30_torch_popes] The Catechism's rejection of divination rests on the honour owed "to God alone". [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] And in the Americas, peyote and the honoured dead were condemned as idolatry. [cite:fa_harvard_peyote] [cite:vt34_ency_extirp]

Three findings stand out. The first is that the strongest religious objection was not that the arts fail but that they go around the proper channel: the Catechism says divination conceals "a desire for power over time, history" and over others, and contradicts the honour owed to God. [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116] The question was who may ask the hidden, and through whom. The second is that bans moved. Rome allowed the Chinese rites it had forbidden; the United States protected the peyote it had banned; a university now studies under licence the compounds the law once shut away. [cite:fa_brit_rites] [cite:og_mtsu_airfa] [cite:fa_jhu_psych]

The third finding is the one this shelf owes its readers. Not every forbidden art was a suppressed truth. A founding medium of Spiritualism demonstrated her own trick on a stage; a government's psychic program ran for over twenty years and produced nothing usable; the tarot's Egyptian origin was invented in 1781. [cite:fa_wiki_fox] [cite:fa_wiki_stargate] [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy] The record gives the authorities their due where they were right about fraud, and holds them to account where they burned what they did not understand.
