Plan Dalet: The Document and the Argument

On 10 March 1948, as Britain prepared to withdraw from Palestine, the general staff of the Haganah, the largest pre-state Jewish armed force, issued an operational plan. For decades a familiar account held that Palestinians left their homes in 1948 of their own accord, at the urging of Arab leaders, before any Jewish forces acted. This document stands squarely in the way of that account, and its existence is not in dispute. What historians dispute is what it was for.

Plan Dalet, in Hebrew Tochnit Dalet, or Plan D, was the operational plan for the period before and after the British withdrawal. Its village provisions covered operations against villages inside and near the territory to be held. Brigades were to encircle and search them. Where they met resistance, they were to destroy the village, "setting fire to, blowing up, and planting mines in the debris," and to expel the population beyond the borders of the state. Villages were to be garrisoned or demolished according to their strategic position. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] Put simply, the plan told commanders to hold villages that submitted, and to destroy those that resisted and expel their inhabitants.

The text is a primary document, and historians agree on what it says. Working from the same archives, they disagree about its purpose. The disagreement falls broadly into two readings, with others in between.

The first reading treats the plan as a master plan. Walid Khalidi set it out in a 1961 essay titled "Plan Dalet: Master Plan for the Conquest of Palestine." Ilan Pappé developed it in his 2006 book The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] In this view the document was the blueprint for deliberately removing the Arab population from the territory of the Jewish state.

The second reading belongs to Benny Morris, whose archival work documented the expulsions of 1948 in detail. Morris reads Plan Dalet as a military plan to secure territory and lines of communication before the Arab states invaded. In his account, its village provisions gave local commanders discretion, which they used in some places and not in others. Expulsions were decided locally rather than ordered from the center. The outcome was produced by war and by decisions at brigade level, not by a single master plan. [cite:wiki_plan_dalet] Other historians stand between these two positions.

The disagreement is narrower than it first appears. Neither reading supports the older account of a purely voluntary departure before any Jewish military action. Morris, whose reading is the less sweeping, is also the historian whose research documented the expulsions in detail. The dispute is about design and cause. Did the text produce the depopulation by intention, or did it supply the frame within which local commanders made it happen?

The plan was carried out in April and May 1948. Operation Nachshon, the fall of Haifa, Jaffa and Safed, and the depopulation of several hundred villages belong to those weeks. They are taken up in the chapters that follow ([scene:s10|independence and the Nakba]), where the reader can judge how closely events fit the document.

This book records the text and the competing readings. It does not resolve an argument the record itself has not resolved. What is settled is the document's language, its date and its authorship by the Haganah general staff. What remains open, among serious historians, is the intent behind it.
