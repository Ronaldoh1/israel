In November 1917 Britain made a promise about a country it did not yet govern. Arthur Balfour's letter of 2 November, favouring "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, was signed while the land was still Ottoman: British forces had taken Beersheba on 31 October and would receive Jerusalem's surrender only on 9 December. [cite:w_sinai_palestine] [cite:w_balfour_decl] Two days later General Allenby entered the city on foot and placed it under martial law, promising that lawful life would go on and that the holy places would be kept by existing custom. [cite:rs_al_nli] [cite:rs_al_proclamation] The people the letter called "existing non-Jewish communities", by one estimate roughly 700,000 Muslim and Christian Arabs and about 90 percent of the population, had not been asked, and the letter guaranteed them civil and religious rights, not political ones. [cite:balfour_letter] Nor was it Britain's only commitment. Sir Henry McMahon's letters of 1915–16 had pledged British support for Arab independence to Sharif Hussein of Mecca, and the Sykes–Picot Agreement of May 1916 had divided the same lands with France. [cite:w_mcmahon] [cite:w_sykes_picot]

This stage asks how that letter, which had no legal force, was made secure within five years: written into an instrument of the League of Nations, and built into a British government sitting in Jerusalem. The League Council approved that instrument, the Mandate for Palestine, conditionally on 24 July 1922; the League recorded it as in force on 29 September 1923, a date John Quigley disputes. [cite:rs_md_lon1923] [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] It asks with equal weight who resisted, by what means, and with what result. The answer is not one decision but a chain of them, taken in London, Paris, San Remo, Geneva, Cairo and Washington, and contested at each link.

The cast is wide. On the British side stood a War Cabinet that approved the declaration over the written objection of Edwin Montagu, its only Jewish member; Ronald Storrs, governing Jerusalem; Winston Churchill as Colonial Secretary; and Herbert Samuel, appointed the first High Commissioner in 1920. [cite:balfour_letter] [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel_hc] The Zionist Organization worked through Chaim Weizmann and Nahum Sokolow, and through a movement that argued with itself: with Louis Brandeis's American faction over who would control the money, and later with Ze'ev (Vladimir) Jabotinsky over territory. [cite:einstein_papers_vol12_doc168] [cite:sunnafiles_jordan] Palestinian Muslims and Christians organized together in associations and national congresses led by Musa Kazim al-Husseini, while the Husseini and Nashashibi families competed for offices that Britain filled. [cite:rs_mca_palquest_musakazim] [cite:palquest_musa_kazim] Britain installed Hussein of Mecca's sons Faisal and Abdullah as rulers of Iraq and Transjordan. [cite:w_cairo1921] The victorious powers decided, the League of Nations supervised, and in Washington a Congress lobbied by American Zionists acted over the objection of its own State Department. [cite:jvl_lodge_fish]

The sections that follow trace that chain roughly in the order it was forged. They begin with [scene:s06|the three wartime promises] and Britain's reasons for wanting Palestine at all, then with Jerusalem under a military governor before any mandate existed. In Paris in 1919 Faisal signed an agreement with Weizmann on a condition of his own, and an American commission measured local opinion and saw its report shelved. The League's Covenant then supplied the machinery that turned the letter into law, by a route followed in [scene:blg_s01|the account of Article 22], and lawyers still argue over whether Palestine's mandate kept that article's rule. Alongside the diplomacy run the congresses and delegations of the Palestinian national movement, the immigrants of the Third Aliyah, the riots of 1920 and 1921 with the founding of the Haganah and Britain's two inquiries, and the creation of a permanent Zionist fund. Samuel's choice of a Mufti and his new Supreme Muslim Council show the administration building institutions of its own. The Cairo Conference and Hussein's refusal to sign widen the view to the region. The Churchill White Paper and the separation of Transjordan narrowed the promise even as the Mandate's text wrote it into law. The American campaigns end in a congressional resolution. The stage closes with [scene:rd2_council|the Legislative Council that never sat] and with the office of High Commissioner, which governed through the rest of the Mandate with no elected chamber beside it.
