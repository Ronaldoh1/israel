Resolution 1701, Winograd and the Dahiya Doctrine

The war ended on paper before it ended on the ground. On 11 August 2006 the Security Council adopted Resolution 1701 by 15 votes to none. [cite:hz5_un_1701] [cite:hz5_wiki_1701] Lebanon's cabinet accepted it unanimously the next day; Israel's cabinet approved it on 13 August by 24 votes to none with one abstention; the ceasefire took effect at 08:00 on 14 August. [cite:hz5_wiki_1701] What the resolution required of each side, what Israel's own inquiry concluded about the war, and the doctrine Israeli commanders drew from it shaped every round of fighting that followed.

What 1701 required. The resolution called for "the immediate cessation by Hizbollah of all attacks and the immediate cessation by Israel of all offensive military operations." Israel was to withdraw all its forces from south Lebanon "in parallel" with the deployment of the Lebanese army and the UN force. Between the Blue Line and the Litani river there was to be "an area free of any armed personnel" other than the Lebanese army and UNIFIL, whose ceiling was raised to 15,000 troops. The resolution called for full implementation of the Taif Agreement and of Resolutions 1559 and 1680, "that require the disarmament of all armed groups in Lebanon," and asked the Secretary-General to propose a delineation of Lebanon's borders, "including by dealing with the Shebaa farms area." Its preamble stressed "the unconditional release of the abducted Israeli soldiers" and encouraged efforts to settle "the issue of the Lebanese prisoners detained in Israel." [cite:hz5_un_1701]

Each side read it its own way. Nasrallah said on 12 August that Hezbollah would "not be an obstacle to any decision taken by the Lebanese government," and on 14 August that its fighters would not be forced to give up their weapons, citing a weak Lebanese army and continued Israeli occupation. Israel's Foreign Ministry spokesman, Mark Regev, said on 18 August that the resolution required a Hezbollah-free zone south of the Litani and that Israel would act if Hezbollah was not disarmed. [cite:hz5_wiki_1701] The record over the next eighteen years supports neither side's claim to have complied. Hezbollah kept fighters and weapons south of the Litani and rebuilt an arsenal estimated by 2024 at 120,000 to 200,000 rockets and missiles. Lebanon recorded more than 7,000 Israeli violations, most of them overflights, and a UN report of 2015 described near-daily Israeli drone and jet flights over Lebanon. [cite:hz5_wiki_1701] The same resolution was the base text for the ceasefire of November 2024. [scene:hz8_terms|The 2024 terms]

The prisoners. Two weeks after the ceasefire, on 27 August 2006, Nasrallah told Lebanon's New TV that Hezbollah had not thought, "even one per cent," that the capture would lead to a war of that size; asked whether he would have ordered the raid had he known, he said: "I say no, absolutely not." [cite:hz5b_aj_nasrallah] The question that Hezbollah said began the war was settled two years later. On 16 July 2008, in an exchange run by the Red Cross at the border, Israel released Samir Kuntar, convicted in 1979 of killing an Israeli man and his four-year-old daughter, with four other prisoners. [cite:hz5b_aj_swap] Hezbollah handed over the remains of Ehud Goldwasser and Eldad Regev, whose fate had been unknown, and Israel returned the bodies of Hezbollah and other fighters as part of the exchange. [cite:hz5_wiki_war]

Israel's inquiry. Under public pressure, Olmert appointed a commission of inquiry chaired by the retired judge Eliyahu Winograd. Its interim report of 30 April 2007 faulted Olmert, Defence Minister Amir Peretz and Halutz for choosing "an immediate and intensive military strike" without "a detailed, comprehensive, and authorized military plan." Peretz and Halutz both left their posts. [cite:hz5_mjl_winograd] The final report, released on 30 January 2008, called the war "a serious missed opportunity" from which Israel emerged without a military victory, found "serious failings and shortcomings in the decision-making processes and staff-work" in both the political and military echelons, and assigned the army the main responsibility for the military outcome. [cite:hz5_jvl_winograd] On the most criticised decision, the ground offensive of the last days, in which more than 30 soldiers died, it said "There was no failure in that decision in itself," judging it on what was known at the time, but found the offensive had not improved Israel's position or curbed rocket fire. [cite:hz5_jvl_winograd] [cite:hz5_ap_winograd] It made no personal recommendations; Winograd said Olmert had acted in what he believed was the interest of the state, and Olmert's office said it was "breathing a sigh of relief." [cite:hz5_jvl_winograd] [cite:hz5_ap_winograd] Olmert announced his resignation in July 2008, under corruption investigations as well as the war's verdict. [cite:hz5_mjl_winograd]

The Dahiya doctrine. The lesson some Israeli commanders drew was the opposite of restraint. In early October 2008 the head of Northern Command, Major General Gadi Eisenkot, told Yedioth Ahronoth: "What happened in the Dahiya quarter of Beirut in 2006 will happen in every village from which Israel is fired on. We will apply disproportionate force on it and cause great damage and destruction there. From our standpoint, these are not civilian villages, they are military bases." He added: "This is not a recommendation. This is a plan. And it has been approved." [cite:hz5_jps_khalidi] [cite:hz5_inss_shalom] He dismissed the alternative of chasing rocket crews: "This won't be another 'launcher hunt.'" [cite:hz5_inss_shalom] Writing that month for the Institute for National Security Studies in Tel Aviv, Zaki Shalom called it "indeed a new policy of exercising force against Hizbollah." [cite:hz5_inss_shalom] An INSS researcher, Gabi Siboni, had argued the same year for a disproportionate response aimed at Hezbollah's assets and "the centers of civilian power" that support it, and Giora Eiland, writing for the same institute that November, proposed warning Lebanon's government that its army and civilian infrastructure would be destroyed if Hezbollah attacked. [cite:hz5_gs_dahiya] [cite:hz5_wiki_dahiya] Israel's stated rationale is in Eisenkot's own words: villages used to fire at Israel are military bases, and the cost of firing must be high enough to deter it. When Eisenkot became chief of staff, the army's 2015 strategy document did not name the doctrine but said that in a war in Lebanon "tens of thousands of targets" would be attacked. [cite:hz5_gs_dahiya]

How it was read. On 27 December 2008, less than three months after Eisenkot's interview, Israel launched Operation Cast Lead in Gaza. [cite:gz_wiki_castlead] [scene:gz5_castlead|GAZA: Cast Lead] The UN fact-finding mission led by Richard Goldstone, in its September 2009 report, traced Israel's tactics in Gaza to Lebanon in 2006, where "a concept known as the Dahiya doctrine emerged," involving disproportionate force and great damage to civilian property and infrastructure; it concluded that "what was prescribed as the best strategy appears to have been precisely what was put into practice," and described the Gaza operation as "a deliberately disproportionate attack designed to punish, humiliate and terrorize a civilian population." [cite:hz5a_un_goldstone] [cite:hz5a_csm_goldstone] Israel had not cooperated with the mission. In April 2011 Goldstone wrote that Israel had not intentionally targeted civilians as a matter of policy, citing Israel's own investigations; Netanyahu said the reversal "must lead to the shelving of this report once and for all," while Goldstone's three co-authors said they stood by it. [cite:gz_jvl_goldstone] [cite:gz_aj_goldstone_coauthors] Commentators have since described Israel's strikes on civilian infrastructure in Gaza after October 2023 as a possible extension of the doctrine. [cite:hz5_wiki_dahiya] The connection that can be documented is narrower and firmer: the commander who said the 2006 destruction of the Dahieh would be repeated in every village became the army's chief of staff, and his strategy promised tens of thousands of targets in the next Lebanon war. [cite:hz5_gs_dahiya] Whether that amounts to a policy of collective punishment, as its critics argue, or to lawful deterrence against an armed group fighting from towns, as its authors argue, is a legal and moral argument; the words themselves are on the record.
