The Bureau Watching, 1945–1980

The Federal Bureau of Investigation is the American agency charged with catching foreign spies and unregistered foreign agents on American soil. Its record with Israel begins before the state existed and runs, in this part, to the summer of 2026. Unlike the CIA and the NSA, the FBI's tie to Israel is not mainly a partnership; for most of its history it is a file of investigations, a few of which ended in court. This section covers the first thirty-five years: the arms network of 1945–48, the early counterintelligence cases, and the Bureau's part in the NUMEC inquiry.

The arms network, 1945–1948. The story of how a network founded at Rudolf Sonneborn's New York apartment in July 1945 bought weapons, aircraft and machinery in the United States and moved them to the Haganah through neutral countries is told in full in [scene:s07d|the Sonneborn meeting] and [scene:s07e|the arms network in operation]. What matters here is what the Bureau knew. Federal agencies, the FBI and the Customs Service among them, documented parts of the operation while it was running, after President Truman's embargo on arms for Palestine of December 1947. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The prosecutions that followed, under the Neutrality Act, were few. Charles Winters was convicted and served 18 months; Hank Greenspun and Al Schwimmer were convicted and served no prison time; all three were later pardoned, Greenspun by Kennedy in 1961, Schwimmer by Clinton in 2000 and Winters posthumously by George W. Bush in 2008. [cite:nbc_bush_pardons_winters] A reference account of Israeli espionage in the United States summarizes the outcome: the FBI's investigations produced a few convictions, but most offenders avoided prison. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp]

The Bureau was watching on other grounds too, and the two should not be merged. FBI records obtained under the Freedom of Information Act show that between 1947 and 1948 it wiretapped activists linked to the International Workers Order, and kept the Zionist Organization of America under scrutiny, because it suspected Communist influence, not because of the arms network. [cite:cato_fbi_wiretap] The earlier wartime surveillance of the Bergson group, begun at the State Department's instigation, is told in [scene:fa_s01|who Washington watched]. And in 1952 the FBI's file on the donor Abraham Feinberg records an order to register as a foreign agent for a publication he ran, which he closed rather than register, as told in [scene:s07f2|one donor, one decision]. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file]

The first counterintelligence cases. Once the state existed, the Bureau's concern shifted from smuggling to espionage. The reference account records a 1948 complaint by Secretary of State George Marshall that Israeli delegates at the United Nations knew Truman's instructions "almost as soon" as they were sent, and an FBI finding in 1954 that Israeli intelligence had cultivated sources inside the American government. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] Stephen Green, writing in the Christian Science Monitor in 1989 from his research on Israeli intelligence in America, described a 1956 case in which an Israeli official offered payment to Earl Jensen, an American embassy employee, for classified material; Jensen cooperated with the FBI and the Justice Department and passed selected documents to two Israeli contacts, who were apparently not arrested or prosecuted. [cite:ow5_csm1989] Green described another between 1976 and 1979, when an assistant Israeli army attaché, Colonel Yosef Langotsky, repeatedly entered secure areas of the Pentagon and tried to recruit employees; the Defense Department cut off his access and Israel recalled him in early 1979. [cite:ow5_csm1989] Both are one researcher's accounts; neither led to a prosecution.

NUMEC. The Bureau's longest Israel-related file concerned the uranium missing from the NUMEC plant in Apollo, Pennsylvania, found in 1965. Its first contact, in June 1966, was an interview with the company's president, Zalman Shapiro, about whether he should register as a foreign agent; he was not asked about the missing material. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] The Atomic Energy Commission had told Congress that February that "an inquiry by the FBI is not now warranted." [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] The Bureau reopened the case at the CIA's request in April–May 1968, and on 3 September 1969 Hoover told the CIA that the FBI was discontinuing its active investigation. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] Green reported that in 1969, following FBI recommendations, NUMEC lost its uranium-processing contracts and its president's security clearances were lifted. [cite:ow5_csm1989] In April 1976 Attorney General Edward Levi told President Ford that the Justice Department could not rule out a criminal charge, and the FBI interviewed Shapiro again in 1977 and conducted further interviews into 1980. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec] No one was charged. The CIA's view and the case's documentary record are set out in Part Three; the question of whether a diversion occurred remains officially unresolved. [cite:ow3_nsa_numec]

How the Bureau worked on friendly services. A Senate Intelligence Committee study of 1978, as summarized by Jeff McConnell in 1986, found that the FBI investigated "friendly" foreign services only in response to specific complaints, that what it developed rarely reached prosecutors, and that the CIA and the State Department discouraged penalties against friendly services. [cite:ow5_merip] John Davitt, the head of the Justice Department's internal-security section until 1980, told the New York Times that Israel's intelligence service had been "the second most active" in the United States in his time, after the Soviet Union's. [cite:ow5_merip] The two statements describe the same structure from two angles: a high level of activity, and a posture of response rather than pursuit.

What the record does not settle. The FBI's own files for most of these cases have not been released in full. The 1954 finding and the Jensen case are known from secondary accounts rather than published FBI documents. Whether NUMEC uranium reached Israel is unproven either way. And the 1978 Senate study is cited here through a 1986 article, not from its own text.
