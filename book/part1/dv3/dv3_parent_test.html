The Parent Test: What Rome Gave, and What It Withheld

Part One set the test that every city in this volume must pass. A parent is a power that supplied something the Jewish national home, and later the State of Israel, could not have had without it, at a time when no one else was supplying it. The test names the city's institutions, lists what each supplied, asks whether it was needed, lists what each withheld or opposed, and dates the verdict. [scene:dv1_three_functions|The test] London passed it in part. This section applies it to Rome.

Which Rome. As with London, the word covers several bodies. Vatican City, the state created in 1929, is a territory of under half a square kilometre with no army, no trade and a few hundred citizens; it appears nowhere in the record of Israel's making. [cite:dv1_wiki_vatican] The Holy See, the Church's government, is the body that acted: the popes, their Secretaries of State and their diplomats. The worldwide Catholic Church, with its bishops, schools and voters in many countries, is a third body, which the Holy See can teach but not command. And outside all three lies the Christianity that did most to make a Jewish state popular in the West, Protestant restorationism and dispensationalism, which Rome had no authority over and whose central doctrine it condemned. [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] [cite:catholic_answers_rapture_tract]

What Rome supplied. Measured against the definition, the list is short. The Holy See supplied no letter of promise, no legal frame, no company, no money and no arms. It did not vote for partition, because it had no vote, and there is no record that it asked Catholic governments to vote for it. [cite:dv3_jias_latam_1959] Three things can fairly be entered on the positive side. First, its silence during the partition vote of November 1947 removed what could have been a serious obstacle, at a moment when thirteen of the thirty-three votes in favour came from Catholic Latin America. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] [cite:dv3_unproject_181] Second, Nostra Aetate in 1965 withdrew the charge of collective Jewish guilt for Christ's death from Catholic teaching, and so removed one of the oldest religious arguments against Jewish life anywhere, the land included. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] Third, recognition in 1993–1994 gave Israel diplomatic standing with the government of the world's largest church. [cite:dv3_wiki_fund_agreement] Each of these is real. None of them was something the movement or the state needed and could not get elsewhere. The silence was motivated by the Holy See's own aim for Jerusalem; the teaching changed seventeen years after the state existed; and the recognition came forty-five years after it.

What Rome withheld or opposed. This side of the ledger is long and continuous. In 1904 Pius X told the movement's founder the Church "could never sanction" a Jewish return. In 1922 the Secretary of State formally objected to the Mandate's terms. In 1943 the Secretariat of State wrote privately against a Palestine "under Jewish predominance." From 1948 the Holy See pressed for Jerusalem to be taken out of the Jewish state, asked help for the Arab refugees in an encyclical that did not name Israel, and withheld recognition for forty-five years. [cite:dv3_jvl_vatican] [cite:dv3_wiki_redemptoris] Its doctrine condemned the prophetic scheme on which Protestant support for Israel rested, and after accepting the state it insisted that the state be judged "not in a perspective which is in itself religious, but in their reference to the common principles of international law." [cite:dv3_usccb_ccc676] [cite:dv3_jcr_notes_1985] Since 2015 it has had a treaty with the State of Palestine, and its present pope calls two states "the only" solution while noting that Israel does not accept it. [cite:dv3_vr_palestine_2016] [cite:dv3_allisrael_leo_2025] The opposition was also costly in the way Part One said genuine opposition would be: the founding prime minister complained in 1949 that a great religion had "a historical reckoning" to settle with Israel, and Israel's governments spent decades seeking the recognition Rome withheld. [cite:jcpa_vatican_israel_recognition] [cite:dv3_lewy_2009]

The front question, weighed. The previous section tested the claim that this opposition was a cover for support. Every private document available points the same way as the public record, no money moved from Rome to the movement, the decisive Catholic votes in 1947 are credited to their own delegates, and the wartime pope's conduct is best explained, in the most recent archival study, by the protection of the Church's own institutions. [cite:dv3_jewishnews_kertzer] [cite:dv3_jias_latam_1959] The claim is not supported. The narrower charge, that Rome put its own position first and chose silence at moments when speaking would have cost it something, is supported, and it cuts against the Holy See's moral record rather than toward any hidden partnership.

Where the theological parent really was. The volume's title assigns Rome the theological function, and the record does show a theological parent of political support for Israel. It was Protestant: the restorationists of nineteenth-century Britain, and the dispensationalist Bible of 1909 that turned millions of American Christians into supporters of a Jewish state. [scene:bc_s05|Protestant restorationism] [cite:wiki_scofield_bible] That parent lived in London and in the United States, the other two cities of this volume, not in Rome. The finding matters for the composite in Part Six: the theological and political support for Israel came from the same two Protestant powers that supplied its finance and its force.

The verdict. Rome was not a parent of the State of Israel in the sense this volume uses. From 1904 to 1965 it was an opponent on doctrine, and from 1922 to 1993 an opponent in diplomacy, whose opposition was real, internally consistent and costly to the movement. From 1993 it became a recognizer, and since 2015 it has kept a treaty relationship with Palestine alongside its relations with Israel. If the family image is kept at all, Rome belongs in it as the relative who refused for nearly ninety years to come to the wedding, then came, and still sits on both sides of the aisle. The theological parent the title looks for existed, but it was Protestant, and it lived in the two cities that are the subjects of Parts Two and Four.
