Whitehall, Not the Guildhall: What the British State Did

Everything in this part so far has concerned the City in the narrow sense, the corporation, its market and the companies registered there. The decisions that turned a political movement into a national home, and a national home into a state, were taken somewhere else. They were taken by Foreign Secretaries, Cabinets and Colonial Secretaries, in Whitehall and Westminster, and recorded in Cabinet papers and command papers rather than in the Corporation's records. This section gathers those decisions, each told at length in an earlier volume, so that the reader can see in one place what London, as the capital of a state, actually did. The striking thing about the list is that it runs in both directions.

The first sponsorship, 1839–1840. The British state's interest in a Jewish presence in Palestine began half a century before political Zionism. In 1839 Anthony Ashley-Cooper, Lord Shaftesbury, an evangelical peer and the stepson-in-law of the Foreign Secretary, pressed Lord Palmerston to sponsor Jewish settlement in Palestine under British protection. In 1840 Palmerston instructed his ambassador at Constantinople to urge the Ottoman sultan to encourage Jewish settlement, and the new British consulate in Jerusalem was given a brief to protect Jews. [cite:wiki_christian_zionism] [cite:wiki_shaftesbury] The motive, as [scene:bc_s05|Restoration before Zionism] shows, was both theological and imperial: a protected Jewish presence would be a British foothold in Ottoman Syria. It drew on no Jewish request, and it settled no one.

The promise, 1917. The decisive act came in wartime. On 2 November 1917 the Foreign Secretary, Arthur Balfour, signed a letter declaring that the government viewed "with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people," and sent it to Lord Rothschild at 148 Piccadilly, asking him to pass it to the Zionist Federation. [cite:wb_bl_addms41178] [cite:wb_ra_balfour] The letter was drafted with the Zionist leadership, among them Chaim Weizmann, whose wartime work on acetone for British munitions had given him access to the Prime Minister and to Balfour. [cite:balfour_letter] The War Cabinet approved it for reasons ministers could name: the Suez Canal and the route to India, the goodwill Weizmann had earned, and a mistaken belief in a unified and powerful "world Jewry" that could be won to the Allied side, a belief that was itself an antisemitic premise. [cite:cohen_palestine_great_powers] The only Jewish member of the Cabinet, Edwin Montagu, opposed the policy in a memorandum of 23 August 1917, calling Zionism "a mischievous political creed." [cite:wb_tna_cab21_58_218] [scene:s06|The Balfour Declaration] and [scene:rd2_addressee|why the letter went to Walter Rothschild] tell the whole story. Two facts in it bear directly on this part. The addressee was chosen because the Zionist leaders "had no address" and he had a fine one; and nothing in the record of the letter gives the City of London Corporation any part in it. [cite:wb_jc_rothschild2017]

When the letter was written, Palestine was still Ottoman. British forces took Beersheba on 31 October 1917 and Jerusalem only on 9 December. [cite:w_sinai_palestine] Britain was promising a country it did not yet hold.

The legal frame, 1920–1923. The promise became international law in stages. At San Remo on 25 April 1920 the Allied powers assigned Britain the Mandate for Palestine. The League of Nations Council approved the Mandate's text in July 1922, and it entered into force on 29 September 1923, a date that has since been challenged. [cite:rs_a22_frus] [cite:rs_md_lon1923] [cite:rs_md_quigley_oj] The Mandate wrote the Balfour Declaration into binding articles, obliged Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and "close settlement by Jews on the land," and recognized a Jewish public body to work with the administration. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] [cite:rs_md_avalon] The first High Commissioner, appointed in 1920, was Sir Herbert Samuel, a British Jew and a committed Zionist. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel_hc] For a quarter of a century the British state then governed the country: its passports, courts, police, railways, ports and currency. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] [scene:s07|The Mandate] and [scene:blg_s01|by what right] set out the text and the legal disputes over it.

The retreat, 1939–1949. The same state then turned against the policy it had launched. On 17 May 1939, in the command paper Cmd 6019, it allowed some 75,000 Jewish immigrants over the next five years and provided that "no further Jewish immigration will be permitted unless the Arabs of Palestine are prepared to acquiesce in it." [cite:rs_rd4_wp_cojs_text] [cite:rs_rd4_wp_frus_d841] Zionist leaders read it as an abandonment of the promise of 1917, and it came at the moment Europe's Jews most needed somewhere to go. [cite:whitepaper1939] [scene:rd4_white_paper|The White Paper of 1939] gives its terms and the fight over whether it broke the Mandate. After the war, the Labour government's Foreign Secretary, Ernest Bevin, failed to find a settlement. In February 1947 the Cabinet referred the question to the United Nations "without British recommendations." [cite:rs_rd5_cojs_bevin_1947] On 26 September 1947 the Colonial Secretary told the UN that Britain "was not prepared to undertake the task of imposing a policy in Palestine by force of arms." [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_creechjones] When the General Assembly voted for partition on 29 November 1947, by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, Britain was among the abstainers. [cite:rs_rd5_un_yearbook_181] [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit]

The Mandate expired at midnight on 14 May 1948, hours after the State of Israel was declared in Tel Aviv, with no power ready to carry out the partition plan. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_declaration] [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_creechjones] Britain gave the State of Israel de facto recognition only on 29 January 1949, eight months after its declaration, and de jure recognition on 27 April 1950; [cite:dv2_fathom_aridan] and the historian Raphael Langham describes Bevin's acceptance as "grudging." [cite:rs_rd5_langham_exit] Washington had recognized the state eleven minutes after it was declared, and Moscow gave full recognition on 17 May. [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:wiki_soviet_arab_israeli] [scene:rd5_britain_exits|How Britain left] follows the last year of the Mandate in full.

London and Israel since. The relationship did not end in 1949, but its character changed. The City appears in the later record as one financial partner among many. In January 2018 the Lord Mayor, Charles Bowman, a senior partner at PwC, led a business mission to Tel Aviv and Jerusalem, met Israel's economy minister to discuss a possible free-trade agreement and the governor of the Bank of Israel to discuss financial links, laid a wreath at Yad Vashem, and also met Palestinian business leaders in the occupied territories. He described the mission as part of building ties with growing markets after Britain's departure from the European Union. [cite:dv2_jn_bowman_2018] [cite:dv2_jc_bowman_2018] That is the Lord Mayor's ordinary ambassadorial role, the same one he plays in dozens of other countries. It is documented, and it is modest.

Set out together, the British state's record supports a clear conclusion and rules out a popular one. It supports the conclusion that London, meaning the British government, was the indispensable sponsor of the Jewish national home between 1917 and 1939: it issued the promise, wrote it into international law, governed the country for a quarter of a century, admitted immigration under its terms, and let the movement's institutions grow under its protection. It rules out the claim that this sponsorship was continuous or unconditional. From 1939 the same government restricted immigration, abstained on partition, refused to enforce it, left without an orderly handover, and recognized the new state late and reluctantly. A parent that launches a child and then tries to hold it back is still a parent. But it is a different kind of parent from the one the familiar story describes, and the next part of this volume shows that the role of principal sponsor passed, in these years, to Washington, though not without conditions of its own: the United States kept an arms embargo on all the belligerents through the war of 1948.
