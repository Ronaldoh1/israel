The Cards, the Lots and the Changes

The tarot's history is mostly documented, and the documented version is less ancient than the popular one. Decks with an added suit of picture trumps appear in Milan and Ferrara around 1440 to 1450 as cards for a trick-taking game played by the nobility. A Dominican sermon of about 1450 to 1470 condemned dice, playing cards and "triumphs" together, as gambling. [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy] No use for fortune-telling is documented for more than three centuries. In 1781 the French scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin claimed the trumps preserved the wisdom of ancient Egypt, a claim Egyptologists found no support for; an essay in the same volume first linked the cards to divination. The Rider–Waite–Smith deck of 1909, designed by Arthur Edward Waite and painted by Pamela Colman Smith, gave the modern cards their images. [cite:vt30_wiki_taromancy]

Geomancy, the reading of patterns made by marks on the ground or on paper, is named first in Trent's list of repudiated arts in 1564, together with sortilege, the casting of lots. [cite:vt30_fordham_rule9] No papal document specifically condemning the tarot was found; the Church's objection today falls under the Catechism's general rejection of divination. [cite:vt30_va_ccc2116]

The oldest book of chance on this shelf is Chinese. The I Ching, the Book of Changes, has its earliest layer in the Zhou dynasty, when it was called the Zhou Yi. It is built from sixty-four hexagrams, each made of six lines, solid or broken, and a person who consults it casts yarrow stalks or coins to find a hexagram and its text. Commentaries called the "Ten Wings" were attached to it, and it was made a canonical classic in 136 BCE. [cite:fa_ency_iching] It is one of the few books of divination that became a classic of a state's learning.

The I Ching also reached Europe by an unexpected road. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries a Jesuit missionary noticed a numerical correspondence between the hexagrams and the binary system, and the German philosopher Gottfried Leibniz claimed the hexagrams were an ancient Chinese prefiguring of binary arithmetic. [cite:fa_ency_iching] The book of an art Europe's churches condemned was read by one of Europe's great mathematicians as a forerunner of the number system on which every computer now runs.
