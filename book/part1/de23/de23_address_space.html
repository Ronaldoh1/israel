The Address Space: a Map Drawn as Property

Urbit is a piece of software, and most of what it does is ordinary for a computer network: it runs a personal server, stores a user's data and sends encrypted messages to other users. What makes it matter to this book is the way its author divided the network's names. Every Urbit identity is a number, and the numbers were cut into a fixed hierarchy before anyone used them. At the top are 256 "galaxies," an 8-bit space. Each galaxy can issue 255 "stars," giving 65,280 in all, a 16-bit space. Each star can issue 65,535 "planets," about 4.29 billion, a 32-bit space, which is where ordinary users live; planets in turn issue "moons" for devices, and below them sits a practically unlimited tier of free "comets." [cite:de23_smith_burrows] [cite:de23_addrspace_2016] Since 2019 the register of who owns which identity has been kept on the Ethereum blockchain, in a set of contracts called Azimuth, and Tlon, the company that makes the main Urbit app, describes each identity as a cryptographic proof on Ethereum that a user holds much as one holds a digital token. [cite:de23_tlon_faq] [cite:de23_bloglist] [cite:de5_wiki_urbit] The company behind it is the subject of [scene:de5_tlon_exit_and_return|Part Five]; this section is about the map: who drew it, how he described it, and who came to own it.

What he called it

On 13 January 2010, writing as Mencius Moldbug on a short-lived technical blog, he introduced Urbit as "a programming environment specified as a single stateless, frozen function." He wrote that the hardest problem it faced was allocating the namespace, which runs into a known trade-off in computer science called Zooko's triangle: a naming system cannot easily be at once secure, decentralised and readable by people. His answer was a compromise, and he gave it a name. "This quasi-solution is a design I call digital feudalism," he wrote, "of which more later." [cite:de23_moronlab_2010] The magazine The Point, reviewing Urbit in 2022, reported that the project's 2010 whitepaper gave its section on address allocation the same title. [cite:de23_point_2022] Earlier versions of the hierarchy, according to a 2017 report in The Verge summarised by the sociologists Harrison Smith and Roger Burrows, named the tiers "Lords," "Dukes" and "Earls," and Yarvin derived the scheme from the libertarian theory of homesteading, John Locke's idea that a person comes to own unclaimed land by being the first to work it. [cite:de23_smith_burrows] Two later accounts, in Compact and The New Yorker, report that in his original design Yarvin gave himself the position of "prince." [cite:de23_compact_2025] [cite:de23_kofman]

The feudal vocabulary gave way to a property vocabulary when the project went public. In June 2016 Yarvin and his co-founder Galen Wolfe-Pauly published "The Urbit Address Space," which put the idea in one line: "If Bitcoin is money, Urbit is land," and Urbit was "digital land, not digital money." [cite:de23_addrspace_2016] A post the same summer, "Common Objections to Urbit," which the Urbit blog lists under Yarvin's identity, took the land comparison further: "Curtis invented Urbit. So by the homestead principle, he started out owning all of it." It likened the result to land ownership in a real city, "complex and asymmetrical," and described the government plainly: "Urbit is designed to govern itself collectively by address space ownership," which it called "effectively, 'proof of stake.'" [cite:de23_objections_2016] Four years later Urbit's blog still used the image, describing an Urbit identity as "a self-authenticating property title," and gave the practical reason for scarcity: "Urbit IDs are scarce in order to discourage spam and abuse." [cite:de23_value_2020] Compact reported that Yarvin has called Urbit "a transparent plutocracy," a phrase that means rule by owners in proportion to what they own, openly declared. [cite:de23_compact_2025]

The same texts stated an ideal that pulled the other way. The 2016 post said ownership had been deliberately scattered "across a diverse set of entities" so that the system would be "unlikely to collapse back into a single landlord," and it set out conventions to keep it that way: address blocks should split and never merge, galaxies and stars should have different owners, and galaxies should belong to individuals rather than corporations. It also admitted that Urbit "does not live up to these ideals at present." [cite:de23_addrspace_2016] "Common Objections" answered the charge that Urbit was not really decentralised with an argument that will be familiar to any reader of [scene:de6_the_program|Part Six]: "When it's impossible to eliminate centralized power, it becomes necessary to tame, stabilize, and limit it." [cite:de23_objections_2016] Josh Lehman, who ran the Urbit Foundation from 2021, rejected the feudal label outright when a journalist put it to him in 2022: "No. Facebook is digital feudalism. When you don't have explicit hierarchies, it's way worse." [cite:de23_astra_2022]

Who owned the map

The first published distribution shows how concentrated the top tier was at the start. As of 1 June 2016, Tlon held 95 of the 256 galaxies; 50 were reserved for urbit.org, the future community foundation; 40 went to Tlon's employees and their families, 24 of them to Yarvin personally; 34 went to Tlon's outside investors, who are not named in the post; and 37 went to 33 other people, for donating, contributing code or winning contests. [cite:de23_addrspace_2016] No published document shows which investors received galaxies, or that Peter Thiel or his Founders Fund holds one; the investment by Founders Fund in Tlon itself is documented, and is set out in [scene:de5_tlon_exit_and_return|Part Five]. In 2020 the company explained the origin of its own holding in the same homesteading terms: all of the address space had once belonged to Yarvin, and "Tlon bought half the address space from Curtis." [cite:de23_value_2020]

An independent count three years later found the concentration largely intact. The researcher Francis Tseng calculated that as of 2019, 185 of the 256 galaxies were owned by Tlon, its employees and the employees of the Urbit Foundation, along with almost 38,000 of the network's roughly 65,000 stars, a calculation reported in Compact in 2025 under the verdict that "address space ownership is highly concentrated." [cite:de23_compact_2025] Smith and Burrows, using Tseng's shares, put Tlon at about 37 per cent, urbit.org at 19, Tlon employees at 15, outside investors at 13 and donors and contributors at 14. [cite:de23_smith_burrows] The founder's own share changed hands in January 2019. In "A Founder's Farewell," he wrote that he had reassigned his personal galaxies to urbit.org, which remained in Tlon's custody, and kept "a few thousand stars" in long time-locked contracts and a nonvoting minority stake in Tlon, which he said gave him no power over either. [cite:de23_farewell_2019] Urbit's own posts give him 24 galaxies in one place and 20 in another; the difference is not explained, and both describe the period before 2019. [cite:de23_addrspace_2016] [cite:de23_objections_2016]

Ownership of a galaxy carries a vote. The 2016 "Interim Constitution" set up a four-part structure on a Roman model, a consulate, a galactic senate of galaxy holders, a congress of stars and an assembly of planets, but stated that "for the interim, full authority is held by the (Roman style) consulate," which it described as effectively Tlon, and that the document "may be updated arbitrarily." It promised that Tlon would eventually "surrender governance of Urbit to its users." [cite:de23_constitution_2016] Once the register moved to Ethereum, the Galactic Senate became a working body written into the contracts: voting is "a power available to galaxies," on two kinds of proposal, upgrades to the contracts and documents recording decisions, with a quorum of 64 galaxies and an absolute majority at 129, and each vote closing at thirty days. [cite:de23_voting_docs] [cite:de23_governance_page] The Senate took its first concrete action, an upgrade to the contracts, in June 2021. [cite:de23_bloglist] Planets and stars have no vote. That is the plutocracy the founder described: the rulers are visible, and they are those who own the scarcest addresses.

What Urbit does, and for how many

In 2022 a star traded for about $6,000 on the open market and galaxies changed hands privately at values reported to exceed one million dollars. [cite:de23_point_2022] [cite:de23_astra_2022] By August 2024 the price of a star had fallen, on market data cited by CoinDesk, from a peak of about $27,000 in late 2021 to a little over $1,000. [cite:de23_coindesk_2024] An ordinary planet is no barrier to entry: Tlon's app generates one for each new user and keeps the keys on the user's behalf. "We custody the wallet for you, but it is yours," the company says, and a user can take full control and run the server alone. [cite:de23_tlon_faq]

The number of people using it has stayed small, and no audited count has been published. The Point in September 2022 described the system as "a bare-bones messaging server, with, at most, a few thousand active users." [cite:de23_point_2022] CoinDesk reported in May 2023 that the number of running Urbit servers had roughly doubled that year and that about 90 developers worked on it in a given month. [cite:de23_coindesk_2023] The New Yorker, after an estimated $30 million of investment over the project's life, found it closer to the Usenet discussion forums of the 1990s than to a feudal society. [cite:de23_kofman] The Foundation's blog in 2025–26 shows technical work continuing, including a new runtime and tools for running language models on a personal server, but gives no user figures. [cite:de23_bloglist] The main consumer product is Tlon Messenger, a chat app. [cite:de23_tlon_faq]

Two common claims about Urbit fail. It is not an anonymity tool: ownership and keys sit on a public blockchain, and each identity is a permanent, named account rather than a disguise. [cite:de23_tlon_faq] [cite:de23_smith_burrows] And no document ties Urbit to crime or illicit use; a widely repeated suggestion that it was built to hide wrongdoing has no source behind it. What the design does is plainer. It makes identity scarce, ownable and saleable, and it gives the owners of the scarcest identities the vote.

Interpretation, not finding: the Patchwork in miniature

Smith and Burrows read Urbit as a working model of Yarvin's politics. Its constitution, in their reading, contains no democratic voice; it is "necessarily governed by a technocratic elite of stars and galaxies," and "the only liberty is exit," which makes it a digital companion to the sea colonies of [scene:de22_seas_and_zones|Part Twenty-Two] and to the Patchwork of sovereign corporations set out in [scene:de2_core_texts|Part Two]. [cite:de23_smith_burrows] The Sophia platform holds a similar reading: the address space as a charter written in code, with the owners as shareholders and the founder, at first, as the prince. The strongest counter-reading comes from the record. The scarcity has an engineering reason, spam, that has nothing to do with politics. [cite:de23_value_2020] Any user can leave at no cost, an exit no state offers so cheaply. The founder gave away his galaxies in 2019, and in 2025 a vote of the galaxy owners, not the founder, chose the Foundation's board. And the Foundation's own director called the explicit hierarchy fairer than the hidden one of a large platform, while its fullest outside portrait found a chat forum rather than a fief. [cite:de23_farewell_2019] [cite:de23_astra_2022] [cite:de23_kofman] On that reading the feudal names matched his politics, but what was built is a small, open, owner-governed network.
