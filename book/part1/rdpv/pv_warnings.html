"Let Palestine Be Left Alone": The First Warnings, 1899–1914

The warning reached the founder of political Zionism in his own lifetime, from Jerusalem itself. Yusuf Diya al-Khalidi had been mayor of Jerusalem, had sat for the city in the first Ottoman parliament, and had taught at the Oriental Academy in Vienna. [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki] On 1 March 1899 he wrote to Zadoc Kahn, the chief rabbi of France, and asked that the letter be passed to Herzl. He called the Zionist idea in itself "natural, beautiful and just." But he set two facts against it: Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire, and it was inhabited by others. And he wrote: "in the name of God, let Palestine be left alone." [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki] [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_intercept]

Herzl answered on 19 March. He did not deny that the land was inhabited. He argued that Jewish immigration would benefit the people already there: "It is their well-being, their individual wealth, which we will increase by bringing in our own." And he asked: "who would think of sending them away?" [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_intercept] [cite:rs_rd1_khalidi_wiki] It is one of the earliest documented exchanges between a Palestinian notable and the movement's leader, and each man's position in it would be repeated for fifty years.

The warning became public in the Arabic press. From 1905 the journalist Najib Nassar wrote against Zionism in papers in Cairo and Beirut, and in 1908 he founded his own paper, al-Karmil, in Haifa. The Ottoman authorities closed it for two months in 1909, and in 1910 he was tried on a complaint from the Jewish community alleging incitement and acquitted. In 1911 he collected his articles as a book, Zionism: Its History, Objectives and Importance, and in the summer of 1914 he urged Palestinian youth to press Arab landowners not to sell land to Jews. [cite:rs_rd1_palquest_nassar] In Jaffa, two Arab Orthodox Christian cousins, Issa and Yousef El-Issa, founded a paper on 15 January 1911 and gave it a name that answers every later claim that the identity was invented after 1948: Filastin, "Palestine." [cite:wiki_falastin] [cite:nli_filastin_archive]

What they were warning about had already begun. In 1910 the Ottoman district governor of Nazareth, Shukri al-Asali, tried to stop the sale of the land at al-Fula in the Jezreel Valley, which the Beirut landowner Elias Sursock was selling to the Zionist land company's buyer, Yehoshua Hankin. The buyer's side recorded the opposition: Arthur Ruppin called the governor "a rabid anti-Zionist," and wrote that permission was obtained in the end through Hankin's friends in Constantinople. [cite:rs_rd1_ruppin_emek] [cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] Al-Asali took the fight to the Ottoman parliament, where he argued that Jewish settlers were taking control of rural villages. In 1911 Ruhi al-Khalidi and Sa'id al-Husayni, the deputies for Jerusalem, raised Zionism there too, and al-Khalidi was careful to say that what he opposed was immigration, not the residence of Palestine's own Jews. [cite:rs_rd1_asali_wiki] [cite:rs_rd1_ruhi_wiki]

The distinction al-Khalidi drew runs through the whole of this part. Their spokesmen did not object to the Jews who already lived among them. They objected to an organized movement from abroad that meant, in its own words, to build a national home on their land. By 1914, fifteen years after Yusuf al-Khalidi's letter to Herzl, that objection was in their newspapers, their parliament and the files of a district governor. Jewish-held land then came to about 418,000 dunams, around 2 percent of the country. [cite:israelfactcheck_ottoman_land_laws] The warning had come early. The scale had not yet arrived.
