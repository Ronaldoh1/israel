Labour and Palestine, 1939–1945

In July 1945 Clement Attlee became Prime Minister at the head of a Labour government. [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] On Palestine, Labour came to office with a record, going back six years, of opposing the policy it now inherited, and with a conference resolution on Palestine adopted seven months earlier. Whether that record would become policy was a question American diplomats were already weighing by the end of the month.

The record began in May 1939. In the House of Commons debate of 22–23 May on the White Paper, whose terms and reception are set out in [scene:rd4_white_paper|the White Paper of 1939], Attlee voted against the policy, and so did Hugh Dalton. Herbert Morrison told the House that the government should not adopt a policy "as to which grave doubt exists as to its consistency with the Balfour Declaration and the Mandate." [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] [cite:rs_rd4_lab_jvl_debate] Labour members argued in 1939 that the White Paper's limits on immigration and on land transfers were "contrary to the terms of the Mandate." [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] This was a legal argument as well as a political one: the objection was not only that the policy was unwise but that Britain, as the mandatory power, had no right under the Mandate to adopt it. Of the three men named here, one was Prime Minister by July 1945.

In December 1944, with the war not yet over, the party's annual conference adopted a resolution on Palestine. It held that there was no case for a Jewish national home "unless we are prepared to let Jews, if they wish, enter this tiny land in such numbers as to become a majority." It went on: "Let the Arabs be encouraged to move out, as the Jews move in." And it proposed re-examining "the possibility of extending the present Palestinian boundaries." [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] Each clause cut against the White Paper. The White Paper had capped Jewish immigration and made further entry after five years depend on Arab acquiescence; the resolution called for immigration on a scale to produce a Jewish majority. The White Paper had promised an independent Palestine state within ten years; the resolution spoke of moving the existing population and redrawing the frontier.

The phrase about Arabs moving out did not appear from nowhere. Seven years earlier, in July 1937, the Peel Commission's report had recommended an "exchange of population" on the model of the 1923 Greek–Turkish exchange, compulsory in the last resort, the first official British document to put transfer in writing; that proposal and the Zionist movement's response to it are told in [scene:pl_s01|Peel's "exchange of population"]. [cite:wiki_peel_commission] The Labour resolution differed in its language: it spoke of Arabs being "encouraged" to move, not compelled, and it was a party conference's statement, not a royal commission's recommendation to the government. [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] But it shows that the idea of moving Palestine's Arab population, first put on paper by a British commission in 1937, was still being written into the programme of a major British party in 1944, now as a call from a party whose leaders, by the State Department's account, had consistently supported the Balfour Declaration.

The sources here record the resolution's words as quoted in an American diplomatic memorandum. They do not include its full text from Labour's own records, the debate that preceded it, who moved it, or how Zionist, Arab or British officials responded to it at the time. What the resolution meant to those who voted for it, and how seriously its authors intended the passage on the Arab population, therefore cannot be settled from this record.

What the new government was likely to do was assessed in Washington by the end of July. A memorandum signed by William Yale of the State Department's Division of Near Eastern Affairs, dated 30 July 1945 and prepared for the Potsdam conference, set out the Labour government's probable Palestine policy. [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale] Yale recorded that Labour leaders, Labour members of Parliament and the major trade unions "have consistently given their support to the Balfour Declaration," and he recalled the party's arguments of 1939 that the White Paper's limits were contrary to the Mandate. But he did not expect the conference resolution to become policy. His forecast was that the Labour government would not try to implement it, and would instead "modify or repeal the White Paper policy of 1939," above all on immigration, since the remaining quota would soon be exhausted. He also expected the government to consult the Soviet and American governments before deciding Palestine's future status. [cite:rs_rd4_lab_frus_yale]

The distance between the two columns is the mechanism this section turns on. A party conference could adopt a resolution; only a government could change the policy of the mandatory power, and a government answered to considerations a conference did not have to weigh. Yale's reading was that the Labour government would act on the part of its record that was narrow and legal, its objection to the White Paper's immigration limits, and would leave aside the part that called for moving a population and redrawing borders. He also read the decision as one Britain would no longer take alone: the future status of Palestine, in his forecast, would be settled only after consulting the Soviet and American governments.

The memorandum is a forecast, not an outcome. Whether the Labour government modified the White Paper, how it handled immigration once the quota ran out, and how its relations with Washington over Palestine developed belong to the postwar period and are told in the next stage of this history. What this record establishes is the starting point: a party pledged by its own words in 1939 and 1944 against the White Paper, and an American diplomat predicting in July 1945 that it would keep the first pledge in a modified form and not the second.

Much of the British side of these years is not documented in the sources used here. They do not name the Colonial Secretaries who served during the war, the High Commissioners who governed Palestine on Britain's behalf, or the deliberations of the wartime cabinet on Palestine's future, and they do not cover the appointment of the Labour government's Foreign Secretary. Who governed Palestine for Britain during the war, and how that government's thinking moved between 1939 and 1945, is therefore a question this chapter cannot yet answer.
