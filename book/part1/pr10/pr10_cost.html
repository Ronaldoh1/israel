No Power, No Signal, No Office

A month into the first ceasefire, in February 2025, Reporters Without Borders described the press of Gaza working among ruins. The army had destroyed newsrooms and journalists' homes. Islam al-Zaanoun of Palestine TV said: "With no place to work, no internet or electricity, I was forced to stop working for several days." Fuel was scarce, travel slow and costly, and reporters queued for hours for water and food like everyone else. Journalists who had left Gaza could not return. "Reporters need multimedia and security equipment, internet and electricity," said the group's editorial director, Anne Bocandé. [cite:pr_rsf_feb2025]

A year later, four months after the October 2025 ceasefire, RSF's Middle East desk head, Jonathan Dagher, said they were "at breaking point." Electricity and internet were scarce, slow and expensive: internet access sometimes cost ten times the usual price. Cameras, tripods, batteries, phones and safety gear were short. Many reporters lived in tents or damaged flats. The price of fuel had risen from about 7 shekels in 2023 to nearly 75. About half the territory, east of the line held by the army, was closed to them. On 21 January 2026 an Israeli drone killed three freelancers working for Al Sharq TV and AFP; the army said it had struck "suspects who activated" a Hamas drone that threatened troops, and that the details were under review. Without Contracts

Many of those reporting Gaza to the world are freelancers, paid by the story. After the ceasefire, with the world's attention elsewhere, the assignments fell. "In 2026, we are no longer the main story," said Nour Swirki of Al Sharq TV, whose live reports had fallen from six or seven a day to one or two. NPR's Anas Baba said that reporting had become "a struggle for survival rather than just a journalism job," with freelancers "working without contracts, without stability and without protection." "We are all targets, vulnerable at any given moment, and no place is safe," said Swirki. Thaer al-Moustafa, who works with the Guardian, put it in three words: "We need protection." [cite:pr_rsf_feb2026] In July 2025, at the height of the hunger, AFP had asked Israel to let it evacuate its freelancers. [cite:pr_aj_afp2025]

The Organizations

The press-freedom groups have counted the dead, published the names, and gone to court. In April 2026 the Committee to Protect Journalists, with the Foreign Press Association, the Union of Journalists in Israel and Reporters Without Borders, filed an emergency motion asking Israel's Supreme Court to rule at last on independent access to Gaza. "There are no substantiated or specific threats against journalists, and journalists pose no threat to Israeli troops," the Committee said. [cite:pr_cpj_motion2026] The state has argued that unescorted entry endangers journalists and troops. [cite:pr_yahoo_state_hcj] As of October 2026 the court had not ruled. [cite:pr_wlrn_ban_full] The published record does not include totals of the money these groups have given Gaza's journalists; it records what the journalists say they lack: equipment, protection, contracts and a way out.
