Palestinian Classrooms: Three Authorities, One Story Contested

No Palestinian child is taught under a single, sovereign education system. Depending on where they live, the curriculum is set by the Palestinian Authority, delivered by a United Nations agency, or increasingly replaced by Israel's. Each layer is fought over, and each is paid for or permitted by someone else.

The Palestinian Authority's books

The Palestinian Authority writes the curriculum for schools in the West Bank and, until the war, Gaza. Its current textbooks date from 2016, with the largest revisions in the 2020–21 edition. [cite:nat_gao_unrwa2026] The European Union's 2021 study of those books, examined in the part on how the story is told, found that they met UNESCO standards against incitement while expressing "a narrative of resistance" and antagonism toward Israel, and found some material it called hateful; each side reads the study as proof of its case. [cite:gei_faq] [cite:impactse_gei] How donors have used the books' content as a condition of funding is set out in the section on three ways a country holds the pen.

The United Nations schools

The UN agency for Palestinian refugees, UNRWA, runs schools for refugee children in five places: Gaza, the West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon and Syria. It does not write its own curriculum; it teaches each host government's. According to a January 2026 report by the US Government Accountability Office, it educates more than 540,000 children in about 700 schools. Before 7 October 2023 it ran 96 schools with more than 45,000 pupils in the West Bank and 288 schools with more than 298,000 pupils in Gaza, where formal schooling stopped that day. [cite:nat_gao_unrwa2026]

The agency reviews the Palestinian Authority's books itself. In its 2024–25 review it examined 13,149 pages and flagged problems on 507, about 3.85 percent. Of 435 issues it found, 349 concerned departures from United Nations positions; 138 involved the use of the word "Zionist" and 95 involved maps. Teachers are given guidance on how to handle flagged passages. The same report found gaps in the State Department's reporting to Congress on the agency, and noted that the United States cut its funding to UNRWA in March 2025. [cite:nat_gao_unrwa2026]

East Jerusalem: the curriculum changes hands

In East Jerusalem, which Israel annexed and most countries regard as occupied, the question is no longer only what the Palestinian books say but whether Palestinian pupils will use them at all. About 125,000 Palestinian pupils study there, in municipal, UNRWA and church schools. In August 2023 the Israeli government decided to raise the share of pupils in municipal schools studying the Israeli curriculum from 24 percent to 45 percent by 2027–28. In October 2024 the Knesset passed laws banning UNRWA's operations in areas under Israeli control, and Israeli officials said its pupils would move to schools teaching the Israeli curriculum. [cite:nat_national_ej2025]

The shift has been fast. According to figures cited by Al Jazeera, a few hundred pupils in East Jerusalem studied the Israeli curriculum about fifteen years ago; more than 10,000 did in 2020, more than 27,000 in 2025, according to the Israeli rights group Ir Amim, and Palestinian officials estimated more than 45,000, about 38 percent, for 2026–27. A school principal described inspectors confiscating original Palestinian textbooks from pupils' bags. [cite:nat_aj_ej2026] Ir Amim reports that the government's five-year plan for East Jerusalem requires every new school it funds to teach the Israeli curriculum, at a time when the municipality itself counts 1,461 classrooms missing or substandard and only 20 of 1,204 planned new classrooms had been added. [cite:nat_iramim_2025] The principal described the replacement curriculum as erasing Palestinian history and identity. [cite:nat_aj_ej2026]

What the Palestinian case shows

Everywhere else in this part, a state decides what its own children learn. Palestinian children are taught from books their own authority writes but others fund and inspect, in schools a UN agency runs with others' money, or, in East Jerusalem, from a curriculum written by the state with which their own people are in conflict. Whatever one concludes about the content of the Palestinian books, the people whose story they tell have the least control over who holds the pen.
