Three Areas

The Interim Agreement. The second Oslo agreement, signed on 28 September 1995, divided the West Bank into three kinds of territory. In Area A, the main Palestinian towns, the new Palestinian Authority held full civil and security control. In Area B, most of the villages, it held civil control while security was shared with Israel. Area C, defined simply as everything outside A and B, stayed under full Israeli civil and security control. At the start, Area A was about 3 percent of the West Bank, Area B about 23 to 25 percent, and Area C about 72 to 74 percent. [cite:wb_wiki_oslo2areas]

The redeployments that stopped. The arrangement was meant to be temporary. Israel was to hand over further parts of Area C in stages, with the transfers due to be completed by the end of 1999. The Wye River Memorandum of 1998 set out a further 13 percent, which would have reduced Area C to about 61 percent; Israel carried out a withdrawal from 2 percent, and during the second intifada it reoccupied the whole territory. By 2013 the commonly cited figures were about 18 percent for Area A, 22 percent for Area B and 63 percent for Area C (the sources' figures sum to slightly more than 100), and the temporary map had become the lasting one. [cite:wb_wiki_oslo2areas]

Islands. Areas A and B are not two blocks of land. They are 165 separate enclaves with no territorial contiguity between them, each surrounded by Area C. [cite:wb_wiki_oslo2areas] To travel from one Palestinian town to another, Palestinians pass through territory Israel controls, which is where, as Part Six shows, the checkpoints, the barrier and the restricted roads came to stand.

Settlement in the peace years. The Oslo agreements did not freeze settlement. In the year of the first agreement, 1993, 116,300 settlers lived in the West Bank outside East Jerusalem; by 2000, when the talks collapsed, there were 198,300, by Peace Now's count.
