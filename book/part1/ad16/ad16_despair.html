Despair: When the Promise Fails in the Body

A promise that fails can be measured in wages, debts and homes, as the earlier parts of this book have done. It can also be measured in deaths. In 2015 the economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton reported that death rates among middle-aged white Americans, which had been falling for decades, had turned upward, driven by drug and alcohol poisonings, suicide and liver disease. They later called these "deaths of despair" and tied them to the collapse of a way of life for Americans without a four-year degree. Their account became one of the most cited explanations of why the Dream's failure matters. It is also contested, and the overdose record since 2023 has moved in a direction their account did not predict. This section sets out the finding, the dispute and the latest data, and then turns to a related measure that is not disputed: how long Americans live depends strongly on how much they earn.

The original finding

Case and Deaton's paper, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in November 2015, studied white non-Hispanic Americans aged 45 to 54. Their death rate had fallen by about 2 per cent a year from 1978 to 1998. After 1998 it rose by about half a per cent a year, through 2013. Had the 1998 rate simply held, about 96,000 deaths would have been avoided between 1998 and 2013; had the earlier decline continued, about 488,500 would have been avoided between 1999 and 2013, a toll the authors compared to the AIDS epidemic. [cite:ad16_princeton_cd] The rise was concentrated among those with a high-school education or less, whose all-cause death rate rose 22 per cent; deaths from drug and alcohol poisoning in that group roughly quadrupled and suicides rose 81 per cent. For college graduates, death rates fell. Over the same years midlife death rates kept falling in France, Germany, Britain and Sweden, and among Black and Hispanic Americans in the United States. [cite:ad16_princeton_cd] The paper itself said the data could not establish a cause, and named the spread of opioids and economic insecurity as candidates.

The age-adjusted rate used here and below is a death rate per 100,000 people recalculated as if each year's population had the same age structure, so that years can be compared without an ageing population distorting the trend.

In their 2020 book, Deaths of Despair and the Future of Capitalism, Case and Deaton widened the claim. They estimated that about 600,000 midlife deaths would have been avoided had the pre-1999 decline continued, and pointed to causes inside the system this book has traced: a job market that no longer offered a secure career to workers without a degree, the cost of employer health insurance pressing on wages, and the pricing power of drug makers. [cite:ad16_cato_cd2020] They reported that in 2017, 84 per cent of Americans aged 25 to 64 with a bachelor's degree were employed against 68 per cent of those with only a high-school diploma, and that wages for white men without a degree fell 13 per cent from 1979 to 2017. [cite:ad16_cato_cd2020] [scene:ad4_wage_split|Part Four] and [scene:ad8_costs_and_coverage|Part Eight] document those two mechanisms in detail.

The critics

The strongest challenge came from the economist Christopher Ruhm. Using county data from 1999 to 2015, he found that counties with worse economic decline did see faster growth in drug deaths, but that the link was weak and mostly explained by other factors; in his preferred estimates, changes in economic conditions explain "less than one-tenth of the rise in drug and opioid-involved mortality rates." [cite:ad16_ruhm] He concluded that the epidemic was better described as a drug problem, driven by the availability and price of opioids, than as a despair problem, and that improving the economy, though worthwhile for other reasons, was unlikely to cut overdose deaths much.

A second criticism concerns the wage evidence. The economist David Henderson, reviewing the 2020 book for the Cato Institute's Regulation, argued that measuring with a price index that rises more slowly (the personal consumption expenditures index rather than the consumer price index) turns the 13 per cent fall in non-college white men's wages into a 4.5 per cent rise from 1979 to 2017. [cite:ad16_cato_cd2020] That is the same measurement dispute set out in [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two] and [scene:ad4_wage_split|Part Four]: the choice of price index changes the direction of the wage record for some groups, though not the gap between those with and without a degree.

The overdose record, including the decline

Drug overdoses are the largest of the three causes, and the national count is the clearest series. Deaths from overdose rose from 25,785 in 2003 to 70,237 in 2017, jumped during the pandemic to 91,799 in 2020 and 106,699 in 2021, and peaked at 107,941 in 2022, an age-adjusted rate of 32.6 per 100,000, against 8.9 in 2003. In 2023 they fell slightly, to 105,007. [cite:ad16_db522] Provisional counts, which the National Center for Health Statistics revises as death records are completed, show the fall accelerating: an estimated 81,313 deaths in 2024 and 69,973 in 2025, a drop of almost 14 per cent in a year and the third annual decline in a row; deaths involving opioids fell from 55,296 to 44,564. [cite:ad16_nchs_2025] On these provisional figures, 2025 deaths were about 35 per cent below the 2022 peak (69,973 ÷ 107,941 = 0.65), though still more than two and a half times the 2003 count.

The decline is counter-evidence to any reading in which overdose deaths simply track the state of the Dream. Between 2022 and 2025 the federal minimum wage did not change, house prices relative to income stayed near their highs, and belief in the Dream fell further. [cite:us13_dol_minwage] [cite:us14_jchs_pti] [cite:ad1_wsjnorc25] Overdose deaths still fell by a third. That pattern fits Ruhm's emphasis on the drug supply better than an account in which economic despair is the main driver. It does not refute Case and Deaton's finding about the education gap in midlife mortality, which concerns a longer period and more causes than overdoses alone. Suicide, the second cause, has not fallen in the same way: there were 48,824 suicides in 2024, an age-adjusted rate of 13.7 per 100,000, the tenth leading cause of death. [cite:us16_cdc_db548]

Income and the length of a life

Whatever the cause of deaths of despair, the link between income and longevity is not in dispute. Raj Chetty and colleagues matched tax records to death records for 2001 to 2014 and found that at age 40 the richest 1 per cent of men could expect to live 14.6 years longer than the poorest 1 per cent; for women the gap was 10.1 years. [cite:ad16_jama_chetty] The gap widened over the period: life expectancy rose by 2.34 years for men and 2.91 years for women in the top 5 per cent of incomes, and by 0.32 and 0.04 years in the bottom 5 per cent. [cite:ad16_jama_chetty] Place mattered: among the poorest quarter, life expectancy differed by about 4.5 years between the best and worst areas, and in some areas rose by more than four years while in others it fell by more than two.

What explained those differences between places is itself a finding that cuts against easy readings. Life expectancy for low-income people tracked health behaviours, smoking above all, and was higher in places with more immigrants, more college graduates and more local government spending. It was not significantly correlated with access to medical care, income inequality or local labour-market conditions. [cite:ad16_jama_chetty] The poor live shorter lives everywhere, but how much shorter depends on where they live and how people there live, more than on the local level of inequality.

The same gradient appears in how people feel. In January 2025, 38 per cent of adults in households earning under $40,000 said they were very satisfied with their personal lives, against 55 per cent of those earning $100,000 or more. [cite:us22_gallup_pl25] National life expectancy, 79.0 years in 2024, was the highest on record, up from 78.4 in 2023, which shows that the overall trend has recovered even as the gaps by income remain. [cite:us16_cdc_db548]

What this adds to the account

The despair record supports a narrower conclusion than either its advocates or its critics sometimes draw. It is documented that for Americans without a degree, middle age became more deadly from 1999 and that the gap with graduates widened; it is documented that the poorest live ten to fifteen years less than the richest and that the gap grew. What is contested is how much of the rise in deaths came from the failing economic promise rather than from a flood of cheap, potent opioids, and the decline in overdoses since 2023, with the economy's mechanisms unchanged, weighs on the side of the drug supply. The finding that bears most directly on this book is the education divide: the credential that [scene:ad7_college_cost_pell|Part Seven] showed to be the main ladder of the Dream also marks, in the vital statistics, the line between a lengthening life and a stalled one.
