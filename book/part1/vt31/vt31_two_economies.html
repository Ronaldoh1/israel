Two Economies of the Sacred

The two previous sections traced the institution's side. The tithe moved from a gift to a church law in 585 and to a royal tax in 779. Attendance moved from custom to a canon of around 300, to the excommunication of 1215, and to the "grave sin" of today's Catechism. Part Twenty-Four set out the other side for the second and third centuries: no documented tithe among the Gnostic groups, no new code of commandments, no attendance rule, and a hostile record that nonetheless shows women teaching and laymen performing priestly functions ([scene:vt24_how_they_lived|How They Lived]). This section puts the two side by side across the whole period the book covers, from the second century to the Cathars, names the exceptions on both sides, and then gives the platform's reading of what the contrast means, labelled as a reading, followed by the strongest arguments against it.

The exceptions, stated plainly

The table supports the thesis Book Two tests on payment, obligation, women and the sanction for leaving. It is weaker on intermediaries and authority, and the exceptions run in both directions. On the inner side, the second- and third-century exceptions are set out in [scene:vt24_how_they_lived|How They Lived]: Valentinians who sat inside the ordinary congregations, a hostile charge that the teacher Marcus collected "a very large fortune" from wealthy women, and the Manichaeans, a separate religion, whose lay Hearers fed the Elect. [cite:vt24_ah3_15] [cite:vt24_ah1_13] [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] Tertullian adds that Marcion was a member of the Roman church before his expulsion, and that his gift of money was handed back to him. [cite:vt24_tert_praescr] The later case is the Cathars of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. They rejected the Catholic Church's land and wealth, and their perfecti lived in poverty, but the believers supported them and competed to house them. [cite:vt31_wh_cathars] [cite:vt31_wiki_credentes] They also had bishops, apparently organised at a council at Saint-Félix in 1167 whose surviving record is disputed, and a two-tier membership. Perfecti kept lifelong vows of chastity and abstinence from meat, and believers competed to house them. [cite:vt31_wiki_stfelix] [cite:vt31_wiki_credentes] Their story belongs to [scene:vt28_cathars|Part Twenty-Eight].

On the institutional side, the early church also asked for no tithe, and the money it did collect was used largely for the poor. Justin's president used the Sunday gifts for orphans, widows and prisoners; even the Herstal capitulary reserved part of the tithe for poor relief, and Charlemagne's division gave the poor one of four shares. [cite:vt20_justin_67] [cite:vt31_wiki_herstal] [cite:vt31_wiki_tithe] The obligation to attend and to pay grew over centuries, often pushed by kings, and the institution itself later abolished the sale of indulgences and dropped the fixed tenth. [cite:vt31_brit_indulgence] [cite:vt31_cic1260]

The scholarly version: office and access

Two scholarly arguments give the contrast a mechanism. The sociologist Max Weber held that authority born of a charismatic founder is unstable and must be "routinised" if a movement is to survive, by rules that decide who holds power. One result is "office charisma": a sacred office such as holy orders conveys sanctity to its holder's acts whatever his personal character. [cite:vt31_swatos_routin] Read through Weber, a sacrament valid because of the office rather than the person, a succession list, and a regular revenue to support the office are the normal form a lasting religious body takes, not an aberration. Elaine Pagels, in The Gnostic Gospels (1979), made the sharper historical argument: doctrines such as bodily resurrection served "an essential political function" by grounding the authority of the bishops who claimed succession from the witnesses, while gnosis offered "the theological justification for refusing to obey the bishops". [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Both are attributed arguments, and both have critics, noted below.

Interpretation, not finding: appropriation, suppression, occultation, recovery

The Sophia platform reads the two economies as one story in four movements. In this reading, the earliest teaching offered direct access, the kingdom within and knowledge that frees, and asked nothing in return. An institution then formed around that teaching and appropriated it: it kept the sayings that could be read as supporting its own offices, such as the kingdom "among you", and placed a priest, a sacrament and a payment between the believer and what the sayings had offered freely. Next came suppression. Teachers who said the office was unnecessary were named heretics from Irenaeus onward, their meetings were banned by imperial law, and later movements that revived the claim, the Cathars above all, were destroyed by crusade and inquisition. Occultation followed: what could not be destroyed survived hidden, in a jar at Nag Hammadi, a codex bought in Cairo, the Ethiopian canon, and orders that taught in secret. Recovery is the modern phase, in which the buried texts return and readers can again compare the two economies for themselves.

The core of this reading is an argument about incentives. In this view, what threatens a mediated institution most is not a rival doctrine but the claim that the institution is unnecessary, because a believer who can reach God directly owes no tithe, needs no confessor and fears no exclusion. Read this way, the sequence in the record (a voluntary community, then the obligatory tithe, the yearly confession to one's own priest and the penalty for leaving) is the shape such an institution would be expected to take, and the hostility to gnosis is the expected response to a teaching that made that shape optional. The reading is a lens on the record. It does not add facts to it, and the documents in this part neither prove nor require it.

The strongest counter-readings

The first counter-reading concerns the canon. Bruce Metzger, in his 1987 study of the New Testament canon, asked whether the canon is "a collection of authoritative books" or "an authoritative collection of books", and argued for the first: the church recognised books that communities were already reading as authoritative, over a long and gradual process, rather than conferring authority by decree. [cite:vt31_audit_metzger1987] On that view the exclusion of the Gnostic gospels reflects which texts the majority of congregations already used, not a decision by an office protecting its revenue; Part Twenty-Three shows that the lists were still disputed centuries later ([scene:vt23_lists_and_letters|Lists and Letters]).

The second concerns elitism. The Valentinians, by Irenaeus's account, divided humanity into the material, the "animal" and the spiritual, held that the spiritual were saved "because they are spiritual by nature", and called ordinary church members "animal". [cite:vt24_ah1_06] An inner tradition open only to a spiritual few is not obviously more democratic than a church open to anyone baptised. Critics of Pagels, among them Henry Chadwick and Hyam Maccoby, argued that her account idealised the Gnostics and passed over this elitism. [cite:vt24_enc_pagels]

The third concerns motive. Irenaeus stated his purpose as exposing "knowledge falsely so called" for the sake of believers he thought were being misled. [cite:vt24_ah1_pref] The heresy hunters wrote, in their own terms, as pastors protecting their congregations from what they believed to be falsehood, and they did so as a minority with no state power until the fourth century. A pastoral motive and an institutional interest can coexist. The record shows both at different times, and it does not show that the second was the cause of the first.

The fourth is structural. Every group in the table that lasted more than a generation built offices: the Valentinians claimed succession, the Manichaeans had an Elect, and the Cathars had bishops and lifelong vows. [cite:vt24_ect_flora] [cite:vt24_wiki_manich] [cite:vt31_wiki_stfelix] On this view the difference between the two economies is one of degree and of power, not of kind. The inner traditions did not avoid mediation. They lost before their own offices could become an establishment.

What the documents settle is narrower than either reading. The compulsory tithe, the obligatory Mass, the yearly confession, the anathema on the canon and the penalty for leaving are all the institution's own law, and none of them existed in the first two Christian centuries. The inner-tradition texts that survive tell their readers to lay down no rule, to find the kingdom within and not to sell the teaching, and no tithe or attendance law is recorded among the groups that read them. Whether that contrast was the reason those groups were condemned is the question [scene:vt32_verdict_two|Verdict II] answers in three registers.
