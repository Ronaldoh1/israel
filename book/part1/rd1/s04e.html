A Bank for Palestine, and an Offer for East Africa

In the eighteen months from early 1902 to the summer of 1903 the Zionist movement ran on two tracks at once. On one, it built a bank inside Palestine. On the other, under the pressure of a massacre in the Russian Empire, its own Congress voted to look seriously at land in East Africa. The two tracks pulled in opposite directions, and the conflict between them nearly broke the organization apart.

The first track began in London. On February 27, 1902, the Jewish Colonial Trust founded a subsidiary, the Anglo-Palestine Company, with initial capital of about £50,000.[cite:apc_wiki] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] Its chairman was David Wolffsohn, the Trust's co-founder and later Herzl's successor. Its manager was Zalman David Levontin, one of the founders of Rishon LeZion in 1882, whom Herzl had recruited personally.[cite:apc_wiki] The parent company in London raised capital from small shareholders; the subsidiary was the arm that would put the capital to work on the ground, as [scene:s04c|the Trust's own history] describes.

The company opened its first branch in Jaffa in 1903. From there it financed land purchase and agriculture and supported the credit cooperatives through which settlers borrowed. By 1914 it had branches in Jerusalem, Beirut, Hebron, Haifa, Safed, Tiberias and Gaza.[cite:apc_wiki] Its name later changed twice: it became the Anglo-Palestine Bank around 1930, and Bank Leumi in 1950–51.[cite:apc_wiki] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] The line runs unbroken from a £50,000 subsidiary of 1902 to one of the major banks of the State of Israel, though the bank's later operations are a different story from its founding years, and are not told here.

What "settlement financing" meant in practice can be seen in a single loan. The Anglo-Palestine Company lent to the Ahuzat Bayit association, which used the money to build the first neighborhood of what became Tel Aviv in 1909, six years after the Jaffa branch opened.[cite:apc_wiki] One bank, one association, one neighborhood: the record documents the financial link directly. The founding of the neighborhood itself, its lots and its first families, belongs to the story of the Second Aliyah and is told there.

The second track began with a massacre. At Easter, on April 6–7, 1903, a pogrom broke out in the town of Kishinev. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, 49 Jews were killed and more than 500 injured; 700 houses were looted and destroyed and 600 businesses and shops looted; about 2,000 families were left homeless; and the losses were put at 2,500,000 gold rubles.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev] Incitement had come first, in print. P. Krushevan's newspaper Bessarabets had incited the population with a steady stream of articles, among them a blood libel.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev]

The same encyclopedia goes further, and its words should be read as its own judgment. Local and central officials, it states, prepared the pogrom, "evidently with the backing of the minister of the interior, V. Plehve," and the town's garrison of 5,000 soldiers did not intervene.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev] The word "evidently" marks an inference from the evidence, not a document of instruction, and the role of Plehve rests in this book's sources on this one reference work. Herzl's own meeting with Plehve later that summer belongs to the story of his diplomacy and is told in that section.

The world responded. Protest meetings were held in London, Paris and New York. In the United States a protest petition was handed to President Theodore Roosevelt for transmission to the tsar, and the tsar refused it. The poet Bialik answered the pogrom with his poem "In the Town of Death."[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev] Kishinev was not the end. A second riot in the town in October 1905 killed 19 Jews and injured 56, and some Jewish youth formed self-defense units.[cite:rs_rd1_ejud_kishinev]

The pogrom fell inside a movement of people far larger than Zionism. Between 1881 and 1914, according to the historian Shmuel Ettinger, more than 2.5 million Jews left Eastern Europe, about 80,000 a year. About 2 million went to the United States, about 350,000 to Western Europe, and about 300,000 to other overseas countries, Palestine among them.[cite:rs_rd1_ettinger] The proportions matter for everything that follows. The great majority of Jews leaving Eastern Europe in this period chose America. Palestine was one destination within a category of about 300,000. The question in 1903 was urgent and practical: where could people in danger go now?

That question produced the offer. After the Kishinev pogrom, Joseph Chamberlain, Britain's Colonial Secretary, offered Herzl the Guas Ngishu plateau in British East Africa for Jewish settlement.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] [cite:wiki_kishinev_pogrom] The territory lay in what is now Kenya, not Uganda, but the plan has been remembered ever since, inaccurately, as "the Uganda Scheme." The documented record ties the offer's timing to the pogrom. It does not establish what longer-term purposes, if any, Chamberlain or the British government had in making it; the record documents the offer and its trigger, not Britain's wider calculations, which belong to the later story of British policy.

The Sixth Zionist Congress opened in Basel on August 23, 1903, with about 600 delegates, the largest Congress to that date. It was the last Herzl would chair.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] He put the offer before it with careful limits. In his opening address he said of East Africa that "Zion this certainly is not and can never become," and described it as only an auxiliary colonizing measure.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] He also called it a Nachtasyl, a night shelter: a temporary refuge from immediate danger, not a replacement for the Land of Israel.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki]

The question before the delegates was narrow. They were not asked to accept East Africa as the national home. They were asked whether to send a commission to examine it. The Congress voted 295 in favor and 178 against, with 98 abstentions.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_c] [cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] Another source used in this book gives the vote as 295–177.[cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The difference is a single vote and does not change the outcome, but it is a disagreement between sources, and the more widely given figure, 295–178, is the one used here.

The numbers describe a divided body. With 98 abstaining, the 295 in favor were only just over half of the 571 delegates who voted or abstained. Then members of the Russian delegation walked out, arguing that the plan violated the Basel Program.[cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] [cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_c] The sources differ slightly on who left: one describes the Russian Zionist delegation walking out, another speaks of members of the delegation.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] [cite:rs_rd1_israeled_sixth] Either way it was a stronger act than a recorded vote against.

Both sides had a serious case. Herzl's, as his own framing of a night shelter implies, was a case about time. People were being killed now; a great power was offering land; and a refuge, explicitly temporary and explicitly not Zion, could save lives while the political work for Palestine went on. His opponents' case was about the program the movement had adopted six years earlier. The Basel Program named Palestine. To send a commission elsewhere, in their view, was to break the founding commitment of the organization they belonged to. The record does not show, however, that every Russian Zionist shared the walkout, nor that the rift was never repaired. The movement went on functioning after 1903, and the question was settled two years later.

The familiar account has it that Palestine was always the only place Zionists were seriously prepared to build. The record of 1903 does not support that. Six years after Basel, the movement's own Congress voted to examine a territory in East Africa, as a considered answer to escalating violence and not as a gesture, and the vote split its delegates badly enough that one delegation left the hall. It was the nearest the movement came, after 1897, to turning away from Palestine. At the same time the record limits the point: what passed was an inquiry, framed by its own sponsor as a shelter for the night. Whether the 1903 vote would have led anywhere had Herzl lived is a question no document can answer. How the matter ended, at the Seventh Congress of 1905, is told in [scene:s04f|the next section].

Some things the sources leave unsettled. They do not give the exact date of Chamberlain's offer, beyond placing it after the pogrom. They rest the role of Plehve on a single reference work's inference. They do not say how many of the 98 abstainers sympathized with either side, or what each delegate's reasons were. What they do establish is the shape of 1902–1903: a bank opening its doors in Jaffa while a massacre in Kishinev drove the same movement to vote on whether to look for refuge somewhere else.
