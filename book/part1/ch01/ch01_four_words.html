Four Words: Hebrew, Jewish, Israeli, Zionist

Four words are often used as if they meant the same thing: Hebrew, Jewish, Israeli and Zionist. They do not. One is a language and an ancient name. One names a people and a religion. One is a citizenship. One is a political movement. Each has its own history, and the groups of people they describe overlap without matching. Much of the confusion in arguments about this region starts when one of the four is quietly swapped for another, so that a statement about a state is heard as a statement about a people, or a statement about a people is taken as a statement about a political program. This section takes the four words in turn, sets out what the record says about each, and ends with a diagram of who belongs to which.

Hebrew: a language and an ancient name. The word first appears in the Bible at Genesis 14:13, which calls Abram "Abram the Hebrew," ha-ʿIvri. [cite:fw_bg_gen1413] [cite:fw_thetorah_hebrews] Where the word comes from is not settled. The rabbis of the early commentary Genesis Rabbah already offered several explanations: that Abram stood on one side and the whole world on the other, that he descended from an ancestor named Eber, or that he came from across the river. Britannica notes that it may come from ʿever, "the other side." [cite:fw_thetorah_hebrews] [cite:fw_brit_hebrew_people] Many scholars have also linked the word to the Ḥabiru or ʿApiru, a marginal social class named in Near Eastern texts of the second millennium BCE, but there is no consensus on that link. [cite:fw_thetorah_hebrews] In the Hebrew Bible itself, "Hebrew" is mostly the name that other peoples use for the Israelites, or that Israelites use of themselves when speaking to foreigners; it is rarely their everyday name for themselves. Reference books follow a rough convention, "Hebrews" for the earliest period, then "Israelites," then "Jews" after the exile in Babylon, though the ancient texts do not follow it strictly. [cite:fw_brit_hebrew_people]

Today the word mostly names a language. Hebrew is a Semitic language of the Northwest branch, alongside Aramaic. [cite:fw_brit_hebrew_lang] A common line holds that for centuries it was used only in prayer and scripture. Half of that is right. Hebrew stopped being anyone's everyday spoken language over several centuries; scholars usually put the end somewhere between about 200 and 400 CE, with Aramaic taking its place in daily speech, and Britannica dates the start of the decline as early as the third century BCE. [cite:fw_wiki_mishnaic] [cite:fw_aha_medieval] [cite:fw_brit_hebrew_lang] But it never shrank to prayer alone. Through the Middle Ages it remained a working written language for poetry, both religious and secular, for some legal contracts, for philosophy and science, and for grammar and dictionaries. [cite:fw_aha_medieval] What came back in the late nineteenth century was Hebrew as a spoken mother tongue. Eliezer Ben-Yehuda moved to Jerusalem in 1881 and raised a son described as the first child to grow up speaking modern Hebrew; teachers who made Hebrew the language of their schools played a large part. [cite:fw_mjl_benyehuda] In 2018 the Knesset's Nation-State Law named Hebrew "the state's language" and gave Arabic a "special status"; NPR reported that this removed Arabic's earlier standing as an official language. [cite:fw_adalah_nsl] [cite:fw_npr_nsl]

Jewish: a people and a religion. To be Jewish is understood both as belonging to a people and as belonging to a religion, and Pew found that American Jews weigh the two differently: some see it mainly as religion, others as culture or ancestry. [cite:fw_pew2020_report] Under traditional Jewish law, a Jew is anyone born to a Jewish mother or converted according to that law. [cite:fw_mjl_whoisjew] In 1983 the American Reform rabbinate added that the child of a Jewish father may also be presumed Jewish, a presumption to be confirmed by public acts of Jewish identification such as a Hebrew name or a Bar or Bat Mitzvah. [cite:fw_ccar_1983] Israel's Chief Rabbinate, which controls Jewish marriage in Israel, does not accept every definition the movements use. [cite:fw_mjl_whoisjew] In Pew's 2020 survey of American Jews, 27 percent described themselves religiously as atheist, agnostic or "nothing in particular" while still considering themselves Jewish by ancestry or culture. [cite:fw_pew2020_report]

Nor is there one Jewish appearance. The Beta Israel of Ethiopia were recognized as Jews by Israel's Sephardi Chief Rabbi in 1973, and Operation Solomon in 1991 flew more than 14,000 of them to Israel. [cite:fw_ynet_betaisrael] Israel's statistics bureau counted 168,800 residents of Ethiopian origin at the end of 2022. [cite:fw_israelfact_immig] India had three historic Jewish communities, the Bene Israel near Mumbai, the Cochin Jews of Kerala and the Baghdadi Jews who came in the British era; most have since emigrated, chiefly to Israel. [cite:fw_jvl_india] In Kaifeng, China, a Jewish community is recorded from the Middle Ages, and several hundred people there still claim that descent, though Israel and Orthodox authorities require them to convert formally. [cite:fw_wiki_kaifeng] In the United States, Pew found that 8 percent of Jewish adults identify as Hispanic, Black, Asian, multiracial or another race, and 15 percent of those under thirty; the Jews of Color Initiative, counting differently, estimates at least 12 to 15 percent. [cite:fw_pew2020_key] [cite:fw_joci_counting] Israel's statistics bureau put the world's Jewish population at nearly 15.9 million on 1 January 2026, about 46 percent of them in Israel, as reported by i24NEWS; the Jewish Agency's estimate for the start of 2024 was 15.8 million. [cite:fw_i24fr_rh2026] [cite:fw_toi_wjp2024] Most of the world's Jews live outside Israel.

Israeli: a citizenship. An Israeli is a citizen of the State of Israel, whatever his or her religion or ancestry. [cite:fw_cbs_st02] In its release of 9 September 2026, Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics put the population at about 10.3 million: about 7.83 million "Jews and others," about 2.17 million Arabs, and about 304,000 foreign nationals. [cite:fw_uanews_rh2026] [cite:fw_i24fr_rh2026] Arabs are therefore about a fifth of the population. Most are Muslims, about 1.85 million at the end of 2025; Christians number about 184,000, most of them Arabs; and the Druze about 150,000 (at the end of 2022). [cite:fw_jns_muslims] [cite:fw_israelfact_christians] [cite:fw_cbs_druze2023] The bureau's "others" are mostly immigrants who came under the Law of Return, which since 1970 admits the children, grandchildren and spouses of Jews, but who are not registered as Jews themselves. [cite:fw_cbs_117_2026] [cite:fw_jvl_lor] Recent releases no longer report Jews as a separate headline figure; the last separate count was 7.2 million in 2023, about 73 percent. [cite:fw_wiki_israelijews]

Two details keep the count honest. First, the bureau counts residents, not only citizens. Its figures include East Jerusalem since 1967 and the Golan since 1982, and most Palestinians in East Jerusalem hold permanent residency rather than citizenship, as many Golan Druze long did. [cite:fw_cbs_st02] [cite:fw_toi_ej2020] [cite:fw_wiki_druze] Second, Israel's own records keep citizenship apart from a second entry called le'om, usually translated "nationality," which records a person as Jewish, Arab and so on. In 2013 Israel's Supreme Court refused to let a group of citizens register their nationality as "Israeli," holding that no distinct Israeli nationality had been shown to exist. [cite:fw_versa_ornan] In Israeli law, then, "Israeli" is a citizenship and not a nationality, which is one reason the words are so easily confused.

Zionist: a political movement. Zionism is the movement that sought a Jewish national home, and later a state, in the Land of Israel, and that has supported that state since 1948. [cite:fw_je_basel] [cite:fw_brit_basel] It is a political position, not a birthright: a person can be Jewish without being a Zionist, and a Zionist without being Jewish. It also did not begin with Theodor Herzl, though he gave it its organization. In 1843 Yehuda Alkalai, a Sephardi rabbi near Belgrade, called for organized settlement in the land, and later settled there himself. [cite:fw_jvl_alkalai] In 1862 Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer argued that redemption could begin by human effort, and the socialist Moses Hess argued that the Jews would remain a homeless people until they had a country of their own. [cite:fw_je_kalischer] [cite:fw_brit_hess] After the pogroms of 1881 Leon Pinsker published Auto-Emanzipation anonymously in 1882 and became a leader of the "Lovers of Zion" societies; the first Bilu pioneers landed at Jaffa in July 1882, and Baron Edmond de Rothschild began funding the new colonies in late 1882. [cite:fw_brit_pinsker] [cite:fw_jvl_firstaliyah] [cite:fw_ramathanadiv] Nathan Birnbaum coined the word "Zionism" in 1890. [cite:fw_jvl_birnbaum]

What Herzl added was the organization. His book Der Judenstaat appeared in February 1896, and it treated Argentina as well as Palestine as possible territories. [cite:fw_wiki_judenstaat] In August 1897 the First Zionist Congress in Basel founded the Zionist Organization, elected Herzl its president, and adopted a program seeking "a publicly and legally assured home in Palestine." [cite:fw_wiki_firstcongress] [cite:fw_je_basel] Even then the destination was argued over: in 1903 the movement voted to study a British offer of land in East Africa, in present-day Kenya, before rejecting it in 1905. [cite:fw_jvl_uganda] The movement had many schools: political, practical, labour, religious, revisionist and cultural, which disagreed about borders, methods and whether a state was the goal at all. [cite:fw_wiki_types] Who came first moves the start of the story back by half a century. It does not change how the state was built, which is the subject of the volumes that follow.

Jews who are not Zionists, and Zionists who are not Jews. Opposition to Zionism has come from Jews from the beginning. In 1885 American Reform rabbis declared that Jews were "no longer a nation, but a religious community" and rejected a return to Palestine, a position the movement revised in 1937. [cite:fw_aja_pittsburgh] The socialist Bund, founded in Vilna weeks after the Basel Congress, opposed Zionism and argued for Jewish life where Jews already lived. [cite:fw_wiki_bund] Within Orthodoxy, the Satmar rabbi Joel Teitelbaum argued in 1961 that Zionism is incompatible with Judaism, and Neturei Karta, founded in Jerusalem in 1938 (some accounts say 1937), rejects the State of Israel outright; it numbers in the low thousands, and other Haredi bodies have disavowed its activities. [cite:fw_wiki_vayoel] [cite:fw_wiki_nk] In January 2019 the American group Jewish Voice for Peace declared itself opposed to Zionism. [cite:fw_972_jvp] These positions are told more fully in [scene:s00b3|Dissent from Every Lineage].

Zionism has also had non-Jewish supporters. Christian Zionism grew out of English Puritan "restorationism" in the seventeenth century and later out of dispensationalist theology, which reads Jewish return to the land as part of a prophetic sequence. [cite:fw_wiki_christianzionism] In 1891 the American evangelist William Blackstone petitioned President Benjamin Harrison to "give Palestine back" to the Jews. [cite:fw_wiki_blackstone] Christians United for Israel, founded in 2006, announced in December 2020 that it had passed 10 million members, a figure it counts itself and has not defined. [cite:fw_heritagefl_cufi] How many Jews call themselves Zionists depends heavily on how the question is asked. Recent American surveys range from 37 percent, in a survey for the Jewish Federations, to about 80 percent "Zionist or slightly Zionist," in an opt-in panel run by the Jewish People Policy Institute, and federation leaders put part of the gap down to disagreement over what the word means. [cite:fw_inss_jfna] [cite:fw_jppi_2024] [cite:fw_jweekly_2026]

The diagram above puts the three groups of people side by side. The circles overlap, but none contains another. A person can be Jewish, Israeli and Zionist at once; or Jewish and Zionist but not Israeli; or Jewish, living in Israel, and opposed to Zionism, as Neturei Karta, founded in Jerusalem, is; or Jewish and neither, the position of the Satmar movement's founding text and of the organization Jewish Voice for Peace. About a fifth of Israel's residents are Arab, and some of the bureau's "others" are not registered as Jewish. And there are Zionists who are neither Jewish nor Israeli, the Christian Zionists among them. Hebrew has no circle, because it is a language and an ancient name, not a group anyone joins today.

What the record shows, and what it does not. The record settles the distinctions themselves. Hebrew is a language and an ancient name; Jewish is a people and a religion, entered by birth or conversion and held by believers and non-believers alike; Israeli is a citizenship shared by Jews, Arabs and others; and Zionism is a political movement whose ideas go back to the 1840s and whose organization dates from 1897. Not all Jews are Zionists, not all Israelis are Jews, and not all Zionists are Jews. Treating any of these words as a synonym for another produces errors in both directions: it turns criticism of a state or a movement into an accusation against a people, and it turns a people into the agent of a state's policy. The record also leaves some things open. The origin of the word "Hebrew" is debated; no single date marks the end of spoken Hebrew; the share of Jews who are Zionists depends on how the word is defined and how the question is asked; and Israel's headline statistics now combine Jews with "others," so the exact Jewish share of the population in 2026 is not published as a single figure.
