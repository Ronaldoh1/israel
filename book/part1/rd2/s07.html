The Mandate: Palestine as a Named Polity

The promises of the war years became a system of government in two steps. At San Remo in April 1920, Britain was assigned the Mandate for Palestine; in July 1922 the League of Nations ratified it. [cite:san_remo_1920] The League, founded in 1920 under the Treaty of Versailles, presented the mandate system as Woodrow Wilson's way of distinguishing temporary trusteeship, aimed at eventual self-government, from outright colonial annexation. In practice, mandates were run essentially as colonies. [cite:san_remo_wiki] [cite:mandate_text] Both halves belong in the record: the legal design, and the administrative reality that diverged from it.

A durable popular image holds that Palestine was an empty desert until Jewish pioneers made it bloom. The documents of the Mandate describe something else entirely: an administered country with a name, a population, institutions and citizens.

From 1922 to 1948 Britain governed Palestine as a working state administration. It issued passports, minted currency, printed stamps, ran the police and the courts, and staffed a civil service, all under a High Commissioner in Jerusalem. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] The first High Commissioner, appointed in 1920, was Herbert Samuel, a British Jew and committed Zionist. He has been described as the first Jewish governor of Palestine since the Roman period, and he was appointed by the same Cabinet that had issued the Balfour Declaration. [cite:wiki_herbert_samuel_hc] His appointment was debated at the time. It sharpened Arab suspicion that the Mandate was built to deliver the Balfour promise. It also placed the declaration's contradiction inside the administration itself: one government charged at once with fostering a Jewish national home and with safeguarding the rights of the existing Arab majority.

The civic apparatus can be described in specifics. The Palestinian Citizenship Order of 1925, issued under Article 7 of the Mandate, came into force on August 1, 1925, and created a legal citizenship of Palestine. Roughly 70,000 passports were issued under it, brown documents stamped "BRITISH PASSPORT / PALESTINE." Their holders, Arab and Jewish alike, used them to cross borders, trade, study abroad and perform the hajj, the Muslim pilgrimage. The word "British" on the cover has led casual observers to misremember them ever since; the citizenship they recorded was Palestinian. The historian Lauren Banko's study The Invention of Palestinian Citizenship, 1918–1947 traces how this legal status was created, with its own bureaucratic history, population records and disputes over who qualified. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] From 1927 the Palestine pound circulated as legal tender, and Palestine issued its own postage stamps.

Many of those passports survive, kept by descendants of Palestinian families as evidence of a civic existence before 1948. [cite:banko_palestinian_citizenship] They are a different kind of evidence from a population estimate: individual, dated, named. They do not settle modern arguments about citizenship, return or statehood, and the record gives no count of how many survive. What they establish is narrower, and firm: a formal, legal Palestinian citizenship existed under British administration decades before 1948.

The population those documents described was overwhelmingly Arab at the outset. At the start of the Mandate, Palestine had roughly 600,000 Arabs and 60,000 Jews, about 90 percent and 10 percent respectively. (Sources differ here: the account of the Churchill White Paper puts the Jewish population at about 80,000 in 1922.) By 1947, the last full year of the Mandate, the figures were roughly 1.3 million Arabs and 600,000 Jews. Jewish growth came largely through waves of immigration from Europe between the wars, heaviest in the 1930s as antisemitism in Europe intensified. [cite:mandate_palestine_wiki]

Land ownership tells a related story. The Jewish National Fund, a Zionist land-purchasing body, had been founded in 1901. By 1948, the Fund and other Zionist institutions had acquired about 9.8 percent of Mandate Palestine through purchase. [cite:jnf_wiki] After roughly fifty years of organized, well-funded buying, Jewish institutional landholding remained under 10 percent. Most of the territory of the state that emerged in 1948 was acquired not through purchase but through the war of that year and the displacement that accompanied it. Those events belong to a later part of this book.

None of this asserts that a modern Palestinian nation-state existed, or prejudges any political claim. It establishes that for twenty-six years a polity called Palestine was named in international law and functioned as one, with a citizenry, a currency, courts and a civil service. Whatever judgment one reaches about what came afterward, it did not happen to an empty land. [cite:mandate_text]
