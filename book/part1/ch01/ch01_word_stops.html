The Word That Stops the Conversation

The previous section showed how the word works as a shield in institutions, through definitions, laws and policies. This section asks why it works on individual people. Most of the time no law is involved. A person raises a question about Israel, its founding or its policies, someone answers with the charge of hatred of Jews, and the person goes quiet. Seeing why that happens is the first step to being able to keep thinking when it does.

Two different things under one word

Hatred of Jews as Jews is real, old and lethal; the Holocaust documents where it can lead, and it must be named wherever it appears. Criticism of a state, of its government's policies, or of a political movement is something else: an ordinary part of political life, applied to every other country on earth. Treating the second as if it were the first is the conflation this volume has traced from the start, and it is contested inside the Jewish world itself, by religious anti-Zionists and by the scholars who wrote the Jerusalem Declaration specifically to protect criticism of Israel from the charge. [cite:def_wiki_jda]

Why the charge lands before the reasoning starts

Being publicly accused of bigotry is a social threat: it endangers a person's reputation, friendships and work. Research has measured how the brain treats such threats. In a study published in Science in 2003, Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman and Kipling Williams scanned people's brains while they were shut out of a simple online ball-tossing game. Being excluded activated the anterior cingulate cortex, a region also involved in the distress of physical pain, and the more it activated, the more upset people said they were. "Rejection of any form still appears to register automatically in the brain," Lieberman said. The same study found something hopeful: people whose reasoning regions in the prefrontal cortex became more active showed less of that distress. [cite:ws_sd_eisenberger]

A popular way of describing this is the "amygdala hijack," a phrase the psychologist Daniel Goleman coined in 1995 for moments when the brain's fast threat response seems to override slower reasoning. Neuroscientists now treat the phrase as a metaphor rather than a literal account: the amygdala works mostly in concert with the reasoning regions, not against them, and how strongly a person reacts depends partly on how they interpret what is happening. [cite:ws_pt_dixon] The more accurate picture is still striking. The alarm comes first and automatically; the reasoning can catch up, but only if the person gives it the chance.

A second line of research explains why the accusation targets decent people most effectively. Psychologists Ashby Plant and Patricia Devine distinguished in 1998 between two reasons people avoid prejudice: because they personally believe it is wrong, and because they fear how others will judge them. [cite:ws_plant_devine] Someone who genuinely does not want to be a bigot feels the accusation most sharply. Their own conscience becomes the lever.

The chill, measured

The effect is not only a feeling; it shows up in surveys. In the spring of 2024 the Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression and College Pulse surveyed 58,807 students at 257 American colleges. The topic they most often said was hard to discuss openly on campus was the Israeli-Palestinian conflict: 55 percent said so, up from 26 percent a year earlier. [cite:ws_fire_2025] Many causes feed that number, including real harassment of students on both sides. But it measures what this section describes: a documented political question that a majority of students now treat as unsafe to raise.

Words of contempt run in more than one direction

Labels that shut down thought are not the property of one side. The Hebrew word goy means "nation" and in ordinary speech simply means a non-Jew, much as "gentile" does in English. It can also be used with contempt. The clearest documented case came from one of Israel's most powerful religious figures. In October 2010 Rabbi Ovadia Yosef, former Sephardi chief rabbi of Israel and spiritual leader of the Shas party, told his weekly sermon audience that "Goyim were born only to serve us." The American Jewish Committee called the remarks "abhorrent and an offense to human dignity and human equality." [cite:ws_jta_yosef2010] The record names who said it and who condemned it. It does not support turning one rabbi's sermon into a claim about what Israelis or Jews in general believe, which would be the same mistake, made in reverse, that this section describes.

A case from 2026

In February 2026 the broadcaster Tucker Carlson, interviewing the US ambassador to Israel, proposed genetic testing of "everybody in the land" to find "Abram's descendants." The ambassador, Mike Huckabee, answered that Carlson was drawing on "a dangerous conspiracy theory," the claim that Ashkenazi Jews descend from the Khazars. [cite:kz_jta_carlson2026] Two separate questions were tangled together. One was about evidence: where Ashkenazi ancestry comes from, a question genetics can study and the next part of this volume examines in full. The other was about rights: whether ancestry should decide who may live in a land, a standard applied to no other people. The first public replies answered the second by labelling the first, and the strongest evidence on the ancestry question, DNA from actual Khazar graves, barely appeared in the coverage. [scene:ch01_khazar_pen|The Khazar question] sets out what that evidence shows.

Pushback is not proof, either way

It is tempting to treat the force of a reaction as evidence. If people push back hard, the reasoning goes, they must be hiding something. The record does not support that rule. True histories are defended fiercely too: the Holocaust is met with forceful pushback against those who deny it precisely because it happened. And false stories are defended just as fiercely, as a later part of this volume shows with states that write their founding stories into law. Pushback by itself proves nothing about whether a claim is true.

What can be tested is what the pushback does. A reply that engages the evidence, even angrily, is part of an argument. A reply that replaces the evidence with a label about the questioner's motives ends one. That difference is visible in any exchange, and it is the honest form of the instinct that "the pushback is the tell."

Recognizing the mechanism does not mean dismissing every charge of hatred; many are sincere and many are true. It means being able to feel the alarm and still ask the question it was meant to stop: is what was said hatred of a people, or a question about a state and its record?
