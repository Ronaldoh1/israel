Enterprises Charged, and One That Was Not

The most useful comparison for Epstein is with cases in which prosecutors treated the abuse as the work of an organisation rather than one man. In New York, federal prosecutors charged the singer R. Kelly under the racketeering law, as the head of an enterprise whose staff recruited, arranged travel for and isolated his victims; he was convicted and sentenced to 30 years. [cite:ep8_doj_kelly2022] They did the same with Keith Raniere, the leader of NXIVM, who was sentenced to 120 years. [cite:ep8_doj_raniere2020] The tool exists, and it works.

It does not always work, and the book should say so. In July 2025 a jury acquitted Sean Combs of racketeering conspiracy and sex trafficking, convicting him only of transporting people for prostitution. [cite:ep12_opb_combs2025] An enterprise case is harder to prove than a case against one person. That is a real reason prosecutors hesitate. It is not a reason never to try, and in Epstein's case no one tried: the people who scheduled, recruited and flew for him were immunised in 2008 and never charged after 2019.

The private intelligence firm

One more case shows a part of the machinery Epstein's also had, the effort to manage the people who might expose him. In 2017 Ronan Farrow reported in the New Yorker that Harvey Weinstein had used Black Cube, an Israeli-founded private intelligence firm, hired through the law firm of his lawyer David Boies, to find out what the actress Rose McGowan would write in her memoir and to try to stop the New York Times publishing its investigation. An operative using a false name befriended McGowan. The effort failed; the Times published, and Weinstein was fired within days. [cite:ep12_nhpr_blackcube2017] Part Six set out the reputation work done for Epstein. The Weinstein case shows how far that work can go when money is no object.
