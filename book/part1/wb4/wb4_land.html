How the Land Was Taken

Elon Moreh, 1979. In the first years the army requisitioned private Palestinian land for settlements "for military needs." In 1979 the landowners of Rujeib, near Nablus, challenged the seizure of their fields for the settlement of Elon Moreh, and Israel's Supreme Court ruled in their favour. Under Article 52 of the Hague Regulations, it held, private land could be taken only for genuine military needs, and here political and ideological considerations, including pressure from Gush Emunim, had been dominant; a military administration, it added, cannot create facts on the ground meant to outlast military rule. [cite:wb_dweikat]

"State land." The government did not stop settling; it changed the method. After Elon Moreh the authorities turned to a stringent reading of the Ottoman Land Code of 1858, under which land that was not registered and continuously cultivated could be declared the property of the state. Between 1979 and 1992 they declared 908,000 dunams of the West Bank state land, about 16 percent of the territory, by the Israeli human rights group B'Tselem's count. Israel's position is that such land is public land that an occupying power may administer in trust. [cite:wb_wiki_stateland] Once declared, it became the reserve on which most new settlements were built.

The Civil Administration. In 1981 the military government created, by Military Order 947, a Civil Administration to run the territories' day-to-day affairs. It sits under the Defence Ministry's Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories, issues the permits Palestinians need to enter Israel, to travel and to work, and approves the building of housing in settlements. [cite:wb_wiki_civadmin] For the next four decades, the same office would decide both where Palestinians could build and where settlers could.
