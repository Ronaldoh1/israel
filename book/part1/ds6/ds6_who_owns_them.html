Who Owns the Scrolls?

The scrolls were found in territory that changed hands twice within twenty years. After the war of 1948, Qumran passed under Jordanian control, and the later finds were kept at the Palestine Archaeological Museum in East Jerusalem, where the international team worked. [cite:ds_cojs_acq] [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] In the war of 1967 Israeli soldiers captured East Jerusalem and found the scrolls intact in the museum, which was renamed the Rockefeller Museum in honour of the Rockefeller family's contribution. [cite:ds_cojs_acq]

Jordan has never accepted the transfer. In January 2010, when scrolls were on display in Toronto, Jordan asked Canada to seize them, saying that Israel had taken fourteen scrolls from a museum managed by Jordan in 1967, and citing the 1954 Hague Convention on the protection of cultural property in war. [cite:ds_abc_jordan] Israel refused to negotiate; its foreign ministry said that "The scrolls have no relation to Jordan or the Jordanian people." [cite:ds_abc_jordan] One scroll remained on the other side of the line: the Copper Scroll, found in Cave 3 in 1952, is in the Jordan Archaeological Museum in Amman. [cite:ds_vq_copper]

The Copper Scroll is the strangest object in the collection, and the most concerned with money. It is engraved on copper rather than written in ink, and it is not a religious text but a list of places where treasure was buried or hidden. [cite:ds_vq_copper] Too corroded to unroll, it was sliced into thin strips by H. Wright Baker, a professor of mechanical engineering in Manchester, England. [cite:ds_deseret_copper] It lists sixty-four deposits, from Hebron to Mount Gerizim to Qumran, including ingots of gold and silver, jars, bowls and vestments; the scholar Theodor Gaster estimated the precious metals at 138 tons. [cite:ds_deseret_copper] No treasure from the list has been found. Some scholars think it records the hidden wealth of the Temple, the community or later rebels; others doubt that any group held so much and read it as folklore. [cite:ds_vq_copper] The archaeologist Amos Kloner put it bluntly: "I'm sure the temple treasures were destroyed." [cite:ds_deseret_copper]

The question of ownership turns on the same distinction as everything else on this shelf: between the text and the object. No court has held that anyone owns the words of Isaiah; Israel's Supreme Court said as much in 2000. [cite:ds_abc_qimron] But the leather, the jars and the copper are property, held by states that fought over the land they came from. The caves were in no state when the scrolls were hidden; they have been in two since the scrolls were found.
