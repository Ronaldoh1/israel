What It Costs, and Who Pays

The question of what the architecture costs has two answers, and the volume has kept them apart. The first is the money spent by the network itself, which its donors pay and which the filings record. The second is the costs that fall on others: the public, the taxpayers of states and cities, and the people whose rights or votes the architecture touches. This section sets out both, then closes the volume with what the record settles, what it leaves contested and what it leaves open.

The network's own money. The money AIPAC and its allies spend is private, legal and disclosed in the channels the law requires, and it is paid for by the donors who give it. Measured against the sums at stake, it is not large. Over $100 million in a cycle is a large sum in primaries, where a single race can be decided by a few million dollars; it is small next to the more than $3 billion a year in American military aid that the network supports. [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026] [cite:pm_wapo_suppliers] The comparison does not show that the spending buys the aid. The aid predates AIPAC's committees by decades. It shows the proportion: the price of the political effort is a small fraction of the policy it defends.

The costs that fall on others. Three of the volume's channels place costs on people who did not choose to pay them. The trips are paid for by a charity, so its donors deduct their gifts, and critics argue that the public bears part of the cost of the travel through the tax deduction; defenders answer that the same is true of every charity. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] The anti-boycott laws in many of the thirty-eight states require contractors to certify their position on one country, a requirement the Arkansas Times refused; the courts have divided, and the Arkansas law stands. [cite:pm_wiki_antibds] [cite:pm_aclu_waldrip] And the bond purchases place the savings of public funds in a single foreign government's debt, a choice that a former Pennsylvania investment chief warned against and that some Palm Beach County residents have challenged in court. [cite:pm_icij_bonds] These are not costs in the sense of money taken; they are costs in the sense of choices made for the public by officials whom the network courts.

The cost to the political process. The last cost is the one the record measures least well. Critics of the network argue that its principal effect is not the races it enters but those it never has to enter: members who decline to criticize Israel because they have seen what happened to Bowman, Bush and Massie. [cite:factcheck_udp_2024] [cite:pbs_massie_takeaways] Defenders answer that members who support Israel do so because they and their voters believe in the alliance, and that the network's money follows positions rather than creating them. The filings cannot settle this argument, because the effect critics describe would leave no filing. What the filings do show is that the network's spending has helped defeat members of both parties, that it has lost several of its largest campaigns, and that it has spent most heavily in primaries. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2022] [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026]

What is settled. The structure and the scale. The network is built from organizations with documented legal forms; its spending is disclosed, and since 2021 it has grown from millions to more than a hundred million dollars a cycle. The recipients, the targets and the trips are on the record, in the United States and in Britain and Canada. The Israeli government's own spending on influence has grown fastest of all since 2023.

What is contested. What the money does. Whether it changes votes or follows them; whether its primary spending decided races that had other causes; whether accusations of antisemitism against its critics are accurate in each case or used as a weapon; whether the trips educate or recruit; whether the anti-boycott laws protect states or penalize speech; and how to count, since the trackers' totals for the same cycle differ widely.

What is open. The donors behind AIPAC's lobby and foundation, which are not public; the travel of state and local officials, which no federal body records; the per-cycle recipient lists built from the Federal Election Commission's own files, which are still being compiled for this library; the full results of the 2026 cycle, which was still running when this was written; and, outside the few countries with disclosure laws, almost everything about the lobby abroad.

The architecture is not hidden. Most of the figures in this volume come from filings that the organizations made under laws that require them to, or from journalists and researchers who have read those filings. What the filings do not show is intent, and the volume has not supplied it. A reader who wants to know whether this architecture is an alliance's ordinary advocacy or something more has the same documents to read as its defenders and its critics, and now has them in one place.
