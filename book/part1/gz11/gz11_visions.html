What They Said They Want

"Waterfront property." In February 2024, four months into the war, Jared Kushner, President Trump's son-in-law and former senior adviser, spoke at Harvard. "Gaza's waterfront property could be very valuable," he said, "if people would focus on building up livelihoods." From Israel's perspective, he would "move the people out and then clean it up"; for the civilians, "I would just bulldoze something in the Negev"; and, he added, "I am not sure there is much left of Gaza at this point." [cite:gz_jc_kushner2024]

"We'll own it." A year later, on 4 February 2025, with Benjamin Netanyahu beside him at the White House, President Trump said that "the U.S. will take over the Gaza Strip," that "we'll own it," that it could become "the Riviera of the Middle East," and that its people should go to other countries; the aim, he said, was ending "the death and destruction." [cite:gz_whyy_riviera] Asked days later whether Palestinians would have the right to return, he said: "No, they wouldn't." He would build "safe communities, a little bit away from where they are, where all of this danger is." His press secretary and secretary of state had said any relocation would be temporary. "Think of it," he said, "as a real estate development for the future." [cite:gz_pbs_noreturn] On 26 February he posted on his own social network an AI-generated video titled "Trump Gaza": a giant gold statue of himself, yachts off the beach, dollar bills falling from the sky, Elon Musk dipping bread in hummus and Netanyahu drinking by a pool. [cite:gz_fortune_aivideo]

Israel's government. On 17 February 2025 Netanyahu said he was "committed to US President Trump's plan for the creation of a different Gaza," and his defence minister, Israel Katz, set up a body to oversee the "voluntary departure" of Gazans. [cite:gz_aj_netanyahu_feb25] In May Netanyahu called the plan "so revolutionary," summed it up as "Residents of Gaza who want to leave can leave," and made it one of his conditions for ending the war, alongside the return of the hostages and Hamas's disarmament and removal. [cite:gz_jweekly_may25] In July the government's plan for a "humanitarian city" on the ruins of Rafah, to hold 600,000 people at first and eventually the whole population, drew the former prime minister Ehud Olmert's verdict: "It is a concentration camp. I am sorry." The opposition leader Yair Lapid called it "a bad idea from every possible perspective"; the government said any transfer would be "voluntary." [cite:gz_aj_humcity] On 7 August Netanyahu said Israel intended to take control of all of Gaza, but "we don't want to keep" it: "We want to have a security perimeter. We don't want to govern it," and would hand it to Arab forces that would give Gazans "a good life." [cite:gz_mm_netanyahu_aug25] In September his finance minister, Bezalel Smotrich, told a real estate conference in Tel Aviv that Gaza was a "bonanza" that "pays for itself," that "we have to see how we are dividing up the land in percentages," and that a "business plan" was "on President Trump's desk." Netanyahu has said he does not intend to re-establish settlements in Gaza. [cite:gz_toi_bonanza]

The prospectus. On 31 August 2025 the Washington Post published a 38-page prospectus circulating in Washington, titled the Gaza Reconstitution, Economic Acceleration and Transformation Trust, the "GREAT Trust." It proposed an American-run trusteeship of at least ten years. Gaza's people would leave "voluntarily" or move into "restricted, secured zones" while the strip was rebuilt. Each person who left would receive $5,000 in cash, four years of rent subsidy and a year of food. Landowners would receive digital tokens redeemable for apartments in "AI-powered, smart cities." Its financial model counted $23,000 saved for every person who left, and a nearly fourfold return on $100 billion in ten years. It was drawn up by Israelis who had helped create the Gaza Humanitarian Foundation, with financial modelling by a team then working at Boston Consulting Group, which said the work had not been approved and dismissed two senior partners. [cite:gz_bg_great]

The alternative. In March 2025 Arab leaders meeting in Cairo adopted an Egyptian plan to rebuild Gaza in under six years for about $53 billion, under a committee of Palestinian technocrats, so that its people could "stay on their land without displacement." The White House rejected it, saying it did not "address the reality that Gaza is currently uninhabitable"; Israel's foreign ministry called relocation an "opportunity for the Gazans to have free choice based on their free will." [cite:gz_nbc_arabplan]

None of these statements is in dispute; each is on the record in the speaker's own words. What they show, side by side, is a consistent subject. The land is described in the language of property, development and return on investment. The people who live on it appear as a cost to be relocated, or as beneficiaries of a choice to leave. Their stated reasons were security, the defeat of Hamas and a better life for Gazans who chose to go. In September 2025 the plan that became the basis of the ceasefire said: "No one will be forced to leave Gaza, and those who wish to leave will be free to do so and free to return," and "Israel will not occupy or annex Gaza."
