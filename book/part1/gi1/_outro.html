Part One established that "Greater Israel" is a loose English rendering of Eretz Yisrael HaShlema, "the Whole Land of Israel", a phrase whose territory has depended on who was speaking. [cite:gi1_wiki_greaterisrael] [cite:gi1_pipes_calumny] The widest claims rest on Genesis 15:18 and Deuteronomy 11:24. Scripture's own boundary lists in Numbers 34 and Ezekiel 47 are narrower and stop at the Jordan, and "the river of Egypt" is read by some as the Nile and by others as the Wadi el-Arish. [cite:gi1_bg_gen1518] [cite:gi1_bg_deut1124] [cite:gi1_ebg_riveregypt] [cite:gi1_ebg_brookegypt] Three versions of the claim can be traced to named carriers. The maximal version was held by a minority of religious nationalist groups and never adopted as policy. The Revisionist claim to both banks of the Jordan was carried by Jabotinsky, Betar and the Irgun. The post-1967 claim to the land between the sea and the Jordan passed from the Movement for Greater Israel, founded in the summer of 1967 with Labor figures among its founders, into the Likud platform of 1977 and the government guidelines of December 2022. [cite:gi1_wiki_eastbank] [cite:gi1_wiki_mgi] [cite:gi1_jvl_likud1977] [cite:gi1_jns_guidelines] Each version met opposition from other Zionists and other Israelis: the territorialists, the Labor leadership that accepted partition, the Allon Plan and the withdrawals of 1982, 2000 and 2005. Netanyahu said in August 2025 that he felt "very much" connected to the vision, while Olmert had declared it finished in 2008.
