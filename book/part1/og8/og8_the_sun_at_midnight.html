The Sun at Midnight

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The narrator of Apuleius's novel, initiated into the mysteries of Isis, says he saw "the sun at midnight shining brightly". [cite:ts13_cam_apuleius] The phrase is the strangest in the ancient record of initiation: a light where there should be none. In Cusco, the Inca kept the hearts of their kings in a golden sun, and the Church carried its host through the same streets in a monstrance of golden rays. [cite:vt35_wiki_tupacamaru] [cite:vt35_dean_muse] On the mountain of Qoyllurit'i, pilgrims still carry their images to meet the first rays of the morning. [cite:vt35_unesco_qoyllur]

The inner reading takes the sun at midnight as the sun within: a light that shines in the darkness of the closed eye, which the mystery traditions said could be known directly. Read this way, the outer suns of this book, the Punchao, the Coricancha's gold, the monstrance, were images of something the worshipper was meant to find inside. The reading notices that when the outer sun was seized, it was seized as gold and as trophy: the conquistadors melted the temple walls for a ransom, and the Punchao may have ended in a king's treasury. [cite:vt35_wiki_coricancha] [cite:vt35_julien_punchao] What can be melted is the outer sun.

The reading must carry a warning that the record supplies. The Mexica sun was not a private light: it was a state god that, in its own theology, had to be fed with human hearts. [cite:vt35_wiki_tonatiuh] [cite:vt35_smith_tzompantli] The inner reading does not romanticise that. It takes from the old suns the image of light in darkness, not the altars.

The record limits the reading. Apuleius wrote a novel, and its account of initiation is literature; the Inca and Mexica sun cults were religions of empire, and the record does not show their priests teaching an inner light. [cite:ts13_cam_apuleius] [cite:vt35_wiki_willaqumu] The reading is the reader's.
