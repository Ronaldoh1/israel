Counting the Country: People and Land Before Partition

Two slogans have long stood in for the facts about Mandate Palestine's population and land. One describes "a land without a people." The other holds that Palestinian Arabs owned 94 percent of the land. The Mandate's own statistics refute the first outright and complicate the second. Because nearly every later argument about 1947 leans on these figures, they are worth setting down plainly.

Britain's census of 1922 counted 757,182 people, of whom 83,794 were Jews, about 11 percent [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The census of 1931 found Jews at 17 percent, Muslims at 73 percent and Christians at 9 percent [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The Jewish population had doubled in the intervening years, but the growth in the non-Jewish population was larger than the entire Jewish population. Of the Jews counted in 1931, 42 percent had been born in Palestine and 58 percent abroad. British records count 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946 [cite:ssrn_mandate_immigration].

By the end of the Mandate the scale had changed, but the proportions had not reversed. UNSCOP, the body that would later draw on the 1945 land survey described below, estimated the population at the end of 1946 at about 1,846,000: some 1,203,000 Arabs, or 65 percent, and 608,000 Jews, or 33 percent [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. On the eve of partition, the country was full of people, and about two-thirds of them were Arab.

Land is the harder subject, and the one most often misused. The key source is the 1945 Village Statistics survey, compiled for the Anglo-American Committee and later used by UNSCOP. It classified land by ownership, by use and by tax category [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. Measured against it, the partition plan of 1947 stands out: the plan allotted about 56 percent of the territory to the Jewish state at a time when Jews owned under 7 percent of the land [cite:historyskills_partition]. The proposed Jewish state would also have contained a large Arab minority.

The disparity is real. So is a caveat that the second slogan tends to erase. The claim that Palestinian Arabs privately owned 94 percent of the land counts large areas of state and public land as Arab-owned. The 1945 survey itself separates private ownership from state land, and an honest comparison has to use its categories rather than collapse them [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. Jews owned a small share of the land, but much land was held by the state rather than privately.

Neither correction cancels the other. "A land without a people" is refuted by every census. The 94 percent figure overstates private Arab ownership by folding in state land. What remains when both slogans are set aside is a country that was about two-thirds Arab in 1946, in which Jews owned under 7 percent of the land and were offered about 56 percent of the territory. Every serious argument about 1947 starts from these figures.
