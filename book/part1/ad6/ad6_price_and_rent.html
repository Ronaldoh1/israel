Price and Rent: What a Home Costs a Working Household Now

The promise attached to the postwar mortgage was simple enough to put in a statute. The Housing Act of 1949 set the national goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family". [cite:ad6_prrac_timeline] The working version that followed was that a household with steady earnings could buy a house in early adulthood, pay it down over a career and own it outright by retirement, while renting was a stage on the way. This section measures that version against the current record with four numbers: how many years of income a home costs, how much of the household it takes to carry the loan, how old buyers are when they first manage it, and what renters pay meanwhile. A fifth question, whether large investors are buying up the houses, is answered at the end, because it is the most widely repeated explanation and the record on it is narrower than the claim.

Years of income: the price-to-income ratio

The simplest test of affordability divides the median price of a home by the median household income. A median is the middle value: half of homes cost more, half less, and the same for incomes. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS) computes this ratio each year for existing single-family homes. Across 1980 to 1999 it stayed between 3.1 and 3.4; it was 3.2 in 1988. It climbed to 4.7 in 2005 during the housing bubble, fell to 3.3 in 2011 after the crash, and stood at 4.2 in 2017. [cite:ad6_jchs_2018] It was 4.1 in 2019 and 5.0 in 2024. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] Dividing 5.0 by 3.2 gives 1.56: a typical home now costs a little over one and a half times as many years of the typical household's income as it did in the 1990s. Between 2019 and 2024 alone, median single-family prices rose 48 per cent while median income rose 22 per cent. [cite:us14_jchs_pti]

Two cautions keep that number honest. First, the ratio did not "double", as a widely repeated figure holds; it rose by roughly half. Second, JCHS has revised its own series between editions: a January 2024 release put the 2022 ratio at a record 5.6, while the October 2025 release describes 2024's 5.0 as nearly matching or returning to the record. [cite:ad6_jchs_2024] [cite:ad6_jchs_2025] The editions agree on the shape, a long plateau near 3 followed by a climb to about 5, and differ on the exact peak. National averages also hide a wide spread: in 2024 the ratio was over 12 in San Jose, 10.8 in Los Angeles and 7.3 in New York, but 2.9 in Akron and 2.8 in Toledo. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] In much of the country a house still costs about what it cost relative to income a generation ago; in the large coastal metros where many of the best-paid jobs are, it does not.

Carrying the loan: the interest rate

A buyer does not pay the price; a buyer pays the mortgage. The monthly cost of a 30-year fixed loan depends on the price and on the interest rate. On 15 October 2026 the average 30-year rate in Freddie Mac's weekly survey was 7.40 per cent, against 6.27 per cent a year earlier. [cite:ad6_pmms] By the standard amortisation formula, each $100,000 borrowed for 30 years costs about $617 a month in principal and interest at 6.27 per cent and about $692 at 7.40 per cent; at 3 per cent it would cost about $422. The same house therefore costs a new buyer about 64 per cent more each month at today's rate than at 3 per cent ($692 divided by $422 is 1.64), before taxes and insurance. Insurance itself has risen sharply: JCHS reports that average monthly home insurance premiums rose 72 per cent from 2019 to 2025. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] Among existing owners, about 24 per cent, or 20.7 million households, spent more than 30 per cent of income on housing in 2024, up 4 million since 2019, and in March 2026 only 23 per cent of homes listed for sale were affordable to a household earning $75,000 or less, against 49 per cent in March 2019. [cite:us14_novoco_jchs2026]

Who gets in, and when

The share of American households that own their home was 65.0 per cent in the second quarter of 2026, the same as a year earlier and below its peak of 69.2 per cent in 2004 and the 25-year average of 66.3 per cent. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] [cite:us15_nahb_q226] Over the longer run the rate has moved within a narrow band: about 63 to 66 per cent from 1965 through the mid-1990s, a rise to 69 in the mid-2000s, a fall to about 63 in 2016 and a recovery to 65. [cite:ad6_fred_hor] Among households headed by someone under 35 the rate was 35.2 per cent, down from 36.4 per cent a year earlier. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226]

The figure most quoted about young buyers comes from the National Association of Realtors (NAR), the realtors' trade association. Its 2025 survey of recent buyers found that first-time buyers made up 21 per cent of purchases between July 2024 and June 2025, the lowest on record, and that their median age was 40, the highest; repeat buyers' median age was 62. [cite:ad6_nar_2025] The survey's method limits what the number can show. NAR mailed questionnaires to 173,250 recent buyers and received 6,103 usable answers, an adjusted response rate of 3.5 per cent. [cite:ad6_nar_2025] It counts only people who actually bought in that year, so its median age describes buyers, not the typical age at which Americans first own; in a year when many younger households are priced out, the buyers who remain are older partly because of who dropped out. The Census under-35 rate, drawn from a large household survey, points the same direction with a smaller move. It does not follow, and should not be said, that a typical buyer now finishes paying a 30-year loan at 70: many owners sell, refinance or repay early, and NAR's buyers are not a sample of the population.

Renting: the burden in the middle

A household is "cost-burdened", in the standard definition used by housing researchers, when it spends more than 30 per cent of its income on housing. In 2024 a record 22.7 million renter households, 49 per cent of all renters, were cost-burdened. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] The low-rent stock shrank at the same time: units renting for under $1,000 a month fell 30 per cent, from 8.3 million in 2014 to 5.8 million in 2024, while units at $1,000 or more rose 46 per cent and those over $2,000 doubled. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] Rent is the cost that most directly blocks the path to ownership, because every month of high rent is a month of savings not made toward a down payment.

Here the record also shows the market working as the textbook says it should. After a wave of apartment construction, national rents fell 0.5 per cent in the year to the first quarter of 2026, and they fell in most Southern and Western markets, where most of the new building went up, while they rose in most Midwestern and Northeastern markets. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] Asking rents for professionally managed apartments were still 29 per cent above their 2020 level. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26] Where cities allowed supply to expand, prices responded; that is consistent with the argument, set out in the previous section, that local building rules act as a tax on housing in the places where prices sit far above the cost of construction. [cite:ad6_glaeser_gyourko]

The investor question

A widely repeated claim holds that Wall Street firms are buying up America's houses and pricing families out. The record supports a narrower version. Large investors in single-family rental homes are a creation of the 2008 crash: in late 2011 no investor owned 1,000 or more such homes, while by 2015 institutional investors held an estimated 170,000 to 300,000. [cite:ad6_gao_sfr] By 2022, 32 institutional investors owned about 450,000 homes, the five largest nearly 300,000. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) put that at about 2 per cent of the nation's single-family rental stock, but about 25 per cent in the Atlanta metro. [cite:ad6_nlihc_gao] The Congressional Research Service, surveying estimates in June 2026, puts institutional owners at roughly 3 to 5 per cent of single-family rental homes nationally, and a smaller share of all single-family homes; by the Census Bureau's 2024 rental-housing survey, about three quarters of the 15.7 million one-unit rentals belonged to owners with ten or fewer properties, and under 2 per cent to owners with more than 100. [cite:ad6_crs_inst]

Two things explain why the claim feels larger than the stock figures. Investors of all sizes, most of them small, bought about 30 per cent of single-family homes sold in December 2025 by one data firm's count, against about 18 per cent in 2018, while large and very large investors accounted for about 3 per cent each; and the large firms' holdings are concentrated in Sunbelt cities, where GAO found they make up a large portion of the single-family rental market. [cite:ad6_crs_inst] [cite:ad6_gao_sfr] The research on effects is mixed. GAO's review found that institutional investors may have contributed to rising prices and rents, and may also have helped stabilise neighbourhoods after the crash; a 2026 GAO study found that population growth and other market fundamentals drove rent increases in six metros; other studies link investor activity to higher prices and reduced access for first-time buyers. [cite:ad6_gao_sfr] [cite:ad6_crs_inst]

The federal government has now acted on the claim. President Trump's Executive Order 14376 of 20 January 2026 directed federal housing agencies to stop financing or facilitating purchases by large institutional investors and asked Treasury to define the term; it did not itself ban purchases. [cite:ad6_dechert_eo] Congress then passed the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, signed on 11 July 2026, which bars any investor controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying more, with exemptions for build-to-rent, new and renovated homes, foreclosures and over-55 communities, and civil penalties of $1 million or three times the purchase price, whichever is greater. [cite:ad6_mayer_road] [cite:ad6_crs_inst] As of October 2026 the ban had not yet taken effect: it begins 180 days after enactment, on about 7 January 2027, and expires after 15 years. Since build-to-rent homes are exempt and made up 11 per cent of new single-family homes in 2025, the law caps one channel of investor growth rather than investor ownership as such. [cite:ad6_nlihc_son26]

The cost record, in sum, shows the designed core of the housing Dream intact and its price stretched. The 30-year loan still works; ownership rates sit near their long-run band; and where supply grew, rents fell. But a home costs about half again as many years of income as in the 1990s, the monthly cost of borrowing has risen, buyers are older, and half of renters pay more than the federal benchmark.
