From Three Militias to One Army, May 1948 – February 1949

It is often assumed that Israel's army was a single national force from the day of independence. It was not. On 14 May 1948 three armed bodies claimed to fight for the new state: the Haganah; the Irgun, commanded by Menachem Begin; and Lehi, led by three men. Each had its own command and its own arms. Making them one army took four months, an order, a confrontation on a Tel Aviv beach in which Jews killed Jews, and an assassination that the new government itself called terrorism.

The order of 26 May 1948. Twelve days after the declaration of independence, the provisional government headed by David Ben-Gurion issued an order establishing the Israel Defense Forces. It outlawed any other armed force and required the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi to merge into the new army. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] The order was the instrument; the question was whether the undergrounds would obey it. The war with the Arab states, told in [scene:rd5_invasion|the section on the Arab states' war], was then in its second week, and the new army was being formed while it fought.

The Altalena, June 1948. The test came within a month. In June 1948 the Irgun brought an arms ship, the Altalena, to the coast, carrying weapons that it intended partly for its own units. Ben-Gurion refused to let the cargo bypass the army. The two positions could not both stand: an Irgun that kept its own weapons was an army beside the army, which the order of 26 May had outlawed. On 22 June IDF units under the command of Yitzhak Rabin shelled the ship off the Tel Aviv beach. Sixteen Irgun members and three IDF soldiers were killed, nineteen dead in all. The Irgun's separate units were then disbanded. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair]

The episode fell during the first truce of the war, which ran from 11 June to 9 July, when arms were reaching the Israeli army from Europe. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce1] The dispute over the Altalena was therefore not about whether weapons should come in, but about who would hold them once they arrived. The same commander, Rabin, would sign the order to expel the inhabitants of Lydda three weeks later. [cite:rs_rd5_palquest_lydda]

The killing of Bernadotte, September 1948. The second test came from Lehi. Count Folke Bernadotte had been appointed United Nations mediator on 21 May 1948; he had supervised the first truce and proposed a settlement giving the Galilee to Israel, the Negev to the Arabs and Jerusalem to the United Nations, which both sides rejected. [cite:rs_rd5_jvl_truce1] On 17 September 1948 members of Lehi assassinated him in Jerusalem. The government's response was the strongest it had: it declared Lehi a terrorist organization and arrested its members. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] The word matters: the government of the new state applied it, in its own name, to a Jewish underground.

The arrests did not last. In February 1949 the Lehi members were released under a general amnesty. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] The record gathered here does not say why the amnesty was granted, how many were held, or whether anyone was tried for the killing.

Absorbed, then promoted. The pattern that emerges is not one of suppression alone. Within four months of its founding the state had established a monopoly of armed force, by order, by shelling and by dissolution. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] Within thirty-five years it had placed the leaders of both absorbed undergrounds in the prime minister's office. Menachem Begin, whose ship the army had shelled, became prime minister in 1977. [cite:wiki_altalena_affair] Yitzhak Shamir, one of Lehi's three leaders, became prime minister in 1983. [cite:wiki_bernadotte_assassination] The line of armed undergrounds that runs through the years before 1948 ends not in their disappearance but in succession to the state's highest office. Begin's new party drew a public protest in a letter signed by Albert Einstein and Hannah Arendt in December 1948, discussed in [scene:rf_s01|the section on the dissenters].

Two readings of this sequence are possible, and the record supports parts of each. One stresses the state's resolve: a provisional government, at war, fired on its own citizens rather than tolerate a private army, and named a Jewish underground's assassination of a UN mediator for what it was. The other stresses how quickly the line was redrawn: the arrests ended in an amnesty within five months of the killing, and the men who had led the Irgun and Lehi later became prime ministers. Both are true of the same events.

What the record does not settle. The sources gathered for this book give the dead of the Altalena but not the fate of its cargo. They name Lehi's members as the assassins of Bernadotte but not the individuals who planned or carried out the killing, and they record the amnesty without its terms or its reasons. They do not say how the Haganah's own structure was carried into the new army. These gaps are real, and this section does not fill them.
