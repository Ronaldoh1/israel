Two Real Histories, Not One Winner

Two related claims circulate constantly. One holds that Jews are the indigenous people of the land and Arabs recent colonizers, or the reverse. The other holds that there is no distinct Palestinian people, only Arabs in general. The shared assumption beneath both is that only one population has a real, ancient claim, and that the other's national identity was invented recently and has no roots. Both rest on the same error. They collapse three different questions into one: whether a population was present, whether its culture was continuous, and when it formed a national identity in the modern political sense. The documented record, from the Ottoman period through 1948, treats these as separate questions with separate answers.

The first answer concerns Jewish presence, and it is not in doubt. Ancient Jewish kingdoms existed in parts of the land, and Jewish religious, cultural and historical connections to the region are documented across archaeological, textual and religious sources spanning more than three thousand years. Mainstream historians of every political orientation accept this continuity. [cite:substack_historical_record_balfour] On its own, however, ancient presence does not settle modern questions of sovereignty or statehood. Historical presence and modern political legitimacy are related but distinct.

The second answer concerns the Arab population, and it is equally documented. According to the Ottoman census of 1871–72, the population of Palestine, excluding Bedouin, was 381,954, roughly 85 percent Muslim, 11 percent Christian and 4 percent Jewish. The presence of this Arabic-speaking population under Ottoman rule is documented continuously for centuries, and modern genetic and archaeological studies find strong Levantine continuity consistent with long-term local ancestry. [cite:cjpme_historical_demography] [cite:factually_archaeological_evidence] The land was not empty, and its people were not newcomers. A counted, continuous population, however, is not yet the same thing as a formal national identity.

The third answer concerns national identity, and here the leading Palestinian historian has documented the timeline himself. Rashid Khalidi, Edward Said Professor of Arab Studies at Columbia and a past president of the Middle East Studies Association, argues in his foundational study Palestinian Identity: The Construction of Modern National Consciousness that formal Palestinian national identity is modern. In his account it grew out of nationalist discourses emerging among Ottoman peoples in the late nineteenth century and sharpened during what he identifies as the critical years of 1917 to 1923. [cite:wiki_palestinian_nationalism] [cite:cup_khalidi_palestinian_identity] Khalidi is explicit that this modern consciousness drew on a much older regional history and population. He does not treat its late emergence as evidence that the people or their culture were invented, and his finding should not be read that way.

The same pattern describes Zionism. Formal Zionist national identity was organized at the First Zionist Congress in Basel in 1897, a modern political movement built on older religious and cultural material, as earlier sections of this book document. The emergence of formal Palestinian national identity in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries follows the same structure. A standard that says "this national identity is recent, therefore illegitimate," applied to one side and not the other, is an inconsistent standard rather than a historical finding. The parallel concerns how national consciousness formed. It does not imply that the two movements' histories run alike in every other respect.

Put together, the record supports three facts at once: an ancient and continuous Jewish connection to the land, a substantial and continuously documented Arab population under Ottoman rule, and modern national identities on both sides that formed through comparable political processes in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. None of these cancels the others. The question "who is truly indigenous" frames history as a single contest with one legitimate winner, and the documents do not support that frame for either side. They also do not resolve any present-day question of sovereignty or statehood. They show the shape of the history on which those questions rest.
