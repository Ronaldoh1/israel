Who Pays to Be Heard: Lobbying, Campaign Money and the Revolving Door

The civics version of how rules are made is short. Citizens elect representatives, representatives write and pass laws, and agencies carry them out. Generations of schoolchildren learned it from classroom units and from the Schoolhouse Rock cartoons that began airing on Saturday mornings in 1973, and the naturalisation test still asks new citizens to name the country's economic system as "capitalism" or a "free market economy". [cite:ad1_loc_shr] [cite:ad1_uscis2025] [scene:ad1_as_taught|The Dream as taught] Every rule this book has examined so far, from the tax treatment of a health plan to the bankruptcy treatment of a student loan, passed through that process. This section asks who else takes part in it, with what money, and what the evidence shows about whether that money changes the result. It keeps two questions apart throughout: what is disclosed, which is a matter of record, and what the disclosed money caused, which needs its own evidence.

The disclosed total

Lobbying, in the legal sense, is paid contact with members of Congress, their staff or senior executive officials on behalf of a client, on legislation, rules or policy. Under the Lobbying Disclosure Act, lobbyists and the organisations that employ them file reports with the Clerk of the House and the Secretary of the Senate, and since the Honest Leadership and Open Government Act, signed by President George W. Bush on 14 September 2007 after votes of 96 to 2 in the Senate and 411 to 8 in the House, those reports have been due every quarter rather than twice a year. [cite:ad15_wiki_hloga] OpenSecrets, a non-profit that compiles the filings, counted $3.30 billion in reported federal lobbying in 2008, by 14,125 registered lobbyists. Spending then held between about $3.2 billion and $3.5 billion a year for a decade, dipping to $3.16 billion in 2016, before rising to $3.73 billion in 2021. [cite:ad15_247_lobby] It reached $4.27 billion in 2023 and $4.44 billion in 2024. [cite:ad15_statista_lobby] OpenSecrets put 2025 at a record of about $5 billion, up 14 per cent on 2024 and the largest one-year rise since 2008; LegiStorm, which counts the same filings differently, put it at $5.24 billion. [cite:us3_os_lobby2025] [cite:ad15_legistorm_2025] These are nominal dollars, not adjusted for inflation, so part of the rise since 2008 is price growth rather than more activity.

Which industries spend

The money is not spread evenly. In 2023 the health sector, which in OpenSecrets' classification covers drug makers, insurers, hospitals and professional bodies, reported more than $739 million, the most of any sector. The two largest single spenders were the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, a business federation, at $69 million, and the National Association of Realtors, the trade body of real-estate agents, at $52 million; a year earlier the order was reversed, with the Realtors at $82 million and the Chamber at $81 million. [cite:ad15_nbc_2023] Within health, pharmaceuticals and health products reported $226.8 million in the first half of 2025 alone, and that industry has outspent every other industry in all but one quarter since 2010. Real estate reported $34.1 million in the second quarter of 2025, and the insurers' trade body, America's Health Insurance Plans, $4.1 million. [cite:ad15_os_q2_2025] The single most lobbied bill of 2025 was the budget reconciliation law, on which 2,145 clients reported lobbying. [cite:ad15_os_q2_2025]

These are the industries whose rules decide much of what earlier parts measured: the price of drugs and insurance ([scene:ad8_insurance_machine|Part Eight]), the terms on which homes are bought and sold ([scene:ad6_price_and_rent|Part Six]) and the treatment of private student loans in bankruptcy, changed in 2005. In the years that change was under consideration, Sallie Mae, the largest student lender, spent $9 million lobbying Congress (1999–2005) and its political action committee gave more than $130,000 to members of the judiciary committees ([scene:ad7_student_debt_law|Part Seven]). [cite:ad7_npr_burd] The spending and the change are both on the record; that the one produced the other is not documented; the provision was one section of a broad bankruptcy law. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] Lobbying is not one-sided in the filings: consumer groups, unions and advocacy groups also register and report. But the sums are of a different order: two single organisations, the Chamber and the Realtors, together reported $121 million in 2023. [cite:ad15_nbc_2023]

Campaign money after 2010

Lobbying pays for access and argument; campaign spending helps decide who holds the seat in the first place. On 21 January 2010, in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission, the Supreme Court held by five votes to four that federal limits on independent political spending by corporations were unconstitutional, overruling its 1990 decision in Austin and part of its 2003 decision in McConnell. The majority held that independent expenditures "do not give rise to corruption or the appearance of corruption", while upholding the rules that require such spending to be disclosed and labelled. [cite:ad15_lii_cu] [cite:ad15_brennan_cu15] Two months later a federal appeals court applied the same reasoning to groups that only spend independently, and the result was the super PAC: a committee that may raise unlimited sums from individuals, companies or unions, provided it does not coordinate with candidates or give to them directly. [cite:pm_wiki_superpac]

The Brennan Center for Justice, a law and policy institute that supports campaign-finance limits, counted more than $1 billion from sources that did not disclose their donors flowing to committees supporting candidates of both parties in 2024. Roughly 44 per cent of the money raised to support Donald Trump, $481 million, came from ten individual donors; the ten largest donors backing Kamala Harris accounted for nearly 8 per cent, $126 million. [cite:ad15_brennan_cu15] Concentration at the top is a feature of both coalitions; what the numbers show is that a small number of donors can now supply a large share of a campaign's outside support.

Does the money change the outcome

Disclosure shows who paid; it does not show what the payment bought. The most cited attempt to measure influence is a 2014 study by the political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page. They compared the stated preferences of middle-income Americans, of affluent Americans and of organised interest groups on 1,779 policy questions asked in surveys between 1981 and 2002 with whether the policy was later adopted. Policies with low support among the affluent passed about 18 per cent of the time and those with high support about 45 per cent, and business-oriented groups had roughly twice the influence of mass-membership groups. They concluded that "mass-based interest groups and average citizens have little or no independent influence". [cite:ad15_jr_gilens]

Two critiques followed in 2015. Omar Bashir showed that the preferences of middle-income and affluent Americans were so closely correlated that the statistical model could not reliably separate their effects, and that the model explained less than a tenth of the variation in outcomes. In the study's own data, when 80 per cent or more of middle-income Americans favoured a change it happened 47 per cent of the time, against 52 per cent when the affluent were that keen; in the 185 cases where the two groups disagreed, the middle-income side got its way 47 per cent of the time. [cite:ad15_bashir] Peter Enns found that the two groups ranked policies so similarly (a correlation of 0.94 across 1,836 questions) that policy usually lands where the middle would want it even if only the affluent were heard, a result he called "coincidental representation"; he found much larger gaps between strong partisans than between income groups. [cite:ad15_enns] The honest summary is that the affluent and organised business are better placed to shape policy, and that the size of the advantage on ordinary questions is disputed.

The revolving door

Lobbying's value also comes from who the lobbyist knows. The 2007 law lengthened the period in which a former senator may not lobby Congress from one year to two, barred senior Senate staff from lobbying the whole Senate for a year, and barred senior House staff from lobbying their former office or committee for a year. [cite:ad15_wiki_hloga] A study in the American Economic Review measured what such connections are worth. Lobbyists who had worked for a senator saw the revenue they generated fall by 24 per cent when that senator left office, and the fall was larger the more senior and powerful the senator had been. The authors read this as evidence that lobbyists are paid for access to particular officials, not only for expertise. [cite:ad15_aer_revolving]

One trail: the free government tax filer

One recent case shows the full chain from disclosed lobbying to a rule change to revenue, and also why the chain is not proof of cause. Direct File was a free Internal Revenue Service program, built under the Biden administration, that let taxpayers with simple returns file online directly with the government. It accepted 140,803 returns in 2024 and 296,531 in 2025. [cite:ad15_ap_directfile] Commercial tax-preparation firms lobbied against it. Intuit, which makes TurboTax, reported about $3 million in federal lobbying in the first three quarters of 2025; H&R Block reported $2.4 million and warned in its filings that Direct File could hurt its revenue; and the American Coalition for Taxpayer Rights, which represents both, reported $270,000. Groups including Oxfam America lobbied in support. [cite:ad15_bt_directfile] The July 2025 budget law gave the IRS $15 million to study a public-private alternative; a provision to end Direct File outright was removed from the bill. On 5 November 2025 the Associated Press reported an IRS email stating that "IRS Direct File will not be available in Filing Season 2026". [cite:ad15_bt_directfile] [cite:ad15_ap_directfile] As of October 2026 no relaunch had been announced.

The stated reasons belong beside the money. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who also served as IRS commissioner, said the program "wasn't used very much" and that there were "better alternatives", and Republican lawmakers called it a waste of public money; Intuit said it "regularly engages with policymakers" on tax simplification. [cite:ad15_ap_directfile] [cite:ad15_bt_directfile] The use figures are real: 296,531 returns is a small share of all individual returns. The lobbying, the policy change and the revenue are each documented. That the lobbying caused the change is an inference, and on the record it remains contested.

Where it works as promised

Several parts of the taught version hold. The spending is visible: quarterly lobbying reports and campaign-finance filings make it possible to write every figure in this section, which is not true in many democracies. Middle-income and affluent Americans agree on most policy questions, so on most votes the outcome matches what the middle wants, whoever is heard. [cite:ad15_enns] Congress also narrows practices that large employers had relied on, with votes from both parties: in 2022 a bill sponsored by a Democratic and a Republican senator, passed by voice vote in the Senate, ended forced arbitration of sexual-harassment claims, the subject of the next section. [cite:ad15_npr_efaa] And Direct File itself was built inside the same system that later closed it, which shows that the outcome is a matter of who holds office, not a fixed result.

Interpretation, not finding: the rules as the extraction layer

The Sophia platform reads lobbying, campaign finance and the revolving door as the layer that keeps the rest of the Dream's machinery in place: industries that profit from a mechanism pay to keep it, so the taught story of representative government covers a system in which those who gain from the rules help write them. Read through this lens, the Direct File case is one visible instance of a common pattern. The documents in this section support a narrower statement: industries spend heavily, connected lobbyists' revenue tracks their former employer's time in office, and the affluent's preferences predict outcomes better. The strongest counter-readings are that the measured influence gap on ordinary questions may be small because preferences overlap; that industries often lobby against one another, so spending does not add up to one interest; that much lobbying is defensive or informational; and that both parties have enacted rules opposed by the largest spenders. No document examined here shows a common plan.
