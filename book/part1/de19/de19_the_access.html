The Access: What Was Reached, and What the Record Shows

The executive order that created DOGE on 20 January 2025 gave it a technical mission, "modernizing Federal technology and software," required a DOGE team of at least four employees in every agency, and told agency heads to "ensure USDS has full and prompt access to all unclassified agency records, software systems, and IT systems." [cite:de19_eo14158] The legal shape of that office is the subject of [scene:de17_what_doge_was_legally|Part Seventeen]; this section follows what its teams actually reached. Two months later, on 20 March 2025, a second order, "Stopping Waste, Fraud, and Abuse by Eliminating Information Silos" (Executive Order 14243), told agency heads to ensure that designated federal officials "have full and prompt access to all unclassified agency records, data, software systems, and information technology systems." [cite:de19_eo14243] That order does not mention DOGE by name. [cite:de19_eo14243] What follows separates three kinds of record: what auditors and courts found, what whistleblowers alleged under their own names, and what was reported from anonymous sources. They carry different weight, and the section says which is which at each step.

The first public conflict over access came within two weeks, and over classified material, which the order did not cover. On 1 February 2025, according to a former official who spoke to the Associated Press, DOGE members gained access to classified information at the Agency for International Development (USAID); the agency's two senior security officers, John Vorhees and Brian McGill, who had refused to hand over material in restricted areas because the team lacked the necessary clearances, were placed on leave. Musk responded on X: "USAID is a criminal organization. Time for it to die." [cite:de19_pbs_usaid] What became of USAID is the subject of [scene:de20_the_cuts|Part Twenty].

Treasury: the payment systems

The Bureau of the Fiscal Service in the Treasury runs the systems through which most federal payments flow. The Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's audit agency, reviewed the DOGE team's access there from 20 January to 11 April 2025 and published its findings on 28 April 2026. One DOGE employee had access to three payment systems from January to February 2025 and could view, copy and print their data. He was also "inadvertently granted temporary access to create, modify, and delete data" in one of them; GAO found no evidence that any data was changed. [cite:de19_gao_treasury] He sent payment information to another agency's DOGE team without encryption or approval, and the bureau's security tools did not stop it. [cite:de19_gao_treasury] A letter from Senators Elizabeth Warren and Ron Wyden on 21 July 2026, summarising the GAO report, said the payments concerned foreign aid and could have exposed the personal details of more than 350 people, and that a second DOGE employee had "over the shoulder access" to bureau data that the bureau could not later list. [cite:de19_ww_letter]

GAO found that the bureau had fully implemented only five of fourteen selected controls. It had not made sure the employee agreed to its security rules before he received a laptop, and it had not set its tools to catch unencrypted payment data leaving the bureau. GAO made six recommendations; the bureau agreed with three. [cite:de19_gao_treasury] The Treasury's own inspector general reached a milder overall verdict in report OIG-26-032, dated 8 June 2026: the payment systems' controls were "generally adequate," with weaknesses, and nine significant recommendations, including signed rules of behaviour before access and better controls on personal data sent outside the department. [cite:de19_oig_treasury] [cite:de19_ww_letter] The employee is named, with his resignation and rehiring, in [scene:de18_the_team|Part Eighteen].

The Office of Personnel Management: the personnel files

The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) holds the records of the federal workforce. On 20 October 2026 Judge Denise Cote of the federal court in Manhattan ruled in AFGE v. OPM that OPM had violated the Privacy Act, the 1974 law that limits how agencies disclose records about individuals, by giving its DOGE team access to sensitive personal information, and that the unions had won their claims under the Administrative Procedure Act. She wrote that the government "barely defend[s] its legality." [cite:de19_bgov_afge] She also recorded that DOGE had been dissolved, that one of its agents remained at OPM, and that no DOGE agent had accessed sensitive OPM data since September 2025, which left the remedy undecided. [cite:de19_bgov_afge] This is the clearest merits finding of unlawful access in the record as of October 2026.

Social Security: the master file of numbers

The Social Security Administration (SSA) keeps the Numerical Identification System, NUMIDENT, the master record of every Social Security number issued, with names, birthplaces and parents' names. Charles Borges, the agency's chief data officer, filed a whistleblower complaint in August 2025 alleging that DOGE staff had in June copied NUMIDENT, covering more than 300 million people, to a cloud environment that only DOGE could reach, "without any independent security or oversight mechanisms in place." [cite:de19_nbc_borges] His complaint quoted a memo of 15 July 2025 in which the agency's chief information officer, Aram Moghaddassi, wrote that "the business need is higher than the security risk," and an internal assessment that warned of a "catastrophic impact" if the copy were breached. The agency answered that the data was "walled off from the internet" and that it was "not aware of any compromise." [cite:de19_nbc_borges]

The government's own filing then moved part of the story from allegation to admission. In a Notice of Corrections filed on 16 January 2026 in the union suit over SSA data, the Justice Department disclosed that DOGE team members had used Cloudflare, a commercial service, to share agency data; that "Cloudflare is not approved for storing SSA data"; and that the agency "has not been able to determine exactly what data were shared." [cite:de19_abc_hatch] [cite:de19_ch_afscme] It disclosed that an unnamed political advocacy group had contacted two DOGE team members in March 2025, seeking "to find evidence of voter fraud and to overturn election results in certain States," and that one of them signed an agreement that may have involved using Social Security data to match state voter rolls. The filing does not say any data was shared. The agency referred the two employees to the Office of Special Counsel for possible violations of the Hatch Act, which limits political activity by federal staff, and the filing corrected earlier sworn statements the government had made in court. [cite:de19_abc_hatch]

A further allegation remains open. On 6 March 2026 the agency's inspector general told Congress it was reviewing an anonymous complaint about "potential misuse of SSA data by a former DOGE employee"; press reports described alleged copies of NUMIDENT and the Death Master File and a thumb drive. The agency said the claims had been "strongly refuted by all named parties," and no findings have been issued. [cite:de19_npr_ssaoig]

The Internal Revenue Service, and the labour board

At the Internal Revenue Service, Wired reported in April 2025 that two DOGE staffers, Gavin Kliger and Sam Corcos, were organising a hackathon to build "one new API to rule them all," a single interface over taxpayer names, addresses, Social Security numbers and returns, with Palantir "consistently" named as a candidate vendor. [cite:de19_tc_irsapi] No final contract for that system has been documented. The separate agreement under which the IRS gave Immigration and Customs Enforcement the addresses of about 47,000 people was signed by the Treasury and Homeland Security, not by DOGE, and a federal judge later found about 42,695 of the disclosures unlawful; [scene:ad13_immigrants_latinos|The American Dream] sets out that record. [cite:us10_abc_irs_feb] [cite:us10_cpa_irs_sep]

At the National Labor Relations Board, Daniel Berulis, a security architect, wrote to the Senate Intelligence Committee on 14 April 2025 that DOGE staff had been given powerful administrator accounts exempt from logging from 3 March, that about 10 gigabytes left the case-management system early on 4 March, and that more than twenty login attempts from a Russian address using valid DOGE credentials were blocked. [cite:de19_krebs_nlrb] The board said DOGE "neither requested nor received access" and that "no breach of agency systems occurred." [cite:de19_krebs_nlrb] GAO later found no evidence that two DOGE detailees used the board's systems between 16 April and 25 July 2025, but it deliberately did not examine March, which is with the board's inspector general, whose investigation remains open. [cite:de19_gao_nlrb] The March allegation is therefore neither confirmed nor refuted.

Six more agencies, and the AI tools

On 29 September 2026 GAO reported on six more agencies. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, Education, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the Securities and Exchange Commission said their DOGE teams had access to more than 23 systems, but the extent "could not be determined." The Small Business Administration and Veterans Affairs did not list the systems at all. Only the SEC documented background investigations; none of the agencies let GAO observe their activity logs; and NOAA and the SEC questioned GAO's authority. GAO concluded that "Congress and the public lack assurance" that the systems and data were protected. The consumer bureau disputed the report. [cite:de19_gao_sixagency]

Reuters reported on 23 May 2025, from three unnamed sources, that DOGE was expanding the use of a customised version of Grok, the chatbot of Musk's company xAI, to analyse federal data, and two of them said DOGE staff had told Homeland Security officials to use it though it was not approved there. Reuters could not establish what data went in. The department denied that DOGE had pushed any tool. [cite:de19_reuters_grok] An ethics lawyer, Richard Painter, said that if Musk was directly involved in the decision it could breach the criminal conflict-of-interest law. [cite:de19_reuters_grok] That is a conditional; no finding of Musk's involvement exists. The documented use of an AI tool by DOGE staff is another one, at the National Endowment for the Humanities, where sworn testimony shows ChatGPT used to sort grants for cancellation; [scene:de20_the_cuts|Part Twenty] gives it.

The "master database"

A widely repeated claim holds that DOGE built, or had Palantir build, a single database joining tax, Social Security and immigration records. Reporting in 2025 described plans and Palantir's expanding federal work; Palantir answered that it "is not a vendor on any master database project to unify databases across federal agencies" and "has no contracts with DOGE." [cite:de19_palantir_record] What is documented is narrower and, in its way, more concrete: specific transfers between specific agencies under specific agreements, from tax addresses to ICE to Social Security data run through the citizenship-check system described in [scene:de19_the_courts|the next section]. Whether these add up to one integrated system is not established; the company's contracts are mapped in [scene:de28_ice_contracts|Part Thirty].
