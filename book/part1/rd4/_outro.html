By the end of 1945 the center of gravity of the Zionist political effort had shifted toward the United States. The Biltmore Program, ratified by the Zionist General Council in October 1942, had become the binding platform of the organized movement [cite:biltmore_enc]. The American Zionist Emergency Council, founded in August 1943, gave that platform a national machine of local committees, sample letters, telegram campaigns and direct pressure on named members of Congress [cite:azec_powerbase] [cite:aja_azec_local_1944]. In 1944 Palestine entered the platforms of both major parties, for what the council's own appraisal called the first time in American history [cite:aja_azec_appraisal_1944], and in December 1945 Congress adopted a resolution of support, though in language calling for equal rights regardless of race or creed [cite:frus1945_concres]. Alongside the mainstream ran the Bergson group's campaigns of advertisements and pageants, and the militant fringe whose conduct the movement's leadership denounced.

All of this unfolded against the destruction of European Jewry, coordinated at Wannsee and documented to a scale of six million. And it unfolded under a president whose failing health was concealed from the voters, who had promised Ibn Saud in writing that nothing would be decided on Palestine without consulting Arabs and Jews, and who died on 12 April 1945 [cite:trumanlibrary_recognition] [cite:arabnews_fdr_ibnsaud]. His successor took office with that written promise on the record and a well-organized American campaign for a Jewish commonwealth under way.
