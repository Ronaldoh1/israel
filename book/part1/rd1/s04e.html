A Bank in Jaffa, an Offer in East Africa

For eighteen months, from early 1902 to the summer of 1903, the Zionist movement ran on two tracks at once. It built banking infrastructure inside Palestine, and it seriously considered building its future somewhere else entirely. Later memory tends to hold that Palestine was always the only place Zionists would seriously consider for a state. The record of these months is more complicated.

The first track was financial. On February 27, 1902, the Jewish Colonial Trust, the movement's London-registered bank, founded the Anglo-Palestine Company as its arm inside Palestine, with initial capital of about £50,000. Its chairman was David Wolffsohn, Herzl's close associate and later his successor. Its manager was Zalman David Levontin, one of the founders of Rishon LeZion in 1882. The company opened its first branch in Jaffa in 1903. By 1914 it had branches in Jerusalem, Beirut, Hebron, Haifa, Safed, Tiberias and Gaza.[cite:apc_wiki] [cite:jewishencyclopedia_colonial_trust] It financed land purchase, agriculture and credit cooperatives. Its later names trace an unbroken line: it became the Anglo-Palestine Bank around 1930 and Bank Leumi in 1950–51. The modest subsidiary of 1902 was not yet the bank it would become, but the line from one to the other is direct.

A single loan shows what settlement financing meant in practice. The Anglo-Palestine Company lent money to the Ahuzat Bayit association, which used it to build, in 1909, the first neighborhood of what became Tel Aviv.[cite:apc_wiki] Six years after the Jaffa branch opened, a bank, an association and a loan had produced the beginnings of a city. Tel Aviv's founding is a larger story than this one link, but the link itself is documented.

The second track was territorial, and it began in bloodshed. In April 1903 came the Kishinev pogrom. In its aftermath the British Colonial Secretary, Joseph Chamberlain, offered Herzl the Guas Ngishu plateau in British East Africa for Jewish settlement.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] [cite:wiki_kishinev_pogrom] The offer is remembered as "the Uganda Scheme," but the name is wrong: the territory lay in present-day Kenya. Its timing was no accident. It followed hard on an internationally reported massacre. The documents establish the offer and what triggered it; they do not, by themselves, reveal any longer-term calculations Britain may have had.

The offer went to the Sixth Zionist Congress in Basel in August 1903. The delegates voted 295 to 178 to send a commission to investigate East Africa.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] What they approved was limited: an examination of the territory, not its adoption. Herzl framed the proposal explicitly as a Nachtasyl, a "night shelter," a temporary refuge from immediate danger rather than a substitute for the Land of Israel. Even so, the margin was no landslide, and the question came close to breaking the organization apart.

The clearest sign of the danger came from the Russian Zionist delegation, which walked out of the Congress in protest at the vote.[cite:uganda_scheme_wiki] A walkout is a stronger objection than a dissenting vote. It would be wrong to read it as the view of every Russian Zionist, or to assume the rift was never healed; the movement went on functioning as a single body. But the walkout shows that East Africa was a genuine internal crisis, not a footnote.

Six years after Basel, then, the movement's own Congress came within a contested vote of formally exploring a territory other than Palestine. It did so not as a stunt but in response to escalating pogrom violence. This was the closest the movement came, after 1897, to turning away from Palestine, and it happened in the very months its Palestine bank was opening its doors in Jaffa. How the movement resolved that contradiction, and at what cost to its unity, is the subject of [scene:s04f|the rejection of the East Africa offer].
