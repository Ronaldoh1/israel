Public Money: State and City Treasuries in Israel Bonds

The money in most of this volume flows from private donors into American politics. One flow runs the other way, from American public treasuries to the government of Israel, and it is decided not by Congress but by state treasurers, county officials and pension boards. It takes the form of Israeli government bonds, sold in the United States by a private corporation acting for Israel's Ministry of Finance. This section sets out how the sales work, how much state and local governments bought after 7 October 2023, who bought most, and the disputes the purchases have raised. It relies on an investigation by the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists published in July 2024.

The seller. Israel Bonds, formally the Development Corporation for Israel, was launched in New York in 1951. It is registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission as the underwriter of Israeli government bonds sold in the United States and acts as a private seller for Israel's Finance Ministry. In more than seven decades it has raised $52 billion. It began by selling to individual buyers in the diaspora, and over time expanded to banks, institutional investors and state and municipal governments; its sales have historically risen in times of crisis. [cite:pm_icij_bonds] The ICIJ found that it courted public officials with exclusive trips to Israel that included meetings with senior officials and military tours, with galas and leadership awards, and with conferences at which it paid speakers' expenses. [cite:pm_icij_bonds]

The surge after 7 October. In the nine months after the attacks, American states and municipalities bought more than $1.7 billion of Israeli bonds. [cite:pm_icij_bonds] The purchases were fast and public. Pennsylvania's treasurer agreed to a purchase within an hour of being told of the opportunity on 10 October 2023, and later doubled it. Ohio's treasurer announced $20 million more on 11 October; he had bought $357.5 million since 2019. Palm Beach County, Florida, announced an additional $160 million in October, raised the share of its portfolio it could hold in Israeli bonds from 10 to 15 percent in March 2024, and by April held about $700 million of a $4.67 billion portfolio, calling itself the world's largest investor in Israel Bonds. The State of Florida held more than $250 million. [cite:pm_icij_bonds]

The disputes. The purchases raised three kinds of objection. The first is financial: credit agencies downgraded Israeli government debt during the war, and a former chief investment officer of Pennsylvania had warned in 2021 about Israel's political instability and the bonds' lack of an open market, advice that was not followed. Israel Bonds answers that Israel has never missed a payment of interest or principal in its history. [cite:pm_icij_bonds] The second is ethical: the ICIJ documented trips and hospitality offered to the officials who decide the purchases, and a law professor, Richard Painter, said the mixing of personal and official business "seem[s] to go well beyond" what is acceptable; Israel Bonds says its marketing is "legitimate, appropriate, and common practice." [cite:pm_icij_bonds] The third is legal and political: in May 2024 three Palm Beach County residents sued a county official, alleging that the investments were made for "social, ideological, and political reasons" in breach of fiduciary duty, and the advocacy group DAWN asked the Justice Department whether Israel Bonds should be registered as a foreign agent, which Israel Bonds denies. [cite:pm_icij_bonds]

The bonds belong in a volume about pro-Israel money for a reason that is easy to miss. They are not political contributions, and buying them is legal. But apart from the military aid appropriated by Congress, which other volumes of this library describe, they are the main channel through which American public money goes to the government of Israel, and it is decided by state and local officials, the same officials whom Israel Bonds courts with trips and awards. The record does not show that any purchase was made because of a trip or an award. It shows that the seller offered both, and that the purchases rose sharply when Israel was at war.
