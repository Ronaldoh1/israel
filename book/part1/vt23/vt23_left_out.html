What Was Left Out, by Whom, and in What Document

Ron's thesis for this book holds that the institution "suppressed most of the books". That sentence contains two claims that can be checked separately. The first is about quantity: were most of the books in circulation left out? The second is about action: what did the people who left them out actually do? This section answers both from the documents, book by book, and counts where the documents allow counting. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt23_tert_decretum]

The excluders, document by document

Irenaeus of Lyon, around 180, rejected the Valentinian "Gospel of Truth", accused the followers of Marcus of using "an unspeakable number of apocryphal and spurious writings, which they themselves have forged", and wrote that a group he called Cainites "produce a fictitious history of this kind, which they style the Gospel of Judas". [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_1_20] [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_1_31] That last line is the earliest known mention of the Gospel of Judas, whose Coptic text, found in the 1970s, was published on 6 April 2006. [cite:vt23_wiki_judas] [scene:vt26_gospel_of_judas|The Gospel of Judas] Irenaeus wrote as a bishop in Gaul with no power beyond persuasion; his books were polemic, and he is a hostile witness to what his opponents read. [scene:vt24_the_heresy_hunters|The heresy hunters]

The Muratorian fragment rejected letters to the Laodiceans and Alexandrians as forgeries for Marcion's cause and kept the Shepherd of Hermas out of public reading. [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger] Origen set gospels of the Egyptians, the Twelve, Basilides, Thomas and Matthias on the side of "heresy". [cite:vt23_bt_origen_luke] Serapion of Antioch withdrew his permission for the Gospel of Peter. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he6] Eusebius put the Acts of Paul, the Shepherd, the Apocalypse of Peter, Barnabas, the Teachings of the Apostles and the Gospel of the Hebrews among the spurious, and the Gospels of Peter, Thomas and Matthias with the Acts of Andrew and John among the books of heretics. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] Athanasius named no apocryphal titles in the surviving Greek of his letter but kept the Teaching of the Apostles and the Shepherd for new converts only. [cite:vt23_na_festal39] Jerome set Wisdom, Sirach, Judith and Tobit "among the apocrypha", though the African councils kept them. [cite:vt23_wiki_galeatus] [cite:vt23_na_carthage]

The longest list of rejections is the fifth chapter of the Gelasian Decree, headed in the manuscripts as a decree of Pope Damasus or Pope Gelasius but judged by its modern editor, Ernst von Dobschütz, to be a pseudonymous private compilation of 519 to 553, probably by a writer from southern Gaul. [cite:vt23_wiki_gelasian] It says the Roman church "does not in any way receive" the writings that follow and that they "are to be avoided by catholics". The entries include gospels "in the name of" Matthias, Barnabas, James the younger, Peter and Bartholomew, "the Gospel in the name of Thomas which the Manichaeans use", Acts of Andrew, Thomas, Peter and Philip, revelations in the names of Paul, Thomas and Stephen, the Shepherd, the Assumption of Mary and the Testament of Job. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] One entry, "Leptogeneseos", is Greek for "Little Genesis", a name used for the Book of Jubilees, which the Ethiopian church keeps in its Bible to this day. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt23_wiki_jubilees] The entry on Thomas does not say which Thomas text it means.

The decree also shows that its list was not only about scripture. Among the 62 entries "not received" are the works of Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Lactantius, Cassian and Eusebius's own Church History, alongside amulets and a poem stitched together from Virgil's verses. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] It was a reading guide for Catholics, as much a list of approved and disapproved authors as a list of rejected gospels. It closes by declaring the authors of heresies and their followers under anathema "forever". [cite:vt23_tert_decretum]

Trent, a council called by the papacy, listed what was in rather than what was out. Books it did not list, notably the Prayer of Manasseh and the books known in Latin as 3 and 4 Esdras, were printed in a separate apocrypha section at the back of the official Sixto-Clementine Vulgate. [cite:vt23_wiki_deutero] [cite:vt14_trent_s4]

The texts that resurfaced at Nag Hammadi and in Codex Tchacos are laid out in Parts Twenty-Five and Twenty-Six. [scene:vt25_the_whole_library|The whole Nag Hammadi library] [scene:vt26_gospel_of_judas|The Gospel of Judas]

Counting: what was in circulation and what was kept

No ancient catalogue lists every Christian book in circulation, so any count has to say what it is counting. Three counts are possible from the documents above. The first uses Eusebius's own catalogue of around 325. Counting Paul's letters as the fourteen he recognised, it names 38 titles. Twenty-seven of them, the recognised books, Revelation and all five disputed letters, are in today's 27-book New Testament; eleven are not. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] By this measure, most of the books a fourth-century bishop treated as candidates were kept.

The second count looks only at gospels. Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and the Gelasian Decree between them name at least seventeen gospels outside the four, from the Gospel of Truth and the Gospel of Judas to gospels under the names of Thomas, Peter, Matthias, Barnabas, Bartholomew and Andrew and two infancy books. [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_3_11] [cite:vt23_na_irenaeus_1_31] [cite:vt23_bt_origen_luke] [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] Four of at least twenty-one gospels named in these sources were kept, fewer than one in five. Of the revelations named in the same sources, those of John, Peter, Paul, Thomas and Stephen, one was kept. Measured by gospels and revelations, most were left out.

The third count is of the Nag Hammadi library: 52 treatises in 13 codices, with several duplicates, which leaves about 46 distinct titles by the library's own table. [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi] Only a handful of those titles appear in any of the exclusion lists above. Most were never named and rejected by any surviving document; they belong to the much larger body of early Christian writing that was never a candidate on any bishop's list, as were the letters of bishops and martyr stories that Carthage itself allowed to be read on feast days. [cite:vt23_na_carthage]

What "left out" meant

The documents use three kinds of sanction. Most exclude a book from reading in church: that is what the Carthage canon governs, and what the Muratorian list means for the Shepherd. [cite:vt23_na_carthage] [cite:vt23_ecw_murat_metzger] Some warn readers away, as Athanasius did. [cite:vt23_na_festal39] Two pronounce anathema on people: the Gelasian Decree on authors and followers of heresies, Trent on anyone who rejects the books it listed. [cite:vt23_tert_decretum] [cite:vt14_trent_s4] None of the documents examined here orders any of the left-out books to be burned. The book-burning order of this period that is documented concerns Arius's own writings: Constantine wrote that any treatise of Arius found "let it be consigned to the flames", with death for anyone who hid one. [cite:vt23_na_socrates1] [scene:vt22_myths_tested|Nicaea's myths tested] Later imperial laws against heretical groups and their meetings are part of the next part's story. [scene:vt24_the_heresy_hunters|The heresy hunters]

The excluded books also kept being copied. Codex Sinaiticus, made in the mid-fourth century, after Eusebius had called both books spurious, includes the Letter of Barnabas and part of the Shepherd after the New Testament. [cite:vt23_wiki_sinaiticus] The Gelasian list itself was still being copied in the eighth century, a sign that the books it warned against were still around to be warned against. [cite:vt23_wiki_gelasian]

How much of the thesis holds

On quantity, the thesis holds for gospels and revelations: by the documents' own lists, most were left out. It does not hold for the New Testament as a whole measured against the books bishops actually debated, where 27 of the 38 titles in Eusebius's catalogue were kept. [cite:vt23_na_eus_he3] On action, "suppressed" fits in the sense the documents support: named bishops excluded books from public worship, called them forgeries or heretical, and in the Gelasian Decree and at Trent attached anathemas. No document examined here orders the destruction of a gospel. On the actor, "the institution" needs to be named precisely: until Trent the people doing the excluding were bishops and scholars in Lyon, Alexandria, Antioch, Caesarea, Bethlehem and Carthage and an anonymous sixth-century compiler, with Rome asked to confirm Africa's list rather than issuing its own. [cite:vt23_na_carthage] [cite:vt23_wiki_gelasian] The four Bibles in use today, which the next section compares, still disagree about where the line falls. [scene:vt23_four_bibles|Four Bibles]
