The New Rules, 2023–2026

Until recently, the pressure documented in this part worked mainly through reviews, comments and hearings. Since 2023 it has also been written into law. A growing number of American states now tell their schools how to define, report and investigate hatred of Jews, and several have tied that duty to a definition whose examples include certain criticism of Israel. That change moves part of the argument over this history from the publisher's office to the statute book.

The definition at the centre

The definition most often adopted is the "working definition" published by the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance, an intergovernmental body. Its short text defines hatred of Jews in general terms; it is followed by eleven examples, several of which concern Israel, such as applying "double standards" to Israel or comparing its policies to those of the Nazis. Supporters say the examples describe how hatred of Jews is often expressed today. Civil-liberties groups say the examples turn political criticism of a state into a form of bigotry. [cite:pen_ap_states2025]

What the states have done

Virginia wrote the definition into state law in 2023. The bill, HB 1606, adopted it with its examples about Israel as a tool for recognizing discrimination and hate crimes. A coalition including Palestine Legal opposed it, warning that advocates for Palestinian rights would be "more likely to be investigated and potentially charged with hate crimes." [cite:pen_pl_va2023] On 5 January 2026, days before leaving office, Attorney General Jason Miyares wrote to every school superintendent and school board in the state, telling them to adopt the definition in their codes of conduct and discrimination policies and saying state and federal law required it. Because the definition is in statute, his successors could not drop it without the General Assembly. [cite:pen_ji_miyares2026]

Others followed in 2025. Texas, Arkansas, Oklahoma and Tennessee passed measures making schools more accountable for complaints of hatred against Jewish pupils; several call for using the same definition. Oklahoma's sponsor, state Senator Kristen Thompson, said her state "will not tolerate hatred disguised as political discourse." In Arizona, the governor vetoed a similar bill as an attack on teachers. [cite:pen_ap_states2025]

California went furthest on instruction itself. Assembly Bill 715, signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on 8 October 2025 and in force since 1 January 2026, created a state Office of Civil Rights for schools and a governor-appointed Antisemitism Prevention Coordinator to develop resources and oversee training. It also tightened the rules against discriminatory instructional materials and let the state investigate districts and order corrective action. [cite:pen_elestoque_ab715] In August 2026 the governor named the office's first director; the coordinator's post had not yet been filled. [cite:pen_edsource_ocr2026] Critics in the state's districts said it was still unclear how the office would tell bigotry from political criticism of Israel. [cite:pen_elestoque_ab715]

What changes, and what does not

The new laws address something real: protecting Jewish children from harassment and bigotry at school is a duty no one in this argument disputes, and their sponsors cite reports of such harassment as the reason for them. The open question is narrower: whether a teacher who describes the West Bank as occupied, or uses the word Nakba, or assigns a Palestinian historian, could now face a complaint under a definition that lists criticism of Israel among its examples. The laws do not say so in terms, and this book found no reported court ruling that tests them against classroom teaching.

One way to read the change is as a shift in where the risk falls. In the system described earlier in this part, a publisher's safest course was to avoid offending any organized group. A teacher now works under the same logic, with a state office and a legal definition behind one side of the argument. The Virginia record showed advocates asking publishers to replace "occupation" with "control of." Under the new rules, the word a teacher chooses can itself become a matter for an official complaint.

The same structural point applies whichever way the law points. A state that wrote one side's vocabulary into its rules for schools would be holding the pen exactly as Israel's ministry does, only with less public argument. Whether these laws protect pupils without narrowing what can be taught is a question the record cannot yet answer.
