Iran's Programme: From Atoms for Peace to 60 Percent

Iran's nuclear programme began as an American project. On 8 December 1953 President Eisenhower offered nuclear technology for civilian use to the world in his "Atoms for Peace" speech at the United Nations, and in 1957 the United States and the Shah's government signed a civil nuclear cooperation agreement under that programme. In 1967 the United States supplied a five-megawatt research reactor for Tehran, together with highly enriched uranium to fuel it. [cite:ir4_brook_afp] The Tehran Research Reactor went critical in November 1967; it ran at first on uranium enriched to about 93 percent, the level used in weapons, and was converted to 20 percent fuel in 1993. [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] Iran signed the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) in July 1968, its parliament ratified it in February 1970, and the treaty entered into force for Iran on 5 March 1970. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] As a party without nuclear weapons, Iran gave up the right to build them and accepted IAEA inspection of its nuclear material, in exchange for the treaty's promise of access to civilian technology.

The Shah's plans

In 1974 the Shah founded the Atomic Energy Organization of Iran (AEOI) and announced plans for about 23,000 megawatts of nuclear power, with some 23 plants and "the development of a full nuclear fuel cycle." [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] In 1976 he raised the AEOI's budget from $31 million to $1 billion. [cite:ir4_brook_afp] In 1974 Iran had lent $1 billion to France's Eurodif enrichment consortium, and the West German firm Kraftwerk Union had begun building a power plant at Bushehr. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] A National Security Decision Memorandum signed by Henry Kissinger on 22 April 1975 set out conditions under which the United States would approve the reprocessing of American-supplied fuel in Iran, preferably in a multinational plant. In July 1975 the US embassy in Tehran judged the Shah's motives "Not Entirely Clear" and suggested his interest in plutonium might be a way of keeping a weapons option open. A cooperation agreement was initialed in 1978 but never signed; the revolution "effectively nullified" it. [cite:ir4_nsa_ebb268]

Halt, revival and the Khan network

The revolution of 1979 froze the programme, and the Bushehr contract was terminated. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] It did not stay frozen. In 1987 Iran acquired technical drawings for the P-1 gas centrifuge, the machine used to enrich uranium, from the network run by the Pakistani metallurgist Abdul Qadeer Khan. [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] In 1990 Iran opened talks with the Soviet Union on finishing Bushehr, and in January 1995 it signed an $800 million contract with Russia's atomic energy ministry to complete the plant under IAEA safeguards. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] Iran's centrifuge work, by contrast, was not declared to the IAEA. How the US and Israel later used a copy of the P-1 to test the Stuxnet computer worm is told in Part Eight. [scene:ir8_shadow|The shadow war]

Disclosure, 2002–2006

On 14 August 2002, at a press conference in Washington, Alireza Jafarzadeh of the National Council of Resistance of Iran, the political arm of the exiled opposition group the People's Mojahedin (MEK), described two undeclared sites: a facility near Natanz, built under cover of a desert-eradication project through a front company, and a heavy-water plant at Arak run through another. [cite:ir4_iw_ncri] [cite:ir4_carn_noncomp] The NCRI is an opposition group with its own aims, and its claims were at the time allegations; the two sites it named are the enrichment plant and the heavy-water reactor that the 2015 nuclear deal later regulated by name. [cite:ir4_aca_jcpoa]

In November 2003 the IAEA reported many breaches of Iran's safeguards agreement and a "policy of concealment," while finding "no evidence" that Iran was trying to build a bomb. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] In October 2003 Iran had agreed with the British, French and German foreign ministers to suspend enrichment. [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] On 24 September 2005 the IAEA Board found Iran in non-compliance with its safeguards agreement by 22 votes to one, with 12 abstentions; on 4 February 2006 it referred Iran to the UN Security Council, which passed binding resolutions demanding suspension and imposing sanctions between 2006 and 2010, from Resolution 1696 to Resolution 1929. [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] Iran maintained throughout that its programme was peaceful and that enrichment was its right under the NPT.

What the inspectors and the analysts found about a weapon

In December 2007 the US National Intelligence Estimate judged with "high confidence" that Iran had halted its covert nuclear weapons programme in the autumn of 2003, and with "moderate confidence" that it had not restarted it as of mid-2007; the White House said the risk of Iran acquiring a weapon remained "a very serious problem." [cite:hz11_wh_nie2007] [cite:hz11_npr_nie2007] On 8 November 2011 the IAEA published an annex to its report on the "possible military dimensions" of Iran's programme. It described information it judged "consistent in terms of technical content, individuals and organizations involved, and time frames": a structured programme, the AMAD plan under Mohsen Fakhrizadeh, mostly halted in late 2003; a containment chamber for high-explosive tests at the Parchin military complex; work on a neutron initiator; and studies of fitting a nuclear-sized payload to the Shahab-3 missile. For the first time the Director General said Iran "might be continuing to pursue some of the activities." Iran rejected the documents as "forgeries and fabrications." [cite:ir4_sipri_annex2011] [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline]

The agency's final assessment, presented to its Board on 15 December 2015, closed the file. It found that a range of activities relevant to developing a nuclear explosive device had been conducted before the end of 2003 as a coordinated effort, that some continued afterwards but "did not advance beyond feasibility and scientific studies," and that there were "no credible indications" of such activities after 2009 and none of nuclear material being diverted. [cite:ir4_wnn_pmd2015] [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] Both readings that later circulated are therefore incomplete: the IAEA did not find that Iran had never had weapons work, and it did not find that weapons work continued after 2009.

From 3.67 to 60 percent

Iran first enriched to 20 percent at Fordow in January 2012; the interim deal of November 2013 rolled that stockpile back. [cite:ir4_wiki_timeline] [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] The 2015 deal, the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), capped enrichment at 3.67 percent and the stockpile at 300 kg for 15 years, and limited Iran to 5,060 operating first-generation centrifuges for ten years. [cite:ir4_aca_jcpoa] Its making and its killing are told in Part Seven. [scene:ir7_deal|The deal] After the United States left the deal in May 2018, Iran exceeded the 300 kg limit on 1 July 2019 and enriched to about 4.5 percent a week later. [cite:ir4_aca_timeline] Having resumed 20 percent enrichment, it began enriching to 60 percent at Natanz on 16 April 2021, days after sabotage at the site that Iran presented as the reason. [cite:ir4_ap_60pct]

By 17 May 2025 the IAEA counted 408.6 kg of uranium enriched to 60 percent, up 133.8 kg in three months, and 9,247.6 kg of enriched uranium in all. About 42 kg of 60 percent material is, in theory, enough for one weapon if enriched further to 90 percent. The agency called Iran "the only non-nuclear-weapon state to produce such material," a matter of "serious concern." Iran's foreign ministry and AEOI called the report reliant on "unreliable" sources; Netanyahu's office said it showed Iran was "totally determined to complete its nuclear weapons program." [cite:ir4_ap_may2025] On 12 June 2025 the IAEA Board found Iran in non-compliance with its safeguards obligations by 19 votes to three, with 11 abstentions, citing "many failures" since 2019 and unexplained uranium traces at undeclared sites; it was the first such finding in almost 20 years. Iran called the resolution without "technical and legal basis" and announced a new enrichment site. [cite:ir4_aje_bog2025] Israel struck Iran the next morning. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] [scene:ir10_twelve|The Twelve Days] In a CNN interview during that war, the IAEA's Director General, Rafael Grossi, said the agency "did not have any proof of a systematic effort to move into a nuclear weapon." [cite:ir4_trib_grossi] The US intelligence community had told Congress in March 2025 that Iran was "not building a nuclear weapon," while recording that its stockpile was at its highest level. [cite:hz11_bg_gabbard]

The fatwa and the treaty

Iran's government has cited a religious ruling by Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei as proof of peaceful intent. The ruling is reported to have been issued orally in October 2003; Iran cited it in an official statement at the IAEA in August 2005, saying that "the production, stockpiling and use of nuclear weapons" were forbidden under Islam, and in 2010 Khamenei called their use "haraam." [cite:ir4_wiki_fatwa] Its status is contested. No written text has been published; the research group MEMRI concluded in 2012 that "no such fatwa ever existed," a finding the historian Juan Cole disputed. [cite:ir4_hudson_fatwa] Senior Iranian figures have also signalled that the position could change: in 2021 an intelligence minister compared Iran to a "cornered cat," and in 2025 the adviser Ali Larijani said Iran would have "no choice" but to build a weapon if attacked. [cite:ir4_wiki_fatwa] After the war of 2026 began, a bill to leave the NPT was tabled in Iran's parliament on 28 March 2026; the foreign ministry said Iran "has never sought, and does not seek, nuclear weapons." [cite:ir4_aca_npt2026] As of late September 2026, with the Kremlin publicly urging Iran to stay, no formal notice of withdrawal had been reported, and Iran remained a party to the treaty. [cite:ir4_k24_npt]
