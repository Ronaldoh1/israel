Counting the Country: People and Land Before Partition

In 1922 Britain, now governing Palestine under the Mandate, counted the country's people. That census, and the counts and surveys that followed, are the record against which every later argument about 1947 has to be checked. Two slogans have often stood in their place. One describes "a land without a people." The other holds that Palestinian Arabs owned 94 percent of the land. The Mandate's own statistics refute the first outright and complicate the second.

The 1922 census counted 757,182 people. Of these, 83,794 were Jews, about 11 percent [cite:jvl_census_1931]. By the census of 1931, Jews made up 17 percent of the population, Muslims 73 percent and Christians 9 percent [cite:jvl_census_1931]. The Jewish population had doubled in the intervening years. Yet the non-Jewish population had grown by more than the entire Jewish population. Of the Jews counted in 1931, 42 percent had been born in Palestine and 58 percent abroad. British records count 376,415 Jewish immigrants between 1920 and 1946 [cite:ssrn_mandate_immigration].

By the end of the Mandate the numbers were larger, but the proportions had not reversed. UNSCOP, the committee that would later draw on the 1945 land survey described below, estimated the population at the end of 1946 at about 1,846,000. Some 1,203,000 were Arabs, or 65 percent, and 608,000 were Jews, or 33 percent [cite:rebelion_unscop_figures]. On the eve of partition the country was full of people, and about two-thirds of them were Arab.

Land is the harder subject, and the one most often misused. The key source is the 1945 Village Statistics survey, compiled for the Anglo-American Committee and later used by UNSCOP. It classified land three ways: by ownership, by use and by tax category [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. Measured against it, the partition plan of 1947 stands out. The plan allotted about 56 percent of the territory to the Jewish state at a time when Jews owned under 7 percent of the land [cite:historyskills_partition]. The proposed Jewish state would also have contained a large Arab minority.

That disparity is real. But the second slogan erases a distinction the survey itself makes. The claim that Palestinian Arabs privately owned 94 percent of the land counts large areas of state and public land as Arab-owned. The 1945 survey separates private ownership from state land, and an honest comparison has to keep its categories apart rather than collapse them [cite:wiki_village_statistics]. Jews owned a small share of the land, but much of the rest was held by the state, not privately.

Neither correction cancels the other. "A land without a people" is refuted by every census. The 94 percent figure overstates private Arab ownership by folding in state land. Set both slogans aside and what remains is a country that was about two-thirds Arab in 1946, in which Jews owned under 7 percent of the land and were offered about 56 percent of the territory. Every serious argument about 1947 starts from these figures.
