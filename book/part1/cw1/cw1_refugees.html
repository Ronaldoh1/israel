The Refugee Question After Lausanne: UNRWA, Paris and the Reintegration Fund, 1950–1953

Neither the Canal dispute nor the plans for a defence command touched the largest human question the war of 1948 had left behind. The Conciliation Commission's conference at Lausanne in 1949 had ended without a settlement of the refugee question; the positions that failed to meet there are told in [scene:lau_s01|Lausanne, 1949]. In the four years that followed, the question moved along two tracks at once. One was diplomatic: the Commission tried again, in Paris in the autumn of 1951, with a package of five specific proposals. The other was administrative: a new UN agency, UNRWA, took over relief for the refugees, and the General Assembly gave it money to do more than provide relief. The two tracks were meant to converge in a settlement. By 1953 they had not, and a new generation of Palestinian students had begun to organize politically.

UNRWA's first report covered the period from 1 May 1950 to 30 June 1951. It gave the agency's headquarters as Beirut and divided its work into five districts: Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Gaza and Israel. It employed 133 international staff and 5,840 local staff. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Israel's appearance on the list is easily overlooked; in its first year the agency counted Israel among the districts where it worked, though the report as excerpted does not give the numbers it served there. The agency's first director, Howard Kennedy, was succeeded by John B. Blandford Jr., who arrived on 4 July 1951. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951]

The money that paid for this came overwhelmingly from one government. The report lists cash contributions for the period: the United States $27,450,000, Britain $6,200,000, France $2,285,714, Canada $894,313 and Israel $50,000. It lists separately the direct contributions made in the region by the host and neighbouring states: Egypt $1,961,300, Iraq $980,000, Lebanon $457,800 and Jordan $323,900. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] The two lists measure different things. The first is money paid to the agency; the second is support given directly in the region, by states several of which were also hosting refugees. Read together, they show who paid for the refugee system in its first year: the United States far more than any other government, Britain second, and Israel a small cash contribution. How the American share was voted in Congress, alongside the first US grant aid to Israel, is told in [scene:cw1_grant_aid|the first US grant aid].

From the start the money was meant to do two things. The General Assembly's Resolution 393 (V) had recommended $20 million for relief and $30 million for a reintegration fund for the year to 30 June 1952. [cite:rs_cw1_un_yearbook_1951] Reintegration, as the Assembly later put it, could come "either by repatriation or resettlement," and the choice between the two was the political question the agency could not answer for itself. [cite:rs_cw1_ga_res513]

In Washington the case for paying was made in the language of stability. On 27 July 1951 President Truman wrote to the congressional committee chairmen that the refugees' condition was "one of the principal causes of tension in the Near East." He added that the Arab League had endorsed a large resettlement program for Palestinian refugees in Arab states. [cite:rs_cw1_truman_unrpr_letter] That second statement is Truman's account of the League's position, and it should be read as such: the record cited here does not include the League's own resolution, and it sits uneasily beside what the Arab delegations said in Paris weeks later.

The Paris conference opened at the Hôtel de Crillon on 13 September 1951 and ran until 19 November. The Commission put five proposals to the Arab delegations on 17 September and to Israel on 21 September. The proposals were mutual cancellation of war-damage claims; repatriation by Israel of "a specified number of Arab refugees"; payment by Israel of a global sum in compensation; mutual release of blocked accounts; and revision of the armistice agreements. [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] Read as a whole, the package asked each side to give something on a point where the other most wanted movement: Israel on return and compensation, the Arab states on war damages and on revising the armistices. The record cited here does not say how the Commission itself described the logic of the package.

To make compensation discussable, the Commission needed a figure, and its Refugee Office supplied one. It valued the land the Arab refugees had abandoned at 100 million Palestine pounds, about $280 million at the rate then current, and their movable property at about £P20 million. The land covered 16,324 square kilometres, of which 4,574 square kilometres were cultivable. [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] The valuation gave the negotiation a figure to discuss. It did not settle who would pay it, to whom, or on what terms.

Israel's answers, as the Commission recorded them, were consistent and specific. It refused mutual cancellation of war damages, on the ground that the Arab states had been the aggressors and should bear war damages. It rejected repatriation, because "major considerations of security and of political and economic stability made the return of Arab refugees impossible." It offered compensation, but as a global sum that would end the refugee problem for Israel. And it attached a condition of its own: any settlement must unfreeze the Jewish accounts blocked in Iraq. [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] That last condition linked the Palestinian refugees' property to the property of Iraq's Jews, whose departure from Iraq in these years is told in [scene:rd6_exodus|the Jews of the Arab world]. In Israel's case the two losses were part of one account.

The strongest form of Israel's position followed from its premises. A state that held the Arab states responsible for the war, and that judged the return of the refugees a danger to its security and to its political and economic stability, could argue that it should not be required to take them back. A global payment would close the matter. And if property was to be compensated, the property Jews had left in an Arab state belonged in the same reckoning. Each of these points is stated in the Commission's report in Israel's own terms. [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985]

The Arab delegations answered from the opposite premise. "There could be no limitations on the return of the refugees," they said. Compensation should be individual, paid in cash, and not tied to Israel's ability to pay. They accepted the release of blocked accounts. On the fifth proposal, Syria said that revising the armistices sought to "ratify a fait accompli." [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] The strongest form of this position, too, followed from its premises. Return, on the Arab reading of Resolution 194, was a right belonging to each refugee, not a concession to be measured out by number; compensation was owed to individuals for their own property, not to be negotiated down to a lump sum that ended a state's responsibility; and changing the armistice lines before a settlement would have turned the results of the war into borders. On one proposal, the release of blocked accounts, the Arab delegations said yes; Israel's recorded answer on that point was its Iraqi condition.

The Commission's verdict named both sides. Neither side, it found, showed "a willingness substantially to recede from its rigid position," and it ended the conference on 19 November 1951. Its report named the two refusals that had stopped it. Israel was not prepared to implement paragraph 11 of Resolution 194, the paragraph on return. The Arab governments were not prepared fully to implement paragraph 5, the paragraph calling for peace negotiations. Together, the Commission wrote, these positions had made it "impossible for the Commission to carry out its mandate." [cite:rs_cw1_unccp_a1985] That finding is the best answer the record gives to the question of who blocked a settlement at Paris. It does not assign the failure to one side. It names a refusal by each, on the paragraph of the same resolution that the other side most needed.

The General Assembly's response was to fund the administrative track on a larger scale while protecting the diplomatic one. Resolution 513 (VI), adopted on 26 January 1952, endorsed a programme of $50 million for relief and $200 million for reintegration over about three years from 1 July 1951. The reintegration was to come "either by repatriation or resettlement," and the whole programme was declared to be "without prejudice to the provisions of paragraph 11 of resolution 194 (III)." The budget for 1952–53 was set at $118 million: $100 million for reintegration and $18 million for relief. [cite:rs_cw1_ga_res513] The phrase "without prejudice" carried the weight of the resolution. It allowed the UN to pay for resettlement without conceding that resettlement replaced return. Whether the refugees would accept that distinction was another matter.

The clearest test came in Sinai. According to Jonathan Adler, writing in 2023 from the papers of Blandford and of Labouisse, UNRWA had begun working with Egypt on Sinai schemes as early as July 1950. In June 1953, Adler writes, the agency reserved $30 million of its $200 million reintegration fund for a siphon scheme to resettle 60,000 refugees from Gaza on nearly 52,000 acres. Palestinians objected to the scheme as a threat to the right of return. [cite:rs_cw1_newlines_sinai] The objection put into practice what the Arab delegations had said in Paris: resettlement funded "without prejudice" to return was, to the Palestinians who objected, a prejudice all the same. What became of the scheme and of the opposition to it belongs to Gaza in 1955, told in Stage Two.

The refugees' own politics were taking shape in these same years, among students. Yasir Arafat, studying engineering in Cairo, took part in founding the Palestinian Students League in 1951, was elected its president at the start of the 1952 academic year, and held the post until he graduated in 1956. [cite:rs_cw1_palquest_arafat] George Habash came to politics by a different road. His family was expelled from Lydda after the town fell on 11 July 1948. He had been general secretary of the student society al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa in 1949–50, graduated from the American University of Beirut in 1951, and in 1952 opened a free clinic for refugees and the poor in Amman with Wadi Haddad. [cite:rs_cw1_palquest_habash] In 1953 both were at the beginning: one led a students' league in Cairo, the other ran a clinic in Amman.

When Habash's own movement began is itself disputed. One account places the founding of the Arab Nationalist Movement at the beginning of the 1950s, at the American University of Beirut, by founders who included Habash and Hani al-Hindi. [cite:rs_cw1_encyc_anm] Another dates it to December 1956, when Habash announced the movement at a general congress in Amman under the slogan "Unity, Liberation, Revenge." [cite:rs_cw1_palquest_habash] The two dates may describe different things, a circle formed at the university and a movement publicly launched, but the sources do not say so, and the question is left open here.

What the record of 1950–1953 does not settle should be stated plainly. It shows the positions each side took at Paris, in their own words, and the Commission's finding that both refused to move; it does not show what either would have accepted under sustained pressure, and no power in the record cited here applied any. It gives Truman's statement that the Arab League had endorsed a large resettlement programme, but not the League's own text, and that statement does not square easily with the Arab delegations' insistence on unlimited return; the sources do not reconcile the two. It gives the Refugee Office's valuation, but no agreed figure was ever reached, and the record does not show how the valuation was calculated beyond its totals. It does not give the number of refugees UNRWA served in its Israel district. And the account of the Sinai scheme rests on one historian's reading of the agency's papers, reported in a single article. By the end of 1953 the UN had a large fund, an agency with 133 international and 5,840 local staff, and a valuation of the refugees' land. It had no agreement on whether they would go home, and, by the one account cited here, Palestinians had objected to the largest resettlement scheme yet proposed as a threat to their return.
