From Reassurance to Alarm: The Estimates of January–May 1963

In October 1962 the State Department told its posts abroad that American scientists had found "no evidence" at Dimona of preparation for weapons production. [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d87] By the end of May 1963 the President of the United States was telling the French foreign minister, on the French record of the conversation, that both their countries would be blamed for an Israeli bomb. The documents of these months record no new American visit to the reactor; they record a change in how Washington read it. Over five months a sequence of estimates, memoranda and one presidential directive moved Washington's view of Dimona from a question settled by inspection to a capability with a forecast date. This section follows those documents in the order they were written, because the order is the argument.

NIE 30-63, 23 January 1963. A National Intelligence Estimate is the coordinated judgment of the American intelligence community, prepared for the President and his senior advisers; it is the government's formal assessment, not one agency's speculation. [cite:wilsoncenter_kennedy_dimona_61_62] National Intelligence Estimate 30-63, dated 23 January 1963, judged that at maximum capacity Dimona "could produce sufficient plutonium for one or two weapons a year." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The sentence is conditional at both ends. It speaks of full power, which the reactor had not reached, and of what it "could" produce, not of what Israel intended to do with it. The estimate addressed capability; it did not claim that weapons had been built or that Israel's government had decided to build them. [cite:wilsoncenter_kennedy_dimona_61_62] It was nonetheless the first formal judgment of this period to put a number on what the reactor could yield.

Airgram A-517, 13 February 1963. Three weeks later the embassy in Tel Aviv gave a different reading. On 13 February 1963 the embassy's airgram A-517, written by the science attaché Robert Webber, judged that Israel was "not making a serious effort" to build weapons at that time. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The two documents need not contradict each other on their own terms. The estimate spoke of what the reactor could do once it ran at full power; the airgram spoke of what Israel appeared to be doing in early 1963. But they pointed policy in different directions. On one reading, the embassy's assessment, together with the October circular, supported patience, and the estimate's supported pressure.

Komer's warning. Inside the White House, Robert Komer of the National Security Council staff took the second view. He warned Kennedy that Israel might have a "very limited capability by 67-68," and he pressed Seaborg, chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission, for an early re-inspection of the site. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The source does not give the exact date of Komer's note. Its significance is that a member of the President's own staff was now putting a date range on an Israeli capability and asking for an American team to go back.

Sherman Kent, 6 March 1963. A central document of the spring was a memorandum of 6 March 1963 by Sherman Kent of the CIA. It did not ask whether Israel would acquire nuclear weapons; it asked what would follow if it did. Kent made four judgments. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

First, a nuclear-armed Israel would become "more rather than less tough" toward its neighbours. Second, the Arab states would feel "profound dismay and frustration," and the United States would be among the main targets of their resentment. Third, they would turn to the Soviet Union. Fourth, Israel would seek American acceptance of its capability. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

Each judgment was a forecast of behaviour, not a finding of fact. Taken together they set out why the matter was an American problem and not only a regional one. On Kent's reading, an Israeli bomb would harden Israel's posture, turn Arab anger against Washington, open the region further to Moscow, and then present the United States with a request to accept the result. The memorandum treated Israeli acquisition as a real prospect with consequences that would land on American policy; it did not say that Israel had decided to acquire the weapon.

NSAM 231, 26 March 1963. The memorandum reached the President through McCone, the Director of Central Intelligence. On 26 March 1963, the day after Kennedy received Kent's estimate from McCone, McGeorge Bundy issued National Security Action Memorandum 231. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] It gave three instructions. It ordered "every feasible measure to improve our intelligence on the Israeli nuclear program." It ordered prompt arrangement of "the next informal inspection." And it asked for proposals to forestall nuclear programmes in the region. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The memorandum also shows a small discrepancy in the record. The editors of the National Security Archive's collection describe it as addressed to the Departments of State and Defense and the CIA. The document itself is addressed to State, the Atomic Energy Commission and the Director of Central Intelligence. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] This book follows the document's own list, on which the Atomic Energy Commission was among those charged with acting. The day after NSAM 231, the State Department's telegram 658 instructed the ambassador in Tel Aviv to seek "semi-annual" visits to Dimona, the opening move of [scene:s11b|the battle of the letters]. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

SNIE 30-2-63, 8 May 1963. In May the intelligence community put dates on its judgment. Special National Intelligence Estimate 30-2-63, of 8 May 1963, judged that Israel "could detonate a domestically developed nuclear device by late 1965, but a more likely date would be sometime in 1966," and that a deliverable weapon would take "a year or two more (1967-68)." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The language is again conditional: "could," "more likely." It was a forecast of when a capability could exist, not a report that a decision had been taken. But it converted January's yield estimate into a timetable, and its deliverable-weapon date matched the "67-68" of Komer's warning.

Kennedy and Couve de Murville, 25 May 1963. Dimona had been built with substantial French assistance. [cite:wilsoncenter_kennedy_dimona_61_62] Kennedy raised it with the French foreign minister, Couve de Murville, on 25 May 1963. The conversation survives in two records, and they do not say the same thing. In the French record, Kennedy said that "we would be blamed equally, you for furnishing uranium, and we for the financial aid." In the American record, he said that Israel's position gave "a pretext to the Russians." Couve, for his part, said that Israel could make at most one or two bombs. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters]

The two versions reveal different concerns, and both may be accurate records of different parts of one conversation. The French note has Kennedy thinking about responsibility: American aid money and French uranium would be named together if Israel built a weapon. The American note has him thinking about the Soviet Union: an Israeli programme would hand Moscow an argument. Couve's "at most one or two" happened to match the January estimate's yearly figure.

Why Kennedy cared. The editors of the collection place Kennedy's concern within his general view of proliferation as "a major challenge to American power." He feared that an Israeli bomb would draw the Soviets into the region and lead others to "follow suit." [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] That is the motive the editors' reading supports, and it is consistent with both versions of the Couve conversation and with Kent's third forecast. The documents present it as a matter of American strategy: a world with more nuclear states was one in which American power counted for less, and a nuclear Israel would invite Soviet involvement and imitation. They do not show a motive beyond that, and none should be read into them.

The two readings. The disagreement inside the American government in these months was real, and each side had documents. On one side stood the evidence of inspection and the embassy's judgment. American visits, the latest in September 1962, had found nothing inconsistent with peaceful use, and the State Department had said so to its posts in October. In February the embassy's science attaché judged that Israel was "not making a serious effort." [cite:rs_cw5_frus18_d87] [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The strongest form of this case was that the people closest to the reactor had looked and found no serious effort, and that capability is not intent.

On the other side stood the estimates. NIE 30-63 put potential output at one or two weapons a year at full power. SNIE 30-2-63 dated a first device to late 1965 or 1966. Kent's memorandum treated acquisition as a real prospect with consequences for American policy across the region. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] The strongest form of this case was that a reactor of this size, whatever its operators said, would give Israel the means within a few years, and that a government ought to plan for a capability before it existed, not after.

The estimates themselves kept the distinction that the argument turned on. They spoke throughout of what Israel "could" do and when. None of them stated that Israel's government had decided to build a weapon, and none claimed knowledge of its intentions. [cite:rs_cw5_nsa_letters] [cite:wilsoncenter_kennedy_dimona_61_62] On the documents reached here, what moved Kennedy in the spring of 1963 was a forecast of capability joined to his own view of proliferation, not proof of a decision. By March that was enough to produce a presidential directive; by May it was enough to put a date on the problem.

The longer American effort to watch Dimona, and the arguments about what that effort later found and failed to find, run well beyond this period and are taken up in [scene:cia_s16|the Dimona surveillance loop].

The record leaves several questions open. The documents collected here do not show what information, if any, separated the embassy's February reading from the January estimate, or whether the two were ever reconciled on paper. They do not give the date of Komer's warning. They do not settle whether the French or the American record of the Couve conversation is the fuller account, or whether both are partial. And they do not show what Israel's government had decided by the spring of 1963. The estimates said what Israel could do; what it intended remained, for Washington, a question the next round of inspections was meant to answer.
