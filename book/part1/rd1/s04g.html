The Palestine Office: Settlement Gets a Bureaucracy

With the Uganda question closed, the movement turned to building in Palestine itself, and it did so through offices, companies and agents rather than improvisation. The familiar picture of Zionist settlement before the First World War is one of informal, ad hoc pioneering with little organization behind it. The documents describe something more deliberate.

In 1907 David Wolffsohn, president of the World Zionist Organization, sent Arthur Ruppin to Palestine. In 1908 Ruppin opened the Palestine Office in Jaffa. It became the organization's central operating agency for land purchase and settlement, working directly with the money of the Jewish National Fund, the movement's land-buying trust. [cite:ruppin_wiki] Ruppin would later be called "the father of Zionist settlement." His fuller record, including his own documented views on race, is treated in [scene:s04j|the section on how land was bought]. The concern here is narrower: the institution he founded.

The Palestine Office coordinated land purchase with the Jewish National Fund and helped immigrants settle. It was also the institutional ancestor of the Jewish Agency, established in 1929. [cite:wiki_palestine_office] Large institutions rarely appear fully formed, and this one traces back to a field office in Jaffa doing the same core work on a smaller scale. The line of descent is documented. That does not mean every later practice of the Jewish Agency goes back directly to the methods of 1908. Organizations change. What runs from Jaffa to 1929 is an institutional line, not an unbroken set of procedures.

Alongside the office, Ruppin and Otto Warburg founded the Palestine Land Development Company. Sources date its founding variously to 1908 or 1909. The company bought land and prepared it physically for Jewish National Fund ownership and for settlers' use. By the outbreak of the First World War it had handled roughly 50,000 dunams. [cite:wiki_otto_warburg_botanist] [cite:encyclopedia_otto_warburg]

This Otto Warburg is not the more famous twentieth-century biochemist of the same name. He was a German botanist, born in 1859 and died in 1938, from a wealthy Hamburg banking family. At the Sixth Congress in 1903 he had supported the Uganda Scheme. He later served as the third president of the World Zionist Organization, from 1911 to 1921. He named the Palestine Land Development Company, led it until his death, and brought the Jewish National Fund in as a shareholder. [cite:encyclopedia_otto_warburg] His path shows how contested the movement's direction had been only a few years earlier, and how quickly it settled once the Congress had decided. His early support for Uganda did not mean a weaker commitment to Palestine afterward; his decades of work on Palestine's land suggest the opposite.

Behind the land totals stood named men. Yehoshua Hankin, Arthur Ruppin and Jacob Thon negotiated and carried out land purchases in these prewar years. Hankin was also the chief negotiator of the Sursock purchase, discussed in the section on how land was bought. Thon is recorded as the company's sole manager from 1907 until his death in 1950. The sources do not agree here, however: they also date the company's founding to 1908 or 1909, have Warburg leading it until 1938, and have Hankin becoming its head in 1932. [cite:wiki_yehoshua_hankin] [cite:wiki_arthur_ruppin] A dunam figure in a table is abstract. Naming the people who closed the deals turns it into a historical process with real actors. These men did not work alone. They operated inside the funding, legal and organizational structures described in neighboring sections.

Together these pieces formed a pipeline. Money raised through the Jewish Colonial Trust and the Jewish National Fund flowed into land acquisition through the Palestine Land Development Company, and from there into settlement, coordinated by a dedicated professional office in Jaffa with named purchasing agents. All of it was in place by 1908, eleven years after the First Zionist Congress at Basel and forty years before statehood. The lone pioneer is part of the story. But by then there was a bureaucracy behind him.
