After the Coup: Iran's Oil Settled, 1953–1954

The overthrow of Iran's government in August 1953 is told in [scene:s11|the coup of August 1953]. What followed it is less often told, and it is where the coup's economic consequences were written down. A familiar account treats the coup as a purely Cold War intervention, anti-communist in purpose, whose only lasting effect was to restore the Shah. The documents of the following fourteen months show something more concrete: a negotiated settlement of the oil dispute, under the new prime minister, General Fazlollah Zahedi, that brought American companies into Iranian oil, kept a large British share, and bound Iran to pay compensation to the company whose holdings it had nationalized. [cite:ourhistory_operation_ajax_shadow]

Washington moved within weeks. On 14 October 1953 Herbert Hoover Jr. was appointed as the Secretary of State's consultant on the Anglo-Iranian oil dispute, and he visited Iran from 17 October to 4 November 1953. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_hoover] The appointment put the United States directly into a quarrel that had until then been between Iran and Britain, and between Iran and one British company, the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company.

Iran's government put its own proposal on the table while Hoover was in the country. On 1 November 1953 Foreign Minister Entezam proposed an international consortium that would buy oil from the National Iranian Oil Company, a consortium in which the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company could take part but without majority control. On 5 December 1953 a British–Iranian communiqué promised a solution that would take account of "the national aspirations of the Persian people" regarding their resources. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_hoover] The two documents are worth reading together. Entezam's design kept Iran's own national company as the seller and the foreign companies as buyers; the communiqué gave the principle of national aspiration a place in the formal record.

The settlement that emerged eight months later was built differently. The agreement in principle with the consortium was signed on 5 August 1954. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company took a 40 per cent holding and Royal Dutch Shell 14 per cent, the rest going to American and French companies, with two operating companies incorporated in the Netherlands. [cite:rs_cw2_fo_consortium] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_ratify] The State Department's press release of 7 December 1954 named the American participants. Five companies, Gulf, Socony-Vacuum, Standard Oil (New Jersey), Standard Oil of California and the Texas Company, each held 8 per cent, and each could transfer up to one-eighth of its share to other American firms within six months. [cite:rs_cw2_dsb_consortium] The French share is not stated in the documents fetched. By arithmetic from these figures (40 per cent, plus 14, plus five times 8), 6 per cent remained for the French participant; the Foreign Office statement itself says only "American and French companies". [cite:rs_cw2_fo_consortium] [cite:rs_cw2_dsb_consortium]

The same day, 5 August 1954, the Iranian and British delegations issued a joint statement on compensation. Iran agreed to pay the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company a net £25 million, over ten years, interest-free, beginning on 1 January 1957. [cite:rs_cw2_compensation54] The terms were then carried through Iran's own institutions. Iran's Senate passed the oil bill on 28 October 1954 by 41 votes to 4, with 3 abstentions; the Shah signed it on 29 October, and the agreements took effect that day. Hoover called the outcome a "significant victory". [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_ratify] [cite:rs_cw2_dsb_consortium]

The mechanism is clearest when the settlement is followed as a flow of money and rights. The oil lay under Iranian soil and had been nationalized. Under the settlement it was worked through a consortium of foreign companies operating through two companies incorporated in the Netherlands, in which the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, Royal Dutch Shell, American and French companies held fixed shares. The Anglo-Iranian Oil Company, which had lost its position by nationalization, came back with the largest single share and a promise of £25 million. Five American companies entered with 8 per cent each. Iran paid the compensation, on a ten-year schedule that would begin in 1957.

Who gained can be read from the record. The British company kept a continued stake, now shared. The American companies gained access to Iranian oil, which one account describes as the first time American companies had held such access. [cite:ourhistory_operation_ajax_shadow] [cite:rs_cw2_dsb_consortium] Iran, for its part, obtained a settlement of the dispute, a formal acknowledgment of its "national aspirations" in the December communiqué, and a ratification by its own Senate; it gave up £25 million and the design Entezam had proposed. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_hoover] [cite:rs_cw2_compensation54]

How far the settlement "ended Iranian national control" of the industry is the point most worth arguing. That is how one account of the consortium characterizes it: the agreement, negotiated under Zahedi, ended Iranian national control of its oil industry and created an international consortium of American and European companies alongside the British. [cite:ourhistory_operation_ajax_shadow] Set beside the primary documents, the characterization has real support and a real limit. In its favour, Iran's own proposal of November 1953 had foreign companies buying from the National Iranian Oil Company, with Anglo-Iranian denied majority control; what was signed in 1954 instead created foreign-held operating companies, incorporated abroad, in which Anglo-Iranian held the largest share. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_hoover] [cite:rs_cw2_fo_consortium] Against an unqualified reading, the settlement was negotiated with Iran's government, ratified by Iran's Senate and signed by the Shah, and the December communiqué wrote Iran's "national aspirations" into the record. [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_hoover] [cite:rs_cw2_frus_iran_ratify] Whether a government installed after the coup could have negotiated otherwise is the question [scene:s11|the account of the coup] leaves to the reader; the documents of 1954 do not answer it.

The record fetched for this section leaves several things open. It does not set out the precise division of rights between the National Iranian Oil Company and the two operating companies after October 1954, so how much formal ownership Iran retained, as distinct from control of production, cannot be stated from these documents. It does not name the French participant. And it does not show how the £25 million figure was arrived at, or what claims the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company had pressed before agreeing to it.

The scene this section draws on also carries the story forward to 1957, when the Shah established the security service SAVAK under General Teymur Bakhtiar with American help, and it states that Israel's Mossad had a co-founding role. [cite:globalsecurity_savak_cia_training] That claim belongs to a later period and is tested in [scene:cw4_iran|Iran and Israel after 1957]. The historian David Menashri, as cited in this volume's first stage, dates the first Israeli intelligence contact with SAVAK to September 1957, when Bakhtiar offered Israel's ambassador an exchange of intelligence, so a founding role is not stated here as established.
