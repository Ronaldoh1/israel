The Papal Judges

The first step was Ad abolendam, issued by Pope Lucius III at Verona on 4 November 1184. It condemned the Cathars, the Waldensians and others by name, and told every bishop to visit his diocese once or twice a year and put residents on oath to say what they knew of heretics. Those who would not recant were to be handed to the lay power for "due punishment"; anyone who protected them lost the right to hold office, go to law or make a will. [cite:vt28_wiki_adabolendam] The Fourth Lateran Council of 1215 made the arrangement general: condemned heretics were to be "handed over to the secular rulers", their property confiscated, and rulers were to "publicly take an oath" to remove heretics from their lands. [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3]

In 1229, as the war against the Cathars ended, a council at Toulouse ordered every parish searched and forbade lay people to own the books of the Old and New Testaments; "we most strictly forbid their having any translation of these books". [cite:vt28_wiki_toulouse] Gregory IX then turned the bishops' duty into a papal office. In 1231 he set the penalties, burning for those who persisted and prison or penance for those who repented, and began naming papal "inquisitors of heretical depravity", mostly Dominican and Franciscan friars. On 20 April 1233 he set up the tribunal for France. Its judges opened cases on their own initiative. [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9]

The last step followed a murder. After Cathar sympathisers killed the inquisitor Peter of Verona in April 1252, Innocent IV issued Ad extirpanda on 15 May. It authorised torture to extract confessions, provided it did not cost life or limb, and required city authorities to carry out the inquisitors' sentences on pain of excommunication. Heretics' houses were to be razed and their confiscated property divided in three: a third to the city, a third to the officials who handled the cases, and a third to the bishop and inquisitors. [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda]

The Church did not itself execute anyone. It handed the condemned to the secular arm, which was bound by oath to burn them. [cite:vt28_herodote_1233] [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] The registers show what the machine did in practice. On 13 May 1239 about 183 people were burned in Champagne. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] Bernard Gui, inquisitor at Toulouse, left a book of 916 sentences on 636 people or communities: about 45 people were burned, nearly 30 per cent of the decisions ended in release, and more than half imposed prison or penance, often yellow crosses sewn on the clothing. [cite:vt28_mednet_gui] The bishop Jacques Fournier questioned the villagers of the Pyrenees for seven years and burned five; his register, taken to Rome when he became Pope Benedict XII, is the source of the historian Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's portrait of the village of Montaillou. [cite:vt28_wiki_fournier]

Historians weigh the design differently. Thomas Madden reads it as an attempt to bring investigation and procedure to a field ruled by mobs and lords; Walter Ullmann called it "the denial of justice or a hideous caricature of it". [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] What both readings accept is the novelty: a standing office that went looking for dissent.
