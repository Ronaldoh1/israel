Washington Against Its Allies: The United Nations, the Cease-Fire and the Money, October–December 1956

The familiar account of Suez says that the United States stopped the war by standing on principle at the United Nations. That account is true as far as it goes. The primary record shows something more layered: an American government that had not been told of the plan its allies had made with Israel, that moved the question out of a deadlocked Security Council and into the General Assembly, that carried the Assembly against Britain and France, and that then, when votes alone did not finish the matter, held back money and oil until the invaders agreed to leave. This section follows that sequence from the first vetoes of 30 October 1956 to the last British and French troops leaving Port Said on 22 December. The secret arrangement that set the war in motion is told in [scene:cw3_sevres|the Sèvres Protocol]; Peter Hahn writes that American officials did not anticipate the collusion before hostilities began. [cite:rs_cw3_shlaim_sevres] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn]

The Office of the Historian of the State Department summarizes Washington's course in two sentences. The United States voted for the UN resolutions condemning the invasion and "approving the creation of a U.N. peacekeeping force," and it "pressured Britain and France to accept a United Nations ceasefire on November 6." [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] The State Department's own documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS), lays out the same weeks in the order its editors found them in the files: deadlock in the Security Council, the convening of the first emergency special session of the General Assembly, the Anglo-French bombardment and the creation of the UN Emergency Force (30 October–5 November); then the assault on the Canal Zone, "the threat of Soviet intervention" and acceptance of a cease-fire (5–6 November); then "the European oil supply problem and the activation of the Middle East Emergency Committee's plan" and the Anglo-French decision to withdraw (7 November–4 December). [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3] The order matters. Diplomacy at the UN came first; the oil and IMF measures documented below came after the guns had stopped, and were used to turn a cease-fire into a withdrawal, though Hahn counts financial pressure among the reasons the cease-fire itself was accepted.

The Council deadlocked, 30–31 October. Israel's campaign in Sinai had begun at 17:00 on 29 October. [cite:rs_cw3_idf_sinai] The next day, at the Security Council's 749th and 750th meetings, two draft resolutions were put to the vote, one American (S/3710) and one Soviet (S/3713/Rev.1). Each called for Israel to withdraw. Britain and France vetoed both. [cite:rs_cw3_unu_ufp] The United States and the Soviet Union had both asked the Council for an Israeli withdrawal, and two of Washington's allies had stopped it.

The way around the veto was a procedure known as Uniting for Peace, under which an emergency session of the General Assembly could be called when the Council could not act. On 31 October, at its 751st meeting, the Council adopted Resolution 119 (S/3721), citing the "lack of unanimity" of its permanent members at the two meetings of the day before and "Considering that a grave situation has been created by action undertaken against Egypt." It called the first emergency special session of the Assembly under that procedure. [cite:rs_cw3_unispal_sc119] [cite:rs_cw3_unu_ufp] The vote was 7 to 2, with France and the United Kingdom against and Australia and Belgium abstaining. [cite:rs_cw3_unispal_sc119] The British and French votes against it did not stop it; the resolution was adopted. The mechanism shifted the forum from a body in which two of the invaders held a veto to one in which, as the following days showed, they could only be outvoted.

Resolution 997, 2 November. The emergency session convened at 5 p.m. on 1 November 1956. After some five hours of debate it adopted Resolution 997 (ES-I), drafted by the United States (A/3256), at about 4:20 a.m. on 2 November. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d467] The text was a cease-fire resolution. It urged an immediate cease-fire and a halt to moving forces and arms into the area; it urged the parties to the 1949 armistice agreements to withdraw behind the armistice lines, to stop raids across them and to observe the agreements; it recommended that members send no military goods to the area; it urged that the Suez Canal be reopened once the cease-fire took effect; it asked the Secretary-General to report on compliance; and it kept the Assembly in emergency session. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_997] [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d467]

Sixty-four states voted for it. Five voted against: Australia, France, Israel, New Zealand and the United Kingdom. Six abstained: Belgium, Canada, Laos, the Netherlands, Portugal and South Africa. [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_997] The Canadian abstention was the most consequential of them, and it was not a vote for the invaders. Canada's Lester Pearson abstained because the text took no United Nations step toward a settlement. He wanted the Secretary-General authorized to arrange a UN force, and Dulles said that the United States would welcome a Canadian proposal. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d467] In the documents used here, the idea of a UN force, rather than a bare demand that the armies stop, first appears at that point.

The UN Emergency Force, 4–7 November. Three more resolutions followed within two days. Resolution 998 (ES-I) of 4 November passed by 57 votes to none, with 19 abstentions, among them Britain, France, Israel, Egypt, the Soviet Union and its allies. Resolution 999, adopted the same day, passed by 59 to 5 with 12 abstentions; the five against were the same five who had opposed 997. Resolution 1000 of 5 November passed by 57 to 0 with 19 abstentions. [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_998] [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_999] [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_1000] These were the resolutions that created and organized the UN Emergency Force, UNEF. Their operative texts were not among the documents fetched for this book, so their provisions are not described here beyond that. [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_998]

On 7 November, at the Assembly's 567th plenary meeting (document A/3354), Resolutions 1001 and 1002 (ES-I) were adopted. [cite:rs_cw3_undl_1001] [cite:rs_cw3_undl_1002] The language of 1002 shows what had not yet happened, a day after the guns fell silent. It "Calls once again upon Israel immediately to withdraw all its forces behind the armistice lines" established by the agreement of 24 February 1949, and "once again upon the United Kingdom and France immediately to withdraw all their forces from Egyptian territory." [cite:rs_cw3_unispal_1002] The Jewish Virtual Library gives its vote as 65 to 1 with 10 abstentions; that figure rests on that source alone. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_1002] The text of 1001 was not fetched.

The table answers one question that the familiar account blurs: how broad the line against the invasion was. Read one way, it was an American achievement. Resolution 997 was the American draft, and the United States carried the Assembly against its own allies. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d467] [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] Read another way, the consensus was broad but not universal, and not simply Washington's. On the roll calls recorded here, only the three invading states, Australia and New Zealand voted no. Canada and several other Western states abstained on 997 because they wanted a UN force rather than a bare cease-fire, and the Soviet bloc abstained on the resolutions that created that force. [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_997] [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_998] [cite:rs_cw3_unproj_1000] Both readings fit the record. The United States wrote the first text; the proposal for a UN force came, in this record, from Canada's Pearson, with Dulles's welcome, and Hahn credits Eisenhower with organizing the force.

The cease-fire, 6 November. British paratroopers jumped onto Gamil airfield and French paratroopers onto Port Fuad on 5 November, and Royal Marine commandos landed on 6 November. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] An East German newspaper, the Berliner Zeitung, reported from Cairo that day that British paratroopers had taken the Port Said airfield, called the invasion fleet "the largest fleet to operate in the Mediterranean since the Allied landing in Sicily," and relayed Egyptian claims of civilian casualties without verifying them. [cite:rs_cw3_ghdi_berliner] The same day, under what Hahn calls American "political and financial pressure," the belligerents accepted the cease-fire. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] General Sir Charles Keightley's despatch records that it was ordered from London at 17:00 GMT on 6 November and took effect at 23:59 GMT that night; Hahn gives 7 November as the day it took effect. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The Soviet notes of 5 November, which FRUS files under the same days, are the subject of [scene:cw3_soviet_hungary|Moscow's notes and Budapest]. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3]

The day after the cease-fire the telephone lines between Washington and London were busy. On 7 November Eisenhower and Eden spoke at 8:43, 9:55 and 10:27 a.m.; Eisenhower sent Eden and Guy Mollet messages about the Gruenther mission; and on 8 November at 3:15 p.m. the President spoke with Treasury Secretary Humphrey. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3subch1] The FRUS list gives the times and the participants; the call to the Treasury can be read as the first sign in it that the next phase would be fought with money.

Pressure on Israel ran on a separate track the same day. On 7 November Hammarskjöld told Ambassador Lodge that General Burns had reported that Israel intended, and might already have begun, to force the observers of the UN Truce Supervision Organization out of Gaza. Hammarskjöld warned that persistent Israeli defiance could provoke so strong a reaction in the UN that Israel might be expelled. [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d534] Israel's reply to that pressure came within a day, and is told in the next sections.

Sterling. The financial lever worked on Britain, and its mechanism is documented by James Boughton, writing for the International Monetary Fund. The Bank of England defended the pound's parity of $2.80 with Britain's reserves, and treated $2 billion as the floor for that defence. The Anglo-French offensive, Boughton writes, "greatly accelerated the drain on U.K. reserves," and a "massive loss of reserves" in November pushed them below that floor. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] Below that floor the parity was in danger, and the help Britain needed would have to come with American agreement.

Humphrey's position, as Boughton records it, was that the United States would support Britain when Britain was "conforming to rather than defying the United Nations." Harold Macmillan tried to meet Humphrey in late November and failed. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] The condition was the same one the Assembly had voted: leave Egypt. On 3 December, once the withdrawal had been agreed, Humphrey proposed an immediate drawing of $561 million plus a stand-by credit of about $739 million, $1.3 billion in all, equal to 100 per cent of Britain's quota at the Fund. On 4 December Macmillan announced the November losses and "an immediate approach" to the IMF. The Fund's Executive Board approved the package with one abstention, Egypt's, and Britain drew the $561 million at once. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] In a working paper on the same events, Boughton adds that all four combatants, Britain, France, Israel and Egypt, obtained IMF financing, which was "highly unusual for the time." [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_wp]

Oil. The second lever was fuel. By mid-November, Britain and France said, Europe's oil supplies had fallen "25 to 30 percent below their normal needs." Until the end of November, according to a FRUS editorial note, the United States refused to put "the plan of action prepared by the Middle East Emergency Committee" into effect, offering the oil "if those two countries complied with the United Nations resolutions" and withdrew. [cite:rs_cw3_frus10_d234] At a conference with the President on 25 November, Eisenhower explained the delay in his own words: "we have held up this measure until the invading powers accepted immediate withdrawal of their troops." Hoover urged that the emergency committee be started "very soon," and the President wanted it announced as a measure to prevent "widespread suffering in Europe." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d612] The record thus shows the oil held back as leverage and then released with a humanitarian explanation, by the President's own direction.

The force arrives, the invaders leave. UNEF's first troops arrived in Egypt on 15 November. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] The Anglo-French force ashore did not shrink at once; it peaked on 1 December at 13,500 British and 8,500 French troops. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] Britain and France completed their withdrawal from Port Said on 22 December 1956; Keightley's despatch records the last troops aboard at 17:10 GMT. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Israel's forces remained in Sinai and Gaza, and the Assembly's demand on Israel, repeated in Resolutions 1123 and 1124 of January and February 1957, became the subject of [scene:cw3_withdrawal|the withdrawal fight of 1957]. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_1124]

The quarrel among the Western allies did not last. The Office of the Historian links the American public criticism of Britain and France to Eden's resignation in January 1957, and records that by March 1957 relations between Washington and London had recovered under Harold Macmillan. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] The wider settlement of 1957 is told in the closing section of this stage.

Principle or money? Historians and the official record give two answers to why Eisenhower turned on his allies, and they are not exclusive. The first is the one the Office of the Historian and Hahn emphasize. Washington had not been told of the collusion; it feared that the Soviets "would intervene to assist Nasser"; and in the same week it was making a "strident condemnation of the Soviet intervention in Hungary"; on this reading, a government condemning one intervention that week was poorly placed to excuse another. [cite:rs_cw3_milestones_suez] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] On that reading the cease-fire was an act of policy and principle, carried through the UN.

The second answer puts the levers at the centre. FRUS places the oil supply problem and the Treasury at the heart of the November decisions, and Hahn himself describes the pressure as "political and financial." [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3] [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_ch3subch1] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Boughton and the 25 November memorandum show the mechanism directly: British financing was tied to compliance with the UN, and oil was held back until withdrawal was accepted. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] [cite:rs_cw3_frus16_d612] The strongest form of this case is that resolutions had been passed since 2 November without moving a single soldier out of Egypt, and that the withdrawal came only when Britain could not defend its currency without American consent.

There is a strong reply to the second case, and it comes from the same documents. The UN votes, the Soviet threats and the British cabinet's own decision of 6 November to accept the cease-fire all came first. The IMF package was proposed on 3 December, after the cease-fire and after the decision to withdraw, not before them. [cite:rs_cw3_boughton_fd] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] On this reading money did not stop the war; it fixed the terms of the exit and made sure the withdrawal, once promised, was completed. The record supports that sequence. It does not show which consideration weighed most with Eden's cabinet on 6 November.

Several things remain open. The operative texts of Resolutions 998, 999, 1000 and 1001 were not fetched for this book, so the terms on which UNEF was created are known here only from the votes. The vote on Resolution 1002 rests on a single source, and that source does not name the state that voted against it. The content of the Eisenhower–Eden telephone calls of 7 November and of the Gruenther messages is not described in the document list that records them. And the weight each lever carried in London, the votes, the Soviet notes, the reserves or the oil, is a matter of inference: the documents show that all four were in play in the same weeks, and that Washington used the last two deliberately, but not which of them decided the outcome.
