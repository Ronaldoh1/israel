Egypt in Yemen, 1962–1967

Israel entered 1967 in recession; Egypt entered it at war. When its army moved into Sinai in May 1967, part of it was somewhere else. Since the autumn of 1962 Egyptian troops had been fighting in Yemen, in a civil war that had begun as a short commitment and had not ended. The familiar story of the May crisis sets Egypt's forces against Israel's as if the two armies faced each other at full strength. The record of the Yemen war complicates that picture. It does not explain the crisis, and no source cited here claims that it does, but it is part of the condition of the army Nasser sent forward.

A short mission. Egypt entered Yemen's civil war in September 1962, after a coup overthrew the country's monarchy. According to the historians Eugene Rogan and Tewfik Aclimandos, Anwar Sadat was among the aides who assured Nasser that a short, small-scale mission would be enough. [cite:rs_cw6_rogan_aclimandos] The first deployment, according to Foreign Policy, was a single company of commandos. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen]

It did not stay small. Nasser himself later said that the force had grown to "70,000 troops." [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen] Bruce Riedel, writing for the Brookings Institution, gives the expeditionary force's peak at the same figure, 70,000. [cite:rs_cw6_riedel_cia] The war was also a contest between states. Saudi Arabia supplied the royalists loyal to the deposed Imam, al-Badr, while Egypt carried the war beyond Yemen's borders, striking Saudi territory and the Aden Protectorate from the air. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen] Two Arab governments were thus on opposite sides of a war in the peninsula through the same years as the Arab summits of 1964 and 1965.

The attempt at Jeddah. In 1965 Nasser went to Jeddah to make peace with King Faisal of Saudi Arabia. The agreement did not hold. According to Foreign Policy, the Yemeni factions rejected a settlement that had been made over their heads. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen] The two outside powers could agree with each other; they could not deliver the parties actually fighting. Egypt's army remained committed.

What it cost. The cost to Egypt is given in two figures that are often set against each other as if they disagreed. They measure different things. Riedel gives Egypt's losses by 1967 as 25,000 casualties, a figure that counts the wounded as well as the dead. [cite:rs_cw6_riedel_cia] Foreign Policy gives at least 10,000 Egyptian dead over the five years of the war. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen] The two need not conflict; neither can be checked against the other from these sources, because neither breaks its total down.

Still there in May 1967. Egyptian forces were still in Yemen when Nasser moved his army into Sinai in May 1967. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen] How many were there at that moment is not established by any source consulted for this book. The peak of 70,000 is a figure for the war's height, not for May 1967, and it should not be read as the number missing from Sinai. Their withdrawal, settled at the Khartoum summit, belongs to the aftermath of [scene:s12|the Six-Day War]. [cite:rs_cw6_fp_yemen]

The historians' judgment. Rogan and Aclimandos, in their chapter of the Cambridge volume on the origins and consequences of 1967 edited by Wm Roger Louis and Avi Shlaim, call Yemen "an unwinnable war, Nasser's Vietnam." They conclude that the campaign "fatally impaired" Egypt's performance in June 1967. [cite:rs_cw6_rogan_aclimandos] Their claim is carefully bounded. They do not argue that Yemen alone caused Egypt's defeat; they argue that it weakened the army that had to fight. [cite:rs_cw6_rogan_aclimandos] The chapter as consulted here is its published abstract, so the evidence on which the judgment rests in detail cannot be set out from it.

Washington's reading of the balance. The United States government had its own assessment of what Egypt's army could do, and it was made in the crisis itself. On 22 May 1967 Nasser announced the closure of the Strait of Tiran, told in [scene:cw6_tiran_june4|the last section of this stage]. The next day, 23 May, the CIA's Office of Current Intelligence produced an assessment that Johnson had requested. It judged, according to Riedel, that Israel could defend itself against any combination of Arab enemies and could launch a major offensive. A joint paper by the CIA and the Defense Intelligence Agency at the end of May went further: Israel, it judged, would take Sinai in "several days." [cite:rs_cw6_riedel_cia] The same analysis called Arab unity "fiction," and found no serious military coordination among the Arab states. [cite:rs_cw6_riedel_cia]

Riedel's summary, as consulted here, does not tie these judgments to Yemen, and it would go beyond the sources to say that the war in the peninsula was what the analysts had in mind. On one reading, though, the two records point the same way. Egypt's army had spent five years in a war its own leaders had expected to be short, at a cost Riedel puts at 25,000 casualties, against forces backed by another Arab government; and Washington's analysts, looking at the balance in late May, saw neither Arab military superiority nor Arab military coordination.

What the record does not settle. Three things remain unsettled here. The number of Egyptian troops in Yemen in May 1967 is not verified. The breakdown of the casualty figures is not given. And the degree to which Yemen, rather than other causes, impaired Egypt's army in June is a judgment, made by Rogan and Aclimandos with an explicit limit on it, not a measured finding.
