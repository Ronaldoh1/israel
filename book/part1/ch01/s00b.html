Semitic Is Not a Synonym for Jewish

Much of the modern argument over Israel turns on a single word, and the word has a history of its own. The familiar version holds that "antisemitic" simply means anti-Jewish, and that criticism of Israel therefore counts as antisemitism. The record shows something more layered: a linguist's term, a nineteenth-century coinage aimed at Jews, and a twenty-first-century dispute over whether the word should reach political criticism of a state.

The root is Shem, in Hebrew שֵׁם, one of the sons of Noah in the genealogy of Genesis 10. In 1781 the German scholar August Ludwig von Schlözer coined the term "Semitic" (semitisch) for the family of languages that the biblical genealogy traced to Shem. [cite:wiki_semitic_etymology] It was a classification of languages, not of races or religions. It worked the way "Romance languages" does: a family label, named after Rome, that covers French, Spanish and Italian without implying that their speakers are one people. Schlözer's framework grouped Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Ge'ez, Akkadian and Phoenician together. Through most of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the word stayed in that classroom sense, alongside "Indo-European" and "Sino-Tibetan." Words change their meaning over time, and that is normal. What matters is tracking how and when this one changed.

The family today includes Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic, Amharic, Tigrinya, Maltese, Ge'ez, Syriac and Mehri, as well as extinct Akkadian. By the word's original meaning, its speakers, among them Arabs, Jews, Ethiopians, Assyrians, Chaldeans and Maltese, are all Semitic peoples. This is a textbook definition, not a political claim. Peer-reviewed population genetics points in the same direction for the Levant, finding substantial shared ancestry among many of the region's populations. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] [cite:haber_2013_bronze]

That applies directly to Palestinians. They speak Arabic, a Semitic language. According to the population genetics literature, including Nebel and colleagues in 2000 and Behar and colleagues in 2010, they descend from populations that have lived in the region continuously since antiquity, overlapping substantially with the ancestral pool of Levantine Jewish populations. [cite:nebel_pop_genetics_2000] [cite:behar_2010_shared] On linguistic and genetic grounds alike, Palestinians meet the definition of a Semitic people. That fact does not resolve any particular dispute between Israelis and Palestinians. It resolves one narrower thing: the conflict cannot coherently be described as Semites against anti-Semites.

The word "antisemitism" itself took its political meaning in 1879. Wilhelm Marr, a German journalist and agitator who was a Lutheran, not a Jew, published Der Sieg des Judenthums über das Germanenthum (The Victory of Judaism over Germandom) and founded the League of Antisemites the same year. [cite:wiki_marr_1879] He made "antisemitismus" the name of his movement as a euphemism for Judenhass, Jew-hatred, meant to sound scientific and modern rather than crude and religious. Moshe Zimmermann's 1986 biography, Wilhelm Marr: The Patriarch of Anti-Semitism, documents that the choice was deliberate. The adjective was older: in 1860 the Jewish scholar Moritz Steinschneider had written of "antisemitic prejudices" to attack Ernest Renan's claim that Semitic peoples were inferior. [cite:wiki_antisemitism_etym] Nothing in Marr's writing or his league's activity shows concern for Arabic speakers or any other Semitic-language population. From Marr onward, antisemitism meant hatred of Jews, and it still does. That settles what the word meant. It does not settle how any particular modern claim should be judged.

The contested shift came much later. The working definition of antisemitism adopted by the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance in 2016 carries 11 attached examples, 7 of which describe criticism of Israel or Zionism as antisemitic under certain conditions. In effect, it pointed the word toward a new target: political criticism of a state and a political movement. [cite:ihra_definition] On March 25, 2021, more than 200 scholars, including Omer Bartov, Christopher Browning, Michael Rothberg and Noah Feldman, signed the Jerusalem Declaration on Antisemitism. It kept the core definition but stated that boycott of Israel, criticism of settlements and criticism of Zionism as an ideology are not in themselves antisemitic. [cite:jerusalem_declaration] A widely repeated form of the same move circulates in op-eds and online: the claim that opposing Israel denies Jews their right to national self-determination and is therefore discriminatory. It appears in pro-Israel commentary (a 2019 formulation by Bari Weiss is among the most cited), in advocacy organizations' own materials, and in critiques of them. [cite:currentaffairs_snappy_hasbara]

The Jerusalem Declaration has serious critics of its own. Dr. Dana Barnett of the Begin-Sadat Center for Strategic Studies, and researchers writing for NGO Monitor, have argued that its exceptions, which permit comparisons of Israel to settler colonialism or apartheid and treat BDS as ordinary non-violent protest, could serve as cover for antisemitic framing rather than guarding against it. [cite:besa_jda_barnett] [cite:ngomonitor_jda_bogus] Credentialed scholars hold each position in print: some say the IHRA definition reaches too far into legitimate criticism; others say the Jerusalem Declaration reaches too far in sheltering the real thing. This book does not resolve that dispute.

What the etymology does is clear ground. Once "Semitic" is understood as a family of languages and their speakers, the conflict appears as what the documents show it to be: a political dispute between a state, a political movement and a displaced population, all of whom, in the word's original sense, are Semitic.
