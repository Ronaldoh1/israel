Work and Vetting: The Gates to a Job

Most adults meet the state's records not at a border but at a job. Before a first day of work an employer may check a new hire's right to work against federal databases, verify past pay through a commercial service, and buy a background report; anyone who works for the federal government or holds a security clearance is now checked not once but continuously. Each of these gates draws on records described elsewhere in this book, and each can close. This section sets out how they work, who decides, what notice and appeal exist, and what changed in 2025 and 2026. A gate's existence is documented; its use against a particular person for a political reason needs a document of its own.

The right to work: E-Verify

E-Verify is an online system run by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, part of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). An employer enters the details from a new hire's Form I-9, the federal employment-eligibility form, and the system checks them against Social Security Administration and DHS records. Congress created it in 1996, in the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act, as a pilot. It is voluntary for most employers but required for those holding covered federal contracts and, in some states, by state law. [cite:de24b_ev_manual] When records disagree the result is a "mismatch," which, the system's own guidance says, "does not necessarily mean" the worker is unauthorised; the worker has ten federal working days to say whether they will contest it, and if they do not respond the employer may close the case. [cite:de24b_ev_tnc] Because it reads Social Security records, any change to those records travels with it. In April 2025, about 6,300 immigrants DHS had identified as people on a terrorist watch list or with FBI criminal records were entered into Social Security's list of the dead; AP reported that the people affected were in the country legally and that without a working number they could not legally work. [cite:de30_ap_ssadead] The broader repurposing of Social Security data for checking voters is in [scene:de30_silos_and_subpoenas|the section on silos and subpoenas].

Past pay and background reports

The second gate is commercial. The Work Number, the Equifax payroll database described in [scene:de24b_benefits|the section on benefits], also sells employment and salary verification to employers and lenders, along with, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), criminal-record, driving-record and drug-test information. [cite:de24b_cfpb_twn] Background-screening firms assemble similar reports from court records and data brokers. The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), the law that governs credit reports, also governs these when they are used for employment: the employer needs the worker's permission, must give a copy of the report before taking adverse action, and the screening company must follow reasonable procedures for accuracy and investigate disputes. [cite:de24b_cfpb_circ]

On 24 October 2024 the CFPB issued a circular saying that the newer products of workplace monitoring, among them dossiers that include collective-bargaining activity and algorithmic scores built from keystrokes, browsing and time off-task, some of them predicting union activity, are often consumer reports under the same law, carrying the same duties. On 12 May 2025 the bureau withdrew it, among 67 guidance documents, saying such guidance does not create rights and some had read the statute too broadly. [cite:de24b_cfpb_circ] [cite:de24b_cs_withdraw] The law itself did not change; what was withdrawn was the regulator's statement that it reaches worker scores.

Continuous vetting

Government work adds a deeper gate. Under a reform called Trusted Workforce 2.0, the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA), which vets about 95 per cent of the federal government, enrolled every national-security clearance holder in "continuous vetting" by October 2021: automated, ongoing checks of criminal, terrorism and financial databases and public records, including credit data. In September 2024 DCSA said more than 3.8 million people were enrolled and began extending continuous checks of arrest records to the much larger "public trust" workforce in non-security posts; social media was not then checked. [cite:de24b_bu_cv] The software behind it, the National Background Investigation Services system (NBIS), has been in development since 2016. GAO testified on 24 February 2026 that $2.4 billion had been spent through 2024 on it and the older systems, with $2.2 billion more projected to 2031, that the reform's completion had slipped to the end of 2028, and that continuous-vetting alerts needing human review had risen from about 30,000 a quarter in 2023 to more than 100,000 in mid-2025. [cite:de24b_gao_nbis]

The military's own personnel records are held in other systems. On 16 March 2026 the Army ordered $3.68 million of work from Palantir to support its Personnel Data Environment, a personnel-data system, through 15 November 2026, under the Army's enterprise agreement with the company; the order sustains a system that already existed. [cite:de24b_govly_pde] What data it holds beyond personnel records, and any link to the Army's retention decisions, is not documented. The rules that let the civil service reclassify and dismiss policy staff are in [scene:de30_rage_to_schedule|the section on RAGE and the new schedules].

Clearances as a political gate

A clearance is also a decision, and in 2025 it became a visible one. On 19 August 2025 the Director of National Intelligence, Tulsi Gabbard, revoked the clearances of at least 37 current and former national-security officials, among them people involved in the 2016 assessment of Russian election interference and members of President Biden's National Security Council. The memo accused them of "politicization or weaponization of intelligence"; according to a source who saw it, it cited no specific evidence. Gabbard wrote that a clearance "is a privilege, not a right." [cite:de24b_kvia_gabbard] In 2025 executive orders against four law firms, Perkins Coie, Susman Godfrey, WilmerHale and Jenner & Block, had restricted their lawyers' clearances and access to federal buildings over the firms' past clients and hires. Four district judges found the orders unconstitutional; the Justice Department moved to withdraw its appeal in March 2026, reversed itself within a day, and argued it before the D.C. Circuit on 14 May 2026, and no ruling had been reported as of October 2026. At least nine other firms settled, pledging pro bono work. [cite:de24b_gma_lawfirms]

If it were connected

Graded as in [scene:de24b_benefits|the first section of this part]. Social Security records into E-Verify: documented, by design; the April 2025 death-file entries show that a change made for an immigration purpose reaches a person's ability to work. [cite:de30_ap_ssadead] [cite:de24b_ev_manual] Financial and criminal databases into continuous vetting: documented, for more than 3.8 million clearance holders. [cite:de24b_bu_cv] Social media into continuous vetting: possible, and DCSA has said public profiles may be added; it was not checked as of 2024. [cite:de24b_bu_cv] Non-immigration flags pushed into E-Verify, so that a job check turns on something other than work eligibility: not supported by any record, and it would need a change in law. Workplace monitoring scores sold to the government: possible, with no document; the withdrawn circular shows that such scores exist and that the regulator has stepped back from them. [cite:de24b_cfpb_circ] Palantir's Army personnel work joined to its immigration systems at ICE: not supported; they are separate orders for separate customers, and nothing shows data moving between them. [cite:de24b_govly_pde]

The clearance revocations are the one place in this part where a gate already closed on people the government described as political opponents. They did not depend on joined data; they depended on discretion. The law-firm orders show the other side: where a clearance sanction reached private lawyers for their clients, four courts said no. How credit and payment records complete the set is the last section's subject.
