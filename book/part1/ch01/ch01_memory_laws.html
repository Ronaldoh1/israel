Every State Guards Its Founding Story

This part has followed the pen through publishers, school boards, ministries and donors. One more instrument sits above them all: the law itself. Several states have written their preferred account of their own history into statute, so that telling it differently costs money, a career or a prison term. Israel is one of them. Setting it beside the others shows what is common to the pattern and what is distinctive about Israel's version.

Four cases on record

Israel. In March 2011 the Knesset passed, by 37 votes to 25, an amendment to its budget law known as the Nakba Law. It allows the finance minister to cut state funding to any publicly funded body that marks Israel's Independence Day as a day of mourning, the day Palestinians commemorate as the Nakba, or that rejects Israel's existence as a Jewish and democratic state. In January 2012 the Supreme Court dismissed a challenge as premature, without ruling on whether the law is constitutional. [cite:ml_adalah_nakba] The same pattern reached the classroom: in 2009 the education ministry removed the word Nakba from a textbook for Arab pupils, and a 2024 law lets it dismiss teachers found to support terror, as the section on three ways a country holds the pen describes. [cite:pen_csm_nakba2009] [cite:pen_ynet_teachers2024]

Turkey. In February 2005 the novelist Orhan Pamuk told a Swiss magazine that "thirty-thousand Kurds and one million Armenians were killed in these lands." Prosecutors charged him under Article 301 of the penal code with "insulting Turkishness," an offence then carrying up to four years in prison. Human Rights Watch called it one of many prosecutions under the article and urged Turkey to abolish it. [cite:ml_hrw_pamuk] It is the clearest case on record of a state using criminal law against people who describe the killing of Armenians in 1915 in terms the state rejects.

Poland. In February 2018 Poland passed a law to punish anyone who blamed "the Polish nation" for Nazi crimes. The chief historian of Yad Vashem, Israel's Holocaust memorial, called it "morally and historically flawed." Under pressure from the United States and vocal opposition from Israel, the Polish parliament amended it in June 2018 to make the offence civil rather than criminal. [cite:ml_jta_poland2018] Here Israel opposed another state's memory law, on the ground that it shielded a national story from the documented record.

The United States. Between 2021 and late 2023, according to PEN America, 40 measures restricting what teachers may say about race, American history and other "divisive concepts" became law or policy in 22 states, covering about 1.3 million public school teachers. [cite:ml_pen_2023] These are not criminal laws, but they use funding and employment, as Israel's Nakba Law does, to set limits on how a nation's past may be taught.

What is common, and what is distinctive

What these cases share is that a government decided a version of history was important enough to enforce. That fact alone does not settle whether the version is true. Turkey prosecutes writers over a finding much of the world has formally recognized; Poland's law was, in the words of Yad Vashem's chief historian, historically flawed; and a state could in principle protect an accurate story by the same means. The law shows which story a state most fears losing. The record shows whether that story is true.

Israel's case has two distinctive features. The first is what is guarded: not a single event but the meaning of the state's founding year, which the same law treats as a cause for celebration and forbids publicly funded bodies to mourn. The second is reach. Turkey and Poland enforce their stories at home. The account of Israel's founding is also protected abroad, through the definition of hatred of Jews that lists certain criticism of Israel among its examples and that 47 governments and hundreds of universities have adopted, as the section on the definition fight sets out. [cite:def_cam_2025] No other state is named in the examples of a definition of bigotry adopted by so many governments. That is the clearest sense in which the pen, on this subject, has been held unusually tightly.
