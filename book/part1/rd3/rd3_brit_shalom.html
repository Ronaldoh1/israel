1925: The Hebrew University and Brit Shalom

On 1 April 1925 Arthur Balfour stood on Mount Scopus above Jerusalem and opened the Hebrew University. Chaim Weizmann presided, as chairman of the university's board [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_hebrew_univ]. In the same year, and prompted by a lecture given at that same opening, a small group in Jerusalem founded an association around a question the rest of the movement answered differently: if Jews and Arabs were both to live in Palestine, on what terms would they share its government? The association was Brit Shalom, and its answer was a binational country in which neither people ruled the other [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom].

The university on Mount Scopus. The university had been a long time in the making. Twelve foundation stones were laid on Mount Scopus on 24 July 1918, and Albert Einstein gave the first lecture there in 1923 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_hebrew_univ]. At the opening in 1925, Balfour performed the ceremony and Weizmann presided. Judah Magnes became the university's chancellor, and later its president. The first degrees were awarded in 1931 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_hebrew_univ].

Balfour's presence was itself an event in Jerusalem. A report in the Detroit Jewish Chronicle of 10 April 1925 described "excitement" among the city's Arabs at a rumour that he would visit the Omar Mosque, and stated that no incidents occurred [cite:rs_rd3_djc_1925_04_10]. That is the extent of what the sources used here record about the Arab response to his visit. Accounts of wider protests have not been verified for this book and are left out.

The founding of Brit Shalom. Brit Shalom was founded in Jerusalem in 1925 on the initiative of Arthur Ruppin. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, the immediate prompt was a lecture by Professor Joseph Horowitz at the university's opening [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. Ruppin chaired the association until 1929 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. Its early members included Ruppin, Jacob Thon, Joseph Lurie, Yitzhak Epstein, Haim Margolis-Kalvaryski and Rabbi Binyamin [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom].

Its program was simple to state and radical in its implications. Brit Shalom sought a binational arrangement in which Jews and Arabs would share the administration of the country equally, regardless of their numbers [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. The last three words were the heart of it. Brit Shalom proposed to separate political power from population: parity of government whichever people was the larger. For Jews, then a minority in the country, it meant giving up any claim to rule by majority should they become one. For Arabs, then the majority, it meant sharing power equally with a minority.

Its members were, in the precise sense, Zionists. They did not reject Jewish settlement or a Jewish national home in Palestine. What they rejected was the model of Jewish sovereignty, and later of partition. In that they were a different current from the Orthodox opposition of the old Yishuv, which rejected the national project itself on religious grounds and whose spokesman was killed in Jerusalem the year before Brit Shalom was founded; that story is told in [scene:dh_s01|Jacob de Haan and the Orthodox opposition] [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom] [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism].

Who belonged, and the dispute over Magnes. Brit Shalom never had more than 200 members [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. It functioned more as a small study circle of academics and intellectuals than as a political party, and it never grew into a mass constituency among either Jews or Arabs [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom] [cite:wiki_brit_shalom]. Its character changed over time. According to the Encyclopaedia Judaica, after 1929 Central European intellectuals influenced by Martin Buber predominated, among them Hugo Bergmann, Hans Kohn, Gershom Scholem, Ernst Simon and Robert Weltsch [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. It did have prominent public support. Albert Einstein, who had given the university's first lecture, publicly backed it, a fact rarely mentioned alongside his name [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom].

The sources disagree about who founded it, and the disagreement concerns its best-known associate. One account used elsewhere in this library calls Martin Buber and Judah Magnes "both founders of Brit Shalom (1925)," and names Gershom Scholem and Henrietta Szold among the colleagues who founded it with them [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom] [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism]. The Encyclopaedia Judaica gives a different picture. It credits the initiative to Ruppin and lists different early members; it places Scholem among those who predominated after 1929; it describes the later members as influenced by Buber rather than naming him as a founder; and it states that Magnes, though he advocated binationalism in this period and his name was often linked to the association, was never a member of it [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom].

The two accounts can be partly reconciled. Magnes advocated binationalism in these years, which may be why his name was so often linked to the association; that is an inference, not a documented finding. Buber's influence on its later members is common to both accounts. But on the narrow question of membership the sources conflict, and this book does not resolve it. The Encyclopaedia Judaica is the more specific of the two, naming founders and dates; the other account reflects how the association has commonly been remembered.

Attack and decline. Brit Shalom drew open hostility from the rest of the movement. Most Zionist parties attacked it [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. The opposition came from both of the dominant currents, Labor and the Revisionists, and some of its opponents explicitly called its members traitors [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom].

The decisive blow came from events. The violence of August 1929, in Hebron above all, undermined the premise on which the association was founded: that Arab political leadership would accept binational equality [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism]. The killings in Hebron and Safed, told in [scene:s07a4|the Western Wall and August 1929], made the argument for parity far harder to make among Jews, and the sources here record no Arab political body that took up the offer. Ruppin left the chair in 1929 [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. The association carried on for a few more years, with the Buber circle now predominant. Its monthly, She'ifoteinu, appeared from 1930 to 1933. By 1933 Brit Shalom had virtually ceased to exist [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom], about eight years after its founding [cite:wiki_brit_shalom].

The arguments, for and against. The sources record the hostility to Brit Shalom more fully than the reasoning behind it. Its own case is documented in its program: parity in government, fixed regardless of numbers, so that neither people would rule the other [cite:rs_rd3_jvl_brit_shalom]. The case against it, as historians reconstruct it from events, is that it depended on an Arab political partner willing to accept binational equality, and that the violence of 1929 showed no such partner in view [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism]. Its opponents inside the movement went further and treated the proposal as betrayal [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom]. The record does not decide whether the binational idea could have worked. It shows that it was never tested, because the association never won a constituency large enough to test it, among Jews or among Arabs.

The binational idea outlived the association. Buber and Magnes went on to found Ihud in 1942, Hannah Arendt warned against an exclusively Jewish sovereign state in 1944, and Buber and Magnes testified against partition before the United Nations commission in 1947. [cite:wiki_ihud] [cite:researchgate_arendt_zionism_reconsidered] [cite:britainpalestine_buber_magnes] That later history lies outside this chapter and is told in [scene:s00b3|Opposition to Zionism across every lineage].

Several questions remain open. The sources do not settle who founded Brit Shalom or whether Magnes ever joined it. They do not record any formal Arab response to its proposals, or name an Arab group with which it negotiated. They give no membership figures beyond the ceiling of 200, and they do not say what Joseph Horowitz argued in the lecture that prompted its founding. What is established is that in 1925, alongside the institution meant to crown the national home, a group of Zionists at the center of the movement proposed sharing the country on equal terms, were attacked for it by most of their own movement, and had all but disappeared within eight years.
