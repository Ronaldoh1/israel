How Israel Was Sold to Americans

Between 1945 and 1948, Americans were given a case for a Jewish state. It is often assumed that they backed Israel because it was a proven strategic ally. The familiar version stops there. The documents do not. The case made to the American public at the time rested on quite different grounds. The strategic argument came later, after a long interval, and against the earlier opposition of the national-security establishment.

The first ground was refuge. The central argument of 1945 to 1948 was humanitarian: some 250,000 Jewish displaced persons in Europe, and the Holocaust behind them. Earlier parts of this book document that crisis in detail.

The second ground was religious. For Christian supporters, a Jewish state in Palestine fulfilled restorationist belief, the conviction that the Jews would be restored to the land, a current this book traces back to 1839. The third ground was political: the new state was presented to Americans as a democracy.

What the case did not rest on, in 1948, was American strategic interest. The national-security establishment argued the opposite. The State Department, the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary and the CIA all warned that backing the new state would harm American interests. They were overruled. At the founding, Israel was not supported as a strategic ally. The strategic professionals regarded support as a liability.

The publicity of the period also carried a hostile picture of Arabs. Pro-Zionist material leaned heavily on the Grand Mufti's wartime collaboration with Hitler, a real and documented episode, and used it as a broad brush. That should be kept apart from what came much later. The organized, well-funded anti-Islam network in the United States is documented mainly from the 2000s, building on currents that emerged after 1967 and after 1979. [cite:cap_fear_inc] Anti-Arab framing was present in 1948. The industry did not yet exist.

The strategic case arrived a generation after the founding. The argument that Israel was a strategic asset grew after its victory in 1967. It was formalized under President Reagan, who called Israel a strategic asset and signed a strategic-cooperation agreement in 1981.

The order of events matters for how the relationship is understood today. Israel was first presented to Americans in terms of refuge, scripture and democracy. The relationship is often defended now in terms of alliance and security. The second set of terms was not available in 1948, and the people whose job was to judge American security interests at the time said so in writing. Reading today's framing back into the founding misdescribes both periods. It is one of several founding narratives examined in [scene:pn_s01|seven narratives, checked].
