Kristallnacht: The Turn to Open Violence

On November 7, 1938, at the German embassy in Paris, a 17-year-old named Herschel Grynszpan shot the German diplomat Ernst vom Rath. Grynszpan was a German-born Polish Jew living in Paris. He had just learned that his parents had been forcibly deported from Germany to the Polish border town of Zbąszyń, where thousands of expelled Polish Jews were being held under harsh conditions. Vom Rath died of his wounds two days later, on November 9. [cite:ushmm_kristallnacht] [cite:yadvashem_november_pogrom]

Those are the documented circumstances behind the shooting. Recording them does not excuse the killing of a diplomat. It records why a teenager did what he did, a matter of historical fact separate from any judgment of the act. It matters, too, because of what the Nazi regime built on top of it.

The familiar version of what followed describes spontaneous popular outrage, a mob reacting to an assassination. The pretext was real. The spontaneity was not. Historical analysis identifies the Propaganda Minister, Josef Goebbels, as seeing in vom Rath's death the moment to unleash the violence he had long advocated. The pogrom that followed was coordinated across Germany, Austria and the Sudetenland within a narrow 48-hour window. [cite:wiki_kristallnacht] That speed and reach are themselves evidence of organization. Real popular participation is also documented, and it would be wrong to claim that everyone who joined in was an agent of the state. The point concerns the top: the direction and the timing came from the regime.

The scale can be stated precisely. Over the night of November 9–10, 1938, rioters destroyed more than 1,400 synagogues and prayer rooms across Germany, Austria and the Sudetenland, burning or desecrating Torah scrolls and other religious objects. Approximately 7,500 Jewish-owned businesses were looted and destroyed. The shattered glass that littered the streets afterward gave the event its name: Kristallnacht, the Night of Broken Glass. [cite:wiki_kristallnacht] [cite:jmberlin_november1938]

The human toll went beyond property. More than 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to concentration camps. The historian Nikolaus Wachsmann describes this as

the first mass imprisonment of Jews in concentration camps solely because they were Jews.

At least 91 Jews were murdered during the pogrom itself. Historians note that the true toll, including deaths from mistreatment after arrest and suicides driven by the terror, reaches into the hundreds. [cite:wiki_kristallnacht] Kristallnacht was not the first anti-Jewish violence of the Nazi period; earlier, smaller-scale persecution is documented. What made November 1938 a turning point was mass imprisonment of Jews as Jews, carried out on this scale for the first time.

Then the regime presented the bill. It held the German-Jewish community collectively responsible for the destruction and imposed a fine of $400 million at 1938 rates, extracted from the same community whose property had just been destroyed. [cite:history_kristallnacht] The sources used here record the fine's existence and size, but not how it was collected or what it did to individual families.

By the end of 1938, Jews were barred from schools and most public places in Germany. Abroad there was at least one concrete response: on November 15, 1938, President Franklin Roosevelt recalled the United States ambassador to Germany in protest. Before November 1938, Nazi persecution had worked mainly through legal exclusion. After it, the regime moved toward escalating, open, state-tolerated violence, a path that would lead to the bureaucratic planning recorded at [scene:hol_s04|Wannsee] in January 1942.
