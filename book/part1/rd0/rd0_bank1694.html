1694: The Bank, the War and the Debt

This volume's clock starts in 1694 because of a loan. In that year a group of private subscribers lent the English Crown £1.2 million through a newly chartered company, and the company that held the loan became the Bank of England. The arrangement outlived the war it was raised for. This section reads it as the pattern by which a state at war could borrow large sums from private lenders on a regular footing, and as the structure into which the banking houses of the following sections would lend. To see why it was built, the story has to begin twenty-two years earlier, with a default.

On 2 January 1672 the government of Charles II began what became known as the Stop of the Exchequer. It suspended repayment of Exchequer orders and warrants, the instruments through which the Crown had borrowed. The Stop was planned to last one year. It was extended to May 1673, and then to January 1674. [cite:rs_rd0_stop_exchequer] The sums caught by it were large for the period. The obligations were later estimated at £1,211,065 by the historian Horsefield, and the principal was assessed at £1,328,526 at the end of 1676; both figures come here through a reference summary. The lenders who bore the loss were the goldsmith-bankers of London, and several were ruined. Edward Backwell and Robert Vyner were among those who went bankrupt. [cite:rs_rd0_stop_exchequer]

The Bank of England's own museum draws the line from that default to its founding. Because Charles II had failed to repay the goldsmiths, they were unwilling to lend to the Crown again. When William III needed money for his war against France, he had to find another lender. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] This is the Bank's explanation of its own origins, offered by its museum, and it should be read as such. The Bank's 1970 booklet The Bank of England: History and Functions states the purpose in the same terms: the Bank was "incorporated by Act of Parliament and Charter in 1694" to help "finance the war being fought by William III against Louis XIV of France." [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970]

The scheme came from a Scottish merchant, William Paterson. He proposed that members of the public lend the government £1.2 million, a sum the Bank itself puts at about £200 million in today's money. The subscription was raised in 11 days by 1,268 subscribers. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] The Bank was formally established by Royal Charter on 27 July 1694. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970] According to a reference summary of the national debt's history, which draws on H. Roseveare's The Financial Revolution 1660–1760 (1991), the charter was granted through the Tonnage Act 1694, and Sir John Houblon became the first governor. [cite:rs_rd0_natdebt_wiki] The new institution first did business in the Hall of the Mercers' Company in Cheapside. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970]

Who should be called the Bank's founder is a matter on which the sources place their emphasis differently. The Bank's museum credits the scheme to Paterson, who proposed that the public lend to the government. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] The reference summary, citing Roseveare, credits Charles Montagu with devising the Bank's establishment, and notes that Paterson's plan had been put forward about three years earlier without being acted on. [cite:rs_rd0_natdebt_wiki] The two accounts are compatible rather than opposed: Paterson proposed, and Montagu carried the proposal through. A reader who meets either name presented as the sole founder is meeting half of the record.

The mechanism was simple, and its simplicity was its strength. The subscribers did not lend to the King as private individuals, as the goldsmiths had. They lent through a joint-stock company, "The Governor and the Company of the Bank of England," and became its shareholders. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] The government paid them 8% interest on the loan. The Bank's museum sets that rate against what the goldsmiths had typically charged the Crown: 20 to 30%. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_museum] According to the reference summary, the government also paid a management charge of £4,000 a year on the loan. [cite:rs_rd0_natdebt_wiki] The lender's risk had been changed in kind. Instead of individual goldsmiths exposed to a royal default, as in 1672, the creditor was now a chartered corporation established by Act of Parliament and Charter, whose shares were held by its 1,268 subscribers. That changed who could lend to the state, and on what terms.

Who gained, and who bore the cost, can be stated only as far as these sources go. The Crown gained what it had lost in 1672: access to credit for war, at well under half the rates the goldsmiths had typically charged. The subscribers gained a steady 8% on their money and a share in a chartered company. The cost fell on the government's finances, which owed the interest and the management charge year after year. The sources used here do not say which revenues paid that interest or who, among the King's subjects, ultimately carried it, and this section does not guess.

What the Bank became is better documented than its founders' intentions. The 1970 booklet describes it as "banker to the Government" and as manager of "the Government Stocks which form the bulk of the National Debt." [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970] It dates two later turning points: the Bank Act of 1844, which centralized the issue of banknotes in the Bank, and the Bank of England Act of 1946, which brought the Bank into public ownership. [cite:rs_rd0_boe_hf1970] Until 1946, then, the institution through which the British state borrowed and managed its debt was a privately owned company.

The debt that the Bank managed grew with each war. At the end of the Napoleonic Wars, British government debt reached a peak of about £1 billion, more than 200% of GDP. That figure is given by the reference summary, which takes it from the website ukpublicspending.co.uk, and it should be read as an estimate. [cite:rs_rd0_natdebt_wiki] The scale is the point. A loan of £1.2 million in 1694 had become, a little over a century later, part of a debt of about £1 billion, larger than the whole of a year's national output twice over.

That growth is what ties 1694 to the rest of this volume. A state that borrowed this way needed lenders who could raise large sums quickly, across borders, in wartime. The Napoleonic Wars, which pushed the debt to its peak, were also the years in which one Frankfurt family placed its sons in five capitals and became the most reliable creditor to governments that kept falling, as [scene:s01|the next section] shows. The London house that family founded would later lend to the British state directly, as [scene:bc_s02|the section on N M Rothschild & Sons] sets out. Neither house invented the system. Both lent into one that the Bank of England had organized more than a century before. The privileges of [scene:bc_s01|the City Corporation] and the borrowing machinery of the Bank are separate institutions in the sources used here, and this book keeps them separate.

Several things remain unsettled. The Stop's figures, the £4,000 management charge, the role of Montagu and the £1 billion peak all reach this book through reference summaries rather than the underlying scholarship, and they are given here as those summaries give them. The Bank's account of why it was founded is the Bank's own. The sources do not identify the 1,268 subscribers, so nothing here can say who they were, what trades they followed or how widely the shares were held after 1694. What is established is the sequence: a royal default in 1672, a war with France, a loan of £1.2 million at 8% through a chartered company in 1694, and a national debt of about £1 billion by 1815, managed by that company for the state.
