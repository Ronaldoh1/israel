Truman in His Own Words, 1943–1948

If no document settles which argument decided the President, his own words are the nearest evidence there is. Harry Truman is often remembered as a lifelong and uncomplicated champion of a Jewish state. The record of what he himself said and did between 1943 and May 1948, before any campaign money of the 1948 election entered the story, does not support so simple a picture, and it does not support the opposite picture either. It shows a public conviction, a frank awareness of voters, and a private irritation at the pressure he was under, all in the same man and in the same years. This section sets out those words in order, and identifies each for what it is: a public speech, an official record, a single witness's later account, a private diary.

The first item comes from Truman's years in the Senate. In April 1943, before he was Vice President and long before any decision about Palestine was his to make, Senator Truman spoke at a mass rally in Chicago Stadium urging help for Europe's Jews. According to David McCullough's biography, he said that under "mad Hitler" they were being "herded like animals" into camps. [cite:csm_truman_diary_2003] Whatever weight one gives later political motives, this was said by a senator with no Palestine policy of his own to defend, and it is the earliest documented evidence for the humanitarian side of his outlook.

The second item is the most often quoted, and it needs the most careful handling. On 10 November 1945, seven months into his presidency, Truman met his chiefs of mission from the Arab world. The meeting itself is in the State Department's official record. [cite:frus_1945_chiefs_mission] The words most often attributed to him there are not. They come from William Eddy, the American minister to Saudi Arabia, who attended and who wrote, in a pamphlet published in 1954, that Truman had told the diplomats: "I'm sorry, gentlemen, but I have to answer to hundreds of thousands who are anxious for the success of Zionism; I do not have hundreds of thousands of Arabs among my constituents." The historian John Judis has quoted Eddy's account. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_globe] Three cautions apply to it, and all three should be kept in view. It rests on a single witness. It was written down nine years after the meeting. And Eddy was himself a State Department opponent of Zionism, so his account comes from a witness with a settled view of the policy under discussion. [cite:mondoweiss_judis_globe] None of that proves the quotation false; Eddy was in the room. It does mean that the remark, however vivid, cannot carry the weight of an official record, and a careful reader should treat it as one participant's recollection of what was said.

The third item is public and dated. On 4 October 1946, the eve of Yom Kippur and a month before the midterm elections, Truman issued a statement urging immediate large-scale immigration to Palestine and saying the United States could support a viable Jewish state "in an adequate area of Palestine." [cite:avalon_yomkippur_1946] The statement, the advisers around it and the argument over its timing are the subject of [scene:wh_s01|inside the White House]; here it marks the point at which a president's stated sympathy and an election calendar first visibly coincided.

The fourth item was never meant to be read by anyone else. On 21 July 1947, after Henry Morgenthau telephoned him about the refugees aboard the ship Exodus, Truman wrote in his private diary: "The Jews, I find are very, very selfish." He added that once underdogs gain power, "neither Hitler nor Stalin has anything on them for cruelty." [cite:jvl_truman_diary_1947] [cite:aljazeera_truman_diary] The diary was found at the Truman Library and released by the National Archives in 2003. [cite:cbs_truman_diary] [cite:csm_truman_diary_2003] The entry should be named for what it is. It is a sweeping judgment about Jews as a people, written by a sitting president in anger at a particular telephone call, and it is antisemitic in the plain sense of the word. It is also private, written by the same man who had spoken at the Chicago rally four years earlier and who would recognize Israel ten months later. The entry is evidence of how the pressure felt to him; it is not, by itself, evidence of what his policy would be, and ten months later he recognized the Jewish state.

The fifth group of items brings the record to the decision itself. In November 1947 a memo to Truman titled "The Politics of 1948 and the Jews" analyzed the Jewish vote for the coming election. [cite:jvl_truman_index] In March 1948, after refusing to see Zionist leaders, he met Chaim Weizmann at the urging of his old business partner Eddie Jacobson. [cite:jvl_dejure_1949] [cite:jvl_truman_index] On 14 May 1948 he granted de facto recognition to Israel eleven minutes after its proclamation, against the opposition of Secretary of State George Marshall. [cite:jvl_dejure_1949] [cite:jvl_truman_index] Four days later, on 18 May 1948, he described American Jews as "very emotional." The people around those decisions, Clifford, Marshall and Jacobson among them, are introduced in [scene:s08|the preceding section].

Read together, these items resist every single-motive story. Those who see Truman as a man of principle can point to the 1943 rally, given years before any Palestine decision was his, and to a recognition decision he took against the most respected member of his own cabinet. Those who see electoral calculation can point to Eddy's account, to the Yom Kippur timing and to the November 1947 memo on the Jewish vote. Those who see reluctance can point to the diary, to his refusal to see Zionist leaders until a friend intervened, and to the "very emotional" remark days after recognition. Each reading is built from real documents. None of them accounts for all of the documents.

The fairest summary of the record is that humanitarian conviction, open awareness of voters and private resentment of the pressure all appear in it, in the same man and in the same years, before any 1948 campaign money had changed hands. The record shows that all three were present. It does not show which of them decided the outcome, and this book does not claim to know. The remark attributed to him in November 1945, which would bear most directly on that question, is the one item that rests on a single witness who opposed the policy it describes.
