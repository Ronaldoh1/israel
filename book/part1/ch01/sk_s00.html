How This Book Weighs Evidence

A reader new to this history has every right to suspect that any account of it is opinion dressed up as history. The suspicion is reasonable. The only honest answer is to state the method in advance, so that each claim can be checked against it. Here is that method: what counts as evidence in this book, how the sources were chosen, who produced the work, and how errors are handled.

Every claim rests on one of four levels of evidence, and the prose signals which one is in play. A documented claim rests on a primary record: a law, a court ruling, an official filing, a census, a signed document. A reported claim rests on a named news outlet or a named investigator. A contested claim is one on which credible sources disagree; there, both positions are set out and the author leaves the dispute open rather than settling it. Some questions are stated as unresolved because the record itself does not settle them. How many of Gaza's dead were combatants, a question taken up in later chapters, is one. Where a passage offers a symbolic or interpretive reading rather than a documentary finding, it is labeled as interpretation.

The sources follow one simple principle: each side's own official records are used. Israeli deaths come from Israel's National Insurance Institute and Defense Ministry figures. Palestinian deaths come from the Gaza Health Ministry, United Nations agencies and peer-reviewed studies. Court records and archives come from Israeli, Palestinian, American and international sources alike. Advocacy organizations on every side, among them UN Watch, IMPACT-se, Track AIPAC and the Washington Report, are named as advocacy groups whenever they are cited, so the reader can weigh them accordingly.

This book is the work of Observe The System, an independent research project. No government, party, lobby or donor funds it or reviews it. When a source proves wrong, or has been misread, the claim is corrected and the change is noted.

Every claim carries a citation, and the reader is invited to follow it. Anyone who disagrees with a reading of a document can go to the document itself. That is the point of the apparatus: not to ask for trust, but to make trust unnecessary.

Why might the record differ from what readers learned? Most people meet this history through textbooks, speeches and news headlines. Those are summaries, and summaries leave things out. This book sets the underlying documents beside the summaries so the two can be compared.

The same rules run in every direction. A claim that favors one side gets the same scrutiny as a claim that does not, and the standard applies to this book's own conclusions as much as to anyone else's. Where the evidence is strong, the prose says so. Where it is thin, disputed or absent, the prose says that too.
