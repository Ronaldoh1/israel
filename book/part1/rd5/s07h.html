The Jewish Agency's Campaign at the United Nations

American recognition was one track toward statehood. Admission to the community of nations through the United Nations was another, and the two were separate. The Jewish Agency worked both, and its work on the second began well before the United Nations had any committee on Palestine at all.

The usual account of the partition plan holds that the United Nations arrived at it independently, through the neutral fact-finding of its own eleven-member committee. The documents complicate that picture. In August 1946, more than a year before that committee reported, the Jewish Agency submitted its own detailed partition proposal. It drew boundaries for a Palestinian rump state that bear a notable resemblance to the territory that later became the West Bank. [cite:jewishagency_1946plan] This was formal, prepared diplomatic work, put on paper well in advance of any international process that might adopt something like it.

The proposal was not a one-off tactical move. Historical accounts of the United Nations process note that by 1946 partition had become the Jewish Agency's explicitly avowed policy. [cite:unscop_testimony] In other words, the Agency did not adopt partition in reaction to an initiative from New York. The position was public and settled before the formal process started, and the Agency entered that process already years into a coordinated and prepared diplomatic campaign rather than responding to a fresh proposal from outside. [cite:unscop_testimony]

Two limits on this argument should be stated plainly. First, resemblance is not adoption. The sources document that the 1946 boundaries resemble a later territorial outcome; they do not show that the United Nations committee copied its lines from the Agency's map. Second, a settled commitment to partition did not mean that every detail of the Agency's eventual presentation at the United Nations matched the 1946 document. What was fixed by 1946 was the principle, not every line on the map.

Even within those limits, the timing matters. When the United Nations committee began its work in 1947, one party to the dispute arrived with a worked-out plan and a stated policy, while the question of who else would present a case, and how, remained open. That asymmetry shaped the hearings that followed, a story taken up in [scene:s07i|UNSCOP and the empty chair]. It also placed the diplomatic groundwork for what became Resolution 181 inside the Jewish Agency's own planning, alongside the procurement network in the Americas and the political channels in Washington described in earlier sections.

The same summer that the Agency set out its proposal, however, its relationship with the governing power in Palestine reached a breaking point. Weeks before the August 1946 submission, Britain had raided the Agency's own offices and arrested its senior officials, and the consequences of that raid would soon dominate events on the ground.
