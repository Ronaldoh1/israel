The Power Ledger

Iran is usually described as Israel's most dangerous enemy. A ledger of what each state has shows a less even contest than that description suggests. It also shows where Iran's real strength lies. The figures below come from the World Bank, the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) and the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS). Where they are estimates, or where sources differ, the text says so.

People and money

Iran has about nine times as many people as Israel: about 92 million against about 10.1 million, by the World Bank's figures for 2025. [cite:ir13_fred_irpc] [cite:ir13_fred_ilpc] [cite:hm_fred_ilgdp] Israel's economy is nonetheless the larger of the two. In 2025 Israel's GDP was $610.8 billion and Iran's $362.7 billion, down from $475.3 billion in 2024; the fall reflects the collapse of the rial as well as a real contraction the World Bank estimates at 2.7 per cent. [cite:hm_fred_ilgdp] [cite:ir13_fred_irgdp] [cite:ir13_wb_iran] Per head the gap is wider still: about $60,300 in Israel against about $3,900 in Iran, roughly fifteen to one. [cite:ir13_fred_ilpc] [cite:ir13_fred_irpc]

The military budgets differ even more. SIPRI puts Israel's 2025 military spending at $48.3 billion, 7.8 per cent of GDP and the eleventh-largest in the world. Iran's is $7.4 billion, thirty-eighth. [cite:ir13_sipri_2025] [cite:hz9_sipri_2025] Iran's figure fell 5.6 per cent in real terms in 2025, mostly because of inflation of about 42 per cent, though allocations to its missile and drone makers rose by 44 and 50 per cent. SIPRI warns that Iran's budget "probably represent[s] only a relatively small proportion" of its procurement; one analyst put off-budget spending at about 40 per cent of the total. [cite:ir13_na_sipri] Even allowing for that, Israel spends roughly four times what Iran does, and on SIPRI's published figures more than six times. The $3.8 billion in American military aid Israel received in 2025 alone was equal to about half of Iran's entire budgeted military spending. [cite:ir13_na_sipri] [cite:hm_dn_mou] Saudi Arabia, Iran's Gulf rival, spent $83.2 billion. [cite:ir13_sipri_2025]

Forces and air power

Iran has more soldiers. The IISS counts about 610,000 active personnel, including about 190,000 in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, against the Israel Defense Forces' 169,500 active troops and 465,000 reservists. [cite:ir13_nst_irmil] [cite:hm_almon_idf] Numbers of soldiers count for little, though, between two states that share no border; what decides a war between them is air power and missiles. Iran has about 250 combat-capable aircraft, many bought before 1979, among them American-built F-4s and F-5s and a few F-14s. [cite:ir13_nst_irmil] Israel has 316 combat aircraft. Since 2010 it has bought the American F-35, of which it had ordered 50 by 2016, paid for with American aid and fitted with Israeli electronics. [cite:hm_almon_idf] [cite:ow7_wiki_f35i] In June 2025, and again from 28 February 2026, when some 200 Israeli jets struck more than 500 targets on the first day, Israeli aircraft operated over Iran. [cite:hz8_am_khamenei] Part Ten tells the first of those wars. [scene:ir10_strikes|Part Ten: the strikes]

Missiles: Iran's main weapon

Iran's answer to that imbalance has been ballistic missiles and drones, and the estimates of how many it has are contested. Before June 2025, unnamed estimates put its stock at 2,500 to 3,000 ballistic missiles. [cite:ir13_nst_irmil] In the twelve days of June 2025 it fired about 525 to 550 ballistic missiles and more than 1,000 drones at Israel. By Israeli counts about 86 to 90 per cent of the missiles were intercepted, 28 people were killed in Israel and the Health Ministry counted 3,238 taken to hospital. [cite:ir13_alma_rl] [cite:ir10_toi_numbers] At the start of the 2026 war the Israeli military estimated that Iran had about 2,500 missiles able to reach Israel. In the forty days to 8 April it fired about 650 ballistic missiles and thousands of drones at Israel, and about 6,400 missiles and drones at the Gulf states. [cite:ir13_fdd_missiles] [cite:ir11_airwars] What survived is disputed. An Israeli Air Force official said in April 2026 that just over 1,000 missiles remained. A classified US assessment reported by the New York Times on 12 May put the surviving share at roughly 70 per cent, apparently counting cruise missiles too. The head of US Central Command, Admiral Brad Cooper, called such reports "not accurate" and said more than 85 per cent of Iran's missile, drone and naval industrial base had been damaged or destroyed. [cite:ir13_fdd_missiles] Iran has refused to put its missiles on the negotiating table; in February 2026 it would discuss only its nuclear programme. [cite:ir12_euronews_feb26]

The bomb

On the nuclear line of the ledger the count is not close. Iran has no nuclear weapon. Its stock of uranium enriched to 60 per cent stood at an estimated 440.9 kilograms on 13 June 2025, by the IAEA's last estimate, and by June 2026 the IAEA said it could "not provide any information on the current size, composition or whereabouts" of it. [cite:ir10_isis_jun26] [cite:ir11_iaea_sep7] Israel is estimated by SIPRI to hold about 90 warheads, deliverable by aircraft, by Jericho missiles and possibly by submarine; it neither confirms nor denies them. [cite:ir4_ynet_sipri] [scene:ir4_dimona|Part Four: Dimona]

The American column

The largest entry on Israel's side of the ledger is American. Under the 2016 memorandum of understanding the United States provides $38 billion over 2019–2028, $3.3 billion a year in military financing and $500 million for missile defence. [cite:hm_dn_mou] From October 2023 to September 2025 it gave at least $21.7 billion in military aid, and its own operations in the region cost a further $9.65 to $12.07 billion. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] In June 2025 American THAAD batteries fired about 150 interceptors in Israel's defence, about a quarter of the stock. [cite:hz12_cow_2025] Between 40,000 and 50,000 American troops were based in the region in mid-2025, at least 19 sites including Al Udeid in Qatar and the Fifth Fleet's base in Bahrain. By the end of March 2026 there were more than 50,000. [cite:ir13_aje_usbases] [cite:ir13_nyt_50k] Central Command put the cost of the 2026 war to the United States at $43.6 billion by 3 September 2026. That is nearly six times Iran's budgeted military spending for 2025. [cite:ow7_ap_cost2026] [cite:ir13_sipri_2025] Part Fourteen examines that bill. [scene:ir14_bill|Part Fourteen: the American bill]

On every conventional measure except headcount, the ledger favours Israel, and with the United States beside it the gap is very wide. Iran's power lies in other columns: missiles numerous enough to saturate defences for a time, armed allies across the region documented in Part Nine, and a coastline on the strait that carries a fifth of the world's oil. [scene:ir9_reach|Part Nine: Iran's reach] [cite:ir13_eia_hormuz] Israeli governments say the ledger understates the danger, because a single nuclear weapon would make the conventional balance meaningless; that is the reason they give for the Begin doctrine. [cite:ir13_pbs_bibi2026] Iran's government says it does not seek one. [cite:hz11_ap_gabbard] Whether the gap in the ledger made Iran a threat to be removed, or a weaker state that Israel and the United States could strike at an acceptable cost, is an argument the figures cannot settle.
