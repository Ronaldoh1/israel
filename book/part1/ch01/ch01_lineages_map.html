Where the Lineages Lived: A Map of Jewish Communities

The previous section followed three broad Jewish lineages, Ashkenazi, Sephardi and Mizrahi, through the genetic record. This section puts them on the map, together with the smaller communities that fit none of the three. It shows where each community lived, the language it spoke, the event that changed it most, and where most of its descendants live now. The picture that results is not one diaspora moving in one direction, but some two dozen communities, each with its own documented history, spread across three continents for more than two thousand years.

The names need a word first. Ashkenaz is a name in Genesis, and medieval rabbis used it for Germany; Sepharad appears in the book of Obadiah, and by the ninth century it meant the Iberian Peninsula. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] [cite:jl_wiki_sepharad] Both words also name prayer rites, so a Jew of Iraqi or Moroccan family may be counted as Sephardi in synagogue. "Mizrahi," meaning "Eastern," is a modern umbrella term for Jews from Arab and Muslim lands. Sources date it to the 1940s or the 1950s; writers including Ella Shohat have criticised it as a construction, many people prefer to name their own community, and grouping North African Jews, historically called "Westerners," under "Eastern" is itself contested. [cite:jl_mjl_mizrahim] [cite:jl_wiki_mizrahi] This book uses the terms because the sources do, with those caveats.

Ashkenazi. By the tenth century there were centres of rabbinic learning at Mainz and Worms on the Rhine, and the name Ashkenaz came to mean the Jews of that region. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] After the Crusades of the eleventh to thirteenth centuries they moved east into Slavic lands. [cite:jl_brit_ashkenazi] Royal charters in Poland, from 1264 and 1334, opened the way to real settlement in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries; by the early sixteenth century the centre of Ashkenazi life had shifted to Poland, Lithuania, Bohemia and Moravia, and a tax inquiry of 1764 found that about seven percent of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth was Jewish. [cite:jl_ego_ashkenazi] [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] Their common language was Yiddish. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] Estimates of their share of world Jewry vary: the Jewish Virtual Library puts it at 90 percent before the Second World War, and Britannica at more than 80 percent today. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim] [cite:jl_brit_ashkenazi] According to the Jewish Virtual Library, the United States later became the main Ashkenazi centre. [cite:jl_jvl_ashkenazim]

Sephardi. The Jewish Virtual Library dates a Jewish "Golden Age" in Spain from the Muslim conquest of 711. In 1492 Spain gave Jews who refused baptism four months to leave; about 100,000 went, by that source's estimate, and in 1497 Portugal forced almost all of its Jews to convert. [cite:jl_jvl_sephardim] Large numbers of exiles settled across the Ottoman Empire and in North Africa, and Sephardi communities also formed in Venice, Livorno, Bordeaux, Hamburg and London. [cite:jl_jvl_sephardim] Salonica, today's Thessaloniki, became their great city, with about 35,000 Jews in the sixteenth century and about 80,000 around 1900. [cite:jl_uw_salonica] In 1943 the Germans deported more than 45,000 of its Jews to Auschwitz-Birkenau. [cite:jl_ushmm_salonika] Their language, Judeo-Spanish, also called Judezmo or Ladino, flourished in the Ottoman lands. [cite:jl_jlp_judezmo] A second branch, the Western Sephardim, grew from Portuguese "New Christians" who returned to Judaism in Amsterdam from the late sixteenth century, and in London where a petition to Oliver Cromwell in 1656 led to open Jewish life in England for the first time since the expulsion of 1290. [cite:jl_posen_western] [cite:jl_jcruk_bevis]

Mizrahi and North African. Iraq's community traces its start to the Babylonian exile of about 586 BCE, and the Babylonian Talmud was compiled there. [cite:jl_wiki_iraq] [cite:jl_jvl_ezra] By 1900 Baghdad's roughly 50,000 Jews were more than a quarter of the city. [cite:jl_wiki_iraq] After the Farhud riots of 1941 and an Iraqi law of March 1950 that let Jews leave if they gave up their citizenship, Operation Ezra and Nehemiah flew more than 120,000 of them to Israel. [cite:jl_wiki_iraq] [cite:jl_jvl_ezra] Iran's community traces its roots to the sixth century BCE and wrote in Judeo-Persian; about 80,000 Jews lived there in 1978 by unofficial statistics, and a community of several thousand remains, with a reserved seat in parliament. [cite:jl_jvl_iran] [cite:jl_jlp_judeo_persian] Jews are recorded in Yemen from the third century CE; Operation Magic Carpet airlifted about 49,000 Jews, most of them from Yemen, in 1949 and 1950. [cite:jl_jvl_yemen] [cite:jl_cojs_magic_carpet] Syria's community, in Aleppo and Damascus, dates its presence to the Seleucid period; after the government allowed emigration in 1992, thousands left, mainly for Brooklyn and New Jersey; others settled in Israel and Mexico. [cite:jl_jvl_syria] In Egypt, Jews probably settled in Old Cairo soon after its founding in 641, and the famous Cairo Genizah was found in its Ben Ezra Synagogue; the community numbered about 75,000 at its peak; about 25,000 were expelled or forced out after the Suez war of 1956, and nearly all had left by the early 1970s. [cite:jl_jvl_cairo] [cite:jl_jvl_egypt]

Further east and north, the Jews of Kurdistan spoke a Neo-Aramaic language; the Bukharan Jews of Central Asia spoke Bukhori, a language close to Tajik, and now live mostly in Israel and in Queens, New York; the Mountain Jews of the eastern Caucasus spoke Juhuri; and the Jews of Georgia, by a tradition not confirmed by contemporary records, arrived after 586 BCE. [cite:jl_ej_kurdistan] [cite:jl_ela_bukhori] [cite:jl_ela_juhuri] [cite:jl_jlp_judeo_georgian] In North Africa, sources distinguished the toshavim, the Jews already living there, from the megorashim, the Iberian exiles who arrived after 1391 and 1492. [cite:jl_wiki_toshavim] Morocco's community is documented from the second century CE; more than 90,000 left for Israel in Operation Yakhin between 1961 and 1964, and many others settled in France and Canada. [cite:jl_jvl_morocco] France made Algeria's Jews French citizens in 1870, and almost 130,000 moved to France after Algerian independence in 1962. [cite:jl_jvl_algeria] Tunisia had about 105,000 Jews in 1946, including the old community of the island of Djerba. [cite:jl_jvl_djerba] How these communities left in the twentieth century is told in [scene:s00e2|The Exodus of 1941–1972].

Other communities. Several communities belong to none of the three families. The Romaniotes of Greece, in Ioannina, Arta and Chalkida, are documented from the Byzantine period and spoke Yevanic, a Jewish Greek; the language declined as Romaniotes merged into the Sephardi communities of Greece, and the Holocaust nearly destroyed it. [cite:jl_wiki_yevanic] Rome's community, which the Jewish Virtual Library dates to 161 BCE and calls the oldest in Europe, kept its own Italian rite; it was confined to a ghetto by papal order in 1555, and on 16 October 1943 the Nazis rounded up Jews in Rome and deported them to Auschwitz; by the Jewish Virtual Library's count, only a dozen of those deported survived. [cite:jl_jvl_rome] [cite:jl_jlp_judeo_italian] The Karaites, a movement that accepts the Hebrew Bible but not the Talmud as binding, grew from the followers of Anan ben David in Babylonia about 760 CE, with later centres in Cairo, Crimea and Lithuania. [cite:jl_nwe_karaites] [cite:jl_je_anan]

The Beta Israel of Ethiopia prayed in Ge'ez and spoke Amharic or Tigrinya. Their origins are disputed: the historian Steven Kaplan argues that they formed as a group in Ethiopia between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries, while other accounts place their arrival in the first to sixth centuries. [cite:jl_jvl_ethiopian] [cite:jl_kaplan_beta_israel] [cite:jl_mjl_ethiopian] Israel's Sephardi Chief Rabbi ruled them Jews in 1973; Operations Moses in 1984–85 and Solomon in 1991 brought about 7,700 and 14,400 of them to Israel, by one historian's count. [cite:jl_jvl_ethiopian] [cite:jl_ency_ethiopian] India had three communities: the Bene Israel of the Konkan coast, who spoke Marathi; the Cochin Jews of Kerala, documented by copper plates of about 1000 CE, who spoke Jewish Malayalam; and the Baghdadi Jews, Arabic-speaking merchants who came in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. [cite:jl_ej_bene_israel] [cite:jl_mjl_cochin] [cite:jl_mjl_baghdadi] In Kaifeng, China, a community that most scholars think came from Persia in the twelfth century kept a synagogue until the nineteenth century; between 500 and 1,000 people there are estimated to claim descent today. [cite:jl_iranica_kaifeng] [cite:jl_wiki_kaifeng]

Three further groups appear on these maps with a different status. The Abayudaya of Uganda began practising Judaism in 1919 as a religious movement, and do not claim continuous descent from ancient Israel in the sources consulted. [cite:jl_ijn_abayudaya] The Bnei Menashe of northeastern India say they descend from the tribe of Manasseh; a Chief Rabbi recognised that claim in 2005, but formal conversion was still required, and no peer-reviewed genetic study has confirmed it. [cite:jl_aljazeera_bnei_menashe] [cite:jl_eastmojo_bnei_menashe] The Lemba of South Africa and Zimbabwe have an oral tradition of origin in a place called "Sena"; early genetic studies were read as support, but a 2014 reassessment found the markers used to be unreliable predictors of Jewish ancestry. [cite:jl_ency_lemba] [cite:jl_tofanelli_2014] On the map the Bnei Menashe and the Lemba are marked as claims of descent, not settled facts, and the Abayudaya as a twentieth-century movement.

What the record shows, and what it does not. The record shows Jewish communities spread from the Atlantic to China and from the Baltic to southern Africa, each with its own documented history, language and turning points, and most of them reshaped in the twentieth century by genocide in Europe or by mass emigration from the Middle East and North Africa, much of it to Israel. It shows that the three familiar labels are partly modern conveniences: they overlap with prayer rites, and "Mizrahi" in particular is a term whose dating and fairness are debated. The record does not settle the origins of every community: the dates for the Beta Israel, the Kaifeng Jews and the Georgian Jews are disputed, and the descent claims of the Bnei Menashe and the Lemba are not established. The places on the map are approximate centres, and most population figures are estimates from the sources named. None of it decides anyone's rights, a point made in [scene:s00b2|the section on the lineages].
