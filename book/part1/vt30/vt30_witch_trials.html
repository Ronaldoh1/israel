The Witch Trials: Bull, Handbook and Courts

Between about 1450 and 1750 European courts tried roughly 100,000 people for witchcraft and executed somewhere between 40,000 and 60,000 of them, most of them women. Sophia's catalogue treats the trials as "documented judicial history rather than folklore", and that is the right register. It also names the Malleus Maleficarum as their "procedural template", and the popular version of the story goes further: that the Church burned nine million women, most of them healers and midwives, on the authority of a papal handbook. The record supports some of that and refutes the rest. A pope did authorise two inquisitors to hunt witches in 1484, and one of them wrote the most notorious witch-hunting manual of the age. But most of the killing was done by secular and territorial courts, Catholic and Protestant; the Roman and Spanish Inquisitions were, by the standards of the time, among the more sceptical tribunals in Europe; and the nine-million figure is an eighteenth-century miscalculation. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus]

What the accused were said to do

The charge combined two older ideas. One was maleficium, harm done by magic: a neighbour's cow sickened, a child died, a harvest failed. The other was the diabolical pact: the claim, developed by theologians and inquisitors in the fifteenth century, that witches had renounced their baptism, worshipped the devil and met at night in sabbaths. Earlier church law had been sceptical of the second idea. The tenth-century Canon Episcopi treated night flight as an illusion rather than a real event, the Paris theology faculty's decree of 1398 still appeared to support it, and neither a majority of bishops nor the Council of Trent ever repudiated it; the Malleus opened by arguing against it. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] The descriptions of sabbaths that fill the trial records came from confessions taken by courts that, following manuals like the Malleus, used torture and leading questions. [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus]

The bull and the handbook

On 5 December 1484 Innocent VIII issued Summis desiderantes affectibus. It named two Dominican inquisitors, Henricus Institoris (Heinrich Kramer) and Jacobus Sprenger, and confirmed their authority in the provinces and dioceses of Mainz, Cologne, Trier, Salzburg and Bremen, where local clergy and laity had denied that their jurisdiction reached. The bull described people who "hinder men from begetting and women from conceiving" and blighted crops and vineyards, and it empowered the inquisitors to proceed to "the correction, imprisonment, and punishment" of those accused. [cite:vt30_fordham_summis] It is the clearest papal endorsement of witch-hunting on record, and it is real.

The Malleus Maleficarum was printed at Speyer in 1486 or 1487. Its author was Kramer; Sprenger's name was added as co-author only from 1519, long after his death, and his part is disputed. Kramer printed the 1484 bull at the front, which gave readers the impression of papal approval, but the bull authorised his prosecutions and said nothing about a book not yet written. The book also carried an approbation from the Cologne theology faculty that many historians consider forged in part; two of the named theologians later denied approving it, though Christopher Mackay, its modern translator, disputes the forgery case. [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus] Kramer's own record undercut his authority. His prosecutions at Innsbruck in the Tyrol in the mid-1480s collapsed, and the local bishop dismissed him as "senile and crazy". In 1490 clergy at Cologne condemned the book, and according to the historian Wolfgang Behringer the Inquisition "denied any authority to the Malleus". [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus]

The book taught that women were more prone to the devil's temptation than men, recommended torture, leading questions and deception to obtain confessions, accepted denunciations as evidence, and held death to be the only sure remedy. It ran to about 28 editions between 1486 and 1600. It became a reference for secular courts rather than for the inquisitions, and historians differ on how much of the later persecution it caused; Diarmaid MacCulloch counts it as one factor among several. The worst prosecutions came between about 1560 and 1630, two generations after it appeared. [cite:vt30_wiki_malleus]

Who did the killing

The courts that sent most victims to the stake were those of territorial rulers, towns and kingdoms. About half of all prosecutions fell in the Holy Roman Empire, with its hundreds of small jurisdictions; Poland, the French-speaking lands and Switzerland also saw heavy trials. Protestant Scotland hunted witches hard, while the Protestant Netherlands had among the fewest trials. In south-western Germany between 1561 and 1670, 2,527 of 3,229 recorded executions took place in Catholic territories, against 702 in Protestant ones. Historians still debate what drove such regional differences: one 2017 economic study links the most intense trials to areas where Catholic and Protestant churches competed for adherents, while studies of France tie the decline of accusations to stronger central government. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials]

"Secular courts" does not always mean "not the Church". Several of the worst hunts took place in ecclesiastical principalities, where a bishop was also the territorial prince. In the prince-bishopric of Würzburg under Philipp Adolf von Ehrenberg (bishop 1623–1631), a commission using torture beyond the limits of the imperial criminal code executed an estimated 900 people across the territory between 1626 and 1631, among them between 19 and 43 priests, the bishop's own nephew and at least 49 children under twelve. Complaints by victims' relatives reached the pope and the emperor; the emperor condemned the trials in 1630, and they ended when Swedish troops occupied the city in 1631 and the bishop died. [cite:vt30_wiki_wurzburg] These were state courts, but the state was a Catholic bishop's.

The inquisitions behaved differently. The Roman Inquisition, the papacy's own central tribunal, founded in 1542, restrained Italian courts from liberal use of torture and execution; its Venetian tribunal never convicted anyone of maleficium, and in 1635 the Roman congregation admitted it had "found scarcely one trial conducted legally". Its instruction on procedure in witchcraft cases, the Instructio, warned against hasty convictions and careless executions. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] In Spain, whose Inquisition answered to the Crown rather than directly to Rome, after an auto de fe at Logroño burned six people alive and five in effigy on 1 November 1610, the inquisitor Alonso de Salazar Frías toured the Basque country in 1611 with an edict of grace. He collected nearly 2,000 confessions, 1,384 of them from children, most of which were retracted; he found no objective proof of any witchcraft and argued that judges should not sentence anyone "unless the case can be proven with external and objective evidence". In August 1614 the Council of the Inquisition dismissed the pending cases and issued stricter rules of evidence, which brought witch-burning in Spain to an end. [cite:vt30_wiki_basque] [cite:vt30_audit_spanishinq] [scene:vt28_inquisition|Part Twenty-Eight: the medieval Inquisition]

The numbers and the myths

Named estimates of executions are 40,000 (Scarre and Callow), 45,000 (Brian Levack), 40,000 to 50,000 (Ronald Hutton) and 50,000 to 60,000 (Wolfgang Behringer and Lyndal Roper). About 80 per cent of those convicted were women, most of them over forty. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] The figure of nine million comes from Gottfried Christian Voigt, a Quedlinburg lawyer who in 1784 took about thirty local witch trials, assumed the same rate for every town in Europe over many centuries, and multiplied. The historian David Luebke calls the method "a colossal error of misguided statistical extrapolation"; Behringer named it the "Nine Million Theory" and traced how it became an influential myth in twentieth-century feminist and neopagan writing. [cite:vt30_luebke_ninemillion] [cite:vt30_wiki_voigt] The real toll is large enough to need no inflation, and the inflated one erases the documented victims, whose names survive in trial records, behind a number that never described them.

The belief that the victims were mostly healers and midwives, which Sophia's Cunning Folk entry reflects when it says their suppression destroyed "a body of empirical knowledge, held mostly by women", has also been tested. David Harley's study "Historians as Demonologists: The Myth of the Midwife-Witch" (1990) found that midwives were not prominent among the accused, and a later study of Maltese inquisition records concludes it is "wrong to assume that midwives were accused en masse". The idea goes back to the Malleus itself, which portrayed midwives as stealers of infants for magic. [cite:vt30_harley_midwife] [cite:vt30_um_midwife] Village charmers and healers, the cunning folk, were sometimes among the accused, but the studies cited here do not support the claim that healers as a class were the trials' main target. The Catholic Church still forbids the practices they offered, as the next section shows, but the trials were not, on the record, a campaign against women's medicine.

The trials ended unevenly. Scotland executed its last witch, Janet Horne, in 1727; Britain's Witchcraft Act of 1735 removed witchcraft as a crime; the last capital trial in Salzburg was in 1750, Austria abolished witch-burning and torture in 1768, and Anna Göldi, executed in 1782, was formally condemned for poisoning. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials] The finding that holds against the Church is specific: a pope gave a named inquisitor the authority he asked for, that inquisitor wrote the period's most influential witch-hunting book, and Catholic prince-bishops ran some of the deadliest hunts. The finding that does not hold is the general one, that the trials were the Church's inquisition at work, because the papacy's Roman Inquisition, and the Crown-controlled Spanish Inquisition beside it, were among the first tribunals in Europe to demand evidence and stop the burning.
