The Civil Service and Measures of Corruption

A state is built not only of constitutions and courts but of the officials who carry out its decisions and of the rules that keep those officials working for the public rather than for whoever appoints them. In both countries those rules were contested between 2023 and 2026, and in both the main international measure of perceived public-sector corruption fell in 2025. [cite:us2_wiki_cpi]

The United States: cutting and reclassifying the federal workforce

The federal civil service changed faster in 2025 than in any recent year. By the account of the director of the Office of Personnel Management (OPM), Scott Kupor, about 317,000 federal employees left during 2025: some 144,000 accepted a deferred-resignation offer, about 24,000 were separated involuntarily (17,000 through reductions in force and 7,000 on probation), about 21,000 took early retirement or buyouts, and about 129,000 left through ordinary attrition. About 68,000 were hired by the end of August, mostly for priorities such as immigration and border enforcement. [cite:us2_bloomberg_317k] Government data released in March 2026 showed the civilian workforce 12 per cent smaller in January 2026 than in September 2024. The cuts fell unevenly: the Treasury lost 24 per cent of its staff and Health and Human Services 20 per cent, while the Department of Homeland Security grew by under 1 per cent. [cite:us2_reuters_workforce]

The administration also changed the terms of employment for senior career staff. In February 2026 OPM issued a final rule creating "Schedule Policy/Career", a category for career positions judged to influence policy. Holders keep merit hiring and veterans' preference but lose the standard procedures that protect career employees from removal for poor performance or misconduct; the rule forbids patronage, loyalty tests and use of the category for mass layoffs, and lets the president place positions in it by executive order. [cite:us2_opm_schedpc] It took effect on 8 March 2026. Officials estimated that as many as 50,000 employees could be covered. Kupor said it was "not about political appointments or terminations"; Max Stier of the Partnership for Public Service said it "can be used to remove expert career federal employees", and the largest federal union promised a court challenge. [cite:us2_govexec_schedf] Whether the rule is used as its text promises or as its critics fear is a question of practice that the record does not yet settle as of October 2026.

Israel: the legal advisers and political appointments

Israel's equivalent conflict ran through the government's legal advisers rather than its headcount. The attorney general heads the government's system of legal advisers, and critics argued that Gali Baharav-Miara was targeted for repeatedly telling ministers their actions were unlawful; the cabinet's attempt to dismiss her in 2025 was annulled by the High Court in December of that year. [cite:us2_toi_agfiring] [scene:us2_courts|The courts and the overhaul] Researchers at the Israel Democracy Institute wrote in February 2026 that challenges by government and political figures to the independence of the police, the attorney general's legal advisory system and the judiciary, together with an expansion of political appointments in government companies, had blurred the line between public and partisan interests. In the institute's own survey about 74 per cent of Israelis said corruption was more widespread than in the past, and about 80 per cent said the police were not doing enough to investigate it. [cite:us2_idi_cpi]

Corruption perceptions, 2012–2025

The longest comparable measure for both countries is Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI), which scores perceived public-sector corruption from 0 (highly corrupt) to 100 (very clean) by combining expert and business surveys. The current scale dates from 2012, when the method changed, so earlier scores are not comparable and are not used here. [cite:us2_axios_cpi2024] The United States scored 73 in 2012, peaked at 76 in 2015 and has fallen since, with brief small recoveries, to 69 in 2019, 65 in 2024 and 64 in 2025, its lowest score on the current scale; it has not been among the 20 cleanest-rated countries since 2017. [cite:us2_wiki_cpi] [cite:us2_axios_cpi2024] For 2025 Transparency International pointed to "actions targeting independent voices and undermining judicial independence" and to the freeze on enforcing the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act. [cite:us2_cnn_cpi2025] Israel's score has moved within a narrow band: 60 in 2012 and 2019, a high of 64 in 2016 and 2024, and 62 in 2025, when it ranked 35th of 182 against America's 29th. [cite:us2_wiki_cpi] [cite:us2_idi_cpi] The IDI authors described the 2025 drop as modest but a warning sign. [cite:us2_idi_cpi]

The index measures perception among experts and business people, not the number of corrupt acts, and a country whose institutions expose more corruption can score worse for it. Read with that limit, the series shows a convergence. In 2012 the United States stood 13 points above Israel; by 2025 the gap was two points, almost all of the change on the American side. Israel's score was stable across a decade that included the prosecution of a sitting prime minister, while America's fell across four presidencies from 2016 onward, including four-point drops in 2018 and 2024. [cite:us2_wiki_cpi] The causes the CPI's own authors and the IDI give for the latest declines are similar in kind in both countries, centring on pressure on the independence of prosecutors, police and courts, and the record supports that description for the period from 2023 to 2026 in both. [cite:us2_cnn_cpi2025] [cite:us2_idi_cpi]

Two gaps remain. This book found no Israeli series on the size and political appointment of the civil service comparable to the American figures above, so the Israeli side of this section rests on the legal-adviser dispute and the IDI's survey rather than on headcounts. And neither the CPI nor the workforce figures say anything about the people living under Israeli authority without Israeli citizenship, whose dealings with the state run largely through the military administration covered in Part Nine.
