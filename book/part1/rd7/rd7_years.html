Lost Years: The Case of Edmond de Rothschild

Removing a funder or a leader removes more than his ties. It removes years of work, and the question is whether anyone else would have done that work in those years. The map answers it year by year, from 1882 to 1948, and the clearest way to show how is the case that tests the rule hardest: Edmond de Rothschild of the Paris house.

Edmond did not arrive with a plan for a state, and he did not appear in Palestine overnight. His involvement began in 1882, months after the first colonies were founded. Rabbi Samuel Mohilever appealed to him to save Rishon LeZion, which was collapsing for want of water and farming knowledge, and Edmond agreed. Zikhron Ya'akov, Rosh Pinna and Mazkeret Batya followed in 1883, Ekron in 1884; he made his first visit, anonymously, in 1887. [cite:schama_two_rothschilds] [cite:rs_rd0_firstaliyah_wiki] [cite:rs_rd0_rh_visits] He was not working toward a state. When Herzl came to him in July 1896 he refused to back a political movement, and he stayed away from the Basel Congress; only after about 1914 did he draw close to the Zionist Organization, and in 1929 he became honorary president of the enlarged Jewish Agency. [cite:herzl_diaries_vol2_archive] [cite:rs_rd0_ej_edmond] His work was fifty-two years of building on the ground, from 1882 until his death in 1934, much of it through his own administrators and, from 1899, through the Jewish Colonization Association and then PICA. By 1934 he was reported to have invested over $60 million in Palestine, and PICA held about half a million dunams, twice the Jewish National Fund's holdings at the time. [cite:f_jta_baron]

The map places each tie's work in the years it was done. Edmond's money runs from 1882 to 1934. The Keren Hayesod's runs from 1921 to 1939, the American appeals' from 1946 to 1948, and private and immigrant capital from 1919. A tie without dates is spread over the years its actor was active, and an actor runs from its first dated tie to its last, except where its founding and end are documented. The Jewish Agency counts from 1929, when the Zionist Congress constituted it, not from earlier years its ties happen to mention; the Palestine Office from 1908; Herzl from 1895 to 1904. For each year and each kind of work the map then asks what the removed actor did that year, and whether someone from the same circle, already active, could have done it: another Rothschild for a Rothschild, another Zionist leader for a Zionist leader. A substitute covers only up to the yearly work it had actually shown by then. Money raised in 1946 cannot replace money spent in 1890, and Hovevei Zion, which handed the colonies to Edmond in 1882–83 because it could not pay for them, cannot stand in for him.

What no one covers is not lost for one year only. Work that was built stays built: a colony founded in 1883 was still there in 1948, and so was the land bought for it. The map therefore treats what no one covered as a shortfall against everything built so far, and keeps counting it each year until later work outgrows it. Every year counts from 1882, the first, and a year counts as lost in proportion to the share of the movement's work, all kinds together, that is missing: money, land and settlement, people, arms, diplomacy and organisation, each measured against its own size in 1948. The map also follows what grew from what. Farm money stands on the land it was planted on, so when Edmond goes, his colonies and PICA go with him, and so does the share of later private capital that was invested on PICA's land.

Added up, about 13 years of the movement's work are lost without him. Those years lower the chance of a founding on time in proportion to the 66 years of build-up between 1882 and 1948, and a later founding cannot come before they are made up. The reading is "possible, with great difficulty": about 70 percent on time, about 71 percent ever. Herzl, removed the same way, gives about 69 percent. On this record the founders of the movement's first ground and of its politics weigh about the same, and only Britain's Declaration and Mandate weigh more, at about 49 to 50 percent. The closest documented substitutes come from his own family: his son James, from 1924, could carry about a fifth of his money role.

How much being first should weigh is a choice the record cannot settle, and the map shows both answers. Counted the way above, all kinds of work together, the loss is about 13 years. Counted by whichever kind of work is hardest hit in each year, it is about 35 years: in the 1880s he was nearly all the money there was, and a year in which the money is gone counts as a year lost even if the settlers are there. On that count the chance without him is about 40 percent on time, and he would weigh more than Britain. The map uses the first count because the second also turns small early actors into large ones: removing Aaron Aaronsohn's NILI spy ring, the only armed group on the map in 1909–19, would then cost fifteen years.

Earlier versions of the map put the loss at about 21 years, then about 43. The first measured each actor's whole lifetime of work against every year at once, so that the Jewish Agency, dated from the wrong year, appeared able to replace Edmond's spending of 1902 with money raised in 1946. The second counted only the hardest-hit kind of work, skipped every year before the movement's money reached 5 percent of its 1948 size, which hid 1882–89, when he was nearly the whole effort, and let anyone on the same side, even Hovevei Zion, take over his role. It also left out most of the period's money: private and immigrant capital, the Keren Hayesod after 1932, the JNF's own collections and the Jewish Agency's wartime budget. Each correction applies to every node on the map, not to Edmond alone. Some readings rose with it and others fell; every reading in this appendix uses the corrected rules of 27 November 2026.
