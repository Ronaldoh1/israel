Deir Yassin, the Hadassah Convoy and Tantura

In April and May 1948, in a village west of Jerusalem, on the road to Mount Scopus and on the coast, civilians were killed in numbers that still shape memory of that year. Two opposite simplifications have grown up around these killings. One holds that no massacres happened. The other holds that only one side suffered them. The documents support neither. They also show how such a record is made: much of what is now known surfaced over decades, and a good deal of it came from Israeli historians, students and veterans themselves.

Deir Yassin was a village west of Jerusalem. On 9 April 1948 it was attacked by the Irgun and Lehi, two underground Jewish militias, supported by the Haganah, the pre-state Jewish armed force, and the Palmach. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] [cite:aljazeera_deir_yassin_75] For decades the death toll was given as 254. Current scholarship puts it at about 110, generally within a range of 107 to 120. Many of the dead were women, children and elderly people. A Red Cross representative reported seeing some 150 bodies in a cave. The historian Benny Morris wrote that the killings were followed by mass Arab flight. Material on Deir Yassin in Israeli military archives remains classified, so the record is still incomplete. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The downward revision of the toll is itself part of the history: the larger figure was cited for decades before scholarship narrowed it.

Four days later came a killing on the other side. On 13 April, Arab forces attacked a convoy bound for Hadassah Hospital on Mount Scopus. They killed 78 Jews, most of them doctors and nurses. The attack was a reprisal for Deir Yassin. [cite:wiki_deir_yassin] The two events are bound together in time and in cause. An honest account cannot tell one without the other.

Tantura shows how slowly some parts of the record came to light. The Alexandroni Brigade captured the coastal village on 22 and 23 May 1948. A master's thesis by Theodore Katz recorded testimony of killings after the village had surrendered. Veterans sued him for libel, and he retracted. [cite:npr_tantura] [cite:jewishindependent_tantura] The matter did not end there. In the 2022 Israeli documentary Tantura, veterans in their nineties described killings and a mass grave near what is today the popular Dor Beach. A document shown in the film acknowledges that soldiers dug a mass grave. Other veterans deny that any massacre took place, and the number killed cannot be established. [cite:npr_tantura] [cite:jewishindependent_tantura] What stands on the record is testimony from participants, a document about a grave and continuing denials. There is no firm count.

Taken together, the three episodes defeat both simplifications. Massacres did happen in 1948. In the same weeks they were committed against Arab villagers and against Jews. The Deir Yassin killings came amid the two-sided civil war that preceded the invasion by the Arab states ([scene:s09b|the two escalations]), and in Morris's account they were followed by mass Arab flight.

How this evidence emerged deserves notice. Scholars corrected a death toll. A student's thesis was retracted under legal pressure, then echoed years later by veterans speaking on camera. Archives remain partly closed. The record of 1948 is still being written, and much of it has been written by Israelis examining their own history.
