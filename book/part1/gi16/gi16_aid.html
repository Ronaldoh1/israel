The Aid: What $3.8 Billion a Year Is

The figure most often quoted in arguments about Israel and the United States is $3.8 billion a year. It comes from a single document. On 14 September 2016 the two governments signed a memorandum of understanding (MOU) covering the American fiscal years 2019 to 2028: $38 billion in all, made up of $33 billion in Foreign Military Financing (FMF) grants and $5 billion for missile defence, or $3.3 billion and $500 million a year. Both sides undertook "not to seek changes to the FMF levels for the duration of this understanding", and the agreement runs to 30 September 2028. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:hm_dn_mou] It was the third such agreement: the 1999 MOU had promised $21.3 billion in military aid over ten years and the 2007 MOU $30 billion. [cite:ow_crs_aid2025] Counting every kind of aid since 1946, the Congressional Research Service (CRS) puts the total at about $174 billion in current dollars, or about $298 billion in 2024 dollars. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025]

How the money moves

Three features of the arrangement matter for what follows. First, the money arrives early. Since 1991 Congress has required Israel's FMF to be paid as a lump sum within the first month of the fiscal year, into an interest-bearing account at the Federal Reserve Bank. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Second, most of it has always had to be spent on American-made weapons. Israel was long allowed to spend part of its grant at home, a practice called offshore procurement: up to 26.3 per cent under the 2007 MOU. The 2016 agreement phases this out, slowly until 2024 and then more sharply, "ending entirely in FY2028". [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Researchers at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies (INSS) concluded in February 2026 that the aid is therefore now channelled "almost exclusively" to procurement in the United States. [cite:gi16_inss_aid] Third, the missile-defence money pays for systems built partly in Israel and partly in America, among them Iron Dome, David's Sling and Arrow; The One-Way Mirror follows that pipeline. [scene:ow8_pipeline|The arms pipeline]

Since October 2023

The war that began on 7 October 2023 added emergency money on top of the MOU. On 24 October 2023 the United States transferred two of its own Iron Dome batteries to Israel. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:ow7_twz_irondome] The Israel Security Supplemental Appropriations Act, signed in April 2024, totalled $26.38 billion. Its lines for Israel included $4 billion for Iron Dome and David's Sling, $1.2 billion for the Iron Beam laser, $3.5 billion in extra FMF and $1 billion for artillery and munitions production; another $4.4 billion was to replace American stocks sent to Israel, and $2.4 billion paid for American operations in the region. [cite:hz12b_hac_onepager] [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] Arms sales followed. In August 2024 sales worth more than $20 billion were notified to Congress, including F-15IA aircraft at $18.82 billion. In January 2025 President Trump released a hold his predecessor had placed on 1,800 MK-84 bombs, and on 28 February 2025 Secretary of State Marco Rubio used an emergency declaration to approve nearly $4 billion in sales, saying it "reverse[d] the Biden Administration's partial arms embargo". By May 2025 Israel's Defence Ministry reported more than 90,000 tons of armaments delivered on 800 aircraft and 140 ships. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025]

The fullest independent count is by William Hartung for Brown University's Costs of War project: at least $21.7 billion in American military aid to Israel from October 2023 to September 2025, $17.9 billion in the first year and $3.8 billion in the second, not counting arms sales committed for later years. [cite:gz_cow_aid] [cite:hm_an_costsofwar] The total includes the MOU's own payments for those years, so it is not all additional money. [cite:hm_cow_hartung] The Gaza volume sets this against the cost of rebuilding Gaza. [scene:gz8_bill|Gaza, Documented: the bill]

A shrinking share

What the aid amounts to depends on what it is measured against, and on that the record shows a clear trend. In 2011 estimates by the analyst Gazit, cited by Doug Bandow of the Cato Institute, put American aid at 1.5 per cent of Israel's GDP and 24 per cent of its security spending. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] By 2025–26 the CRS put FMF at "approximately 14%" of Israel's defence budget, and INSS at "roughly 15%" of the budget and "about half a percent" of GDP. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] Israel's own spending meanwhile rose steeply. The Bank of Israel records defence spending at 4.2 per cent of GDP in 2022, 8.4 per cent in 2024 and 7.8 per cent in 2025. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] The 2026 budget, passed by the Knesset 62–55 on 30 March 2026, allotted about $44.8 billion to defence, about $9.5 billion more than the year before. [cite:gi16_bd_budget2026] Set against that figure, the $3.8 billion is about 8.5 per cent; that is this book's arithmetic, and the gap from the CRS and INSS figures reflects different years and budget definitions.

Testing the fungibility argument

A common argument holds that American aid, whatever it is spent on, frees Israeli money for other uses, among them military operations and settlements. It rests on the economist's point that money is fungible: a shekel Israel need not spend on an American jet is a shekel it can spend elsewhere. Bandow made the general case in 2012, applying it to aid to Egypt and Jordan. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] An article in The National Interest in June 2016 applied it to settlements directly. It argued that offshore procurement, then $815 million or just over 26 per cent of the package, let Israel "divert funds from its defense-technology accounts" to settlement projects it estimated at no less than $250 million a year, a figure it called uncertain. [cite:gi16_ni_osp] The United States government once acted on the same logic itself. Loan guarantees given to Israel in 1992 and 2003 could be cut by an amount equal to Israel's settlement spending in the territories, and were: by a net $773.8 million in 1994–97, and by $289.5 million in 2003 and $795.8 million in 2005, according to CRS data. [cite:gi16_jvl_loans] The settlement budgets themselves are documented in The West Bank, Documented. [scene:wb12_settlement|The settlement budget]

The counter-arguments rest on numbers too. The offshore channel that the 2016 article targeted closes entirely in 2028, after which every FMF dollar must buy American equipment. [cite:gi16_crs_aid2025] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] The aid's share of Israel's defence spending fell from about a quarter to about a seventh. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] [cite:gi16_inss_aid] Against the war, the sums are small. The Bank of Israel puts the gross budgetary cost of the war at about NIS 352 billion for 2023–2026, and the special American grant in its accounts at NIS 25 billion for 2023–2025, about 7 per cent of that cost by this book's arithmetic. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] Gazit argued that the requirement to buy in America itself cost Israel about $600 million a year, "a fifth of the nominal" aid, a view the United States government has not endorsed. [cite:gi16_cato_bandow] And Israel's own prime minister now proposes ending the grants. In a CNBC interview in June 2026 Benjamin Netanyahu said, "I want it to start now," until the aid reaches "coming to zero"; formal talks on a new agreement began that month. A joint statement by Israel's Defence Ministry and Rubio set the aim to "gradually transition from aid to a completely reciprocal partnership", and the American ambassador, Mike Huckabee, wrote that the new MOU "ends aid & will be based on trade". [cite:gi16_ji_netanyahu] [cite:gi16_wiki_nextmou] By the latest reporting this book found, in July 2026, no new agreement had been signed.

The test supports a narrower conclusion than either side usually draws. In principle the argument holds: the United States paid for interceptors and munitions used in the wars of 2023–2026, money Israel would otherwise have had to find, and Washington itself once treated settlement spending as offsetting aid. That wartime money is direct support for operations rather than an indirect release of funds. [cite:hz12b_hac_onepager] [cite:gi16_jvl_loans] In scale, though, the regular grant is now a modest and shrinking part of a defence budget that Israel's own taxpayers and borrowing have roughly doubled since 2022, and nothing in the record ties the MOU's dollars to any particular operation or settlement. [cite:gi16_boi_2025] Whether the aid's real weight lies in the money or in the diplomatic backing and access to American weapons that come with it is an argument. INSS, an Israeli institute, stresses the access to American systems and the symbol of the alliance, and also the leverage the aid gives Washington over Israeli policy. [cite:gi16_inss_aid]
