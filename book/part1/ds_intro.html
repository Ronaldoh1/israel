In 1947 a Bedouin shepherd threw a stone into a cave above the Dead Sea and heard pottery break. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] Over the next nine years, eleven caves near the ruins of Qumran gave up the remains of more than nine hundred manuscripts in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek: the oldest copies of the Hebrew Bible ever found, the rule books of a community that called itself the Sons of Light, and the Aramaic originals of the Book of Enoch that only Ethiopia had kept whole. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] [cite:ds_bas_enoch]

The scrolls are a library, and like every library on this shelf they had keepers. They passed from a shepherd to a dealer, from the dealer to an archbishop and a professor, from the archbishop to a newspaper advertisement, and from the caves to a small team of scholars who held most of the unpublished texts for forty years. [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] [cite:ds_iaa_discovery] Ownership is still disputed between states, and forgers have sold fakes to a museum. [cite:ds_abc_jordan] [cite:ds_ng_forgeries] This book follows the scrolls through all of those hands, sets out what the record shows about who wrote them, and separates the documented delay from the conspiracy theories that grew around it.

For readers coming from The Ethiopian Bible, this book closes a circle. The Book of Enoch and the Book of Jubilees, which the Ethiopian church kept when the rest of Christianity let them go, turned up in the caves in the languages they were first written in, copied centuries before Christ. [cite:ds_bas_enoch] [cite:ds_vq_nature] The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads the community's own teachings on light and darkness, on the heavenly temple and on hidden knowledge, as the inner traditions read them.
