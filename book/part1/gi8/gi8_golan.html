The Golan: the One Front Annexed by Law

Of the territories Israel took from its neighbours in June 1967, the Golan Heights is the only one outside the area of the former British Mandate that an Israeli government has placed under Israeli law. That makes it the clearest test case for this part. On the Golan, a territorial claim beyond the 1949 lines is not a matter of reading a map held up at a podium or a minister's speech: it is written in a statute, voted by the Knesset and repeated by later prime ministers. The section sets out that record, the reasons Israel's governments gave for it, the answer of the Security Council and of Syria, and the negotiations in which Israeli prime ministers offered to give much of the plateau back. The United States proclamation of 2019 records the basic sequence in its own words: Israel "took control of the Golan Heights in 1967 to safeguard its security from external threats." [cite:gi8_ucsb_procl9852]

The law of 14 December 1981

The Golan Heights Law passed the Knesset on 14 December 1981 by 63 votes to 21. Its operative clause is one sentence: "The Law, jurisdiction and administration of the State will take effect in the Golan Heights, as described in the Schedule." Prime Minister Menachem Begin told the Knesset that Israel could not wait indefinitely for a sign from Syria that it was willing to negotiate peace, and he gave historical, security and what he called "moral-political" reasons. Contemporary reporting in Time attributed the timing to pressure from the religious parties in his coalition, to the Likud's 1981 election manifesto, to the failure of the American envoy Philip Habib to get Syrian forces out of Lebanon's Beqaa Valley, and to Syria's rejection of the Saudi peace plan put forward by Crown Prince Fahd. [cite:gi8_wiki_golanlaw] The ideological setting of the law, in Begin's government and the Likud platform, is examined in Part Five. [scene:gi5_likud|Part Five: Likud in government]

Washington was not consulted. The State Department said so, President Ronald Reagan said the law "increased the difficulty of seeking peace", and the United States suspended the strategic cooperation memorandum it had signed with Israel on 30 November, only two weeks earlier; it was not reinstated until May 1983. [cite:gi8_wiki_golanlaw] [cite:ow1_wiki_1981isr] Among the people most directly affected, the response was refusal. The Druze of the Golan began a three-day general strike on 17 December, and their leaders wrote to Begin that they were first of all Syrian Arabs and would resist forced Israeli citizenship. A general strike in 1982 lasted about five months and was met with an Israeli blockade of the Druze towns. [cite:gi8_wiki_golanlaw]

Resolution 497: unanimous

Three days after the vote, on 17 December 1981, the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 497 unanimously, fifteen votes to none, with the United States voting in favour. It decided that Israel's decision "to impose its laws, jurisdiction and administration in the occupied Syrian Golan Heights is null and void and without international legal effect", demanded that Israel, "the occupying Power", rescind it, and determined that the Fourth Geneva Convention continued to apply. [cite:gi8_rw_res497] [cite:gi8_wiki_res497] Where the United States did use its veto was on the follow-up: on 20 January 1982 it vetoed a draft that would have called for international measures against Israel under Chapter VII of the Charter. On 5 February 1982 the General Assembly, in emergency session, voted 86 to 21 for a resolution calling for a boycott of Israel; the United States and many Western states voted against. [cite:gi8_wiki_res497] Israel did not rescind the law. Syria has never accepted it, and the United Nations continues to treat the Golan as occupied Syrian territory. [cite:gi8_aje_colombia]

Offers to withdraw, 1994–2000

The statute did not end negotiation over the plateau, and the counter-record here is substantial. In 1994 Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin opened secret contacts with Damascus and proposed a phased partial withdrawal over about three years, within a comprehensive peace and subject to a referendum of Israeli voters; Syria insisted on complete withdrawal as the precondition. Under Ehud Barak the two sides met at Shepherdstown, West Virginia, from 3 to 11 January 2000 without agreement. On 26 March 2000 in Geneva President Bill Clinton presented Barak's latest proposal to Hafez al-Asad, who rejected it because it would have left Israel sovereign over a strip of land north of Lake Tiberias. That meeting was, in one reference account's words, "effectively the end of the negotiations." [cite:gi8_enc_syrtalks] Two Israeli governments, then, were prepared to give up much of the Golan for peace, and the last round failed over a strip of shoreline rather than over an Israeli claim to the whole plateau. Part Twelve sets these offers beside the other withdrawals. [scene:gi12_withdrawals|Part Twelve: the withdrawals]

"Forever": the claim restated

After the Syrian civil war began, Israeli statements hardened. On 17 April 2016 Benjamin Netanyahu held the first official cabinet meeting on the Golan in the 49 years of Israeli rule and said: "The Golan Heights will forever remain in Israel's hands. Israel will never come down from the Golan Heights." [cite:gi8_mfa_cab2016] On 25 March 2019 President Donald Trump signed Proclamation 9852: "the United States recognizes that the Golan Heights are part of the State of Israel." The proclamation cited "aggressive acts by Iran and terrorist groups, including Hizballah, in southern Syria." [cite:gi8_ucsb_procl9852] Syria called it a "blatant attack" on its sovereignty; the European Union and its larger members and the Arab League rejected it; UN Secretary-General António Guterres said the Golan's status had not changed. Israel named a planned community "Trump Heights" in June 2019, and the recognition stood under President Joe Biden. [cite:gi8_wiki_usrecog]

A change of Israeli government in 2021 did not change the policy. On 26 December 2021 the cabinet of Naftali Bennett, the coalition that had replaced Netanyahu's, met on the Golan and unanimously approved a plan of about one billion shekels, roughly $320 million, with two new communities, Asif and Matar. "Our goal today is to double the population of the Golan Heights," Bennett said. The Golan's population was then about 53,000, split almost evenly between Israeli settlers and Syrian Druze. Syria's Foreign Ministry said it rejected "the annexation decision and the policy of seizing lands by force." [cite:gi8_cnn_golan2021]

Who else recognises it

For seven years the United States stood alone, and much writing on the subject describes it as "the only country". That is no longer accurate. On 10 August 2026, three days after President Abelardo de la Espriella took office, Colombia's Foreign Ministry announced that it "recognises Israeli sovereignty over this territory", making it the second state to do so. Israel's foreign minister, Gideon Saar, called it "historic", and Netanyahu thanked Colombia. Syria lodged a complaint with the UN Secretary-General and the Security Council on 12 August, and Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Egypt, Jordan, Oman and Turkey condemned the decision. [cite:gi8_aje_colombia] [cite:gi8_alm_colombia] No third state was found in the reporting reviewed as of October 2026.

What the record shows

On the Golan, the claim is documented at the highest level an Israeli government can give it: a statute in 1981, restated as permanent by Netanyahu in 2016 and backed by a Bennett-led coalition's settlement plan in 2021. The reasons governments gave were security and Syria's refusal to make peace, with history named alongside them by Begin. It is a claim to one specific territory, not to Syria beyond it, and between 1994 and 2000 two prime ministers offered to trade most of it for a treaty. Whether the claim belongs in a "Greater Israel" programme or in a security doctrine is an argument; the statute and the statements themselves are facts. Since December 2024 Israeli forces have also held positions beyond the Golan, on the Syrian summit of Mount Hermon and in the 1974 buffer zone, which the defence minister said in January 2025 they would hold indefinitely; that is the subject of Part Nine.
