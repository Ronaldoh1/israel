Six Channels: How the Money Moves

Political money in the United States does not move through one pipe. It moves through several, each created by a different law or court decision, each with its own limits and its own rules of disclosure. The pro-Israel network uses all of them, and much of the confusion in public argument comes from treating money in one channel as if it had moved through another. A $5,000 check from a political action committee and a $14 million advertising campaign by a super PAC are both "pro-Israel money," but they are different acts, regulated differently, and they say different things about the relationship between a donor and a member of Congress. This section describes the six channels in the order a reader will meet them in this volume.

1. Direct contributions. The oldest channel is a contribution from a political action committee to a candidate's campaign. It is tightly limited. For the 2025–2026 cycle a multicandidate PAC may give a candidate $5,000 per election, and an individual may give $3,500 per election; a primary, a general election, a runoff and a special election each count separately. [cite:pm_fec_limits] Both the PAC and the campaign report the money, so it is the most visible channel and, in dollar terms, the smallest. For decades this was where independent pro-Israel PACs operated, while AIPAC itself gave nothing, [cite:opensecrets_aipac_2019] and it is the channel most people picture when they hear that a member "took money" from a lobby.

2. Earmarked contributions. The second channel multiplies the first. Under federal rules, an individual may give money to a candidate through an intermediary, a "conduit," which passes it on and reports where it came from and where it went. [cite:pm_ecfr_1106] Each donation is still capped at the individual limit, but there is no limit on how many donations an intermediary can steer to the same candidate. AIPAC PAC uses this channel heavily. It runs a website where its members can choose AIPAC-endorsed candidates and give to them; the payments are processed by a company called Democracy Engine, and donors agree that their gifts are "earmarked donations within the meaning of 11 C.F.R. §110.6(b)." [cite:pm_sludge_jeffries] The practical effect is that a PAC limited to $5,000 per election can direct hundreds of thousands of dollars, or more, to a single campaign. Sludge reported in November 2025 that AIPAC PAC had directed more than $1 million in earmarked donations to the House Democratic leader, Hakeem Jeffries, who had told a radio interviewer that AIPAC's PAC could give him "$5,000 or $10,000 per cycle." [cite:pm_sludge_jeffries] Both statements can be true at once; they describe different channels. Earmarked money appears in a candidate's reports as contributions from individuals, so a reader who looks only for checks from AIPAC PAC will miss most of it.

3. Super PAC spending. The third channel is the one that changed the scale of pro-Israel money. A super PAC is a committee that makes only independent expenditures: it may raise and spend unlimited sums to support or oppose candidates, but it may not give money to them or coordinate its spending with them. The form was created in 2010, by the Supreme Court's decision in Citizens United v. FEC and, two months later, by a federal appeals court's decision in SpeechNow.org v. FEC, which held that a committee making no contributions could accept unlimited contributions. [cite:pm_wiki_superpac] AIPAC's super PAC, the United Democracy Project, was formed on 3 January 2022. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] A super PAC's money appears in a race as television, digital and mail advertising, and in the races studied in Part Five that advertising rarely mentioned Israel. [cite:punchbowl_udp_no_israel] A super PAC may also give money to other super PACs, which then spend it under names of their own. Super PACs report like other committees, but a group formed close to an election may not have to disclose its donors until after voters have voted. [cite:pm_wiki_superpac] The "pop-up" committees this makes possible are documented in the 2026 races in Part Five.

4. Lobbying. The fourth channel is not campaign money at all. It is paid advocacy: staff and hired firms who meet members of Congress, their aides and executive officials to argue for policies. Lobbying organizations report what they spend each quarter. In this channel the pro-Israel organizations are not large. In 2018 AIPAC reported about $3.5 million in lobbying, roughly the 147th largest figure in the country, against $94.8 million for the US Chamber of Commerce. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] AIPAC's influence, as even writers sympathetic to it have argued, has never rested on its lobbying budget; it has rested on its network of donors and activists and on the other five channels.

5. Charity-sponsored travel. The fifth channel runs through the House and Senate ethics rules. After the scandal around the lobbyist Jack Abramoff, Congress tightened the rules on privately paid travel in 2007. Registered lobbyists and the organizations that employ them may generally sponsor no more than a one-day trip for a member or staffer, and every privately paid trip must be approved in advance by the chamber's ethics committee and disclosed within fifteen days of return in the House and thirty in the Senate. [cite:pm_citizen_travel] The rules left room for charitable and educational organizations. AIPAC's charitable affiliate, the American Israel Education Foundation, uses that room to pay for week-long trips to Israel. Craig Holman of Public Citizen, who helped draft the 2007 law, told The Intercept that "these trips would be illegal otherwise," and the foundation's donors are redacted from its public tax filings. [cite:intercept_aief_donors] Part Seven follows the trips: who goes, how often, what they cost, and who else runs them.

6. Registered foreign agents. The sixth channel is the only one in which a foreign government's own money appears, and it appears because the law requires it to. The Foreign Agents Registration Act requires anyone working in the United States on behalf of a foreign principal to register with the Justice Department and report the work and the payments. In September 2025 Israel's Foreign Ministry, through a German subsidiary of the advertising group Havas, retained at least two American firms for messaging campaigns, and both registered: Bridges Partners, for a campaign of paid social-media influencers, and Clock Tower X, owned by Brad Parscale, for a larger campaign aimed at young Americans and at how artificial-intelligence chatbots describe Israel. [cite:pc_fara_complaint] [cite:dc_parscale_fara] These are disclosed public-relations contracts, not campaign contributions, and they are described in full in Part Nine.

Two features of the channels matter for everything that follows. The first is that the largest flows are the least personal. Direct contributions, the channel most talked about, are capped and small; the money that changed the outcome of primaries moved through super PACs that never gave a candidate a dollar. The second is that the channels are open to everyone. Labor unions, industries, ideological groups and other foreign-policy constituencies use the same pipes, and in lobbying many of them spend far more. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] What sets the pro-Israel network apart in the record is not a channel no one else can use. It is the combination: the same network active in all six, at every level of government, for a single foreign-policy cause.

The channels also leave gaps, and they should be named. Earmarked money is reported under individual donors' names, so totals depend on how a tracker attributes it. Super PAC money passed to other committees can be followed only as far as the next committee's filings. Charitable donors to travel foundations are not public. And campaign records say nothing about the most important question a reader will ask, which is whether any of it changed a vote. The next section sets out how to read the numbers with those gaps in mind.
