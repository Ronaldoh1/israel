The Prosecutors' Picture

Much of the evidence for the Cathars comes from the Church's side: treatises by theologians who described the heresy, and the registers of inquisitors who questioned villagers and wrote down their answers. In 2012 the historian R. I. Moore argued, in The War on Heresy, that "Catharism" was largely a construction: a picture "contrived from the resources of [the] well-stocked imaginations" of churchmen and later confirmed by Dominican inquisitors. [cite:hr_ihr_moore] He argued that by speaking of "Cathars" and a "Cathar Church", earlier scholars had "begged the essential question", and noted that Catholics usually called these people simply "heretics". [cite:hr_ihr_moore] The historian Mark Pegg, working closely from one inquisition manuscript, had already "whittled away the conventional picture" of Catharism in Languedoc. [cite:hr_ihr_moore]

The case is strongly contested. Reviewing Moore's book, the historian Peter Biller called it rhetorically brilliant but said that "Moore's scepticism has become even more radical", that its use of the evidence runs in one direction, and that denying what the people believed carries a high moral cost; he pointed to historians such as Malcolm Barber and Claire Taylor as more reliable guides. [cite:hr_ihr_moore] Moore, for his part, described his method as "the old-fashioned positivism" of nineteenth-century history, and ended his reply with the words "history is dangerous stuff". [cite:hr_ihr_moore]

This book does not settle the dispute. It notes two things. First, the disagreement is about what the condemned believed, not about what was done to them. The crusade, the massacre at Béziers, the transfer of Languedoc to the crown, the pyre at Montségur and the work of the inquisitors are documented on either reading. [cite:hr_brit_crusade] [cite:hr_wiki_montsegur] Second, the debate is itself an example of the pattern this shelf follows. The office that held the power to define heresy also held the pen that recorded it, so that even the victims' beliefs come down to us largely in their accusers' words.

The same is true of the other heretics in this book, with one exception. Marguerite Porete's own book survives, in her own words, because it was copied by people who did not know who she was. [cite:hr_wiki_porete] The Inner Reading in Part Eight starts there, with a heretic who can still speak for herself.
