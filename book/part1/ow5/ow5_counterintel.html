Warnings, Denials and the Bureau in Israel, 1995–2026

After Pollard the FBI's relationship with Israel ran on two tracks at once. On one, American counterintelligence reports kept naming Israel as an active collector against the United States, and Israel kept denying it. On the other, the Bureau opened an office in Israel, expanded cooperation on terrorism and crime, and described the relationship as one that runs both ways. This section sets the two tracks side by side, and marks which findings rest on documents and which on unnamed officials.

The 1990s warnings. A General Accounting Office report of 28 February 1996 said that an unnamed "Country A" "conducts the most aggressive espionage operation against the United States of any US ally"; press accounts identified Country A as Israel. Its concern was classified military information and sensitive military technology. [cite:ow5_gao_1996] [cite:ow5_wsws_gao] [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] In the same period a memorandum from a field office of the Defense Investigative Service, first reported by Moment magazine in February 1996, called Israel a "non-traditional adversary" and suggested it used ties to American Jews; Assistant Secretary of Defense Emmett Paige called singling out an ethnic group "particularly repugnant" and cancelled it. [cite:ow5_jw_1996memo] [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The two documents should be kept apart. The GAO finding concerns the conduct of a foreign government's services. The memorandum's suggestion that Americans were suspect because of their religion or ancestry is the kind of identity-based inference this book does not accept, and the Pentagon itself withdrew it.

The 2014 briefings. In May 2014 Newsweek reported that American officials had told congressional staff in classified briefings that Israel was "crossing red lines" through espionage aimed at American firms, including energy and medical-technology companies, and that joint trade missions and front companies were used. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The officials were unnamed, and Israel denied the claims. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The report rests on anonymous sources and is labelled here as contested.

The devices near the White House, 2019. On 12 September 2019 Politico reported, citing three former senior officials, that cellphone surveillance devices known as IMSI catchers had been found near the White House and other sensitive sites in Washington, that the FBI and other agencies had linked them to Israeli agents, and that they appeared intended to monitor President Trump and his aides. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] No public rebuke followed. The Department of Homeland Security later told Senator Ron Wyden it could not determine who had placed the devices, and Israel's embassy called the report a "blatant lie" and "absolute nonsense." [cite:ow5_cbs_2019] [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] Israel called the report a "blatant lie" and restated that it does not spy in the United States; President Trump said he found it hard to believe. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] The FBI made no public statement. The original report is known here through a reference summary of it and of the Guardian and Times of Israel coverage; it is a single-outlet claim from unnamed officials, denied by Israel, and is presented as contested.

June 2026. The most recent report concerns the Pentagon's own intelligence agency. In June 2026, according to reporting by NBC News, the Times of Israel and the New York Times as summarized in a reference account, the Defense Intelligence Agency raised its counterintelligence threat assessment for Israel to "critical," its highest level, out of concern that Israel was surveilling senior American officials to learn the administration's internal decision-making on the Middle East, including the war with Iran; a seven-page assessment was said to list the incidents behind the change, and a separate report described Israeli efforts to eavesdrop on named officials. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] NBC News reported a seven-page DIA assessment; it has not been published. Israel's embassy called the report "completely false," saying "Israel does not gather intelligence on American entities, let alone US government officials," and a White House official said "this entire story is false"; the Pentagon declined to comment. [cite:ow5_nbc_dia] It is included because it is recent and directly on point, and it should be read as reported, not confirmed. Its status, as of October 2026, may have changed.

The other track: the Bureau in Israel. In the same decades the FBI built a standing presence in Israel. Under Director Louis Freeh, who took office in 1993, the Bureau roughly doubled its network of legal attaché offices abroad, and Tel Aviv was among the new locations. [cite:fbi_legat_history] An assistant legal attaché post in Jerusalem was later added, by the FBI's own account, to build a relationship with the Palestinian Authority as well. [cite:fbi_israel_story] The Bureau states that its legal attachés "do not conduct foreign intelligence gathering or counterintelligence investigations"; they are liaison officers working through the host country's police and security services. [cite:fbi_intl_ops] An FBI official in Israel, Cary Gleicher, interviewed by the Bureau's own public-affairs office in 2017, described the relationship in reciprocal terms: "The FBI provides a great deal of support to the government of Israel through training opportunities, exchange of intelligence. And the Israelis do an unbelievable amount in the realm of counterterrorism for us." [cite:fbi_israel_story]

The training ties also run outside the Bureau. The best-known programme taking American police leaders to Israel, run by the Jewish Institute for National Security of America since 2002, was created by a former FBI assistant director, Steven Pomerantz; it and the other exchanges are examined in [scene:ow6_exchanges|the police exchanges]. [cite:ow6_reason_cops] And the FBI was itself a customer of Israeli surveillance technology: it bought NSO Group's Pegasus, tested it for about two years, and on 22 July 2021 decided to "cease all efforts" to deploy it, saying there had been no operational use, a story told in Part Ten. [cite:ow10_globe_fbi]

The pattern, and its limits. Across thirty years the findings share a shape: they appear in internal assessments, leaked documents or reporting from unnamed officials; Israel denies them; and no prosecution follows, because the conduct alleged is that of foreign officials whom American courts cannot reach, or because no case is brought. [cite:ow5_wiki_esp] [cite:ow_td_spying2015] The partnership track meanwhile continued and widened. The FBI's own framing is that the relationship is valuable to the United States in counterterrorism, and the public record supports that claim at the level of statements rather than published cases. [cite:fbi_israel_story] What it does not show is any instance after 1987 in which a finding on the counterintelligence track changed the terms of the partnership track.

What the record does not settle. The 1996 GAO report is cited here through quotations rather than its own text. The 2014, 2019 and 2026 reports rest on unnamed officials, and the original articles could not be retrieved. The FBI's joint cases with Israeli police on cybercrime, and its work on the cases of Americans taken hostage on 7 October 2023, lie outside this section, and nothing is said about them here.
