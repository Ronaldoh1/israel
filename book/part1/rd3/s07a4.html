Hebron, 1929

In August 1929, riots that began over disputed rights at the Western Wall spread to Hebron, home to one of the oldest continuous Jewish communities in the world. Sixty-seven Jews were killed [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. Popular accounts often date the start of Arab-Jewish violence in Mandate Palestine to the organized Arab Revolt of 1936. That starting point is seven years too late. Hebron has a specific death toll, a specific and lasting demographic consequence, and a specific political figure whose career it helped to make.

The immediate dispute is well documented. Through 1928 tensions built over Jewish prayer arrangements at the Western Wall, a site owned by Muslim religious authorities. Both sides were active. An Arab campaign asserted Muslim rights over the site; Jews asserted their access for prayer there. Each community saw the dispute as central, as a symbol, to its competing claims [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The record shows real tension on both sides before the riots. It does not settle which particular actions bear more of the blame for the escalation.

In August 1929 the rioting, inflamed by rumors and organized propaganda, reached Hebron. Sixty-seven Jews were killed. In the aftermath the British authorities evacuated the city's Jewish community, which had been there for centuries [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. Organized Jewish life in Hebron did not resume until 1968.

That consequence is what sets Hebron apart. Many episodes of violence in this period caused disruption followed by some form of return. Hebron caused an immediate, total and long-lasting change: a community present for centuries was removed within days and stayed absent from organized communal life in the city for thirty-nine years [cite:wiki_hebron_massacre_1929]. The circumstances of the return in 1968, and what followed it, belong to a later part of the story.

The role of Amin al-Husseini, the Mufti of Jerusalem, a senior Muslim religious authority, is contested and needs careful statement. Some things are documented. In 1928 he led a campaign asserting Muslim rights over the Wall, and his activists distributed inflammatory material before the riots [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. Historians disagree about how far he was responsible for the violence itself: whether he directly organized the riots, or exploited tensions that escalated beyond his control. This book leaves that question open.

On the political consequence, historians are more definite. Several, notably Oren Kessler in a 2024 account, describe 1929 as the event that made al-Husseini's political reputation among Palestinians [cite:wiki_amin_al_husseini]. On that reputation he built his later leadership of the Arab Revolt of 1936 to 1939. Accounts that begin his rise in 1936 miss its foundation. That does not make 1936 less important to his career. It means the career had begun earlier, at Hebron.

The violence also came in the same month that the enlarged Jewish Agency was ratified at Zurich, as described in [scene:us_s03|the enlarged Jewish Agency]. It was followed by the British inquiries and the policy reversal traced in [scene:pf_s01|the Passfield White Paper]. Placed correctly in time, Hebron is not a footnote to the revolt of 1936. It is an earlier turning point, whose consequences for the city, for Palestinian politics and for British policy were visible years before the revolt began.
