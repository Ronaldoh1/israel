"I Am the Truth"

Al-Husayn ibn Mansur al-Hallaj was born around 858 in Fars, in Iran. From about 895 to 910 he made several pilgrimages to Mecca and preached in Fars, Khuzistan and Khorasan, then travelled by sea to India and Turkistan, returning to Baghdad around 908. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj] He was accused of claiming divinity after reportedly saying "Anā al-ḥaqq": "I am the Truth", that is, God. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj]

The charges were not only theological. The authorities linked him to the Qarmatians, a radical movement, and to the Zanj slave rebellion, and his missionary travels were seen as resembling Qarmatian subversion; after his return to Baghdad he was suspected of a political and moral reform effort. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj] He was arrested in Sus and held in Baghdad for about eleven years, from around 911 to 922, through a long trial "marked by indecision". On 26 March 922 he was crucified and tortured to death in front of a large crowd; accounts say he endured it calmly and forgave his accusers. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj] He is remembered in Islamic history as a symbol of both admiration and repression. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj]

Two and a half centuries later the pattern repeated. Shihab al-Din Yahya Suhrawardi, born in 1154, founded the school of Illumination, ḥikmat al-ishrāq, and completed his Philosophy of Illumination in 1186. [cite:sf_wiki_suhrawardi] He was accused of "cultivating Batini teachings and philosophy", that is, hidden esoteric doctrine, and was put to death in Aleppo on the order of al-Malik al-Zahir, a son of Saladin; the most common date given is 1191, though the reports of his death contradict one another. He is known as al-Maqtul, "the one who was killed". [cite:sf_wiki_suhrawardi]

Between those two deaths stands the man who changed Sufism's standing. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, born in 1058, was appointed in 1091 by the vizier Nizam al-Mulk as chief professor at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad, where he lectured to more than three hundred students. [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] After a spiritual crisis left him for a time unable to lecture, he left Baghdad in November 1095, on the pretext of a pilgrimage, gave away his wealth and adopted the life of a poor Sufi. [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] His greatest work, The Revival of the Religious Sciences, in forty books, explains Islamic doctrine and practice as the basis for a devotional life leading to the higher Sufi stages, and it helped make Sufism "an acceptable part of orthodox Islam". [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] He also wrote against the philosophers and against the Nizari Ismailis, whom Part Four follows. [cite:sf_brit_ghazali]

Read together, the three cases show where the line ran. Al-Ghazali's Sufism observed the law, was taught by a scholar who had held the highest post in the capital, and was written in forty orderly books; it became orthodox. Al-Hallaj's spoke of union in words that sounded like a claim to be God and moved among the poor in ways the authorities read as rebellion; it was crucified. [cite:sf_brit_hallaj] [cite:sf_brit_ghazali] The difference was less in the experience claimed than in whether it stayed inside the institutions of the law and the state. That is the same line The Canon found around the inner sayings of the Christian scriptures.
