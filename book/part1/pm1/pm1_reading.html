How to Read a Number

The figures in this volume come from public filings, but almost none of them can be read without a short set of questions. Which election does the figure cover? Was the money given to a candidate or spent for or against one? Whose definition of "pro-Israel" produced it? On what date was it counted? And what does it show about the member who received it, as opposed to the organization that gave it? This section takes the questions one at a time, with examples from the record, so that the reader can apply them to any figure in the parts that follow, and to the figures quoted elsewhere.

One cycle or a whole career. Federal campaign money is organized in two-year cycles that end with each November election. A figure for "the 2024 cycle" covers money given or spent from January 2023 to December 2024. A career total covers every cycle since a member first ran, sometimes thirty years or more. The two are often confused, and the confusion is not always innocent. The career totals compiled by the tracker Track AIPAC, used in Part Four, place Senator Susan Collins of Maine first among all senators of either party, at about $4.1 million; that sum covers every race she has run, not one election. [cite:trackaipac_congress] A figure for a single cycle would be much smaller. Neither number is wrong, but a sentence that quotes the career figure as if it were one election's money misleads.

Given to, or spent for, or spent against. The second question separates the channels described in the previous section. Money given to a campaign, directly or through earmarking, is money the candidate can spend. Money a super PAC spends for a candidate is not given to that candidate at all; the candidate cannot direct it, and in law must not coordinate with it. Money spent against a candidate is the opposite of support. Trackers that compile "pro-Israel money" by member often add independent spending made on a member's behalf to the money the member received, which is a reasonable way to measure who benefited but not a measure of what the member took. In Track AIPAC's list, the Michigan Democrat Haley Stevens shows a career total of about $36.3 million, of which about $1.4 million was given to her campaigns and about $34.9 million was spent by others on her behalf. [cite:trackaipac_congress] Those are different facts, and this volume reports them separately.

Whose definition. The third question concerns the boundary of the category. OpenSecrets' pro-Israel category, the oldest consistent series, counts contributions from PACs and individuals it classifies as pro-Israel. [cite:pm_jvl_pacs] Track AIPAC counts all pro-Israel PACs, including J Street, adds the large individual donors to those PACs, and adds outside spending; it describes its own definition as broad. [cite:trackaipac_congress] [cite:wiki_trackaipac] Other trackers add still more. A research file prepared for this platform before this volume found one aggregator claiming $231.8 million in pro-Israel money for the 2026 cycle against an FEC-based figure of $104.3 million for AIPAC's two committees, a gap explained mostly by what each counted. [cite:pm_sludge_100m_2026] [cite:pm_genus_tracker] This volume uses the narrower, itemized figures wherever they exist, names the tracker whenever it uses a broader one, and does not add figures produced under different definitions.

On what date. Campaign finance is reported in stages, and the same race can produce several "final" figures as late filings arrive. The United Democracy Project's spending against Representative Cori Bush of Missouri in 2024 has been reported at figures from $5.2 million to $8.6 million, depending on the date of the filing and on whether spending for her opponent is included. [cite:factcheck_udp_2024] [cite:rollcall_bush_primary_2024] In the 2026 Kentucky primary against Representative Thomas Massie, FactCheck.org reported in August that the super PAC had spent over $3 million opposing him, while Louisville Public Media's tally of all filings put its spending at $4.1 million. [cite:pm_factcheck_udp_2026] [cite:lpm_massie_gallrein_funding] Where sources differ, this volume gives the range and the date of each figure rather than choosing one.

What the money shows about the member. The last question is the hardest, and the campaign records cannot answer it alone. A member who receives large sums from pro-Israel donors and votes for military aid to Israel may vote that way because of the money, or may receive the money because of the votes, or both, or neither. Donors give to candidates who already agree with them. The Guardian's analysis of the 2022 cycle found that members who received more pro-Israel money were more likely to back military support for Israel, but that party affiliation, not money, best predicted what members said about civilian casualties in Gaza, and that the data could not show that the money changed anyone's position. [cite:guardian_gaza_donors] This volume holds to that limit. It reports what members received, what was spent for and against them, and what they said and did, side by side. It does not claim that a vote was bought unless a document says so, and in the record studied here none does.

The limit runs in the other direction too. The absence of proof that money changed a vote is not proof that it did not. A primary campaign that spends $14 million to remove a sitting member, and succeeds, sends a message to every other member, and the message does not need to be written down to be received. Part Five sets out what those campaigns were, in which races, at what cost and with what result. The reader can judge what they communicated.
