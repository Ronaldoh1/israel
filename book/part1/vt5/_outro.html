Part Five established that the Holy See's standing as a state rests on recognition practice older than Vatican City itself: Article 2 of the Lateran Treaty recognised a sovereignty "inherent to its nature," it kept relations with 16 to 27 states while holding no territory, and by 9 January 2026 it had relations with 184 states, the European Union and the Order of Malta. [cite:vt1_va_lateran] [cite:vt5_wiki_forrel] [cite:vt5_pv_relations2026] Its UN seat was confirmed and widened by resolution 58/314 in 2004 despite the See Change campaign, and US and European courts have treated it as a foreign state, with abuse claims against it failing on where the acts occurred and on whether priests are its employees. [cite:vt5_hsm_58314] [cite:vt5_un_seechange2000] [cite:vt5_jurist_doe2012] [cite:vt5_eapil_jc2021] It is not unchecked. Two UN committees examined its abuse record in 2014 and rejected its view that its treaty obligations stop at the city's walls, and the Holy See rejected theirs. [cite:vt5_crc_co2014] [cite:vt5_cat_co2014] [cite:vt5_rv_cat_reply] On money, the 2009 Monetary Agreement binds Vatican City to EU anti-money-laundering acts, and MONEYVAL's reports of 2012, 2021 and 2024 record real reform alongside weak prosecution and confiscation, which corrects the claim that the IOR is exempt from those rules. [cite:vt5_va_monetary] [cite:vt5_mv_2021mer] [cite:vt5_fatf_fur2024] Part Six turns from the Holy See's outside relations to the people who run it: the popes, the Curia as reorganised in 2022, and the myths of hidden popes.
