Korea and the Turn West, 1950–1951

Non-identification met its first hard test in Korea. When war broke out there in June 1950 and the United Nations condemned North Korea, Israel had to decide whether its promise of friendship "in particular with the United States and the Soviet Union" allowed it to take the American position in a vote. Within a month it had answered the question, and within two months its prime minister had gone much further in private than the government had gone in public. This section follows that turn from the cabinet's first discussion in July 1950 to Ben-Gurion's first visit to the United States as prime minister in May 1951, and then sets out the dispute over why it happened.

A common account holds that Israel was pushed into the Western camp by Moscow's hostility. Another holds that it was bought into that camp by American aid. The record of 1950–51 does not fit neatly into either. The first decisive step was Israel's own, taken at the United Nations and defended in its parliament, and the reasons historians give for it are several, not one.

According to the National Library of Israel's account, Israel's cabinet first discussed participation in the UN coalition in Korea on 2 July 1950, at President Weizmann's home in Rehovot, eight days after the war began. The government's statement, reported on 7 July, read: "The Israel government opposes and condemns aggression whenever it occurs and from whatever quarter it emanates." [cite:rs_cw1_nli_korea] The wording was general. It named no aggressor, and it could be read as a statement of principle that applied to any state. That generality was itself a way of keeping a foot in the old policy while taking a step out of it.

Ben-Gurion was less guarded in the Knesset. On 4 July 1950 he told the members that Israel's vote for the UN resolution condemning North Korea was "a vote for the United Nations and for the principles it embodied", and he rejected calls for Israel to abstain. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The defence was careful. Ben-Gurion presented the vote as loyalty to the United Nations, not to the United States. But the effect was the same. On a question that divided Washington and Moscow, Israel had voted with Washington. Avi Shlaim calls the vote "the decisive break at the declaratory level with the policy of non-identification." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] The words "at the declaratory level" matter: what changed in July 1950 was what Israel said in public, and it would be some time before the rest of its policy followed.

What Israel did, as distinct from what it said, was limited. It offered no troops. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] According to the National Library of Israel's account, it sent $100,000 worth of food and medicine for Korea. It was appointed to the 14-member Peace Observation Commission, which was never sent to the peninsula. It drafted a seven-point peace plan, whose sponsorship passed to Norway. [cite:rs_cw1_nli_korea] Each of these steps placed Israel inside the UN effort while keeping it out of the fighting: humanitarian aid instead of soldiers, observation and mediation instead of combat.

The decision was not unanimous inside Israel. The communist party, Maki, advocated support for North Korea. [cite:rs_cw1_nli_korea] The calls to abstain that Ben-Gurion rejected in the Knesset show that the choice was contested in the chamber as well as outside it, though the sources gathered here do not say which members or parties beyond Maki urged abstention, or how the Knesset divided.

Weeks later, Ben-Gurion went much further in a private conversation. In late July 1950 he told the American ambassador that he planned an army of 250,000, built with American help, that would be "capable and anxious" to aid the United States, Britain and Turkey against Soviet aggression. The ambassador reported to Washington that the prime minister "could not have been more explicit about his desire to commit Israel unreservedly to the West." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Set beside the cabinet statement of 7 July, the offer shows the distance between Israel's public and private positions that summer. In public the government condemned aggression "from whatever quarter it emanates". In private its prime minister offered a quarter of a million men for the West's side against one named power. The offer was also a request, since the army was to be built with American help. Its cost and its terms are not set out in the material available here, and the record gathered for this book does not show any American acceptance of it.

The following spring Ben-Gurion went to the United States. The State Department lists his first visit as prime minister as a goodwill visit, which also promoted Israel Bonds, whose origins are told in [scene:rd6_financing|how the new state was paid for]. The listing names his stops: Knoxville, New York, West Point, Philadelphia, Princeton, Baltimore, Boston, Pittsburgh, Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland and Los Angeles. [cite:rs_cw1_state_visits_1951] The same entry is inconsistent about its length. It dates the visit 3–8 May 1951, and also says he left the United States on 31 May. [cite:rs_cw1_state_visits_1951] The listing does not explain the difference, and this book records both dates as the source gives them. The visit came in the same spring that Israel's diplomats asked Washington for grant aid, the subject of the section on American grant aid.

Why did Israel tilt so quickly? Two readings are on record, and both are interpretations rather than documented findings.

The first reading puts the weight on Israel's own needs. Michael Brecher, as quoted by Shlaim, held that the shift was "catalyzed by the need for arms and economic aid." Shlaim adds three further reasons: the fall in immigration from Eastern Europe, the aim of winning the support of American Jewry, and the need for American backing for German reparations. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] On this view the turn was a choice. Each of the four reasons points west: the arms and the money Israel sought, the Jewish community it hoped to win, and the reparations it needed American support to obtain lay on the American side of the line, while the immigration that had come from the Soviet bloc was falling away. Shlaim's own summary is that "the tilt towards the West was probably inevitable from the start, but it occurred in a surprisingly sudden manner." [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west]

The second reading was the Israeli establishment's own. It held that the rift with Moscow followed the Soviet Union's own "developmental logic", in Moshe Sharett's phrase: Israel was pushed rather than chose. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Its strongest evidence is timing, since Moscow's vote for the internationalization of Jerusalem on 9 December 1949 came half a year before Korea. Shlaim, drawing on Uri Bialer, finds that view too deterministic, and Bialer's evidence cuts the other way: Soviet attitudes, he finds, shifted further after Israel publicly backed the American position on Korea in 1950. [cite:rs_cw1_shlaim_east_west] Moscow had cooled before Korea, and Israel's choice cooled it further. Where the readings differ is on whether Israel had a real choice; the case on each side is set out with the Soviet break itself.

Several matters remain open. The cabinet's discussion of 2 July is known from a secondary account; the minutes, and the positions individual ministers took, are not before us. The American response to Ben-Gurion's offer of a 250,000-man army is not documented in this material. The State Department listing gives two incompatible indications of how long Ben-Gurion stayed in the United States in May 1951. And the weight of each reason for the tilt, arms, aid, immigration, American Jewry, reparations, or Soviet hostility, is a matter of historical judgment on which the scholars cited here disagree. What followed on the Soviet side, from the Prague trial to the breaking of relations in February 1953, is told in [scene:cw1_soviet_break|the Soviet break].
