The Debt That Stays

When the promise of a degree fails, through a programme not finished, a field that did not pay or a life that went wrong, what remains is the loan. Student loans differ from almost every other debt an American family carries in one respect: since 1976 the law has made them hard to wipe out in bankruptcy, the legal process that lets a person who cannot pay start again. This section sets out how large the debt is, how the bankruptcy rule was built step by step, who documented it and who lobbied on it, how collection works without a court, and where the record stands as of October 2026.

How much is owed

Student loan balances almost tripled between 2004 and 2012, when the number of borrowers grew by about 70 per cent, and reached about $994 billion in mid-2013. [cite:us17_nyfed_2013] They passed $1.26 trillion in the second quarter of 2016, $1.48 trillion in 2019 and $1.59 trillion in 2022, and then grew more slowly. [cite:ad7_audit_nyfed2016] [cite:ad7_audit_nyfed2019] [cite:ad7_audit_cr2022] By the New York Federal Reserve's count, drawn from credit reports, they stood at $1.65 trillion in the second quarter of 2026, down $7 billion in the quarter and up $13 billion on the year. [cite:ad7_nyfed_pr] The federal government's own portfolio data, to 30 June 2026, covered 42.3 million borrowers and more than $1.7 trillion. Of these, more than 9.3 million borrowers owing $234 billion were in default, the status a federal loan reaches after a long run of missed payments: about 14 per cent of the $1.64 trillion the government manages directly. Defaults rose by about 1.6 million borrowers and $54 billion in the first half of 2026 alone, and about 1.5 million more borrowers were in late-stage delinquency. [cite:us17_ci_defaults] [cite:ad7_ci_defaults2]

The law, step by step

Before 1976 an educational loan could be discharged in bankruptcy like any other unsecured debt. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] The change began not with a lender but with a public body. Congress created the Commission on the Bankruptcy Laws of the United States in 1970, and in its 1973 report the commission recommended that government educational loans not be dischargeable until five years after repayment began, unless repaying would cause "undue hardship". [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] The federal education department of the time, Health, Education and Welfare, had urged a restriction after reports of graduates escaping their loans. [cite:ad7_iowa_lr] Congress adopted the five-year rule in the Education Amendments of 1976, as a new Section 439A of the Higher Education Act, and delayed its start by nearly a year, partly to await a study by the General Accounting Office. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699]

Two years later Congress wrote a new Bankruptcy Code. The House Judiciary Committee's bill would have made student loans dischargeable again, and its report stated that "less than 1% of all federally insured and guaranteed educational loans were discharged in bankruptcy". That figure is often attributed to the General Accounting Office; the Congressional Research Service traces it to the House report. Representative Allen Ertel, opposing the committee's change, warned that discharge would be "almost specifically designed to encourage fraud", and a floor amendment adopted by voice vote restored the bar, which became Section 523(a)(8) of the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] [cite:ad7_iowa_lr] The rule was written against a stated fear, of graduates filing as soon as they left school, that the numbers Congress had did not show to be common.

Each later change widened the rule or removed a way out. A 1979 amendment extended it from loans owed to a government unit to any loan "made, insured, or guaranteed by a governmental unit" or made under a programme it funded. [cite:ad7_pl96_56] In 1984 Congress removed the words "of higher education", so that loans from any non-profit institution were covered. In 1990 the five-year period became seven, and student loans were taken out of the broader discharge that Chapter 13 repayment plans had allowed. In 1991 the six-year time limit on collecting defaulted federal loans was abolished, so a defaulted federal loan can be pursued for life. [cite:ad7_clla_2022] [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] In 1997 the National Bankruptcy Review Commission recommended repealing the student-loan exception altogether; Congress went the other way, and the Higher Education Amendments of 1998, signed by President Bill Clinton, removed the time route entirely, leaving undue hardship as the only way to discharge a federal loan. The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005, signed by President George W. Bush, extended the same protection to private student loans made by banks and other lenders. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699]

"Undue hardship" is not defined in the statute, so the courts defined it. Most follow the test set out by a federal appeals court in Brunner v. New York State Higher Education Services Corp. (1987): a borrower must show that repaying would leave them unable to keep a minimal standard of living, that this is likely to last for much of the repayment period, and that they have made good-faith efforts to repay. All three must be met. [cite:ad7_iowa_lr] [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] Applied strictly, the test asks borrowers to prove that their hardship will last, not only that it exists.

Who documented it, who lobbied, and what the record does not show

The documented origin of the rule is public: a congressional commission in 1973 and a floor vote in 1978. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] The industry's lobbying record belongs to a later period. Stephen Burd of the New America Foundation, who covered the issue for the Chronicle of Higher Education, reported that Sallie Mae, then the largest student lender, spent $9 million lobbying Congress between 1999 and 2005, the years the 2005 bankruptcy bill was under consideration, and that its political action committee gave more than $130,000 to members of the House and Senate judiciary committees; he also reported that no hearings were held on the private-loan provision. [cite:ad7_npr_burd] A New America analysis in 2007 went further on what is not known: nothing in the Congressional Record explains the change, and on who pushed for it, "No one seems to know". It noted that Sallie Mae's internal strategy document listed preserving the provision's benefits as its second priority, because the rule lets lenders "go to the front of the line" in bankruptcy. [cite:ad7_newamerica_2007] The lobbying spending is a documented fact; that it caused the 2005 change is an inference the public record does not establish, since the spending covered many issues and the provision's sponsors left no explanation.

Collection without a court, and the 2026 status

Most creditors must sue and win a judgment before they can take part of a debtor's wages. For defaulted federal student loans the government can order an employer to withhold up to 15 per cent of disposable pay without a court order, and the Treasury Offset Program can take tax refunds and part of Social Security benefits. [cite:us17_ci_defaults] [cite:ad7_ci_garnish] After warning notices went out in early January 2026, the Education Department paused these involuntary collections on 16 January, saying it needed time to put in place the repayment changes of the 2025 reconciliation act. [cite:ad7_natp_pause] [cite:ad7_collegeave_pause] [cite:ad7_invest_pause] The same act ended Grad PLUS loans for new graduate borrowers from 1 July 2026, capped graduate and parent borrowing, and replaced income-driven plans for new loans with a single Repayment Assistance Plan, which launched in July 2026. [cite:us17_unmc_obbba] The Biden administration's SAVE repayment plan, blocked in court, was ended by a settlement with Missouri and other states announced on 9 December 2025, moving about 7 million enrolled borrowers to other plans. [cite:ad7_npr_save] [cite:ad7_ci_save] Status as of October 2026: the department and the Treasury have said they plan to resume wage garnishment this autumn but have announced no date, and the government has not yet reported how many borrowers have joined the new plan. [cite:ad7_ci_garnish] [cite:ad7_ci_defaults2]

Where it works as promised

The bankruptcy door, nearly shut by statute, was opened somewhat by practice. On 17 November 2022 the Justice and Education departments adopted a standard process: a borrower fills in an attestation form on income, expenses and repayment efforts, the government checks it against its own records, and the Justice Department decides whether to recommend discharge to the judge, who decides. [cite:ad7_doj_2022] By the department's July 2024 count, 1,220 cases had been filed under it to March 2024; 96 per cent of borrowers used the form, and 98 per cent of the cases courts had decided ended in full or partial discharge. [cite:ad7_doj_2024] The department revised the form in May 2025 and the process remains in use as of October 2026, under an administration of the other party. [cite:ad7_ap_form] The numbers are small beside 9.3 million defaulted borrowers, but they show that when the government chose to read "undue hardship" more generously, the courts agreed. The debt is also concentrated: graduates of four-year programmes default at low rates, and fewer bachelor's graduates borrowed, and borrowed less, in 2023–24 than five years earlier. [cite:ad7_ly_draft] [cite:ad7_cb_trends25]

Interpretation, not finding: the toll on the ladder

The Sophia platform reads the student-loan rules as the point where the Dream turns into an extraction mechanism: education is sold as the ladder to a better life, the ladder is financed by debt, and the law then makes that debt a lifelong claim on wages that survives even the failure of the promise that justified it. In this reading the sequence from 1976 to 2005, each step widening the rule while removing exits, looks less like a series of separate decisions than a design. That reading is an interpretation, not a documented finding.

The strongest counter-readings are on the record. The rule began with a public commission and a congressional floor debate, not with a lender, and its stated purpose was to stop borrowers who had gained an asset, their education, from shedding the debt before they could repay, which its supporters on the floor described as protection against fraud. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] [cite:ad7_iowa_lr] The documented lobbying belongs to the 1999–2005 period, and the record does not show that it caused the 2005 change. [cite:ad7_npr_burd] [cite:ad7_newamerica_2007] Congress and administrations of both parties built the rule, and an administration of each party has kept the 2022 process that loosened it. [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] [cite:ad7_ap_form] And the defaults concentrate where the promise itself failed, among borrowers who did not finish or attended programmes with weak earnings, which points to the price and quality of the education as much as to the law that holds the debt in place.
