The Periphery Alliance

Israel's first prime minister, David Ben-Gurion, looked for friends beyond the Arab states that surrounded Israel. In the late 1950s, in the Encyclopaedia Iranica's phrase, Israel sought the friendship of "the neighbors of the neighbors": non-Arab states on the region's edge that had their own reasons to fear Egypt's President Nasser and Soviet influence. This became known as the periphery policy, and its main partners were Iran, Turkey and Ethiopia. With Iran it produced an informal alliance that lasted until 1979. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel]

Trident and SAVAK

The intelligence side of the policy was organised through an arrangement known as Trident, which linked the intelligence services of Israel, Iran and Turkey. The Israeli analyst Yossi Alpher, who wrote its history, records that it was formed through separate meetings in Europe, Ankara and Tehran between 1956 and 1958, at which the heads of the three services set up joint ventures, some of them operations against Nasserist and Soviet influence. Ben-Gurion presented it to Washington as a Western asset. Alpher calls it "a lopsided intelligence alliance under a gloss of pompous protocol": Israel "provided far better information and more intelligence know-how than it received in return." [cite:ir1_alpher_fa]

Iran's partner in Trident was SAVAK, the security and intelligence organisation the Shah created in 1957. In September of that year SAVAK's first head, General Teymur Bakhtiar, offered Israel's ambassador in Paris an exchange of intelligence, and Israel accepted. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] How far Israel helped build SAVAK itself is reported rather than documented in released files. The historian Ervand Abrahamian writes that Mossad, the CIA and the FBI helped the Shah's government establish it; the political scientist Mohsen Milani writes that Mossad and the CIA assisted its creation, as summarised by the Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada in 1991. [cite:ir1_irb_savak] SAVAK became the instrument of the Shah's political repression, as Amnesty International reported in a 1976 briefing. [cite:ir1_irb_savak] Israel's government has not published records of what training Mossad gave it, and the specific claims sometimes made, such as translated interrogation manuals, rest on secondary accounts and are not repeated here.

The route for Iraq's Jews

Iran was also a route out of Iraq. After 1948 Jews in Iraq faced persecution and restrictions on leaving. By 1949, according to historians of the community, the Zionist underground in Iraq was smuggling Jews into Iran at about 1,000 a month, from where they were flown to Israel; when Iraq allowed emigration in 1951–52, between 120,000 and 130,000 Iraqi Jews were airlifted to Israel via Iran and Cyprus. [cite:ir1_wiki_ezra] The Iranica entry records that Iran's role as a transit point for Iraqi Jews made contacts with Tehran more urgent for Israel in the years before the alliance took shape. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel]

Trade, advisers and arms

By the 1970s the relationship was substantial. Israeli official statistics, cited by Iranica, show Israeli exports to Iran rising from $22.3 million in 1970 to $92.4 million in 1975 and $103.2 million in 1977, almost doubling between 1973 and 1974; Israel's recorded non-oil imports from Iran were far smaller, $5.8 million in 1977. Oil, the largest flow, was handled separately and secretly. Israel helped develop Iran's armed forces and trained Iranian officers and students, and its presence was large enough to need an Israeli school in Tehran. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] According to Israeli Foreign Ministry archives, more than 500 Israeli families lived in Iran before the revolution. [cite:ir1_wiki_embtehran]

The most ambitious project came last. In April 1977 Iran and Israel signed six contracts exchanging Iranian oil for Israeli arms. One, code-named Flower, was to develop a missile. On 18 July 1977 Iran's vice-minister of war, General Hassan Toufanian, met Foreign Minister Moshe Dayan and Defence Minister Ezer Weizman in Israel; the minutes of that meeting, from Israel's Foreign Ministry, are held by the National Security Archive. In 1978 Iran paid $280 million in oil as a down payment, and Iranian engineers began building an assembly plant near Sirjan and a test range near Rafsanjan. The project ended with the revolution in February 1979, and the Israeli engineers went home. [cite:ir1_wiki_flower] The deal became public in April 1986, when The New York Times reported on documents left behind by Israeli diplomats in Tehran; according to the report, "the Israelis told the Iranians that the missiles could be fitted with nuclear warheads," and Iranian and Israeli officials interviewed did not deny that the documents were authentic. [cite:ir1_djn_flower] The Israeli journalist Ronen Bergman later reported that Israel planned to supply Iran with technology that was already obsolete; that is his account, and the Shah's side did not live to test it. [cite:ir1_wiki_flower]

Public distance, private alliance

The Shah kept the relationship out of sight. Iran never granted de jure recognition, and distance from Israel, in Iranica's assessment, was one of his main tools for keeping relations with Arab states in order. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] He acknowledged the ties when it suited him. In July 1960 he publicly reaffirmed Iran's recognition of Israel, and Nasser broke relations with Iran in response. [cite:ir1_iranica_israel] In a 1975 interview he openly acknowledged military and intelligence ties with Israel, justified them by Arab hostility in Nasser's time, and added that "the situation has changed," complaining of Israeli press attacks on Iran. [cite:ir1_alpher_fa] Golda Meir's visit to Tehran in July 1965, which began the pipeline negotiations described in the next section, was kept secret at Iran's insistence. [cite:ir1_granta_khalili]

The 1973 oil question

Earlier versions of this library contain two incompatible accounts of the October 1973 war. One says the Shah "joined the Arab-led OPEC oil embargo," cutting off oil to Israel. The other says Iran stayed out and kept Israel supplied. Neither is quite what the sources show. The embargo was declared by the Organisation of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, of which Iran, not an Arab state, was not a member. Standard histories of the crisis list Iran among the producers that did not join and record that it increased production during the embargo and sold at the higher prices; the Shah was the leading advocate of those higher prices. [cite:ir1_wiki_1973oil] Alpher, writing in 2015, nonetheless states that the Shah joined the embargo and cut shipments to Israel. [cite:ir1_alpher_fa] That account is contradicted by the embargo's own membership, and the claim of an Iranian embargo on Israel in 1973 does not appear in the other sources examined here.

The practical question, whether Iranian crude reached Israel during the three weeks of fighting, is harder. On 6 October 1973 Egypt's navy blockaded the Bab al-Mandeb strait at the mouth of the Red Sea, which, as the political economist Helen Thompson notes, cut Israel off from its main supplier, Iran, since tankers bound for Eilat had to pass through it; by then Israel also drew oil from the Sinai fields it had occupied in 1967. [cite:ir1_thompson_energy] The claim, also found in earlier volumes, that the Eilat–Ashkelon pipeline was Israel's "wartime lifeline" in 1973 therefore has its logic backwards. What the record supports is narrower: Iran did not join the embargo, and its oil sales to Israel continued before and after the war, until the last shipments in 1978, which are the subject of the arbitration described in the next section. [cite:ir1_wiki_1973oil] [cite:ir1_toi_2016] [cite:ir1_shomrim_2024] During the war itself the Shah sent a congratulatory telegram to Egypt's President Sadat and allowed six Soviet supply planes to cross Iran to Iraq, then revoked permission for a further thirty-two, according to the recollection of his deputy foreign minister. [cite:ir1_iranwire_1973]

The alliance ended with the Shah. How the revolution of 1979 treated it, and what the new government said about Israel, is the subject of Part Two. [scene:ir2_revolution|The revolution]
