Dealers, a State, a Psychologist's Institute and the Scholars

From the villages, most of the books went to Cairo and into the antiquities trade. One of the finders, Bahij Ali, escorted by the antiquities dealer Dhaki Basta, offered several to a shop in the Shepheard's Hotel and then to a Cypriot dealer, Phokion Tano, who bought the lot. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] Most of the first codex took a different road. A Belgian dealer, Albert Eid, exported it from Egypt, illegally, and in 1949 offered it for sale in New York and Ann Arbor. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:gn_pearse_jung] He found no buyer in America.

The buyer, in the end, was an institute of psychology. After Eid's death his widow sold the codex to the C. G. Jung Institute in Zurich; Robinson dates the sale to 10 May 1952, while Wikipedia gives 1951. The price, according to Robinson, was 35,000 Swiss francs, then worth $8,009. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:gn_pearse_jung] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] It was intended as a birthday present for Jung, who in 1916 had privately published a work of his own, the Seven Sermons to the Dead, in the name of Basilides of Alexandria. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt24_wiki_septem] On 15 November 1953 the Dutch scholar Gilles Quispel brought the codex from Utrecht and it was presented at a public event in Zurich, where the institute's C. A. Meier "baptized" it the Jung Codex. Two weeks later the city's Central Library declined to take it, and it was put in a safe deposit box in the basement of the Leu Bank. [cite:gn_pearse_jung]

The Egyptian state took the rest. The Department of Antiquities seized Tano's collection to stop it being exported, and after the revolution of July 1952 the codices were declared national property. Under Nasser the collection was nationalised, and the compensation paid was four thousand Egyptian pounds. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:gn_bar_robinson] The Jung Codex leaves stayed in Zurich, in the bank, for twenty-two years. On 12 October 1975 they were taken from the Leu Bank and deposited in the Coptic Museum in Cairo, reuniting the library in one place. [cite:gn_pearse_jung]

Possession of the books was one thing. Access to their contents was another, and for three decades it was controlled by a small number of scholars. The first to see them were French: Jean and Marianne Doresse, Henri-Charles Puech and the antiquities official Étienne Drioton. After the break in relations in 1952, Puech kept French leadership by working through UNESCO in Paris, with the help of Antoine Guillaumont. [cite:gn_bar_robinson] The pattern of the following years, in Robinson's own words, was "attempts to monopolize them". [cite:gn_bar_robinson] Scholars who held the rights to a text wanted to publish it first, with a full commentary, and in the meantime others could not see it; according to Pearse's summary, they obstructed publication. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] The Jung Codex itself was published in 1957 without some of its pages, which followed in a supplementary volume in 1961. [cite:gn_bar_robinson]

The monopoly was broken in the 1970s, and the man most responsible was Robinson. Using UNESCO contacts, he helped bring about an International Committee, which first met in Cairo in December 1970, and a complete photographic facsimile of the codices, published by Brill from 1972, with supplements in 1979 and 1984. [cite:gn_bar_robinson] [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] In 1977 his team published The Nag Hammadi Library in English, which, he said, "marked the end of one stage of Nag Hammadi scholarship and the beginning of another". [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] Robinson argued for free public access to the material; even so, the scholarly articles on the find published in 1979 were, Pearse notes, copyrighted and "accessible to almost no-one". [cite:gn_pearse_nh] The critical edition of the whole library was completed only in 1996. [cite:gn_bar_robinson]

Thirty-two years passed between the breaking of the jar and the first complete English translation. For most of that time the books were held by dealers, a bank, a state and a circle of experts, and ordinary readers could not read them. The Apocryphon of John, which curses anyone who exchanges its teaching for a gift, was bought, nationalised and kept behind a scholarly gate for a generation. [cite:vt24_apjohn] The gate was different from the one the bishops had built. The pattern was the same: a small group controlled access to the knowledge, and the rest waited until that group decided to release it.
