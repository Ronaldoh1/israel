The Papal Loans, 1794–1870

The Rothschild family's dealings with Rome are documented, and the documents are about debt. Between 1831 and the 1850s the government of the Papal States, which the popes ruled as kings until 1870, borrowed through the Rothschild banking houses. The pope was the borrower: money ran from the bank to the papal treasury, and interest ran back. This section sets out what the record shows, what it does not, and how loan contracts became a story that "the Rothschilds control the Pope".

The pope's bankers before the Rothschilds

The Papal States already had a banking family of their own. Giovanni Torlonia, a Roman banker, was made Duke of Bracciano by Pius VI in 1794 for administering the finances of the Holy See, and Pius VII made him Prince of Civitella Cesi in 1803. [cite:vt7_wiki_torlonia] His son Alessandro Torlonia (1800–1886) took over the family bank and, in the words of the historian Daniela Felisini, "became the Pope's banker". [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4] Felisini's 2016 study, based in part on the Rothschild bank's Paris archives, is the main scholarly account of the papal debt in this period. [cite:vt11_tv_felisini] The Torlonia family's marriages and its place among the Roman nobility, including the 1823 marriage that joined a Torlonia daughter to the Orsini, are covered in [scene:vt7_orsini_record|Part Seven] and are not repeated here.

Why the pope borrowed in 1831

The first Rothschild loan followed a political crisis. In early 1831 revolts broke out in the northern provinces of the Papal States, the Legations around Bologna. Austrian troops put them down and withdrew on 15 July 1831; by December much of the territory was again in revolt, Cardinal Giuseppe Albani called the Austrians back, and they retook Bologna. France answered by seizing the port of Ancona, which it held until the Austrians left in 1838. [cite:vt11_ce_gregory16] Felisini's chapter on the period describes how the uprisings strained the papal budget and how Torlonia, in partnership with James de Rothschild of the Paris house, brought new methods to the management of the public debt. [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4]

The 1831–1832 loan

The agreement was negotiated by Alessandro Torlonia on behalf of the Holy See with James de Rothschild, signed on 30 November 1831 and put into effect in 1832, under Pope Gregory XVI. Wikipedia's account, drawing on Felisini and older works, gives the amount as £400,000 and refers to "the original 5% contract, signed by Rothschild and Torlonia". [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906, whose article on the family includes a table of Rothschild loans, lists a loan to the "Papal States" in 1831 of 16,000,000 francs. [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] The two figures do not match, and neither comes from the contract itself, which this volume has not seen; the amount should be read as reported, not established. James's brother Carl von Rothschild, who ran the family's house in Naples, travelled to Rome and was received by Gregory XVI in January 1832, when he was given the insignia of the Constantinian Order of Saint George. [cite:vt11_wiki_carl] [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans]

The Rothschild Archive in London, the family's own historical archive, states that the Naples house served "the Papal administration in Rome, on whose behalf five loans were issued between 1831 and 1850". [cite:wa_ra_naples] It does not give their terms. Felisini names James in Paris as Torlonia's partner on the first loan; Wikipedia names Carl in Naples as the family's man in Rome. [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4] [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans]

The 1834 dispute and the loans that followed

The second episode on record shows competition, not control. In July 1834 Cardinal Antonio Tosti became papal treasurer and tried to refinance the debt on better terms. According to Wikipedia's summary of Felisini, the Rothschilds opposed an early refinancing, Tosti approached a group of rival Paris banks, and the two sides were eventually reconciled and shared the new loan. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] The Jewish Encyclopedia tells a similar story from the Rothschild side: after a rival syndicate won a Sardinian loan in 1834, the Rothschilds used their usual tactic of driving down the rivals' bonds, "with the result that the next papal loan was financed by them". [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] Earlier platform material also claimed that Rothschild records exposed embezzlement by Tosti; no source for that was found, and it is not used.

The Encyclopedia's table lists a further papal loan in 1845 of 2,160,000. [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] After the revolution of 1848, the short-lived Roman Republic of 1849 and the restoration of Pius IX by French troops, the papal government borrowed again from the same houses in the early 1850s. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] The historian John Pollard, in his study of papal finance from 1850 to 1950, shows that the budget reforms of Cardinal Giacomo Antonelli, Pius IX's Secretary of State, depended on borrowing from the Rothschilds, a reliance that many contemporaries found unseemly. [cite:vt11_ehnet_pollard]

What the loans paid for is documented only in general terms. The 1831 loan met the deficit opened by the revolts and the cost of order in the Legations; the loans of the 1850s financed a restored government that depended on French troops. [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4] [cite:vt11_ehnet_pollard] The line-by-line uses of the money are in Felisini's archival work, which this volume has seen only in abstract, and are not stated here.

Two events ended the relationship. The Naples house ceased trading in 1863, after Garibaldi took Naples (the Jewish Encyclopedia dates its closure to 1861). [cite:wa_ra_naples] [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] Then, in 1870, the Kingdom of Italy took Rome, and the Papal States, the borrower of all five loans, ceased to exist. [cite:vt11_jvl_rome] How Rome fell and what the Holy See refused afterwards is explained in [scene:vt2_roman_question|Part Two].

The "exclusivity clause": one source, graded

Earlier material on this platform states that the 1831 contract contained a "secret clause" giving the Rothschilds an "exclusive right of first refusal on all Vatican sovereign debt", co-signed by Alessandro Torlonia and "enforced" in 1834. The claim can be traced. Wikipedia's article on the loans describes a narrower clause: the Holy See was to inform the Rothschilds before approaching another firm for a new loan and to give them preference if a rival offered equal terms. It footnotes this to Ignatius Balla, The Romance of the Rothschilds (London, 1913), pages 228–229, and places the clause after Carl von Rothschild's visit to Rome in the wake of the 1834 dispute. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] Balla's book is a popular biography of the family, not a work of financial history. [cite:vt11_tamu_balla] No second source for the clause was found: it is not in the Rothschild Archive's account, the Jewish Encyclopedia's account of the papal loans or the published abstract of Felisini's archival study. [cite:wa_ra_naples] [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4] This volume could not open Balla's text at the cited pages.

Graded: not established. Even in Balla's form, the clause was a preference on equal terms, not exclusivity. It could bind only the government of the Papal States, which ended in 1870. The documented events of 1834 also run against an exclusive right, since the papal treasurer was able to approach other banks at all. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] "Right of first refusal on all Vatican sovereign debt" goes beyond the one source the claim rests on.

How a loan became a trope

The loans drew attacks from the start, and much of the anger was about the lender's religion. Felisini notes that the first loan was controversial for its financial terms and also because Rothschild was a Jewish banker. [cite:vt11_felisini_ch4] Reports that Carl von Rothschild had been allowed to kiss the pope's hand rather than his foot provoked complaints in Catholic circles that the pope was selling Rome to "a Jew". [cite:vt11_wiki_carl] The French poet Alfred de Vigny is quoted as writing that "a Jew now reigns over the Pope"; the German writer Ludwig Börne mocked the loan; the Roman dialect poet Giuseppe Gioachino Belli wrote a sonnet that blamed the pope as much as the bankers. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] Vigny's remark is quoted here from that secondary account; this volume did not locate it in his own journal.

Two things happened later in the century that set the remark into a durable form. In January 1882 the Union Générale, a Paris bank founded to serve Catholic and conservative investors, collapsed; its founder blamed "Jewish-German banks and Freemasons", a conspiracy that historians do not accept. [cite:vt11_wiki_crash1882] In 1886 Édouard Drumont's La France juive, which went through 140 printings in two years, blamed the crash on the Rothschilds. [cite:vt11_wiki_francejuive] The Jewish Encyclopedia of 1906 closed its account of the episode with the remark that, by "a somewhat curious sequel", the Rothschilds were "at the present time … the guardians of the papal treasure". [cite:vt11_je_rothschild] The sentence gives no source, date or detail, and no Holy See document found for this volume describes any such custody; it is an aside in an encyclopaedia, not evidence of a role, and it is the source of the phrase later quoted on this platform.

The idea that "the Rothschilds control the Pope" belongs to a wider family of stories that present one Jewish banking family as the hidden power behind governments, a theme the historian Mike Rothschild, who is not related to the family, traces through two centuries of antisemitic conspiracy theory. [cite:vt7_pw_lasers] It should be named for what it is: an antisemitic trope built on a real but ordinary fact, that a government short of money borrowed from the largest bankers of its day. The record also shows the limits of the lenders' leverage. The Rothschilds pressed, through the Austrian chancellor Metternich, for relief for the Jews of the Papal States, including those of Ancona, from special taxes, property restrictions in the ghetto and bans on professions. [cite:vt11_wiki_papalloans] Rome's ghetto, where Jews had lived for almost three hundred years, was dismantled only in 1870, by the Italian state after the papal government had fallen. [cite:vt11_jvl_rome] Whether the documented ties ran on beyond 1870, as the "unbroken chain" claims, is the subject of [scene:vt11_chain_tested|the next section].
