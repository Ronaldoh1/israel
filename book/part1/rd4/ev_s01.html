"No One Wants Them": Évian, Bermuda and the Closing Doors

In July 1938, delegates from 32 countries gathered at Évian to decide what to do about the refugees fleeing Nazi rule. President Roosevelt had convened the conference after Germany annexed Austria earlier that year. It met from 6 to 15 July. Nearly every delegation expressed sympathy, and nearly every delegation declined to take more people. [cite:wiki_evian]

The reasons they gave were specific. Britain's delegate, Lord Winterton, said the United Kingdom "is not a country of immigration." France claimed it had reached saturation. Brazil asked for certificates of Christian baptism. Canada wanted only experienced farmers. The United States, whose president had called the meeting, offered only to fill its existing German–Austrian quota, which had previously gone unfilled. [cite:wiki_evian] [cite:newhumanitarian_evian] [cite:facinghistory_evian]

These were not governments that had run out of options. They were governments doing what they chose to do, which in most cases was very little. Only one country offered to take a large number: the Dominican Republic, ruled by the dictator Rafael Trujillo, whose soldiers had massacred thousands of Haitians the year before. In Germany, the Nazi party's newspaper drew its own conclusion from the proceedings: "No one wants them." Chaim Weizmann told the Manchester Guardian that the world was now divided into places where Jews could not live and places they could not enter.

One possible destination was kept out of the discussion altogether. At Britain's insistence, any mention of Palestine as a destination was excluded from the agenda. Golda Meir attended as the Jewish observer from Palestine, but she was not permitted to participate. [cite:holocaust_au_evian] [cite:wiki_evian]

Five years later the question returned, in far worse circumstances. In April 1943, with the extermination of Europe's Jews under way, the United States and Britain met again, this time in Bermuda. A program proposing that Britain open Palestine to refugees was not considered. The conference produced little and was widely judged ineffective. [cite:usf_bermuda_evian]

The two conferences framed the problem on which the rest of this period would turn. When nearly every country refused to take Jewish refugees, the argument that Jews needed a place of their own gained force. At the same time Britain, which had kept Palestine off the agenda at Évian, held the one door the Zionist movement was pushing against. Both facts belong in any account of how the state of 1948 came about: the refusal of the many, and the contest over the one. The people who could go nowhere else would soon try to reach Palestine by other means, a story told in the section on [scene:ab_s01|Aliyah Bet].
