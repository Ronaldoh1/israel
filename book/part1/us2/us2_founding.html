Two Founding Plans: A Written Constitution and the Basic Laws

The two states rest on different kinds of founding law. The United States has a single written constitution, drafted in 1787, with a fixed and deliberately difficult method of change. Under Article V an amendment must be proposed by two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or by a convention called at the request of two-thirds of the state legislatures, and then ratified by three-quarters of the states. In more than two centuries that bar has been cleared 27 times, most recently in 1992. [cite:us2_annenberg_art5] Israel has no single document of this kind. Its Declaration of Independence of May 1948 promised a constitution adopted by an elected assembly "not later than the 1st October 1948". The date passed in wartime, the assembly elected in January 1949 renamed itself the Knesset, and on 13 June 1950, after nine debates, the Knesset adopted the Harari Resolution: the constitution would be written in chapters, each a separate Basic Law, with no deadline. [cite:rs_rd6_dickerman_2007] The Road to 1948 volume tells that story in full. [scene:rd6_constitution|The Road to 1948: the constitution that was deferred]

What the Basic Laws are, and how hard they are to change

The chapters arrived slowly. The first, on the Knesset, came in 1958; others followed on Israel Lands (1960), the President (1964), the Government (1968, replaced in 1992 and restored in 2001), the State Economy (1975), the Military (1976), Jerusalem (1980), the Judiciary (1984), the State Comptroller (1988), Human Dignity and Liberty (1992), Freedom of Occupation (1994), Referendum (2014) and Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People (2018). [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws] On 13 July 2026 the Knesset added a fourteenth, Basic Law: Torah Study, by 63 votes to 52. It declares Torah study a "foundational value" of the Jewish people and the state; Haredi parties pressed for it as a shield for their members' broad exemption from military service, and the Movement for Quality Government petitioned the High Court to strike it down the same day. [cite:us2_toi_torahlaw] Sources written before July 2026 therefore count thirteen Basic Laws, and some count differently still; the count used here, fourteen as of October 2026, includes the 2026 law. Part Nineteen returns to the draft. [scene:us19_service|Part Nineteen: who serves]

A common description holds that any Basic Law can be changed by a simple majority of the Knesset. That is true of most, and it is the decisive difference from the American document, but it is not true of all. Basic Law: Freedom of Occupation says it "shall not be varied except by a Basic Law passed by a majority of the members of the Knesset", that is, 61 of 120 regardless of how many are present, and its override clause lets a conflicting ordinary law stand only if passed by that same absolute majority, stated expressly, and only for four years at a time. [cite:us2_jvl_occupation] The electoral-system clause of Basic Law: The Knesset is likewise amendable only by 61 members, and the Referendum law requires a treaty approved by 80 members or a referendum before territory under Israeli law is given up. [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws] [cite:us2a_wiki_basiclaws] Even so, the strongest of these locks is a majority of a single chamber. The 2018 Nation-State Law passed by 62 to 55 and the 2023 reasonableness amendment by 64 to 0 after the opposition walked out. [cite:basiclaw_britannica] Nothing in the Basic Laws resembles the American requirement that three-quarters of the states agree.

The Basic Laws acquired their force over ordinary law from the courts, not from a single founding act. After the two rights laws of 1992, both passed in thin chambers, the Supreme Court held in the Bank Mizrahi case of 1995 that an ordinary law violating their limitation clauses is void, and that the Knesset acts as a constituent body when it passes Basic Laws, relying on the Harari Resolution. [cite:us2_wiki_basiclaws] That judgment is the root of the dispute over the courts that the next section follows. [scene:us2_courts|The courts and the overhaul]

Executive and legislature: separation against fusion

The two systems also join or divide power differently. The American president is elected separately from Congress for a fixed term, and Congress, in two chambers, can neither dismiss a president for policy reasons nor be dissolved by one; the founding design sets the branches against each other. Israel's is a parliamentary system in which the government needs the Knesset's confidence. Voters elect only the Knesset, in a single nationwide district by proportional representation with a 3.25 per cent threshold, and no party has ever won the 61 seats needed to govern alone. [cite:knesset_idi] Every government has therefore been a coalition. The president, elected by the Knesset for a single seven-year term, tasks the member most likely to command a majority with forming one, with 28 days and a possible 14 more; if every attempt fails the country votes again. [cite:president_time] [cite:pm_brookings] That arithmetic produced five elections between April 2019 and November 2022, and it gives small parties weight beyond their size, as the Torah Study law shows. [cite:pm_brookings] Israel votes next on 27 October 2026, a week before the American midterm elections of 3 November. [cite:gi11_reuters_rzp2026] Part Three compares the two electoral systems. [scene:us3_elections|Part Three: elections]

Federal against central

The third difference is where power sits geographically. The American constitution lists the federal government's powers and, in the Tenth Amendment of 1791, leaves the rest to others: "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." [cite:us2_annenberg_10th] Fifty states therefore run their own courts, criminal law, schools, elections and much of health and welfare policy, which is why many later parts of this book report American figures that vary widely by state. Israel is a unitary state with a single tier of local government: 258 local authorities in 2021, made up of 76 municipalities, 126 local councils and 56 regional councils. In 2020 they accounted for 12.8 per cent of public spending and 5.9 per cent of GDP, and 54 per cent of their revenue came from grants rather than their own taxes. Councils' bylaws and budgets require the approval of the Interior Ministry, which also approves their borrowing with the Finance Ministry. [cite:us2_sngwofi_israel] Their main own tax, the arnona, is levied on the floor area of property rather than its value, and an OECD review recommended moving over time to a tax based on property value. [cite:us2_oecd_localfin] What that means for Arab towns' budgets and planning is a matter for Parts Eight and Fifteen. [scene:us8_israel|Part Eight: equality among Israel's citizens] [scene:us15_land|Part Fifteen: land]

The structural comparison is a snapshot: the documents and rules are what they are as of October 2026, and no comparable series measures them over time. What can be measured over time is how each structure has handled the conflicts it was built to contain, which the remaining sections take up through the courts, emergency powers and the integrity of the public service.
