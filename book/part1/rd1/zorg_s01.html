The Machinery: How the Zionist Organization Worked, 1897–1917

The organization founded at Basel is sometimes remembered as a loose collection of enthusiasts without formal structure, a mood more than a movement. The record shows the opposite. Within two years of Basel the Zionist Organization had a written constitution, an elected parliament, a standing executive, a council of national leaders, national branches in the United States and Britain, and a bank. By 1917 it had a land fund, an office in Palestine and a recognized channel to the British government.

Membership was open, and its dues were small. Any individual or group that accepted the Basel Program and paid an annual due, called the "shekel", could join.[cite:palquest_wzo] The sources consulted here do not give its amount or the number of shekel-payers in these years. The principle, however, is clear. The organization was built on mass membership by small annual dues.

Authority flowed from the Congress. Its constitution, adopted at the Third Congress in 1899, created two governing bodies. The Smaller Actions Committee, with seven members, was the executive, and its chairman was the president of the Congress. The Larger Actions Committee, with 37 members, was a council of national leaders.[cite:palquest_wzo][cite:ismi_israel_yearbook] The Congress itself met every year until 1901 and every two years after that; the Fifth Congress of 1901 made the change.[cite:palquest_wzo][cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] Below the central bodies stood national federations, country-level organizations that recruited members and raised money. The Federation of American Zionists was founded in 1898 and later became the Zionist Organization of America. The English Zionist Federation followed in 1899.[cite:w_zf_gb]

The organization's tools followed one another quickly. The Jewish Colonial Trust, its bank, was established in 1899; the Jewish National Fund, to buy land, in 1901; and the Palestine Office in Jaffa in 1908.[cite:palquest_wzo] The mechanism was simple. The Congress, a body of delegates, decided policy. The small executive carried it out between sessions. The federations supplied members and money, and the financial and land instruments turned that money into assets in Palestine. What the machinery could not do is equally plain. It held no governing authority in Palestine, which remained Ottoman territory until 1917, and the consent of governments was still, as the Basel Program itself acknowledged, something to be obtained.

The Congresses between 1898 and 1913 show the organization working out what it was for, often by open argument and recorded vote. The Second Congress, at Basel in 1898, laid the groundwork for the Jewish Colonial Trust. The Third, at Basel in 1899, resolved that the Trust's funds could be spent only in Palestine or Syria, an early decision that bound the movement's money to one region before any territorial alternative had been offered.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] The Fourth Congress met in London in 1900; there religious Zionists led by Isaac Jacob Reines urged that the organization keep a purely political focus. The Fifth, back at Basel in 1901, established the Jewish National Fund. At the same Congress a group called the Democratic Faction, among them Leo Motzkin, Martin Buber and Chaim Weizmann, pressed for Hebrew culture and for more democracy inside the organization. It was also this Congress that moved the meetings to every other year.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b]

The organization's sharpest crisis came over territory. The Sixth Congress, at Basel in 1903, voted "295 in favor, 178 against and 98 abstentions" to send a commission to examine settlement in East Africa, a British offer told in full in [scene:s04e|the East Africa offer].[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] Herzl died the next year. The Seventh Congress, at Basel in 1905, voted against a national home outside Palestine and its vicinity. Israel Zangwill's Territorialists walked out, and David Wolffsohn became president.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] The decision settled that the organization's aim was Palestine, and it was taken by a vote of the Congress, not decreed by a leader.

The next argument was about method. At the Eighth Congress, held at The Hague in 1907, delegates debated "practical" against "political" Zionism. The Congress supported practical work, including a Palestine branch of the organization under Arthur Ruppin, and Weizmann's "synthetic Zionism" gained ground.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b] That branch became the Palestine Office, whose work is traced in [scene:s04g|the Palestine Office]. At the Ninth Congress, in Hamburg in 1909, the practical camp challenged Wolffsohn's leadership. At the Tenth, at Basel in 1911, the first session was held in Hebrew, and the botanist Otto Warburg succeeded Wolffsohn as president. The Eleventh Congress, in Vienna in 1913, backed the founding of a Hebrew University in Jerusalem, which opened twelve years later.[cite:rs_rd1_jvl_congresses_b]

Taken together, these meetings trace a shift in the organization's center of gravity. In its first years it sought a charter through diplomacy and built the financial means to use one. After Herzl's death and the East Africa split, it moved toward building in Palestine directly, while the Democratic Faction's push for Hebrew culture surfaced in the first Hebrew session of 1911 and the university resolution of 1913. The movement's leadership changed in the same direction, from Herzl the diplomat, to Wolffsohn, whose leadership the practical camp challenged in 1909, to Warburg.

The First World War cut across this structure. Before the war the organization's head offices were in Germany, and when war came it declared itself neutral. Its effective leadership passed to London, where Chaim Weizmann, a Manchester chemist and president of the English Zionist Federation, and Nahum Sokolow became its de facto leaders.[cite:ismi_israel_yearbook][cite:weizmann_papers_v9] The English Federation, founded in 1899 as one national branch among several, thus became in wartime the movement's channel to the British government.

That channel was contested within British Jewry itself. In May 1917 the community's official representative bodies, the Board of Deputies and the Anglo-Jewish Association, published an anti-Zionist statement in The Times. Days later the English Zionist Federation held a conference in London.[cite:jpost_why_rothschild] The episode explains a detail of the Balfour Declaration. Because British Jewry's official bodies opposed Zionism, the declaration was not addressed to them. It was instead addressed to Lord Rothschild, for transmission to the Zionist Federation.[cite:jpost_why_rothschild] The declaration itself, and the wider wartime context of competing promises, belong to the next stage of this book: [scene:s06|the Balfour Declaration].

The record here leaves real gaps. It does not give the size of the shekel, the number of members in any year, or the federations' budgets, so the scale of the money that flowed through this structure before 1917 cannot be stated from these sources. It does not show how the 37 seats of the Larger Actions Committee were apportioned among countries, or how far the seven-member executive in practice controlled the Congress rather than answering to it. It does not explain how the neutral organization's German headquarters and its London leaders divided authority during the war. None of these gaps touches the main point: by 1917 the movement was a constituted, voting, dues-paying organization, and its wartime leaders sat in the capital whose decision mattered most.
