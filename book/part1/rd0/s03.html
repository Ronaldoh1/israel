Dreyfus, Herzl and Der Judenstaat, 1894–1896

In December 1894 a French court-martial convicted Captain Alfred Dreyfus of treason. Dreyfus was a Jewish officer of the French army, born in Alsace. He was convicted on forged evidence; at its centre was the bordereau, a note supposed to prove that he had been spying for Germany. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] Among the journalists who covered the case was the Paris correspondent of the Neue Freie Presse of Vienna, Theodor Herzl. Fourteen months after the conviction Herzl published Der Judenstaat, the pamphlet that became the founding text of political Zionism. [cite:herzl_diaries] [cite:herzl_judenstaat]

A familiar account holds that Zionism arose from ancient scripture. As a hope, the return to Zion was much older than Herzl, and other sections of this book trace that hope and the rabbis who first argued for practical settlement. As an organized political movement, though, Zionism took shape in response to a specific, documented modern antisemitism, and it was contested inside Jewish life from the moment it appeared. The way to see this is to follow the sequence closely: the case, the man, the two years between them, and the text itself.

The case. The bordereau was later shown to have been written by another officer, Ferdinand Esterhazy. Once his guilt became clear, the army's General Staff protected him rather than admit that it had convicted an innocent man. Dreyfus was sent to Devil's Island. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] That protection, as much as the original false conviction, is what turned the case into the defining scandal of its era. The Affair divided France into Dreyfusards and anti-Dreyfusards, a rupture that shaped French politics into the twentieth century. Its resolution came after this chapter closes: Émile Zola's open letter "J'accuse!", published in L'Aurore in January 1898, forced the frame-up into public view, and Dreyfus was fully rehabilitated in 1906, twelve years after his conviction. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] The record shows a named culprit, a protecting institution and a documented effort to cover the error. It does not show that the whole French army or government was complicit. Zola and many others who exposed the frame-up were French and not Jewish, and the correction came from inside France as well as from outside it.

Why France mattered. The setting gave the case its force. France had emancipated its Jews in 1791, the first European state to do so. A century later the same country produced the most publicized antisemitic trial of the nineteenth century, with crowds in the streets crying against the Jews while the case ran. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] French Jews had built substantial civic and professional lives in the intervening century, and emancipation had not been meaningless. That is exactly why the Affair felt like a rupture rather than a continuation. Herzl drew the conclusion that if antisemitism could survive in emancipated, republican Paris, no Jewish community in Europe was structurally safe. [cite:wiki_dreyfus]

The man. Herzl was born on 2 May 1860 in Pest, later part of Budapest, to an assimilated, German-speaking Jewish family; he was educated in German and was culturally German-Austrian. The family moved to Vienna in 1878, where he entered the university to study law. He took his doctorate in 1884 and turned almost at once to journalism and playwriting instead of legal practice. In 1891 the Neue Freie Presse, one of the most influential German-language papers in Europe, made him its Paris correspondent. [cite:herzl_diaries] He was secular in outlook and not religiously observant. He did not receive Zionism ready-made from Jewish tradition. His diaries, published after his death, record him constructing it step by step.

The ceremony and the crowd. The usual story says that Herzl saw Dreyfus publicly degraded, heard the crowd, and became a Zionist on the spot. The sources for this book differ on the details of that moment, and the differences are worth stating. The short account says Herzl watched "Death to the Jews" chanted in the streets of Paris. [cite:wiki_dreyfus] The fuller account in the research on his diaries says he heard the crowd shouting "À bas les Juifs!" ("Down with the Jews!"), which is a different phrase, and dates his attendance at the degradation ceremony to 22 December 1894. [cite:herzl_diaries] Neither the wording nor the date has yet been checked against a primary source for this book, so both versions are given here. What the accounts agree on is that Herzl was present and recorded the moment.

The fourteen months. The diaries show that Herzl's thinking did not settle overnight. Between the conviction in December 1894 and the pamphlet in February 1896 he worked through several answers. During 1895 he considered mass conversion of Jews to Catholicism as a way to end antisemitism structurally; he considered social integration through political engagement; and, increasingly, he came to see territorial concentration as the only stable solution. [cite:herzl_diaries] What the Affair did was fix a diagnosis. In Herzl's reading, European antisemitism was not a leftover religious prejudice that modern life would wear away. It survived assimilation and survived the successful entry of Jews into professional and cultural life. Dreyfus was the very picture of that success, an officer of the French army who had married into the elite, and he had still been convicted on forged evidence, with his public humiliation cheered. [cite:herzl_diaries] If safety could not be had inside European societies, Herzl concluded, it had to be had outside them, in a separate polity. That was the argument of Der Judenstaat.

This is the strongest form of the case Herzl made, and it should be stated in its own terms. It was an argument about physical and civic safety, made against a record that included the pogroms and May Laws in the Russian Empire and now a show trial in the first European state to emancipate its Jews. [cite:wiki_may_laws] [cite:wiki_dreyfus] It was also narrower than the later founding story, which presents the state as the fulfilment of an ancient religious commitment. Herzl's proposal answered a specific European failure of the 1890s and turned it into a permanent political program. The religious and historical language that later surrounded it came, in large part, from others.

The diary of June 1895. One passage from these months has become central to a lasting dispute. In an entry dated 12 June 1895, Herzl wrote:

"We shall try to spirit the penniless population across the border by procuring employment for it in the transit countries, while denying it any employment in our own country... Both the process of expropriation and the removal of the poor must be carried out discreetly and circumspectly."

The entry was not published in Herzl's lifetime. It appeared in the Complete Diaries edited by Raphael Patai in 1960. [cite:herzl_diaries] Its meaning is contested. Benny Morris, in Righteous Victims, and Nur Masalha, in Expulsion of the Palestinians, treat it as evidence that Herzl was aware early on that the Zionist project would involve moving the existing population. Other scholars have argued that the passage has been misread, or that it referred to a population other than the Arabs of Palestine. [cite:herzl_diaries] The research behind this book goes further than the evidence allows at one point: it states as undisputed that Herzl publicly assured Ottoman officials that Jewish settlement would benefit the local Arab population while privately entertaining plans for their displacement. That conclusion depends on reading the 1895 entry as Morris and Masalha read it. Scholars who read the entry differently would not accept it, so it belongs inside the dispute, not above it.

What the pamphlet says. Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State, or The Jews' State) was published in Vienna on 14 February 1896. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] It is not a religious text. It reads as a work of political engineering, close at times to a business prospectus. Herzl proposed a "Society of Jews" to act as the political leadership and a "Jewish Company", a chartered economic-development corporation, to carry out the practical work. He proposed an orderly transfer of population, a seven-hour working day, a flag and a bank: the administrative furniture of a nineteenth-century European nation-state, applied to the Jewish question. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] He used "Jew" as a national category rather than a religious one and said that irreligious Jews would be included on equal terms. He rejected rabbinic authority as a principle for organizing the state. The text makes no claim to fulfil prophecy, uses no messianic framing and asserts no divine right. [cite:herzl_judenstaat]

Above all, the pamphlet did not treat Palestine as the only or obvious answer. Herzl put the question directly: "Shall we choose Palestine or Argentine? We shall take what is given us, and what is selected by Jewish public opinion." [cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] Of Argentina he wrote that it was "by natural resources one of the richest countries on earth, comprising a vast extent of territory, a sparse population, and a mild climate." [cite:herzl_judenstaat] The country was then recruiting European immigrants and offering wide, thinly settled land, and Baron de Hirsch's Jewish Colonization Association had already planted Jewish colonies there (see the preceding section). One summary of the text adds that Herzl expected Argentina's government to "gladly provide" land, and that he later wrote that Palestine would give "the Jewish problem" a solution "not only just, but brilliant." [cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896]

How much weight Herzl gave each country is itself read in two ways. One account in the research holds that he treated Argentina and Palestine with roughly equal weight. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Another holds that he considered Argentina first and Palestine second, preferring Palestine for practical and political reasons, chiefly that it would move Jewish public opinion, rather than religious ones. [cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] The Israeli writer and peace activist Uri Avnery went further: in his reading, Herzl "had obviously thought very little about Palestine as a concrete entity" and inserted Palestine into his blueprint as an afterthought. [cite:herzl_judenstaat_1896] Avnery's is an interpretation, not a fact about the text. What the text plainly shows is that at its founding moment political Zionism was open about territory.

One correction is needed. Part of the material behind this book says that Der Judenstaat weighed Uganda as a candidate. It did not. The British offer of land in East Africa, known as the "Uganda" scheme, came in 1903, seven years later, and is told in [scene:s04f|the Uganda offer of 1903]. [cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] The pamphlet's alternatives were Argentina and Palestine.

From pamphlet to movement. A program needs patrons, and in 1896 Herzl looked for two. From that year he sought a charter from the Ottoman Sultan, offering help with the empire's public debt; his diary records a message relayed from Abdul Hamid II that the Sultan could not sell even a foot of the land, because it belonged not to him but to his people. [cite:w_herzl] He did not meet the Sultan until May 1901, and then obtained no charter. [cite:w_abdulhamid] In July 1896 he asked Baron Edmond de Rothschild, the patron of the colonies in Palestine, to back a state secured by charter rather than colonies secured by purchase, and was refused. His diary records his "rude telegram to Edmond Rothschild" and his resolve that "from Rothschild's negative attitude I must salvage everything." [cite:herzl_diaries_vol2_archive] That refusal, and a different version of how it happened, are told in [scene:bc_s04|Edmond de Rothschild's colonies]. Herzl turned instead to convening a congress.

What followed, in brief. The First Zionist Congress met at Basel on 29–31 August 1897, with 200 delegates from 17 countries, and founded the World Zionist Organization. Herzl wrote in his diary afterwards: "At Basel I founded the Jewish State. Perhaps in five years, certainly in fifty, everyone will see it." (Another rendering of the same entry, dated 3 September 1897, reads "In five years perhaps, and certainly in fifty.") The State of Israel was declared fifty-one years later, in 1948. [cite:herzl_diaries] Between 1901 and 1903 Herzl negotiated with Abdul Hamid II, Kaiser Wilhelm II, Pope Pius X and the British Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain, and weighed Argentina, Cyprus, El-Arish in Sinai and East Africa. By the account in the research, Cyprus was set aside in 1902 because of its existing Greek and Turkish population, El-Arish in 1903 for lack of water, and the Sultan refused any transfer of sovereignty over Palestine. [cite:herzl_diaries] (Another source in this book dates Herzl's meeting with Pius X to January 1904, not to 1901–1903.) In August 1903 Herzl provisionally accepted the British offer in East Africa as a refuge after the Kishinev pogrom; the Sixth Zionist Congress voted 295 to 177 to send an exploratory commission, and a minority, mainly delegates from the Russian Empire, walked out. [cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] [cite:herzl_diaries] (A tally elsewhere in this book gives 295 to 178.) Herzl died of heart failure at Edlach, Austria, on 3 July 1904, aged 44. His one visit to Palestine, in October 1898, had been made to lobby Kaiser Wilhelm II. [cite:herzl_diaries] The Seventh Zionist Congress rejected the East Africa scheme in 1905. [cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903]

When Palestine became fixed, and why. The sources disagree on both questions. One account in the research says the Palestine focus was settled through the negotiations at the Basel Congress of 1897. [cite:herzl_judenstaat] Another says Palestine became the only acceptable territory in 1905, decided by the movement's Russian base rather than by Herzl or by the founding text. [cite:herzl_diaries] On the reason, the accounts also pull in different directions. One emphasizes that the choice was a political decision of the movement, not a theological inevitability. The other holds that the Uganda debate was resolved by the religious and historical attachment of most Zionist supporters to the specific land: those who walked out in 1903 were not looking for any refuge but for a return to Zion, and "Zion Zionism" defeated "Territorial Zionism", sometimes called Ugandism. [cite:sixth_zionist_congress_1903] These readings can be reconciled. The founding text was open about territory, and the decision that closed the question drew on attachments the text itself did not invoke. As the research itself cautions, the pamphlet's openness does not make Palestine arbitrary or interchangeable with any other place. Specific religious, historical and communal reasons, examined in later chapters, made it the destination.

A later chapter builds an argument on this point, and it should be identified as an argument. Because the movement chose Palestine, it inherited a body of biblical texts tied to that land: Genesis 15:18, which speaks of a land "from the river of Egypt to the great river, the Euphrates river"; Deuteronomy 11:24; and the borders of Numbers 34. [cite:genesis_1518_text] In Argentina or East Africa, that argument runs, no such texts would apply, and the idea of a "Complete Land of Israel" (Eretz Yisrael HaShlema) could not have arisen. The full case is made in [scene:gia_src_s01|the Argentina counterfactual]. For this chapter it is enough to note that the territorial openness of 1896 is the starting point of that argument.

Contested from the start. Herzl's answer was one Jewish answer among several, and for decades it was not the majority's. Four distinct objections were present at its creation. Reform Judaism had already rejected a return in its Pittsburgh Platform of 1885: "We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community, and therefore expect neither a return to Palestine... nor the restoration of any of the laws concerning the Jewish state." [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885] The Jewish Labour Bund, founded in Vilna in October 1897, the same year as the Basel Congress, rejected Zionism in favour of doikayt ("hereness"): Jewish cultural and national autonomy within the multi-ethnic societies of Eastern Europe, pursued through socialist politics alongside non-Jewish workers. It became the leading Jewish political movement in Eastern Europe, larger through the interwar years than the Zionist parties, and it was destroyed by Nazi and Soviet violence, not by losing the argument. [cite:wiki_bund] Ahad Ha'am proposed a cultural and spiritual centre rather than a state, an alternative within Zionism rather than outright rejection. [cite:wiki_ahad_haam] Orthodox rabbis who opposed political Zionism held that return could come only through divine redemption, and that forcing it by human politics was a transgression; their line runs to today's Neturei Karta. [cite:neturei_karta] These four positions shared a rejection of political Zionism and little else: a socialist workers' movement, an American religious reform movement, a cultural essayist and Orthodox theologians often disagreed with one another. The dissent has continuous successors. Satmar and much of Haredi Jewry reject Zionism today; Jewish Voice for Peace and IfNotNow criticize it; Peter Beinart's The Crisis of Zionism (2012) and Judith Butler's Parting Ways (2012) are critiques written from within Jewish tradition. To record that Herzl founded a modern political movement, distinct from Judaism as a religious tradition, is a matter of historical accuracy, and it is a distinction Herzl himself drew.

A reading, not a finding. The research behind this section also contains an interpretive argument that runs far beyond 1896, and it is set out here as an interpretation, not as documented history. It holds that the modern State of Israel still works on Herzl's 1896 template: secular nationalism as the state's main framing; the split between a "Society of Jews" and a "Jewish Company" surviving in the division of labour between the state and the Jewish Agency; Herzl's vision of citizenship for Jews carried into statute by the Law of Return of 1950; and the question of the non-Jewish inhabitants left unresolved, from the Palestinian Arabs who made up 20% of Israel's pre-1967 population to the more than 5.3 million Palestinians under military occupation since 1967. The same reading connects the 1895 diary's phrase about spiriting a population across the border with the displacement of 750,000 Palestinians in 1948, and lists later steps (the occupation from 1967, the annexation of East Jerusalem in 1980, the Nation-State Law of 2018 with its downgrading of Arabic and its declaration of Jewish settlement as a "national value", and the Gaza campaign from 2023) as the working-out of that framework. It concedes that Herzl did not commit these acts and argues that he set the framework within which others did. Those events belong to later volumes, where their figures and sources are examined. Against this reading stand the dispute over what the 1895 entry meant, the gap of half a century and more between Herzl and the events it lists, and the cautions already noted: the openness of the 1896 text does not make Palestine arbitrary, and a framework is not the same thing as a decision by the people who later acted.

What the record leaves open. Several things remain unsettled. The exact date of the degradation ceremony Herzl attended and the exact words the crowd used differ between the sources here and have not been checked against a primary document. How much weight Herzl gave Argentina against Palestine depends on how one reads a short text, and the accounts used here read it differently. The meaning of the June 1895 diary entry is a genuine scholarly dispute, and this book does not resolve it. The sources also disagree on when Palestine became the movement's fixed goal, at Basel in 1897 or with the rejection of East Africa in 1905. What is not in doubt is the sequence: a documented antisemitic miscarriage of justice in the first state to emancipate its Jews, a journalist whose diaries show his thinking developing over the months that followed, a pamphlet that shopped for territory, and Jewish opposition to his answer from the year it was given.
