The Bergson Group: Rescue by Advertisement

In 1940 a member of the Irgun arrived in the United States and began to work under another man's name. Hillel Kook was a nephew of Palestine's first Ashkenazi chief rabbi. In America he called himself "Peter Bergson," and over the next four years he ran the most aggressive public campaign of the war years on behalf of Europe's Jews. [cite:w_kook] He was Lithuanian-born, and he did not represent the established Zionist organizations. [cite:ushmm_peter_bergson] He came from the Irgun, and he built his operation outside the mainstream bodies and against their opposition.

A common assumption holds that American opinion about Palestine and about the destruction of European Jewry formed on its own, from the news alone. The record of 1940 to 1944 shows something more deliberate. Opinion was worked on, by paid advertising, staged events, celebrity sponsors and lobbying on Capitol Hill. That does not mean the concern it expressed was false. It means that what reached officials and readers in those years arrived through organized channels, and that at least two separate organizations were building them.

Another emissary crossed the Atlantic the same year. Benzion Netanyahu, born Mileikowsky in Warsaw in 1910, had emigrated to Palestine in 1920 and edited the Revisionist newspaper Ha'Yarden in the 1930s. In 1940 Vladimir Jabotinsky, the Revisionist leader, sent him to the United States. Netanyahu served as Jabotinsky's personal secretary until Jabotinsky's death later that year, and then ran the Revisionist movement's American operations as executive director for the following eight years. [cite:jewishjournal_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:wiki_benzion_netanyahu] [cite:tabletmag_bibi_fathers_footsteps] His was a formal, dated post, not a loose sympathy. What that line of descent meant for his son's later politics belongs to another part of this library: [scene:bwm_s01|Benzion Netanyahu and the Revisionist lineage].

Kook's vehicle was a succession of committees, each built around a single demand and each carrying long lists of prominent sponsors drawn from Hollywood, Congress and the press. [cite:w_kook] The first, the Committee for a Jewish Army, appeared in 1941. In 1943 came the Emergency Committee to Save the Jewish People of Europe. Later the group created the Hebrew Committee of National Liberation. [cite:w_kook] The names tracked the shifting goal: first a Jewish fighting force, then rescue, then a claim to speak for a nation.

The method stayed the same throughout. The committees bought full-page advertisements in major newspapers, with stark headlines about the destruction of European Jewry and demands that the government act. [cite:w_kook] The choice was deliberate. Paid space bypassed editorial gatekeeping, since the committees, not an editor, decided what appeared. It also put the message in front of officials and the general public on the same morning, so that a senator and his constituents read the same page. [cite:w_kook] The sponsor lists did similar work. A demand signed by film stars and members of Congress was harder to wave away as the view of a fringe group, even when the organizers behind it were exactly that in the eyes of the Zionist establishment.

The record used here does not say how the advertisements were paid for: who the donors were, how much the campaign cost, or how the money moved. That is a real gap. The campaign's methods are documented; its finances are not, in the material behind this book.

The group's largest single event came on 9 March 1943. "We Will Never Die" was a memorial pageant written by Ben Hecht, produced by Billy Rose and directed by Moss Hart, with music by Kurt Weill. It played twice in one night at Madison Square Garden, to about 40,000 people in all. [cite:w_wwnd] It then went on tour. Among its stops was Washington, where the audience included Eleanor Roosevelt, justices and members of Congress. [cite:w_wwnd]

The pageant brought the campaign's two instruments together: mass public feeling in New York, and an audience of officials in the capital. It turned the killing of Europe's Jews into a public occasion that people attended in person and that the powerful were seen to attend.

Seven months later the campaign moved from the stage to the street. On 6 October 1943, about 400 Orthodox rabbis, in an action organized with the Bergson group, marched to the Capitol and then to the White House to plead for rescue. [cite:w_rabbismarch] President Roosevelt did not receive them. [cite:w_rabbismarch] The record used here gives the fact of the refusal but not the reasons for it, and it should not be filled in with guesses. What it shows is the limit of the campaign's reach at that moment: it could fill an arena and bring rabbis to the Capitol steps, but it could not open the President's door.

The campaign's pressure on Congress took the form of a rescue resolution the Bergson group promoted. That pressure combined with a second force from inside the government. A Treasury Department report charged the State Department with obstructing rescue. [cite:w_wrb] In January 1944, President Roosevelt created the War Refugee Board by Executive Order 9417. [cite:w_wrb] Its rescue work is credited with saving many thousands of lives. Estimates of the number vary, and the sources used here give no single figure. [cite:w_wrb]

How much credit belongs to the Bergson campaign is a fair question, and the record does not settle it. The documented sequence is clear: congressional pressure from the resolution the group promoted, together with the Treasury report, came before the executive order. [cite:w_wrb] What the sources show is that these events preceded the Board. They do not measure how much weight each carried in the President's decision. A reader who credits the Bergson group with forcing the Board's creation has the timing on his side. A reader who credits the Treasury report, an internal charge from one department against another, has the same timing. The two pressures arrived together, and the sources treat them together.

Some of the Bergson group's firmest opponents were fellow Zionists. Mainstream Zionist leaders, including Rabbi Stephen Wise, opposed it, and fought it as a rival: an Irgun delegation operating outside the organizations that spoke for the movement in Washington, with methods far louder than theirs. The government watched it too. The FBI kept the group under surveillance as a foreign-linked operation, and leaders of established Jewish organizations went as far as urging that Kook be deported or drafted [cite:jta_bergson_fbi]; that record is told in the next section, [scene:fa_s01|who Washington watched]. The contest was not between Jews and their critics. It ran inside the Zionist movement and inside American Jewish organized life, between a disciplined establishment and an insurgent wing that had come from Palestine's armed underground.

Each side's position can be put in its strongest form from what the record shows. For the mainstream leaders, the Bergson group was a rival from the Irgun, outside the movement's institutions and answerable to none of them, competing for the same officials' attention. For the group's defenders, its loud methods were what preceded a government rescue agency, and the timing of the War Refugee Board is their evidence. The sources here document the rivalry and the outcomes. They do not resolve which side's judgment the outcomes vindicate.

The operation had two faces. Through 1943 and into early 1944 it ran a genuine rescue campaign during the Holocaust. From 1944, the same machinery turned to a new target: a campaign for a Hebrew state and for the Irgun's revolt against Britain. [cite:w_kook] The tools did not change. The full-page advertisement, the sponsor list and the public event now served a political claim rather than a humanitarian one. In 1944 the Hebrew Committee of National Liberation opened a "Hebrew Embassy" in Washington and registered with the Justice Department as the agent of a foreign principal it called the "Hebrew nation." [cite:djn_bergson_fara_1944] [cite:ushmm_peter_bergson]

The pivot matters for how the whole campaign is read. The pivot invites two readings. A critic could take it as a sign that rescue had been a vehicle for the Irgun's politics all along. A defender could answer that the rescue campaign was real, that it achieved a real result in the War Refugee Board, and that turning to statehood after 1944 was a change of target, not a confession about the past. The record supports the narrower statement: the same organization, with the same methods, pursued rescue first and statehood second.

The Bergson committees were one of two machines working on American opinion and on Congress in these years. The other was the mainstream one: the American Zionist Emergency Council, founded in 1943, which built a disciplined lobby of local committees, letters and telegrams directed at Washington. Its story is told in [scene:s07f|the American Zionist Emergency Council]. The two operated side by side and often against each other. The Bergson group worked through paid media and spectacle, aimed at the public and at officials at once; its line of pressure ran through the advertisements and the pageant to the War Refugee Board. The mainstream council worked through organized constituents, aimed at members of Congress and at the party platforms. Both lines reached results in 1944, one in rescue policy and the other in party politics.

What the record leaves open is the measure of each machine's influence. The documents establish what each did, and in what order the results followed. They do not establish how much of American sympathy in these years was created by these campaigns and how much they merely organized and directed a concern that would have existed anyway. Nor do the sources used here say how the Bergson campaign was financed, or give a single estimate of the lives the War Refugee Board saved. Those questions stay open.
