The Withdrawal Fight, January–March 1957

Britain and France completed their withdrawal from Port Said on 22 December 1956. [cite:rs_cw3_keightley] Israel had not left Sinai or the Gaza Strip. The General Assembly had called on it to withdraw behind the armistice lines in November, and on 7 November had called "once again" for it to do so, a sequence told in [scene:cw3_un_ceasefire|Washington against its allies]. [cite:rs_cw3_unispal_1002] Israel's government had announced on 8 November that it accepted the United Nations resolution and would withdraw; but its own statement that day listed three war aims, destroying the forces that threatened Israel, liberating Israeli territory held by an invader, and securing free navigation in the Gulf of Eilat and the Canal, and said that only the first had been fully achieved. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_cabinet_8nov] The withdrawal fight of January to March 1957 was about the other two. Israel sought to leave only on terms: no return of Egypt to Gaza, and an assured passage through the Straits of Tiran. The United States held that a state which had invaded could not set terms for leaving. How that difference was resolved, and what Israel actually received, can now be read from the primary record on both sides.

The Assembly loses patience. On 19 January 1957 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 1123. On 2 February it adopted Resolution 1124 by 74 votes to 2, with 2 abstentions. The second resolution "Deplores the non-compliance of Israel to complete its withdrawal behind the armistice demarcation line" and called on Israel to complete it "without further delay." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_1124] The vote was nearly unanimous, and the word "deplores" moved the Assembly from requesting compliance to recording its absence. Behind the vote lay the question of what would follow if Israel still did not comply. Many members, as Secretary of State Dulles would write to Senator Lyndon Johnson that month, favoured economic sanctions. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d127]

The aide-mémoire of 11 February. Washington's answer to Israel's demand for terms came in a written statement handed to Ambassador Eban on 11 February 1957 and released on 17 February. Its two parts dealt with the two places Israel was holding. On Gaza it said Israeli withdrawal "should be prompt and unconditional," and that the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) should "move into this area and be on the boundary between Israel and the Gaza Strip." On the Gulf of Aqaba it said that "the gulf comprehends international waters," in which no nation had the right to prevent "free and innocent passage," and that the United States "is prepared to exercise the right of free and innocent passage and to join with others" to secure it. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_aidememoire] [cite:rs_cw3_israeled_ike]

The document gave Israel nothing on Gaza beyond a United Nations force on the boundary. On Tiran it gave a statement of American policy and a declared readiness to act on it, not a treaty or a guarantee. That distinction, between a policy and a guarantee, would govern everything that followed.

Eban saw value in it at once. Meeting Robert Murphy the same day, with Reuven Shiloah present, he said that if nothing else Israel had "focused American thinking on the Gulf of Aqaba problem," and that "a good deal of the momentum in favor of sanctions would evaporate" once the American position was known. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d79] One reading of his remark is tactical: the paper did not meet Israel's terms, but it moved the United States onto Israel's ground on the one issue Israel had called vital, and it might take the pressure for sanctions off the table.

Ben-Gurion holds out. In Jerusalem the Prime Minister played for time. On 15 February the American embassy reported that Ben-Gurion was "pleased with intent of United States suggestions if not their particulars" on Tiran, but feared that UNEF might leave Sharm el-Sheikh before a settlement, in which case Israel would have "no other recourse than to fight or submit." On Gaza, the embassy reported, the "Prospect which he most adamantly opposes is return of Egyptian authority there under any guise." And it added a line of plain observation: "He frankly hoping for time." [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d100]

Three or four days later, around 18–19 February, Ben-Gurion appealed to Dulles "personally to obtain postponement of UN discussions." "Withdrawal under present circumstances will spell disaster for us," he wrote, and for the United Nations to proceed "is to adopt double standard of morality." He recalled Eban for consultation. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d111] The phrase "double standard of morality" was the core of Israel's case, and it deserves to be stated in its strongest form: in Israel's view, as Ben-Gurion's other messages that month show, withdrawal without an assured passage through Tiran, and with Egyptian authority able to return to Gaza, would leave Israel facing the conditions it had gone to war over. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d100] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d132]

The sanctions meeting, 20 February. On the evening of 20 February, from 8:30 to 10:55, Eisenhower and Dulles met the bipartisan leadership of Congress at the White House. The record shows how far the administration was prepared to go and how far Congress was prepared to follow. Eisenhower said sanctions "might take various forms." Dulles listed "German reparation payments to Israel, world-wide sale of Israeli government bonds, and US aid" as areas for consideration. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] Each was a channel through which money reached Israel.

The congressional leaders did not rally to the idea. Knowland proposed a resolution under which failure to comply within 30 days "would properly subject the offender to economic, moral and diplomatic sanctions." Senator Johnson "asked if sanctions were the best move." Fulbright wanted nothing that looked like "a commitment to go to war." Rayburn wanted no "part of a Congressional resolution." [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] Johnson had written to Dulles on 11 February; the Secretary's reply, delivered on 22 February, said that "There is strong sentiment among many members of the United Nations for economic sanctions," that the President would be "reluctant" to see sanctions become the only remedy, and that Israel had received "important assurances" but "apparently has felt that those assurances were not sufficient." [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d127]

The address of 20 February. The same evening Eisenhower took the case to the public in a radio and television address. Its central passage was a question: "Should a nation which attacks and occupies foreign territory in the face of United Nations disapproval be allowed to impose conditions on its own withdrawal?" Israel, he said, "insists on firm guarantees as a condition to withdrawing its forces of invasion," and "the United Nations has no choice but to exert pressure upon Israel to comply with the withdrawal resolutions." [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57]

The address was not only a warning. Eisenhower also told the country that UNEF "should be placed on the Egyptian-Israeli Armistice lines," that the Gaza Strip should no longer be "a source of armed infiltration and reprisals," and that "no nation has the right to prevent free and innocent passage in the Gulf." He reminded his listeners that the United States had given Israel "on February 11 a statement of United States policy." [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57] Ben-Gurion later quoted another line of the speech as part of what Israel had obtained: "we should not assume that if Israel withdraws, Egypt will prevent Israeli shipping." [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] The address, in short, refused Israel's conditions while stating publicly, in the President's voice, the substance of two of them. It did not mention American aid to Israel. [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57]

The money at stake. The levers Dulles named on 20 February show what the United States could have reached in 1957, and what it could not. There was no American military aid to Israel to suspend. In the aid relationship as the library's dashboard traces it, the years 1948 to 1961 were a period of economic aid and loans only, with Washington avoiding arms sales for fear of alienating the Arab oil states; the first American military aid, as loans, came in 1962.

That is why Dulles's list ran to German reparations and the sale of Israel Bonds as well as American aid: two of the three were not American government money at all: one was paid by West Germany, and the other was the world-wide sale of Israel's own government bonds. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] The table of cases in which Washington clearly attached conditions to aid begins much later. Its first entry is 1987, when Israel's cabinet cancelled the Lavi fighter, a programme carrying about $1.8 billion in American aid, under American pressure; its next American-money case is the $10 billion in loan guarantees held for 120 days over settlements in 1991–92.

The episode of February 1957 is not in that table; no sanction was imposed. But it is a documented occasion on which an American President and Secretary of State discussed, with the leaders of Congress, using money that reached Israel to force a change in its policy, and the record of the evening shows Congress declining to go along.

The dispute: was there a sanctions threat? Two readings of the record are possible, and each has its documents. On the first, the United States threatened sanctions in substance: the President said publicly that the United Nations had "no choice but to exert pressure," and the Secretary of State named reparations, bonds and US aid as possible levers to the congressional leadership. [cite:rs_cw3_app_20feb57] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] On the second, there was no formal threat: no American sanction was imposed, Dulles told Johnson the President was "reluctant" to reach for sanctions, leaders of both parties resisted, and the deal of 1 March came through assurances, not penalties. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d127] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d120] [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d169] The two readings agree on what was said and done. They differ on whether pressure that was named, discussed and broadcast but never applied counts as a threat. Ben-Gurion's message of about 22 February, below, shows the Israeli government treating it as one.

The turn, 22 February to 1 March. About 22 February, two days after the address, Ben-Gurion told the American embassy that Israel was prepared to withdraw its military forces from Gaza, and would not occupy Sharm el-Sheikh "after Israel's free passage through Tiran Straits had been assured." Eban would make a "further effort to reach understanding with USG." Against sanctions, he said, "we shall resist it with every ounce of our moral strength." [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d132] The concession on the army in Gaza and the defiance on sanctions came in the same message.

A week later the shape of the settlement was clear in Washington. At 5:36 p.m. on 28 February Dulles told the British, Canadian and French envoys that Israel would announce, in effect, "its unconditional withdrawal in the areas now occupied." It would get nothing beyond the prior United Nations resolutions, while stating "assumptions" already contained in United Nations texts and its own "expectations." The American position on Aqaba, he said, was unchanged from the aide-mémoire of 11 February. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d169]

On 1 March the Israeli government authorized the Foreign Minister, Golda Meir, to announce at the United Nations that "Israel will withdraw from the Gaza Strip and Sharm el-Sheikh." The announcement rested on stated assumptions, among them "That the take-over from the military and civilian control of Israel will be exclusively by the UNEF." It also declared that interference with Israeli ships "will be regarded by Israel as an attack entitling it" to act in self-defence. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] [cite:rs_cw3_katif_gaza] The assumptions were Israel's own statements, recorded in the Assembly; nothing in the sources used here shows any other government signing them. The self-defence clause, by contrast, was a warning addressed to Egypt and to the future.

A further document stands behind the decision, and it is one this book has not been able to read directly. In his address to the Knesset on 5 March, Ben-Gurion said that a letter from Eisenhower dated 2 March preceded the cabinet's approval of the withdrawal from Gaza, which he dates to 2 March. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] The letter itself was not fetched for this book and is known here only through Ben-Gurion's account. His dating also sits awkwardly with the record of 1 March, when the government had already authorized Meir to announce the withdrawal; the sources do not explain whether the cabinet of 2 March confirmed, completed or formally approved what had been announced the day before.

What Israel said it had won. Ben-Gurion's address of 5 March set out the government's account. The American memorandum of 11 February, he said, "declares that the United States is ready to make use of freedom of passage in the Straits," and it had welcomed a United Nations force at Sharm el-Sheikh. Free passage through Tiran was "vital to Israel, and is also of great importance to other countries," and UN forces now stood at Sharm el-Sheikh and in Gaza. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] Israel completed its withdrawal from Gaza on 8 March 1957 and from Sinai the same month. [cite:rs_cw3_morris_gaza] [cite:rs_cw3_walla_third_kingdom] UNEF remained in Egypt, in Peter Hahn's words, "as a buffer against Egyptian-Israeli hostilities," until Nasser ordered it out in May 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn]

The dispute: guarantees or none? Whether Israel left with guarantees is the question on which the whole episode turns, and the two answers rest on the same documents read differently. Ben-Gurion's case was that Israel left with assurances: the American memorandum on the Straits, Eisenhower's public words of 20 February, his letter of 2 March, and a United Nations force at Sharm el-Sheikh gave Israel what he called the gains of the campaign. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_bg_mar57] The opposing case, put by the historian Uri Bar-Joseph, is that Israel left "without formal security gains" under "brutal" American pressure; on this reading the assurances were unilateral statements and assumptions, and UNEF could be withdrawn at Egypt's request, as it was in 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo] [cite:rs_cw3_origins_hahn] Dulles's own words on 28 February, "unconditional withdrawal," with Israel getting nothing beyond the prior resolutions, support the second reading as a description of the form of the deal. [cite:rs_cw3_frus17_d169] The aide-mémoire's stated readiness to "exercise the right of free and innocent passage" supports the first as a description of its substance. [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_aidememoire]

The arrangement was tested ten years later, when Nasser ordered UNEF out in May 1967; that crisis is told in [scene:s12|the June 1967 war] and, for the question of who fired first, in [scene:iaa_s04|1967]. Bar-Joseph notes that the memory of the 1957 pressure weighed on Israel's cabinet in June 1967. [cite:rs_cw3_barjoseph_hdiplo]

What the record leaves open. Eisenhower's letter of 2 March, which Ben-Gurion said preceded the cabinet's decision, has not been read for this book, and its contents are known only through Ben-Gurion's account. The text of Resolution 1123 of 19 January was not fetched, and is known here only by its date. Whether Eisenhower would in the end have supported sanctions in the General Assembly had Israel refused is not settled by the documents: he named the possibility in public, his Secretary of State listed the levers in private, and he told Congress, through Dulles, that he was reluctant. And whether Israel's 1 March assumptions bound anyone but Israel was, in 1957, a question of interpretation; the sources here show Israel stating them and no other government formally accepting them. What is not open is the outcome. Israel withdrew from Gaza and Sinai without the formal guarantees it had demanded, with a public American statement of policy on the Straits, and with a United Nations force on Egyptian soil that remained only so long as Egypt allowed it.
