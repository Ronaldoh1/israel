Jacob Becomes Israel: What Genesis 32 Says

Before the word "Israel" named a people, a land or a state, it named one man. Its first appearance in the Hebrew Bible is a renaming, given at the end of a night of wrestling, in the thirty-second chapter of Genesis. Most of the ways the word is used today, as the name of a nation, of a promised land, of a modern country, are later layers built on that verse. Reading it on its own terms is the first step in seeing which layer is which.

The story runs through Genesis 32:22–32. Jacob is alone at the ford of the Jabbok river, on the night before he is to meet, and be reconciled with, the twin brother he had deceived years earlier. A figure wrestles with him through the night. The text calls that figure a man and an angel, and Jacob, afterward, in his own words, calls it God, seen face to face. He names the place Peniel, "face of God," because, in his words, "I saw God face to face, and yet my life was spared." Before the wrestling ends comes the verse on which everything else in this section turns. Genesis 32:28 reads, in the rendering consistent across the major translations: "Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel; for you have struggled with God and with men, and have prevailed."[cite:genesis_32_biblegateway] The Hebrew of the final clause, כִּי־שָׂרִיתָ עִם־אֱלֹהִים וְעִם־אֲנָשִׁים וַתּוּכָל, says "for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed."

The verse is documented as the word's first occurrence anywhere in the biblical text. The word Israel appears 2,362 times across the Hebrew and Christian scriptures, 2,284 times in the Hebrew Bible and 78 times in the New Testament, and every one of those uses comes after this one.[cite:genesis_32_biblegateway] The later biblical authors themselves treated Genesis 32 as the name's point of origin. The prophet Hosea, writing centuries afterward, recapitulates the same story in Hosea 12:3–4, which shows that the episode was understood within the tradition as the source of the name, not as one story among many that happened to use it.

What the name means. The Hebrew name Yisra'el (יִשְׂרָאֵל) joins two elements. One is El (אֵל), the ancient Semitic word for God, which was shared across Canaanite, Ugaritic and other Northwest Semitic languages and was not exclusive to Hebrew; it is the same shared root that appears in [scene:s00b|the section on the word "Semitic"]. The other is a root connected to sarah (שָׂרָה), "to strive, contend, or prevail." It is the root that also gives Sarah, Abraham's wife, her name, with connotations of "princess," or of one who contends with authority.[cite:wiki_israel_name] This derivation is not contested. It is the consistent reading of the standard Hebrew lexicons, Brown-Driver-Briggs among them, of rabbinic commentary and of Christian biblical scholarship alike.

The grammar allows two readings, and both are documented in the scholarly literature. With El as the object, the name means "he struggles with God," which matches the verse's own explanation of it. With El as the subject, it means "God strives," that is, God contending on Jacob's behalf or through him.[cite:wiki_israel_name] The text does not say which was intended, and this book does not choose between them.

The wordplay in the text. The renaming also sets the new name against the old one. Jacob's birth name, Ya'akov, is related to akev, "heel": he was born grasping the heel of his twin, Esau, and the root carries connotations of crookedness and of following from behind. The letters of Yisra'el can also evoke yashar, "straight" or "upright." Several Hebrew-language sources read this as a deliberate contrast built into the text: the grasping, deceptive Jacob against the upright, prevailing Israel, one person before and after a transformation.[cite:wiki_jacob] The two notes should be kept distinct. Sarah, "to strive," is the derivation the lexicons give; yashar, "upright," is an echo the letters allow, a play on sound and sense rather than a second etymology.

The layers added later. The common assumption is that "Israel" has always simply meant the Jewish nation, or the Promised Land. The text says something narrower. At the moment the name is given, it belongs to exactly one person: Jacob, who had deceived his brother and fled his own family, and who wrestles through a night and comes out of it renamed as one who has struggled and prevailed. Generations later the name passes to the descendants of his twelve sons. Centuries after that it becomes attached to a specific territory. Only in 1948 does it become the name of a modern nation-state. Each is a separate layer, added at a separate time, and each is worth tracking on its own rather than read backward into the verse as if the four had always been one thing.

Outside the Bible, the name's history runs on a different track, through inscriptions rather than scripture. Its earliest mention outside the biblical text, on the Merneptah stele, where Israel already appears as a people, is set out in [scene:ah_s01|the section on the ancient record].

What the verse settles, and what it does not. As a primary source, the verse establishes that the word's first appearance names one individual after one event, and the lexicons establish the derivation from El and sarah with high confidence. What the verse does not establish is how later biblical authors, ancient Israelite communities or modern political movements would extend the word. Those are separate and later developments, each documented in its own right. Reading the verse in its original context is not a claim about every later use of the word, in either direction: it neither empties the later meanings of force nor shows that they were present from the start. Nor does the documented etymology support any connection to Egyptian deities. A symbolic reading that breaks the name into IS, RA and EL works on the English transliteration rather than the Hebrew, and it is kept apart, and labeled as a reading, in [scene:s00g|the next section].
