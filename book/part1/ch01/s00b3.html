Dissent from Every Lineage

A common account of Jewish opinion about Zionism runs in one of two directions. In the first, Jewish opposition to the Zionist project is a small and marginal position, held by a handful of eccentrics. In the second, it belongs to one particular community, usually imagined as the Jews of the Middle East set against the Jews of Europe. The documented record supports neither version. Organized opposition to political Zionism, and organized critique of the state it produced, appears on the record in Ashkenazi religious tradition, in Ashkenazi secular socialism, in Ashkenazi-founded Reform theology, among the cultural Zionists who rejected partition, and in the scholarship of Mizrahi Jews living inside Israel itself. The earlier section on [scene:s00c|Zionism and Judaism] set out the distinction between a political movement founded at Basel in 1897 and a religion far older than it. This section follows the people who held that distinction, what each of them actually argued, and what became of them.

The sequence matters, because the traditions differ in kind as well as in origin. Some rejected the Zionist project outright. Some accepted a Jewish national life in Palestine but rejected a Jewish sovereign state and partition. One critiques the state's treatment of its own Jewish citizens. Folding all of these into a single category called "Jewish opposition to Zionism" would flatten a real spectrum, and the record is clearer when they are kept apart.

The General Jewish Labour Bund was founded in Vilna in 1897, the same year as the First Zionist Congress. It organized Jewish workers across the Russian Empire's Pale of Settlement, the region of the 1880s pogroms and the mass emigration that followed them. It was an overwhelmingly Ashkenazi, Eastern European, socialist movement, and it explicitly rejected emigration to Palestine. Its alternative was Jewish cultural and political autonomy where Jewish communities already lived [cite:wiki_bund]. Its position was not indifference to antisemitism: its members lived through the same pogroms that gave the Zionist movement its urgency. Its argument was strategic. The two movements were born in the same year, from the same communities, in response to the same crisis, and one said leave and build elsewhere while the other said stay and fight for the right to be who you are, where you are. The Bund was a serious, organized mass movement, not a fringe dissent.

Most accounts stop at the founding. What happened next is part of the record too. When the Bolsheviks took power in October 1917, the Bund split. A majority faction, believing it was joining a genuine ally on workers' rights, merged into the Communist Party. A minority led by Vladimir Medem rejected Bolshevik rule as undemocratic and hostile to Jewish national rights. Bundists disagreed among themselves over whether the Bolsheviks could be trusted, and decided the question differently. In 1921 the Bolsheviks formally dissolved the Bund, merger or no merger. Its remaining members were imprisoned or fled abroad, under a government now hostile to independent Jewish political organizing of any kind, socialist or not. Mark Liber, one of the Bund's leading figures and its representative on the Executive Committee of the Petrograd Soviet during the revolution itself, was executed during the Stalinist purges in 1937. He was killed by the state his movement had helped bring to power two decades earlier [cite:jewishcurrents_bund_rise_fall] [cite:britannica_bund].

The Polish Bund took a different path. It survived independently through the interwar years and into the Holocaust, and its members fought alongside Zionists and Communists in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. Survivors tried to regroup in Poland after the war and were suppressed again, this time by Poland's new Communist government. Within a single generation, the organization was dissolved twice, by two different governments, one of which its own members had helped install [cite:jewishcurrents_bund_rise_fall] [cite:britannica_bund]. That history shows what happened to the organization. It does not show that the Bund's argument against Zionism was refuted by it. A movement crushed by force has not thereby been proved wrong, and the record of its suppression is not a verdict on its ideas, in either direction.

American Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform of 1885 defined Jewish identity as a religious community rather than a nation awaiting territorial restoration. The movement that adopted it was founded and led by Ashkenazi Jewish immigrants and their American-born successors [cite:pittsburgh_platform_1885]. The timing is the point. Political Zionism as an organized movement dates from the Basel Congress of 1897, twelve years later. The Pittsburgh Platform was not a reaction against Zionism, since there was not yet an organized Zionism to react against. It was a Jewish religious position that existed first, on its own terms, and that did not adopt Zionism's framework when it later appeared. Retellings sometimes reverse the order and treat Zionism as the original position that the 1885 document set itself against, and the dates do not allow that. The record also limits the claim. American Reform Judaism's institutional position shifted considerably over the twentieth century, and what is documented here is the founding position of 1885 and its date, not a permanent stance.

Neturei Karta is today the most publicly visible Haredi Jewish community that opposes Zionism, and it is Ashkenazi in origin and practice. Its ground is theological. Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, the Satmar Rebbe, set out the argument in VaYoel Moshe in 1958. In this reading, Jewish tradition holds that the ingathering of the exiles and the restoration of sovereignty are to come through divine action in the Messianic era, not through human political and military effort beforehand. To establish a state ahead of that time is itself the transgression, and the central one rather than a side issue [cite:wiki_satmar]. The rabbinic sources behind it, the Three Oaths of the Talmud, have their own history in [scene:oaths_s01|the debate over the Three Oaths].

This is not a lukewarm position. It takes the scriptural promises of restoration seriously enough to conclude that a state built by human political action violates them, the opposite of what many outsiders assume religious Jews believe. Neturei Karta continues to hold it as a current practice and appears at public demonstrations [cite:wiki_satmar]. The record is equally clear about its scale: Neturei Karta is a specific and relatively small community, and most Haredi and Orthodox institutions today hold other positions. What is documented is that a serious, theologically grounded opposition from within Orthodox tradition exists and is active, not that it speaks for Orthodox Judaism as a whole.

The binationalists are the hardest case to classify, which is why they matter. Martin Buber and Judah Magnes, with colleagues including Gershom Scholem and Henrietta Szold, founded Brit Shalom in 1925. It advocated a binational state with full civic equality between Jews and Arabs, rather than a Jewish-majority sovereign state [cite:myjewishlearning_brit_shalom] [cite:wiki_brit_shalom]. Brit Shalom was never a mass movement. It worked more as a small study circle of elite academics and intellectuals. It drew fierce, documented hostility from the dominant Labor and Revisionist Zionist currents, some of whose members called its members traitors. It also had notable public support: Albert Einstein backed it publicly, a fact seldom mentioned alongside his name. Brit Shalom dissolved around 1933, roughly eight years after its founding [cite:wiki_brit_shalom] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism].

Historians trace its failure to a specific sequence, not simply to unpopularity. The violence at Hebron in 1929 and the Arab Revolt of 1936–39 undermined Brit Shalom's founding premise, that the Arab political leadership would accept binational equality. And the movement never grew beyond its intellectual core into a mass constituency on either side [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism].

The idea outlived the organization. In 1942 Buber and Magnes founded Ihud as a direct response to the Biltmore Conference's demand for a Jewish Commonwealth, again advocating a binational state with full civic equality [cite:wiki_ihud]. In 1947 the two men testified in person against partition before the United Nations Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP), to David Ben-Gurion's documented fury. Buber told the commission that it was "ethically and politically incumbent" on a regenerated Jewish people to live not merely alongside the Arabs but genuinely with them, developing the country for equal benefit. He called the treatment of the Arab population "the moral acid test of Zionism" [cite:britainpalestine_buber_magnes] [cite:wiki_ihud].

Hannah Arendt reached a related conclusion separately, from outside the Brit Shalom circle. Her 1944 essay "Zionism Reconsidered" warned that pursuing an exclusively Jewish sovereign state, rather than a binational arrangement, risked permanent regional conflict and long-term dependence on a great-power patron for security. She wrote it years before the state was founded [cite:researchgate_arendt_zionism_reconsidered].

The founding of Israel in May 1948 made binationalism moot, and Magnes's position changed with events. He began advocating a confederation between the new Jewish state and a future Palestinian Arab state, a documented development in his thinking cut short by his death that October. Ihud outlived the founding. The following year Buber wrote that "our cause has lost its footing, its face has been sullied, but it has not been overcome." Its publication, Ner, continued until 1964, sixteen years after the state it had argued against creating in that form already existed [cite:wiki_ihud] [cite:fathom_mandate100_binationalism].

Precision about this group is a matter of accuracy. Buber and Magnes are best described as cultural and binational Zionists who rejected the model of sovereignty and partition, not as anti-Zionists in the sense of the Bund or Neturei Karta. Their dissent came from inside the movement's own intellectual core. It shows that the argument was never only between Zionists and their opponents, but also among Zionists over what form Jewish national life in Palestine should take.

The last kind of dissent is different again. The Bund, Reform Judaism and Neturei Karta argued, in their different ways, against the Zionist project itself, before the state existed or regardless of it. The Mizrahi critique comes from people who did move to Israel, live there and are its citizens. Scholars including Ella Shohat, Sami Shalom Chetrit and Yehouda Shenhav documented specific patterns in how the Ashkenazi-dominated Israeli establishment treated Mizrahi Jewish immigrants after 1948: placement in peripheral development towns, discrimination in employment, and the cultural marginalization of Middle Eastern Jewish identity in favor of an Ashkenazi-coded national culture [cite:chetrit_mizrahi_struggle]. The arrival itself, in the transit camps and the affairs that followed, is told in [scene:s00e2|the exodus from the Arab world].

This is a Middle Eastern-lineage critique of the state's conduct, running alongside the Ashkenazi traditions rather than standing in for them. Its limits are also documented. It is a scholarly tradition with named authors and named mechanisms of discrimination. It does not show that all Mizrahi Israelis share it, or that Mizrahi communities are anti-Zionist as a whole. Many Mizrahi Israelis identify strongly with the state.

Placed on one line, the traditions span more than 140 years. Reform's platform of 1885 and Teitelbaum's book of 1958 stand 73 years apart, and none of these currents waited for the others to reach the same conclusion.

The historical traditions say nothing about how many Jews hold any view today. For that, the evidence is polling, and the most detailed recent survey is of American Jews only. The Washington Post surveyed 815 American Jews between 2 and 9 September 2025, with a margin of error of 4.7 points. Sixty-one per cent said Israel had committed war crimes against Palestinians [cite:wapo_jews_poll]. Thirty-nine per cent said Israel had committed genocide and 51% said it had not; among respondents aged 18 to 34, half said genocide [cite:toi_jews_poll]. Forty-six per cent approved of Israel's military conduct and 48% disapproved, and 68% disapproved of Benjamin Netanyahu. The same respondents held other views at the same time. Seventy-six per cent said Israel's existence is vital to the Jewish future, 94% said Hamas had committed war crimes, and 91% blamed Hamas for prolonging the war [cite:jta_jews_poll]. For comparison, in February 2024 Pew had found that 62% of American Jews said Israel's conduct was acceptable.

A second set of figures separates attachment from the label. Jewish Federations data show that about 90% of American Jews support Israel's existence as a Jewish and democratic state, while only 37% call themselves "Zionist" [cite:forward_zionist]. Organized opinion spans a wide range: J Street, pro-Israel and pro-two-state; Jewish Voice for Peace, which is anti-Zionist; and Haredi groups such as Neturei Karta, which oppose a Jewish state on religious grounds [cite:forward_zionist]. The pattern in these numbers is a community and not a bloc. Most American Jews are attached to Israel and critical of the war at the same time.

Two fair readings can be drawn from this record. The first holds that dissent has mostly been held by minorities. Neturei Karta is a small community. Brit Shalom was a study circle that lasted about eight years, and Ihud never grew into a mass constituency. Reform Judaism moved away from its 1885 position over the following century. Many Mizrahi Israelis identify strongly with the state. In the 2025 poll, three in four American Jews called Israel vital to the Jewish future, and about nine in ten support its existence as a Jewish and democratic state. On this reading the dissenters were real, but the main current of Jewish life moved the other way.

The second reading holds that dissent has been a real and continuous current. The Bund was a mass movement, not a fringe, and it ended because two governments crushed it, not because its members changed their minds. The binationalists came from the intellectual center of Zionism itself and testified against partition before the United Nations in 1947. The theological case against a state is still taught and still practised. And the gap between the roughly 90% who support Israel's existence and the 37% who call themselves Zionist suggests that the word and the attachment are not the same thing for many American Jews. Both readings rest on documented facts. They differ over weight, not over the record.

What the record shows plainly is that the dividing line in these debates has not been lineage. Some of the earliest, most organized and most theologically serious Jewish opposition to Zionism was Ashkenazi, and one of the most developed critiques of the Israeli state's conduct was Mizrahi. To frame opposition as something only Middle Eastern Jews do, while European Jews uniformly support Zionism, would invert the evidence. The line runs through every lineage, between those who hold the specific political position of Zionism and those who contest or reject it.

Several things the record here does not settle. It contains no current polling on what share of Ashkenazi, Sephardi or Mizrahi Jews support or oppose Zionism, and it cannot say how any lineage divides today. It documents no distinctly Sephardi tradition of dissent alongside the Ashkenazi and Mizrahi ones. The detailed polling is of American Jews alone, and it measures views of Israel's conduct in a war, which is not the same question as support for Zionism. And none of these traditions shows by itself which side of the argument was right. The record establishes something narrower and firmer: that support and opposition have both had serious, long-standing representation within Jewish life, across every lineage it documents.
