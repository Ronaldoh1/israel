Black Sabbath: Operation Agatha, 29 June 1946

Relations between Britain and the Jewish community of Palestine are often described as having worn down slowly after the war, with no single moment at which they broke. The slow wearing-down is real: from September 1945 the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi had acted together against British targets, from the railways at Lydda in November 1945 to eleven bridges on the night of 17 June 1946 (see [scene:rd5_revolt|the Jewish Resistance Movement]). [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] But the record also holds a dated break, with a codename, a troop count and an arrest total. Twelve days after the Night of the Bridges, on Saturday 29 June 1946, Britain launched Operation Agatha.

The operation. Roughly 17,000 British troops and police searched Jewish settlements, kibbutzim and offices across Mandate Palestine at the same time. More than 2,700 people were arrested. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] The operation was a direct response to the escalating operations of the Haganah, the Irgun and Lehi against British installations. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] What set it apart was whom it reached. Those arrested included senior officials of the Jewish Agency. Agatha was not a narrow sweep for known fighters of the two underground groups. It struck the mainstream political leadership's own institutions. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha]

One reading of that choice of target ties it to what the united resistance had become. The Night of the Bridges had been carried out not by Lehi or the Irgun but by the Haganah, acting through its Palmah. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] The campaign Britain was answering was no longer only the work of the two underground groups, and the raid reached past them to the political leadership.

The sources do not give a site-by-site inventory of what was searched, and they do not name the individuals arrested. What they establish is the scale, the simultaneity and the reach of the operation into the Agency's leadership. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha]

The documents. The arrests were only part of it. British forces also seized a substantial cache of documents from the Jewish Agency's headquarters in Jerusalem. The material was believed to include evidence linking the Agency's leadership to Haganah operations. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] That belief is what gave the seizure its weight. The Jewish Agency had kept a deliberate legal and organizational separation between the political leadership and armed resistance. Documents tying the one to the other threatened that separation directly. The sources describe what the documents were believed to contain; they do not set out the actual contents or the full extent of what was taken. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha]

Two names. The operation carries two names, and each records something. "Agatha" was the official British codename. "Black Sabbath" was the name given by the Jewish community whose institutions were raided, because the raid fell on a Saturday, the Jewish Sabbath. [cite:wiki_operation_agatha] The second name is itself evidence of how the operation was experienced and remembered by those it targeted. There is no evidence that the British name carried any hidden meaning; codenames of the period were, as a rule, assigned without symbolic intent. The community's own name is the more telling of the two for understanding what the raid meant to those on the receiving end.

Why the King David Hotel. The documents did not stay at the Agency. They were taken to be processed and stored in the Secretariat wing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The building needs describing. Its southern wing housed the Mandate Secretariat, the central administrative offices of the Mandate government, and served as the regional British military headquarters. Hotel business went on in the rest of the building at the same time. It was not a hotel that happened to contain a few government offices; it was, in effect, the centre of British administration in Palestine. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

That double character is what made it a target. In the Irgun's planning, the hotel was at once a symbol of British authority and the physical place where the evidence against the Jewish Agency's leadership was held. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] Irgun intelligence assessed that the documents seized in Agatha, including the material believed to implicate the Agency's leadership in coordinating with the Haganah, were being processed and stored in the Secretariat wing specifically. The bombing's documented objective was to destroy that evidence and to strike the administrative apparatus that had just carried out the mass raid and the arrests. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing]

Twenty-three days. The Irgun's planning for what became the King David Hotel bombing began in direct response to Black Sabbath. Its motive, as documented, was specific, retaliation and the destruction of evidence, rather than a general rise in tension. The bombing took place on 22 July 1946, twenty-three days after the raid. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The line from one event to the other is short and traceable: raid, seizure, intelligence assessment, choice of target, attack.

Two cautions belong beside that line. The first is that it does not establish that retaliation for Black Sabbath was the bombing's only motive; the bombing's planning and its stated objectives are taken up in [scene:s07e4|the next section]. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] The second is that tracing a cause is not the same as weighing a deed. The bombing killed 91 people, many of whom had no connection to the administrative work the Irgun meant to strike. [cite:wiki_king_david_hotel_bombing] Setting out the chain from Agatha explains how the hotel came to be chosen. It does not excuse or justify the deaths that followed, and it is not offered here as doing so.

The claim and the record. The familiar account of steadily worsening relations is not wrong so much as incomplete. The armed campaign of 1945–46 and the British response to it built up over months. Within that build-up, Agatha stands out as a single dated operation of a different order, with a troop count, an arrest total, a seizure of the Agency's own papers and a direct line to the deadliest single attack on the British of the whole Mandate period. A gradual decline and a sharp turning point are both in the record.

What the record does not settle. The sources do not name those arrested or say how long they were held. They do not give the contents of the seized documents, so the belief that they tied the Agency's leadership to the Haganah cannot be tested here. They do not record what the British government said, at the time, to justify the decision to arrest the Agency's senior officials. And they do not show how the raid bore on the decisions the Agency's leadership took a few weeks later, in early August 1946, to halt armed action. [cite:rs_rd5_lehi_resistance] Those questions need the British and Jewish Agency archives, which are not part of the material verified for this book.
