The Shareholder Turn: Profits, Buybacks, Pay and Tax, 1970–1986

While union power shrank, the hinge years gave a new answer to an old question, whom a corporation is run for: the shareholder. It arrived in three forms that can each be dated: an argument in 1970 and 1976, a securities rule in 1982, and tax and monetary decisions between 1979 and 1986. None alone explains the wage split, but together they changed what managers were paid to do and what they could do with their firms' cash.

The argument: Friedman and agency theory

On 13 September 1970 the New York Times Magazine published an essay by the economist Milton Friedman titled "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits". [cite:ad4_hoover_friedman] Friedman wrote that "a corporate executive is an employee of the owners of the business", and that there is "one and only one social responsibility of business": to use its resources to increase profits, "so long as it stays within the rules of the game". [cite:ad4_friedman1970] The qualifier is often dropped when the essay is quoted. Managers who spend shareholders' money on social goals of their own choosing, he argued, are in effect taxing the owners without a mandate.

Six years later, the economists Michael Jensen and William Meckling gave the idea a formal structure in the Journal of Financial Economics. Their paper defined "agency costs": the losses that arise when the people who run a firm (the agents) are not the people who own it (the principals), and so may run it for their own comfort rather than for the owners' return. [cite:ad4_jensen_meckling] The practical conclusion drawn in the following decades was to align managers with shareholders by paying them in stock and options. That conclusion links the 1970s argument to the CEO pay figures below. The doctrine has since been publicly qualified: on 19 August 2019, 181 chief executives in the Business Roundtable signed a statement committing to lead their companies "for the benefit of all stakeholders", a departure from the group's earlier shareholder-first principles. [cite:ad4_audit_brt2019]

The rule: SEC Rule 10b-18, November 1982

A company can return cash to shareholders as a dividend, a cash payment per share, or by buying back its own shares on the stock market, leaving each remaining shareholder a larger slice of the firm. Buybacks raise a legal problem: a company buying its own stock can push up its price, which securities law treats as possible manipulation. On 17 November 1982 the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) adopted Rule 10b-18, published in the Federal Register on 26 November, which created a "safe harbor". [cite:ad4_sec_10b18_2003] A company that buys back its shares within four conditions is not deemed to have manipulated the market "solely" because of how, when, at what price or in what volume it bought. The conditions are that it uses one broker a day; avoids buying at the opening trade and near the close; pays no more than the highest independent bid or last independent sale; and generally buys no more in a day than 25 per cent of the stock's average daily trading volume. [cite:ad4_sec_10b18_2003]

The rule's design matters for how it is described. Compliance is voluntary, and the SEC states that there is no presumption that purchases outside the harbor are manipulative; the harbor also does not protect repurchases that are fraudulent or manipulative when the facts are considered as a whole. [cite:ad4_sec_10b18_2003] Rule 10b-18 did not legalise buybacks as such; it gave firms a clear, low-risk path to do them at scale. Later attempts to tighten disclosure have not lasted. The SEC's 2023 rule requiring firms to report daily repurchase data and explain their buyback objectives was vacated by the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals on 19 December 2023. [cite:ad4_debevoise_sec2023] Since 2023 a 1 per cent federal excise tax has applied to net buybacks; it was still being collected at that rate in late 2025. [cite:ad4_spdji_q3_2025]

The scale of buybacks

By the count of S&P Dow Jones Indices, companies in the S&P 500, the 500 large firms that make up the main stock-market index, spent $806.41 billion on buybacks in 2018, $519.76 billion in the pandemic year 2020, $922.68 billion in 2022 and a record $942.55 billion in 2024. In the twelve months to September 2025 the total passed $1 trillion for the first time, at $1.020 trillion. [cite:ad4_spdji_q3_2025] Buybacks are concentrated: the 20 largest buyers accounted for 49.5 per cent of the third-quarter 2025 total, against a historical average of 47.8 per cent. [cite:ad4_spdji_q3_2025] The index's senior analyst, Howard Silverblatt, called the 1 per cent excise tax "a manageable expense" that "has not impacted overall buybacks". [cite:ad4_spdji_q3_2025]

The totals establish size: in 2024, S&P 500 companies spent about $1.50 on buybacks for every $1 of dividends ($942.55 billion divided by $629.62 billion). [cite:ad4_spdji_q3_2025] What they mean is disputed. William Lazonick, the best-known critic, found that 449 companies in the index spent 54 per cent of their 2003–2012 earnings, $2.4 trillion, on buybacks and another 37 per cent on dividends, and argued that stock-based pay gave executives a reason to prefer this to higher wages or investment. [cite:ad4_audit_lazonick] Jesse Fried and Charles Wang replied that gross figures overstate the drain: net of the new shares firms issue, and counting research spending as investment, payouts were about 41 per cent of the income available for investment, and investment was near record levels. [cite:ad4_audit_friedwang] Buybacks also rise and fall with profits, as the 2020 dip shows, which defenders read as firms returning cash they cannot use well. Neither side's totals settle the question of motive.

Pay at the top

The EPI tracks the pay of chief executives at the 350 largest American companies by sales and compares it with the pay of production and nonsupervisory workers. Using "realised" pay, which counts stock options when they are cashed and stock awards when they vest, the average CEO earned 20.6 times as much as a typical worker in 1965 and 30.7 times as much in 1978. The ratio reached 379.6 in 2000, stood at 290.2 in 2024 and, on a projection from data through August 2025, 324.7 in 2025. From 1978 to 2025 realised CEO pay rose 1,316 per cent, adjusted for inflation, while typical workers' pay rose 28 per cent. [cite:ad4_epi_ceo2025] The ratio is an average of firm-by-firm ratios. Earlier editions of the series gave different values for the same years, so figures from different editions should not be mixed. Steven Kaplan and Joshua Rauh argue that top pay rose across many occupations, not only among executives, a pattern they find fits the growing scale of firms and technology better than managers setting their own pay. [cite:ad4_audit_kaplanrauh]

Tax and money: 1979 to 1986

Two other decisions shaped who kept what. The first was monetary. On 6 October 1979 the Federal Reserve chair, Paul Volcker, announced that the Fed would target the quantity of bank reserves rather than the daily interest rate. The federal funds rate reached a record 20 per cent in late 1980, the economy fell into a severe recession, unemployment peaked at 10.8 per cent in late 1982, and inflation fell to 3.7 per cent in 1983. [cite:us11_frh_volcker] Ending double-digit inflation was the stated goal, and it was achieved. The EPI counts the "tolerated excess unemployment" of later decades among the policy choices behind the wage split; that is the EPI's causal reading, not a settled finding. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026]

The second was fiscal. The top federal income tax rate was 70 per cent in 1980. After the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 it was 50 per cent from 1982; after the Tax Reform Act of 1986 it was 38.5 per cent in 1987 and 28 per cent in 1988, the low point of the path since 1980. It rose to 31 per cent in 1991 and 39.6 per cent in 1993, fell to 35 per cent in 2003, returned to 39.6 per cent in 2013 and has been 37 per cent since 2018. [cite:us11_tpc_toprate] The common shorthand "70 per cent to 37 per cent" hides both the 28 per cent trough and the later increases enacted under presidents of both parties. The Congressional Budget Office's long series shows that from 1979 to 2022 average federal tax rates fell for every income group, most for the lowest fifth, while the share of pre-tax income going to the top 1 per cent doubled, from 9 to 18 per cent. [cite:us11_cbo_dist2022] Who pays taxes now is the subject of Part Eleven. [scene:ad11_who_pays|Part Eleven: who pays]

Where it works as promised

The shareholder model has support in the record. Jensen and Meckling's problem is real: owners who cannot watch managers bear costs, and stock-based pay answered it directly. [cite:ad4_jensen_meckling] Friedman's essay placed profit-seeking inside "the rules of the game", which leaves the rules, including labour and tax law, to democratic choice. [cite:ad4_friedman1970] The pay ratio is not on a one-way path: it fell from 379.6 in 2000 to 290.2 in 2024. [cite:ad4_epi_ceo2025] And the money returned to shareholders does not vanish; it goes to whoever holds the shares, including retirement accounts, a point Part Ten takes up. [scene:ad10_pension_shift|Part Ten: from pension to 401(k)] Ownership is concentrated, however: in mid-2026 the wealthiest tenth of households held about 88 per cent of corporate equities and mutual fund shares by value (50.9 per cent for the top 1 per cent plus 37.2 for the next 9), up from about 82 per cent in 1989, while the bottom half held 0.6 per cent. [cite:ad4_audit_dfa_top1eq] [cite:ad4_audit_dfa_90to99eq] [cite:ad4_audit_dfa_bot50eq]

Interpretation, not finding: extraction by design

The Sophia platform reads the hinge years as the moment the Dream became an extraction system: a set of rules written so that the gains from rising productivity flow upward to shareholders and executives while the story of shared prosperity keeps workers working. In this reading, Rule 10b-18, stock-based pay and the decline of private-sector bargaining are not separate events but parts of one machine. The "design" it names is an inference about purpose; no document in this part shows a common plan. The strongest counter-readings are three. First, each change had its own stated rationale, from controlling inflation to curbing managerial waste, and its own supporters across both parties. Second, the measurement critics show that average compensation kept far closer pace with productivity than the popular chart suggests. Third, the same decades saw technological change and global competition that would have pressed on less-skilled wages under almost any rules.
