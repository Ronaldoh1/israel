One Week in December 1948: The Rights Order and the Refugees

In the second week of December 1948 the United Nations General Assembly adopted, on three successive days, the founding texts of the modern human-rights order and its first resolution on the return of the Palestinian refugees. In those same days the refugees themselves were being counted. It is sometimes assumed that the new international system examined the Palestinian displacement and passed judgment on it in 1948. The chronology tells a different story. The machinery that might have judged what happened did not yet exist, and the machinery that was built in the following three years set the Palestinian refugees apart from every other refugee population.

Before 1948 there was no worldwide system of human-rights treaties. The League of Nations' Permanent Mandates Commission reviewed Britain's administration of Palestine, but there was no treaty of individual rights and no court to which an individual could turn. The International Committee of the Red Cross worked under the laws of war, the Geneva Conventions of 1929. Its delegate Jacques de Reynier entered Deir Yassin on 11 April 1948 and reported what he found. That was a record of events, made under the laws of war. It was not a judgment, and no body existed to turn it into one.

Then, in a single week, the General Assembly adopted three instruments. On 9 December it adopted the Genocide Convention. [cite:w_genocideconv] On 10 December it adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, by 48 votes to 0 with 8 abstentions. Article 13(2) of the Declaration reads:

Everyone has the right to leave any country, including his own, and to return to his country. [cite:un_udhr]

On 11 December came Resolution 194, on the return of refugees and compensation for those choosing not to return. Its text, its vote and the two readings of it are set out in [scene:rr_s01|Resolution 194 read as a whole]. The modern rights order was thus written in the same days that the Palestinian refugee population was being counted. [cite:w_genocideconv] [cite:un_udhr]

How many people that count covered was itself disputed from the start. Some 700,000 Palestinians were already refugees by that week, and the UN Conciliation Commission later estimated about 711,000. Israel's official figure was lower and Arab officials' figures higher, as [scene:rr_s01|the next section] shows. The two figures are close, and both are estimates: the roughly 700,000 is a round number, the 711,000 the Commission's own. Neither is a registration count. The UN Economic Survey Mission and UNRWA's registration, which counted 726,000, produced a different total again. [cite:rs_rd6_morris_appendix]

Article 13(2) and Resolution 194 became the two texts most often cited together. Palestinians cite the Declaration's article alongside the resolution as grounds for a right of return. Israel has argued that neither creates a binding right of return for descendants. The text of Article 13(2) itself is not disputed. [cite:un_udhr] What is disputed is its reach, including whether a right "to return to his country" is binding and passes to descendants. The sources used here record the positions; they do not settle the legal question.

The institution that was actually built for the refugees was an agency of relief, not of rights. The General Assembly created the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees, UNRWA, in December 1949, and it began work in May 1950. It provides schools, health care and relief to registered Palestine refugees. Its mandate is relief and works. It is not charged with resettlement, with legal protection, or with securing return. [cite:unrwa_who] Where the refugees lived and how host governments treated them in these years is told in [scene:dsp_s01|where the refugees went].

The decisive step came in 1951. The Refugee Convention adopted that year defined who was a refugee and what protection states owed. Its Article 1D excluded people already receiving protection or assistance from another United Nations agency. Because the Palestinian refugees had UNRWA, the clause left them outside the protection system that covers every other refugee population, and outside the durable-solutions mandate of the UN High Commissioner for Refugees. [cite:w_refconv]

In practice, this meant that through the 1950s the Palestinian refugees had an agency that gave them relief, schooling and health care, but no international body whose task was to protect their legal status or to find them a permanent solution, whether return, settlement where they were, or settlement elsewhere. The one body with a political mandate, the Conciliation Commission created by Resolution 194, was a commission of three member states, not a protection agency. [cite:rs_rd6_refworld_194]

There was also no court. No international criminal court existed to examine the events of 1948. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court was adopted in 1998 and entered into force in July 2002, without retroactive reach. Palestine acceded in 2015, which is why the Court's jurisdiction in Palestinian matters begins in that era. [cite:w_romestatute] The displacement of 1948 was counted, relieved and debated, but it was never adjudicated by any international tribunal.

The common claim that the international human-rights system examined the Palestinian displacement and judged it in 1948 therefore runs ahead of the record. In 1948 the system consisted of declarations and a convention on genocide, with no enforcement body for individual rights. What followed was relief through UNRWA and a refugee convention that, by its own text, did not apply to the Palestinians.

The record leaves open why the drafters of the 1951 Convention wrote Article 1D as they did, and the sources used here do not give the debates. Nor do they settle the legal weight of Article 13(2) for the 1948 refugees and their descendants, which remains the subject of the opposing positions set out above. What is not in dispute is the sequence: rights declared in December 1948, relief organized in 1949 and 1950, protection withheld by the 1951 text, and no court until 2002.
