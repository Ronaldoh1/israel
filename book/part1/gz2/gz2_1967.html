June 1967: Counted, Governed, Pacified

On 6 June 1967, the second day of the Six-Day War, Israeli forces took the Gaza Strip from Egypt. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation] This time they stayed. This section sets out what Israel found there, how it governed, and how it put down the armed and civil resistance of the first four years, including the campaign of 1971 that reshaped the refugee camps and, in the words of the general who led it, kept the strip quiet for fifteen years.

The count. In the summer of 1967 Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics took a census of the newly occupied territories. Its first findings, published on 3 October 1967, counted 356,000 people in the Gaza Strip, 175,000 of them living in refugee camps, and another 33,000 in northern Sinai. [cite:gz_jvl_census67] The census recorded each respondent's status as a 1948 refugee, and its tables survive in digitized form. [cite:gz_levy_census67] Two decades after 1948, the strip's population had grown by roughly a quarter from its post-war level, and about half of it still lived in the camps.

The government. In June 1967 Israel set up a military government for the civilian populations of all the territories it had taken: the West Bank, Gaza, Sinai and the Golan Heights. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation] In 1982, after the peace treaty with Egypt, Israel replaced the military government's civilian functions with the Civil Administration. [cite:gz_wiki_occupation]

Resistance, 1967–1971. Gaza did not settle. Armed factions, the leftist Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine the most prominent among them, attacked Israeli forces and Palestinians accused of collaborating, while civilians organized strikes, boycotts of Israeli goods and demonstrations; in May 1968 women blocked roads and marched on the military governor's offices, and in February 1969 a schoolgirls' riot in Gaza spread to the West Bank. In October 1970 the New York Times correspondent James Feron reported that Gaza was the only area taken in 1967 where attacks continued "unabated," with several killings, grenade attacks or acts of sabotage each week, most of the victims Arabs. [cite:gz_wiki_insurgency]

Sharon's campaign. In 1970 the army's Southern Command under General Ariel Sharon expanded a campaign of what it called pacification, and after the killing of two Israeli children of the Aroyo family in January 1971 it intensified it. Its methods were mass arrests, house-to-house searches, demolitions, deportations, a fence around the strip, and wide roads cut through the refugee camps so that armoured vehicles could move through them. In the summer of 1971 bulldozers demolished about 2,000 homes and displaced nearly 16,000 people in Rafah camp and other camps; about 2,000 of the displaced were sent to El-Arish in Sinai and hundreds to the West Bank. [cite:gz_wiki_insurgency] Drawing on the economist Sara Roy, one account puts the number of relatives of suspected guerrillas deported to detention camps in Sinai at 12,000, and records that displaced families moved in 1972 to "Canada Camp," on the Egyptian side of the Rafah border, took about twenty years to come back. [cite:gz_mada_sinai] The same account records that early versions of the Allon Plan, the Israeli blueprint for the territories, envisioned moving hundreds of thousands of Gaza's Palestinians to Sinai and the West Bank; the plan was not carried out in that form. [cite:gz_mada_sinai]

The verdicts on it. The campaign did what its commander intended. The historian Mark Tessler records that the armed factions were fragmented and made ineffective, and Sharon told Time magazine in 1989 that the strip stayed quiet for fifteen years afterwards. Tessler also records that Israel used collective punishment to deter support for resistance, and quotes an Arab soldier in the Israeli army, Rafik Halabi, who called the main legacy the "festering resentment" of Gaza's people. [cite:gz_wiki_insurgency] Both judgments rest on the same events. The fifteen years of quiet Sharon claimed ended, as Part Three shows, in Jabaliya in December 1987.
