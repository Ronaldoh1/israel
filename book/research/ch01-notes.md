# Volume I, The Baseline: research notes

Verified 2026-10-09. Every claim in `ch01.json` comes from a page fetched in this session. WebFetch returns a model-written summary of each page, not the raw text, so quoted phrases are short. Check any quotation against the original page before it is printed.

The session's web-search budget ran out after seven topics. Five planned topics could not be researched and are listed at the end as open gaps.

## 1. "Israel" and "Palestine" in the ancient record (`ancient_names_record`)
**Agree:** The Merneptah stele (c. 1207 BCE, found by Petrie in 1896) is the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, and Israel is marked as a people, not a place. The Tel Dan stele (c. 840 BCE, found 1993) names the "House of David". The Mesha stele (c. 840 BCE) names Omri, king of Israel. The earliest surviving use of "Palestine" is in Herodotus (5th century BCE). Under Byzantium the region was divided into First, Second and Third Palestine, and Jund Filastin was a province of the Umayyad caliphate.

**Disagree:** Why Judaea became "Syria Palaestina" around 132–136 CE. Feldman (1990) thinks Hadrian probably did it, to punish the Jews, though he concedes the evidence is only circumstantial. Syme and Jacobson, as reported by a secondary blog, read the change as administrative and possibly earlier than the revolt. Bowersock says Masalha's claim of four thousand years of continuity is indefensible, yet praises the book. The Jewish Virtual Library calls the name Roman-imposed and Palestinian nationalism recent.

**Not verified:** The Görg reading of a possible earlier "Israel" (c. 1400 BCE) on a Berlin fragment came only from a blog, so I left it out. I found no primary source for Assyrian *Palashtu* or for the Egyptian *Peleset* records. The Hadrian dispute rests partly on a secondary blog (`rs_ch01_totf_hadrian`). It is attributed as such, but the Jacobson (BASOR 1999) and Syme (JRS 1962) originals were not read.

## 2. Antisemitism as a hatred (`antisemitism_hatred_history`)
**Agree:** Norwich 1144 was the first medieval blood libel. Trent 1475 was repudiated by the Church in 1965. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) ordered distinguishing dress, England imposed a badge in 1218, issued the Statute of Jewry in 1275, expelled its Jews in 1290 and readmitted them in 1656. The Damascus affair of 1840: torture was halted on 25 April and the prisoners were freed on 28 August. Kishinev, 1903. The Protocols: Znamya 1903, Nilus 1905, Ford's 91-part series in 1920, exposure by *The Times* in August 1921, the Bern ruling in 1935, and later use in Hamas's 1988 charter and on Egyptian, Hezbollah and Iranian media.

**Disagree:** None among the sources.

**Not verified:** The York massacre of 1190, the French expulsions (1306, 1394), and the Rhineland massacres of 1096: no page was fetched for them. The date of the Sultan's firman after Damascus: the source gives none, so no date is used.

## 3. The Jews of the Arab world, country by country (`mizrahi_exodus_by_country`)
**Agree:** For Libya (Yad Vashem): 120 killed in Tripoli in November 1945, 12 in 1948, and 30,972 emigrated to Israel. Egypt: 75,000–80,000 in 1948, and the 1956 Suez measures. Algeria: the Crémieux Decree, and about 130,000 who left for France in 1962.

**Disagree:** Whether the Jews who left were "refugees" equivalent to the Palestinians. Beker and the "dual refugee" position say yes. Shenhav (Haaretz 2006, as quoted) and Amayreh (Ahram 2012, an opinion piece) say no. The Stroum Center essay takes a middle line. Totals: Beker gives about 900,000 and about 650,000 to Israel. The simulation's own scene gives 850,000; writers must show both figures.

**Not verified:** The Aden (82 killed) and Aleppo figures come from one outlet, the Center for Israel Education, which cites no documents, so they are attributed. The Mosaic piece on Aden needed a redirect that could not be fetched. I found no figures for Tunisia, Syria or Lebanon beyond those above. The UNHCR statements of 1957 and 1967 and US H.Res. 185 (2008) were not in the fetched text.

## 4. The land's other communities (`other_communities`)
**Agree:** The Druze were conscripted in 1956 and given their own religious courts in 1961; they number about 150,000 in Israel and 24,000–25,000 in the Golan. The Negev Bedouin numbered about 92,000 before 1948 and about 11,000 after. The Circassians live in Kfar Kama and Rehaniya. The Samaritans numbered 146 in 1917 and 654 in 2003.

**Disagree:** How many people live in the unrecognized villages: about 70,000 (ACRI, c. 2011), about 100,000 (MRG), or about 150,000 (Adalah via Al-Monitor). Whether the villagers are "illegal construction" or a dispossessed population.

**Not verified:** When the Circassians arrived: the source gives 1880 plus about ten years in the Balkans, and does not mention the expulsion from the Caucasus. The Golan Druze after the 1981 annexation. The 1957 recognition of the Druze as a separate religious community.

## 5. The Old Yishuv and nineteenth-century Jerusalem (`old_yishuv_jerusalem`)
**Agree:** About 7,000 Jews in 1800; 23,000–27,000 in 1880, about half of them in Jerusalem; halukka; 56,000 left in 1918. McCarthy's Ottoman-register counts exclude foreign nationals.

**Disagree:** Whether Jerusalem had a Jewish majority before Zionism. Israel's UN letter S/10138 (1971) and Flatow (2023) say yes, from the 1870s–80s. Jordan and the United Arab Republic (S/10130) used figures for the sub-district. Frantzman stresses that the city's Jews were largely Sephardi and religious. The 1922 census total for the city differs between sources: 62,578 in S/10138 against 52,081 in the JVL table (the JVL total appears to leave out other groups). Log this in SOURCE-CONFLICTS.md.

**Not verified:** Pierotti's figures as cited by Frantzman: the dating is inconsistent (Pierotti worked in the 1850s–60s), so I left them out. The date of Mishkenot Sha'ananim.

## 6. Who may become a citizen (`return_citizenship_laws`)
**Agree:** These come from the primary texts: the Law of Return of 1950 with its 1954 and 1970 amendments, and the Nationality Law of 1952 (conditions of residence). The 2003 law; the court rulings of 2006 (6–5) and 2012; the lapse in July 2021; re-enactment on 10 March 2022 by 45–15.

**Disagree:** Whether the Law of Return is a refuge law or a discriminatory law (Stein; Adalah's listing; Joppke and Rosenhek's comparative frame). Whether the 2003 law served security or demography.

**Not verified:** The UNHCR Refworld text of the Nationality Law is an unofficial consolidation to 1980. I did not read the 2022 law's quota size or duration.

## 7. Population today (`population_now`)
From the Statistical Yearbook of Jerusalem, Table III/1, for end-2023: Israel 9,699,200 (Arabs 21.3%); Jerusalem 980,700 (Arabs 39.9%); and 1967 for comparison. The PDF's extracted text runs right to left. I fixed the column mapping from the Hebrew/English headers and the internal totals: Israel's Arab share has to be the 21.3% figure. Recheck against the PDF before printing.
