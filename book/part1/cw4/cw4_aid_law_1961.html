The Foreign Assistance Act of 1961: The Legal Frame of American Aid

Volume II followed American money to Israel through the loans and grants of the state's first years, and Stage One of this volume took up the shift of 1951–53 in [scene:iaa_s01|the early aid relationship]. By the end of the 1950s the flow was steady but small, and the law that governed it was a patchwork of programmes and agencies. In 1961 Congress replaced that patchwork with a single statute, the Foreign Assistance Act, and the new administration created a single agency to run it. The Act still governs American foreign aid. That continuity has produced a lasting confusion about what the Act of 1961 contained, and this section begins by separating what was enacted in 1961 from what was added to it later.

The statute and the agency. The Foreign Assistance Act of 1961 is Public Law 87–195, printed at 75 Stat. 424. [cite:rs_cw4_usc_2378_1] [cite:rs_cw4_eo10973] It did more than authorize spending. It required that a new agency replace two existing ones, the International Cooperation Administration and the Development Loan Fund, within 60 days of the Act's enactment. [cite:rs_cw4_eo10973] [cite:rs_cw4_frus61v9_d117] The editors of the State Department's documentary series, Foreign Relations of the United States, note the change in an editorial note on the agency's founding. [cite:rs_cw4_frus61v9_d117]

The President acted by executive order. Executive Order 10973, dated 3 November 1961, directed the Secretary of State to "establish an agency in the Department of State to be known as the Agency for International Development." [cite:rs_cw4_eo10973] [cite:rs_cw4_ebsco_aid] The placement matters to the mechanism. The new agency sat inside the State Department, so the delivery of economic assistance was tied administratively to the conduct of foreign policy rather than standing apart from it. Congress, for its part, kept the power of the purse: for fiscal year 1962 it appropriated $4.515 billion in foreign assistance. [cite:rs_cw4_ebsco_aid]

The structure that resulted had three parts, each with its own power. Congress wrote the statute and appropriated the money year by year. The President, through the executive order, decided how the work was organized and placed it under the Secretary of State. The new agency administered the programmes. A recipient government, Israel included, held none of these powers: in the documents of 1961 the decision to give, and on what terms, lay with Congress and the executive.

One basic fact is missing from the record assembled for this book: the day on which President Kennedy signed the Act. It was not found in the pages consulted, and this section does not supply it. What is documented is the sequence that followed: the 60-day deadline written into the Act, and the executive order of 3 November that met it by creating the agency. [cite:rs_cw4_eo10973] [cite:rs_cw4_frus61v9_d117]

What Israel received. Within this frame, American aid to Israel in the late 1950s and early 1960s was modest, and its military share was small. The series compiled by the Congressional Research Service, reproduced by the Jewish Virtual Library, gives the totals in millions of dollars: 50.8 in 1956; 40.9 in 1957; 85.4 in 1958; 53.3 in 1959, of which 0.4 was military; 56.2 in 1960, of which 0.5 was military; 77.9 in 1961; 93.4 in 1962, of which 13.2 was military and 45.4 economic; and 87.9 in 1963, of which 13.3 was military. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] The same series records that American military aid to Israel from 1959 to 1973 took the form of loans. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table]

Three things stand out in these figures. The first is scale. For fiscal year 1962 Congress voted $4.515 billion for foreign assistance worldwide; Israel's total from the United States for 1962 was $93.4 million. [cite:rs_cw4_ebsco_aid] [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] The second is the military line. It is not empty before 1962: the series shows $0.4 million in 1959 and $0.5 million in 1960, both as loans. It is very small, however, until 1962, when it reaches $13.2 million. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] The jump falls in the year of the decision to sell Israel the Hawk air-defence missile, though the series does not say what the 1962 loans paid for. That decision, and the argument over whether American military aid to Israel "began" in 1959 or in 1962, are the subject of [scene:cw4_hawk|the Hawk decision]. The third is that the totals for 1962 are not fully broken down in the figures used here. Military aid of 13.2 and economic aid of 45.4 together account for $58.6 million of the $93.4 million total; the series as fetched does not say what category the remainder belongs to. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table]

Section 620I: a 1996 provision with a 1961 number. The Foreign Assistance Act is best known today in connection with Israel for one section. Section 620I, codified at 22 U.S.C. 2378-1, provides: "No assistance shall be furnished under this chapter or the Arms Export Control Act to any country when it is made known to the President that the government of such country prohibits or otherwise restricts, directly or indirectly, the transport or delivery of United States humanitarian assistance." [cite:rs_cw4_usc_2378_1] Because the section carries the Act's numbering, it is routinely described as "Section 620I of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961," and the account of it elsewhere in this library implies a 1961 date. [cite:aljazeera_620i_explainer] [cite:jstreet_enforce_law]

That dating is wrong, and the correction can be made from the statute itself. Section 620I was not part of the Act as passed in 1961. The source credit in the United States Code shows that it was added by Public Law 104–208 (division A, title I, section 101(c), title V, section 559), enacted on 30 September 1996. [cite:rs_cw4_usc_2378_1] The number places the provision inside the 1961 Act; the text was enacted thirty-five years later. The condition it sets, on a recipient's treatment of American humanitarian deliveries, was not part of the law that Kennedy's administration administered in 1961 and 1962.

The provision's origin lies not in the Middle East but in the Caucasus. Brian Finucane, writing in Just Security in March 2024, traces it to Turkey's closure of its land border with Armenia in the 1990s. A Humanitarian Aid Corridor Act was introduced in 1994 by Representative Richard Lehman; Senator Bob Dole offered a version in 1995; the measure finally became law as an amendment to an appropriations act. [cite:rs_cw4_finucane_js] According to Finucane, President Clinton found that the law was triggered in Turkey's case but waived it on national-interest grounds, and the provision then lay dormant until Senator Todd Young invoked it in 2017 over Yemen. [cite:rs_cw4_finucane_js]

The two datings can each be traced to their source, and the disagreement is not really one of evidence. The 1961 dating follows the section number and common usage; the 1996 dating follows the statute's own enactment history, which the Code records and Finucane explains. [cite:rs_cw4_usc_2378_1] [cite:rs_cw4_finucane_js] This book uses the second. The point is not pedantic. A reader told that the law has stood since 1961 may conclude that it was designed with Israel in mind, or that it went unused against every recipient for six decades. The record shows a provision written in 1996 about Turkey and Armenia, waived in its first test, and revived in 2017 over Yemen.

Its application to Israel belongs to a much later period. On 13 October 2024 Secretary of State Blinken and Secretary of Defense Austin sent Israel a letter citing Section 620I and NSM-20, a memorandum issued in February 2024, that set 15 measures to be met within 30 days, including a minimum of 350 aid trucks a day; humanitarian groups counted an average of about 42 a day over that period, Israel's own figure for October was 57.7, and the administration said afterwards that Israel had taken some steps and that support would proceed. Israel denies blocking aid. [cite:aljazeera_ultimatum_outcome] [cite:jstreet_enforce_law] Earlier, in May 2024, a State–Defense report to Congress had concluded that Israel was not blocking humanitarian assistance; ProPublica reported that two US agencies, including USAID, had found the opposite and that Blinken rejected their findings, and a senior State Department official resigned in protest. [cite:foreignpolicy_620i_scorecard] [cite:motherjones_620i_letter] According to the account in this library, current as of October 2026, NSM-20 was later rescinded. That episode, the Leahy laws that sit beside Section 620I and the general point that each such law turns on a formal executive finding are told in full in [scene:law_s01|the laws already on the books].

What the record does not settle. On the material used here, the date on which Kennedy signed the Foreign Assistance Act is not established. The CRS figures for Israel are given by year without saying, in the version fetched, whether the years are fiscal or calendar, and the 1962 total is only partly broken down. [cite:rs_cw4_jvl_aid_table] What is settled is the shape of the frame. From 1961 American aid ran under one statute and, for economic assistance, through one agency inside the State Department; Israel's share of it in these years was in the tens of millions of dollars a year, with military aid appearing only as small loans until 1962; and the section of the Act most often invoked against Israel today was not part of it until 1996.
