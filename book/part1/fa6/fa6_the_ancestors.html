The Ancestors

In the Andes, families kept and honoured the mummified bodies of their ancestors, the mallquis. In the campaigns of the archdiocese of Lima against "idolatry", from 1609 to 1670, visitors burned the huacas and the mummified ancestors, and punished native ritual specialists with whipping, exile and in some cases death. [cite:vt34_ency_extirp] The honouring of the dead was treated as the worship of false gods.

In China the same question was argued for more than a century, and decided the other way at the end. Confucian families honoured their ancestors with rites at tablets and graves, and scholars honoured Confucius with ceremonies. The Jesuit missionaries judged these rites probably not superstitious and held that Christian converts could take part within limits; Dominicans and Franciscans took the opposite view and brought the question to Rome. [cite:fa_brit_rites] Rome changed its mind several times.

The Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith condemned the rites in 1645 and lifted the ban in 1656. Clement XI banned them in 1704, reinforced by a bull in 1715, and Benedict XIV in 1742 reaffirmed the prohibition and forbade further debate. Then in 1939 a decree of the Holy See "authorized Christians to take part in ceremonies honouring Confucius" and to observe ancestral rites. [cite:fa_brit_rites] For almost two centuries a Chinese Catholic could not honour his parents' tablets in the way his family had always done; after 1939 he could.

The difference between the two outcomes was not in the practice. Andean families and Chinese families both kept their dead close and honoured them. The difference was in the argument. In the Chinese case a missionary order argued in Rome, for more than a century, that the rites could be tolerated; the record of the Andean campaigns cited here shows no such argument made for the mallquis.
