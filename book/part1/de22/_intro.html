This part opens Book Four, on exit: the idea, central to the Dark Enlightenment texts, that people should be free to leave a state and choose another rather than vote to change their own. It follows what happened when investors and writers in the circle around those texts tried to build places to go. The first section traces the projects from the Seasteading Institute's floating platforms to Paul Romer's charter cities, Honduras's special zones, the venture fund Pronomos Capital, the network-state company Praxis and Balaji Srinivasan's Network School, naming who paid for each. The second follows Próspera, the zone on the Honduran island of Roatán that became a working place, through its charter, its neighbours' complaints, the repeal of its law, a court ruling that voided it and an arbitration claim against Honduras first priced at more than $10 billion. The third asks what Javier Milei's Argentina and Donald Trump's promise of "freedom cities" actually produced, and tests the book's worst-case reading against the part as a whole.
