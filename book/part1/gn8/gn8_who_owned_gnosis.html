Who Owned Gnosis

The Gnostic texts make one claim about their own knowledge again and again: it cannot be owned. It is found within, it is received through recognition rather than through an office, and, in the words that close the Apocryphon of John, anyone who exchanges it "for a gift or for food or for drink or for clothing" is cursed. [cite:vt24_apjohn] [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] The history of the texts, traced through this book, is a history of that claim meeting a series of owners. Set side by side, the owners form a sequence.

The sequence is not a conspiracy. The bishops, the dealers, the Egyptian state, the scholars and the foundation had different aims, and several of them did real service to the texts: the state kept the library together in Cairo, the scholars eventually published it, and the Gospel of Judas codex, left to decay by dealers, was restored once the foundation owned it. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] [cite:vt26_wiki_gjudas] What links them is a structure. In every era the knowledge was valuable, the means of access were scarce, and whoever controlled the scarce thing controlled who could read. In the second century the scarce thing was authority to teach. In the twentieth it was the manuscripts themselves, and then the right to publish them first.

The mechanism matches the one traced in The Canon, step by step. Someone claims direct access, in this case teachers who say the kingdom is within. An office fixes the list and makes itself the authorised reader. The state enforces the boundary, and property moves: the Valentinians' meeting houses went to the catholic church by imperial edict. [cite:vt24_vc365] Access narrows, so that for sixteen centuries the teachers were known only through their enemies' summaries. And when the texts reappear, the institutions that handle them absorb the discovery on their own terms. [scene:cn9_the_pattern|The Canon: the pattern across eighteen centuries]

The points where the gate failed are also the same. It failed when copying became cheap: the facsimiles of 1972 to 1977 and the English translation of 1977 did more to free the Nag Hammadi library than any argument. [cite:gn_bar_robinson] [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] It failed when someone inside the circle argued for openness, as Robinson did. [cite:gn_pearse_nh] It failed when other scholars could check the first reading, as DeConick checked National Geographic's. [cite:vt26_getrel_deconick] And it failed, in the oldest case, because a community kept the tradition alive by practising it rather than owning it: the Mandaeans' teaching survived for centuries not in a vault but in a people, until war nearly destroyed them. [cite:gn_wiki_mandaeans]

Today the whole Nag Hammadi library can be read in translation by anyone with a library card or an internet connection. That is a recent condition, less than fifty years old, and it was won rather than given. The foundation that bought the Gospel of Judas said in 2006 that it would give the codex to the Coptic Museum once the profits from publication had been realised. [cite:gn_nbc_judas] The knowledge that its own texts said could not be sold has been bought and sold for eighty years. The next section, marked as a reading, asks what that might mean.
