"Under God": Which God? The Pledge and the Motto, 1954–1956

Between February 1954 and July 1956 the United States wrote God into two of its national formulas: the words "under God" into the Pledge of Allegiance, and "In God we trust" into law as the national motto. The words added were few, and the question they leave open is a large one. The 1954 law added the single word "God", unqualified. No Trinity, no Christ and no named faith appears in the Pledge, and none appeared in the Knights of Columbus resolutions of 1952–53 that began the campaign or in the joint resolution Eisenhower signed on 14 June 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_kofc_pledge] [cite:rs_cw2_osg_newdow] So a reader is entitled to ask which God the nation declared itself to be under.

The question is sharper than it looks, because in one strand of American Christian argument the answer is not taken for granted. Whether Muslims and Christians worship the same God was a serious enough question at Wheaton College, an evangelical school, that in December 2015 the college placed a professor on leave for writing that they do, as told below. [cite:rs_cw2_rns_hawkins] The historian Mark Silk writes that after the Cold War the term "Judeo-Christian", which framed the 1954 debate, became "an emblem of evangelical hostility to Muslims". [cite:rs_cw2_silk_judeochristian] Inside that argument two different tests can be distinguished. One holds that "Allah", the God Muslims pray to, is a different god from the God of the Bible. The other holds that a God understood without the Trinity, without Father, Son and Holy Spirit, is not the true God. Yet the Pledge names no Trinity and no Christ at all.

The second test has a consequence that is seldom stated. Judaism rejects the Trinity as firmly as Islam does. Dale Tuggy's survey in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy records that Jewish and Muslim thinkers alike have argued that "the trinitarian doctrine is incompatible with God's unity", and that Muslims charge trinitarians with "associating" another with God. [cite:rs_cw2_sep_trinity_judaic_islamic] On the test "no Trinity, no true God", Judaism's understanding of God fails exactly as Islam's does. In Christian theology, by contrast, the God of Israel is the Father, the first person of the Trinity. [cite:rs_cw2_sep_trinity_judaic_islamic] The objection Jewish thinkers raise against the Trinity is the same objection Muslim thinkers raise. Yet the working category of 1950s America, "Judeo-Christian", took in one of these two non-Trinitarian traditions and left the other out. How that came about is the history this section tells. First, the three traditions themselves.

Three Siblings, Shifting Pairs

When arguments over which God are conducted between two of the three Abrahamic traditions at a time, the third drops out of view. Put the three side by side and the pairs shift with the question asked.

On God's oneness, Judaism and Islam stand together against the Trinity. The Shema, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD is our God; the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4), is the nearest thing Judaism has to a creed, and Maimonides' second principle states: "This God is One, not two or more than two, but One whose unity is different from all other unities." [cite:rs_cw2_shema_principles] Sura 112 of the Quran, al-Ikhlas, often treated as a summary of Islam's belief in God's oneness (tawhid), reads in one English translation: "Say: He is Allah, the One and Only… He begetteth not, nor is He begotten; and there is none like unto Him." [cite:rs_cw2_ikhlas] And in the Gospel of Mark, Jesus himself names the Shema as the first commandment: "The first of all the commandments is, Hear, O Israel; The Lord our God is one Lord" (Mark 12:29). [cite:rs_cw2_mark1229]

On Jesus, the pairing changes. Islam regards Jesus (Isa) as a prophet and the Messiah (al-Masih) and affirms his virgin birth to Mary; sura 19 is named for her (Maryam). Mainstream Islam rejects his divinity and his place in a Trinity. Judaism accepts neither his messiahship nor his divinity. [cite:rs_cw2_jesus_islam] [cite:rs_cw2_sep_trinity_judaic_islamic] On this question Islam stands nearer to Christianity than Judaism does.

History ran in shifting pairs too. Moses Maimonides (1138–1204), born in Córdoba, wrote the Guide of the Perplexed in Judaeo-Arabic and served as a court physician in Cairo, to Saladin's vizier and to Saladin's son al-Afdal. [cite:rs_cw2_maimonides_bio] When Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, the Ottoman sultan Bayezid II, by one account, sent ships in August 1492 to carry refugees to his empire and ordered his governors to receive them. The same account, an opinion piece, notes that Bayezid valued the refugees' economic potential; the line often attributed to him, that Ferdinand "impoverished his own country and enriched mine", is reported there without a source. [cite:rs_cw2_972_bayezid]

The pairing "Judeo-Christian", which leaves the third sibling out, is not ancient. Silk dates it as a political term to the interfaith anti-fascism of the 1930s and to its spread as a Cold War watchword in the 1950s. [cite:rs_cw2_silk_judeochristian] That is the period of the Pledge.

The question of which God is not one that outsiders have imposed on American Christianity; it divides Christians themselves, and their institutions have answered it in opposite ways. On 10 December 2015 Larycia Hawkins, a professor at Wheaton College, wrote that Muslims and Christians "worship the same God" and wore a hijab in solidarity. On 15 December the college placed her on leave, citing the statement's "theological implications". [cite:rs_cw2_rns_hawkins] The Catholic Church's official teaching takes the other side. Lumen Gentium 16 (1964) says that Muslims, "professing to hold the faith of Abraham, along with us adore the one and merciful God", and the declaration Nostra Aetate (1965) speaks of them with esteem as worshippers of the one God. [cite:rs_cw2_dialog_samegod] On this point the Catholic Church's teaching since 1964 and the position implied by Wheaton's action in 2015 point in opposite directions.

Part of the dispute is a matter of words rather than of theology. "Allah" is the standard Arabic word for God. Arab Christians use it; Arabic translations of both the Hebrew Bible and the Gospels use it; Arabic-speaking Jewish communities used it; and it is related to the Hebrew El and Eloah and the Aramaic Alaha. [cite:rs_cw2_allah_word] To refuse the word is therefore not, in the linguistic sense, to refuse a different deity: an Arabic-speaking Christian reading either Testament in Arabic meets the same word. Whether Muslim and Christian conceptions of God refer to the same being is a theological question, and the institutions above answer it differently. Whether "Allah" names a separate god is a question about Arabic, and the record answers it.

The history of the 1954 change begins with the Catholic fraternal order that started the campaign, told in [scene:cw1_pledge|"Under God", 1951–53]. According to an account published by The Catholic Company, the Knights of Columbus adopted "under God" for their own meetings in 1951, urged a national change at their 1952 Supreme Council, and wrote to every member of Congress in 1953; seventeen resolutions followed in the House. The American Legion credited the Knights with the amendment on 6 October 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_kofc_pledge]

The next push came from a Presbyterian pulpit with the President in the pew. On 7 February 1954 the Rev. George Docherty preached at New York Avenue Presbyterian Church in Washington, with Eisenhower in attendance, that "there was something missing in this Pledge" and that "an atheistic American is a contradiction in terms". [cite:rs_cw2_liberty_docherty] The sermon was framed by the Cold War. The historian Isaac Kramnick quotes Docherty saying he "could hear little Muscovites repeat a similar pledge to their hammer and sickle flag" and that "it is a theological war". [cite:rs_cw2_ffrf_kramnick] Its religious frame was explicitly Christian. As quoted in later reporting, Docherty said, "If you deny the Christian ethic, you fall short of the American ideal of life," and one account quotes him on "the words of Jesus of Nazareth, the living Word of God for the world" as having shaped the nation. [cite:rs_cw2_ds_eisenhower_flagday] [cite:rs_cw2_bng_docherty70]

Members of Congress introduced bills after the sermon, and the change passed both houses in under four months. Eisenhower signed it on Flag Day, 14 June 1954. [cite:rs_cw2_liberty_docherty] [cite:rs_cw2_ffrf_kramnick] His signing statement said that "the millions of our school children will daily proclaim" the nation's dedication to God, that "we are reaffirming the transcendence of religious faith in America's heritage and future", and spoke of "those spiritual weapons which forever will be our country's most powerful resource". [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_pledge] The phrase "spiritual weapons" presented religion as an asset in the contest with Communism.

The committee reports that carried the bill said the same thing in the language of law, and they took care over establishment. The House report, H.R. Rep. No. 1693 of 1954, stated that the change "is not an act establishing a religion", grounding it instead in "a fundamental belief in God" and in the claim that "the human person is important because he was created by God", set against Communism. The Senate report, S. Rep. No. 1287, called "the spiritual bankruptcy of the Communists" one of "our strongest weapons". As quoted in the federal government's 2003 brief in the Newdow case, neither report names a particular faith. [cite:rs_cw2_osg_newdow]

The motto followed in two separate laws, which are easily confused. The first was a currency act: H.R. 619, approved 11 July 1955, numbered 140 among the public laws of the 84th Congress (chapter 303), provided that all United States currency bear "In God We Trust". [cite:rs_cw2_unt_pl140] The inscription first appeared on paper money in 1957, on the one-dollar silver certificate. [cite:rs_cw2_sconres96] [cite:rs_cw2_hrpt112_47] The second was the motto itself. The House Judiciary Committee's report on H.J. Res. 396, dated 28 March 1956, said it would be "of great spiritual and psychological value to our country to have a clearly designated national motto". [cite:rs_cw2_sconres96] Eisenhower signed the resolution on 30 July 1956. The Statutes at Large print it as Public Law 851, chapter 795, 70 Stat. 732: "the national motto of the United States is hereby declared to be 'In God we trust.'" [cite:rs_cw2_stat70_732] It stands today as 36 U.S.C. §302, the subject of House Report 112-47 of 2011, on reaffirming it as the national motto, and of S. Con. Res. 96, marking its fiftieth anniversary. [cite:rs_cw2_hrpt112_47] [cite:rs_cw2_sconres96]

One widely read account gets the numbering wrong. HISTORY.com attributes the 1956 motto law to Public Law 84-140 and says it also put the motto on paper money. [cite:rs_cw2_history_motto] The statute record settles the point: No. 140 was the currency act of 11 July 1955, and the motto was Public Law 851 of 30 July 1956. HISTORY.com conflated the two laws. [cite:rs_cw2_unt_pl140] [cite:rs_cw2_stat70_732]

On motive the documentary record is consistent. Eisenhower's statement, Kramnick's reading of the sermon and the committee reports all frame the changes as religious revival set against Communism. None of these sources documents any link between the 1954 and 1956 changes and Israel, or any Jewish or Zionist organization. Any reading that connects them to Israel would be interpretation without a documentary basis in these sources. [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_pledge] [cite:rs_cw2_ffrf_kramnick] [cite:rs_cw2_sconres96] The campaign's documented authors were a Catholic order, a Presbyterian minister, the members of Congress who filed the bills and the President who signed them.

That still leaves the question this section opened with. The record supports two readings of which God was meant, and each has real evidence behind it. The first is that the God of the Pledge was deliberately none in particular. The House report denied that the change established a religion. [cite:rs_cw2_osg_newdow] Eisenhower, as president-elect, had said on 22 December 1952 that "our form of government has no sense unless it is founded in a deeply felt religious faith, and I don't care what it is". The historian Patrick Henry, writing in 1981, recorded slight variations in the line across three reports and read it as meant to include other faiths; Will Herberg and others read it as a generic, thinned religion. [cite:rs_cw2_wiki_dontcare] Half a century later Justice O'Connor, concurring in Elk Grove v. Newdow (2004), described the Pledge as acknowledging religion "in a general way: a simple reference to a generic 'God'", noted that it "does not refer to a nation 'under Jesus' or 'under Vishnu'", and wrote that an acknowledgment could not count as ceremonial deism "if it explicitly favored one particular religious belief system over another". She also acknowledged that some legislators in 1954 may have intended a religious message. [cite:rs_cw2_newdow_oconnor] Even Docherty, on one account, meant the phrase broadly: The Daily Signal, writing in 2026, paraphrases him as saying it would encompass people of all faiths, naming Jews and Muslims. No verbatim quotation of that passage has been found, and the archive link to the sermon manuscript is dead, so the inclusion of Muslims is reported, not confirmed. [cite:rs_cw2_ds_eisenhower_flagday]

The second reading is that in practice the God meant was the Christian God, or the "Judeo-Christian" God of the Bible. The campaign was led by a Catholic order and carried by a Presbyterian sermon that spoke of "the Christian ethic" and of Jesus. [cite:rs_cw2_kofc_pledge] [cite:rs_cw2_bng_docherty70] [cite:rs_cw2_ds_eisenhower_flagday] Baptist News Global, marking the seventieth anniversary in 2024, read the change in that light. [cite:rs_cw2_bng_docherty70] Eisenhower's own 1952 line came, in one report, with a qualifier: "Of course, it is the Judeo-Christian concept, but it must be a religion with all men being created equal." [cite:rs_cw2_wiki_dontcare]

That qualifier names the working category of 1954. Silk dates "Judeo-Christian" as a political term to 1930s interfaith anti-fascism, in particular the National Conference of Christians and Jews, and traces its spread as a Cold War watchword in the 1950s; he quotes Eisenhower's 1952 use. Its proponents meant it to bring Jews into a shared religious foundation. Some Jewish writers objected: Trude Weiss-Rosmarin called it "a totalitarian aberration". After the Cold War, Silk writes, the term became "an emblem of evangelical hostility to Muslims". [cite:rs_cw2_silk_judeochristian] Coined to widen the circle against fascism and then Communism, the term was resisted by some Jewish writers and later served to narrow the circle against Muslims. In 1954 it was one frame through which the unnamed God of the Pledge could be understood, and the President himself used it.

Reading the Architecture: Which God?

What follows is interpretation, not a documented finding. It is this book's own reading of a pattern, set out with the evidence for it and against it so that the reader can weigh it. The reading runs as follows. In 1954, six years after the founding of Israel in 1948, the United States declared itself a nation under a God it did not name. A former president of that country had, the year before, called himself Cyrus, the Persian king of the biblical return. And the same country now gives Israel $3.8 billion a year, under a 2016 agreement covering fiscal 2019–2028. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_usaid_table] On this reading, the unnamed God of the Pledge is the God of Israel.

Three things support it. The first is the frame of the time. The category through which the President himself understood the nation's faith was "the Judeo-Christian concept", a term built, as Silk shows, to join Jews and Christians in one religious foundation, and one that excluded the third Abrahamic tradition. [cite:rs_cw2_wiki_dontcare] [cite:rs_cw2_silk_judeochristian] In Christian theology the God of Israel is the Father, so a Christian hearing "God" could hear the God of Israel without contradiction. [cite:rs_cw2_sep_trinity_judaic_islamic] The second is Truman. In November 1953, at the Jewish Theological Seminary in New York, Eddie Jacobson introduced the former president as "the man who helped create the state of Israel"; Truman replied, "What do you mean 'helped to create'? I am Cyrus," casting himself as the king of the return. [cite:rs_cw2_judis_truman_cyrus] The third is the older Christian Zionist line in American Protestantism, associated with the Scofield Reference Bible and told in Volume II, in [scene:s05|the Scofield Reference Bible].

Several things cut against it, and they are also documented. The motive recorded in the documents was religious revival set against Communism: Docherty's "theological war", Eisenhower's "spiritual weapons", the Senate report's "spiritual bankruptcy of the Communists". No source here links either change to Israel or to any Jewish or Zionist body. [cite:rs_cw2_ffrf_kramnick] [cite:rs_cw2_ucsb_pledge] [cite:rs_cw2_osg_newdow] The campaign was started by a Catholic order whose Church did not then recognize Israel at all: the Holy See signed its Fundamental Agreement with Israel on 30 December 1993 and established full diplomatic relations by a protocol signed on 14 June 1994, effective 15 June. [cite:rs_cw2_holysee_israel_protocol] [cite:rs_cw2_kofc_pledge] The money of the time does not match the money of today. US assistance to Israel in the years of the change was $73.6 million in 1953, $74.7 million in 1954, $52.7 million in 1955 and $50.8 million in 1956; the $3.8 billion a year dates from the 2016 Memorandum of Understanding, for fiscal 2019–2028. [cite:rs_rd6_jvl_usaid_table] The Truman line is weaker evidence than it looks: Judis cites it as the basis of Truman's pro-Israel reputation precisely in order to argue that the record of 1947–49 shows a president "filled with doubt and regret", and Judis's own article misdates Cyrus's conquest of Babylon to 593 BCE, where the standard date is 539 BCE. [cite:rs_cw2_judis_truman_cyrus] Truman, besides, had left office before the Pledge was changed; the president who signed "under God" was Eisenhower, and in early 1957 Eisenhower pressed Israel to withdraw from the Sinai over its government's resistance, a confrontation told in [scene:cw3_withdrawal|the withdrawal fight of 1957]. Finally, the documented voices of 1954 that spoke to the question at all spoke either of a God left open to every faith or of a Christian God; none in this record spoke of the God of Israel as such.

The reading is therefore an inference from coincidence in time and from later outcomes, not from any document of 1954 that states it. The evidence for a generic God and the evidence for a Christian God are both in the record; the evidence for an Israel-directed God is a pattern of dates and later sums. This book does not resolve the question, and the reader may weigh it differently.

Some things the record here does not settle. Docherty's full sermon text has not been recovered from a working archive, so whether he named Muslims among those the phrase would include remains unconfirmed. [cite:rs_cw2_ds_eisenhower_flagday] The sources do not show what individual members of Congress who filed the seventeen House resolutions understood by "God", beyond the committee reports' language, and O'Connor herself allowed that some may have meant a religious message. [cite:rs_cw2_newdow_oconnor] And the question that opened this section, whether a God named without the Trinity is the God of Christians, is not one that law or history can answer: Wheaton College's action of 2015 and the Catholic Church's teaching in Lumen Gentium point different ways on it, and the Pledge's own text, by saying only "God", declined to answer it at all.
