The Fulbright Inquiry: Thirteen Parts and the Foreign Agents Act, 1963–1964

The Justice Department's order to the American Zionist Council, told in [scene:s11a|the order of 21 November 1962], was issued while a Senate committee was preparing a wider question: how foreign governments and foreign interests were represented in Washington by people who were not diplomats, and whether the law that was supposed to make that representation visible was working. The published record shows a broader investigation, in which the Agency was the subject of one part out of thirteen. This section sets out the inquiry as a whole and the state of the Foreign Agents Registration Act at the time, so that the Agency's case can be read in its setting.

The mandate. The inquiry was authorized by Senate Resolution 362 of the 87th Congress, second session, and continued by Senate Resolution 26 of the 88th Congress, first session. Both rested on sections 134 and 136 of the Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26] Resolution 26 directed the Committee on Foreign Relations to study the activities of nondiplomatic representatives of foreign principals "tending or intended to influence the foreign or domestic policies or interests of the United States", and to consider existing and proposed legislation and recommend changes. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26] The wording mattered. The committee was not charged with investigating a particular country or lobby; it was charged with examining a category of activity, influence on American policy exercised on behalf of foreign principals by people outside the diplomatic system, and with judging whether the law covering that activity was adequate.

The resolution ran from 1 March 1963 to 31 January 1964. It capped the committee's expenses at $48,600, drawn from the Senate's contingent fund, and required a report with recommendations to the Senate by 31 January 1964. It gave the committee power to hold hearings, issue subpoenas, take testimony and hire temporary staff. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26] The committee's chairman, J. William Fulbright, Democrat of Arkansas, presided over the sessions on the Jewish Agency. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628]

The thirteen parts. The hearings were published as Activities of Nondiplomatic Representatives of Foreign Principals in the United States (88th Congress, 1st session). They run to thirteen parts and 1,998 pages, with hearing dates catalogued from 4 February to 14 May 1963. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] The subjects range across the world. Part 2 dealt with the Philippine sugar lobby and John A. O'Donnell. Part 3 examined payments by the Dominican Republic Information Center to the International News Service, and Parts 4 and 5 took up Dominican sugar lobbying. Part 6 concerned news agencies writing for foreign interests. Parts 7 and 10 followed the Hamilton Wright Organization's work for the Republic of China and for South Africa. Part 8 examined Selvage and Lee's work for Portuguese businessmen. Part 11 concerned I. Irving Davidson's work for Nicaragua's President Luis Somoza, and Part 13 the West German public relations of Julius Klein. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] Part 9 concerned the activities of the Jewish Agency for Israel and related lobbying organizations; the catalogue lists "Jewish Agency for Israel (U.S.)" among the subjects of the set. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings]

The catalogue records reached for this book do not describe Parts 1 and 12. Two parts can be dated more closely. Part 8 records hearings on 12 April and 6 May 1963 and occupies pages 827 to 1209 of the series, close to a fifth of the whole. [cite:rs_cw5_unt_pt8] Part 13 prints a sworn letter of 21 October 1963 from Edwin Hartrich in Düsseldorf about a dispute with General Julius Klein (retired), which shows that the record was still being compiled in the autumn of 1963, months after the last catalogued hearing date. [cite:rs_cw5_sres26] The Jewish Agency's sessions also fell outside the catalogued range: the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported closed sessions in June 1963, and Barda places the hearings on the Agency's funding in August. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The catalogue's dates do not describe the whole of the committee's work.

The question the committee was asking. By June 1963, the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported, the committee had for "several months" been studying the administration of the Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] That framing explains the Agency's place in it. The Jewish Agency–American Section was already a registrant under the Act in 1963, as the agent of the World Zionist Organization and of the Jewish Agency in Jerusalem. The committee's question was therefore not whether the Agency was registered, but how the law was being administered. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630628] On this book's reading, the same question ran through the other parts: sugar lobbying, payments to a news agency and public-relations contracts were each a form of influence on behalf of a foreign principal that the Act was meant to bring into view.

The law in practice. The Act's requirements, registration within ten days and penalties of up to five years in prison, are set out with the order of November 1962. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] How it was administered is visible in the Attorney General's annual report to Congress for 1964, published in April 1965, the nearest such report reached for this volume. The Attorney General counted 419 active registrations at the close of 1963 and 480 at the end of 1964. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] For 1964 he reported that "No prosecutive action for willful violations of the Act was required to be undertaken during the year." [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] Enforcement, in that year at least, worked through disclosure rather than prosecution: after the Department asked registrants for fuller disclosure, more than 200 amended registration statements were filed in 1964. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964]

The report shows where Israel-related activity sat within the registry. It lists the Jewish Agency for Israel, American Section, among registrants, and records that it distributed The Israel Digest, a biweekly news pamphlet, in 8,500 copies. It states that "Two registrants distributed the bulk of Israeli publicity in the United States", and it lists among Israel-related registrants the Israel Government Tourist Office, Israel Information Services and the Israel Philatelic Agency in America. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] It does not mention the American Zionist Council. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] The silence cuts both ways: the report records no registration by the Council, and it records no enforcement action against it either.

The report also shows the law itself in motion. It refers to proposed legislation that would allow the Attorney General to require registrants to "maintain separate books and records" for their foreign principal. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] And it discusses Rabinowitz and Boudin v. Attorney General (376 U.S. 605), decided on 30 March 1964, in which the Supreme Court held that the petitioners' work for Cuba fell within the Act's registration provisions. [cite:rs_cw5_doj_fara_1964] That pattern was not peculiar to 1964. The Act, enacted in 1938, was historically rarely invoked as a criminal statute, and after 1966 its enforcement generally shifted toward civil penalties. [cite:crustnews_aipac_history] That was a general pattern across the Act, and it is the context in which any one organization's non-registration has to be read. In the year after the inquiry, then, the Act was being tested in the courts, tightened on paper and enforced by requests for amendment, while no one was prosecuted under it.

One case among many, or a case of special weight? The two readings of the inquiry rest on different evidence and ask different questions. The first rests on the published record itself. The catalogue shows thirteen parts covering principals from the Philippines, the Dominican Republic, Nicaragua, Portugal, the Republic of China, South Africa and West Germany, of which the Jewish Agency was Part 9. [cite:rs_cw5_ol_hearings] On this reading the committee was doing what its resolution asked: surveying influence by nondiplomatic agents of many foreign principals, of which Israel-related organizations were one group among several, and the Agency an existing registrant whose disclosure was examined like others'.

The second reading concerns weight, not count. Grant F. Smith of IRmep, an advocate of registration, presents the Jewish Agency–American Zionist Council material as the inquiry's central finding: evidence of an Israeli funding conduit into American lobbying. [cite:rs_cw5_smith_antiwar_2013] The strongest form of his case is that Part 9 touched a body the Justice Department had separately ordered to register, and that the sums involved, on the figures in the order and the Senate record, ran into millions of dollars. Kobby Barda, writing in Modern Judaism in 2026, rejects that reading. He calls the arrangement an American lobby operating within the law, and treats the payments as purchases that ended before the hearings. [cite:rs_cw5_barda_2026] The strongest form of his case is that the inquiry's subject was the administration of the Act, that the Agency was already registered under it, and that the committee's chairman, by the account of the Agency's own chairman, said there had been no wrongdoing. [cite:rs_cw5_djn_19630816] Both readings, and the evidence each side brought, are set out in [scene:cw5_jewish_agency_hearing|the Jewish Agency before the committee].

The record answers part of the question and leaves the rest open. It settles the count: the Agency was one subject among many in a survey whose breadth is a matter of record. It does not settle the weight, which depends on what Part 9 showed and on figures for the Agency's payments that remain unreconciled. Nor do the sources reached for this volume show the committee's final report, whether it was delivered by its 31 January 1964 deadline, or what it recommended. What is documented is the inquiry's mandate, its budget, its thirteen parts, and the state of the law it examined: 480 registrants by the end of 1964, none prosecuted that year, and a proposal, not yet law, to make them keep their foreign principals' accounts apart.
