A Fortune Kept Private: How the House of Rothschild Held Its Wealth

Start in the present, where the rules are written down. In February 2023 the Rothschild family's holding company, Rothschild & Co Concordia, offered to buy every share of the Paris-listed bank that it did not already own. The offer was €48 a share, and Reuters put the bank's value at about €3.6 billion. Before the offer the wider family already held close to 55% of the shares and 69% of the voting rights, by Reuters' count (the company's own figures are 54.5% and 66.8%). Other wealthy families joined the shareholder pact, among them the Wertheimer brothers, who own Chanel, and the Peugeot and Dassault families. [cite:wb_reuters_rco_feb6] [cite:wb_rco_presentation] [cite:wb_reuters_rco_feb13] On 11 October 2023 the last outside shares were bought out and the bank left the Paris stock exchange. [cite:wb_amf_squeezeout] From that day there has been no market price for the business. The bank still publishes its yearly accounts, but no one can watch its value change from day to day. Nothing about this was hidden. It was a takeover offer, filed with the market regulator. But its result is that the daily public number is gone.

The same gap opens in other ways. In June 2021 ProPublica reported, from leaked tax records, that the twenty-five richest Americans saw their wealth grow by a combined $401 billion between 2014 and 2018, while they paid $13.6 billion in federal income tax. ProPublica called the ratio, 3.4%, a "true tax rate." The comparison is with growth in wealth, not with income, and that is the point: under American law a gain that has not been sold is not income, and is not taxed. [cite:wb_propublica_2021] The reporters described a pattern known as "buy, borrow, die," a phrase they credit to the tax law professor Edward McCaffery. Hold assets that rise in value; borrow against them, since a loan is not income; and at death pass them on. The heir takes the assets at their value on the day of death, so the gain built up over a lifetime is never taxed as a gain at all. [cite:wb_propublica_2021] [cite:wb_openjurist_1014] [cite:wb_wiki_stepup] For 2026, the first $15 million of each estate passes free of federal estate tax, an exemption Congress has now made permanent and tied to inflation. [cite:wb_kpmg_rp2025_32] [cite:wb_morganlewis_2026]

A public figure can also be whatever a contract declares. On the Albanian coast, in a protected wetland known for its flamingos, land at Zvërnec was agreed for sale in November 2025 at an estimated €5.5 million and completed in April 2026 at a declared €122 million (AFP; Reuters gives about €110 million), for a resort project in which Jared Kushner is a confirmed investor. Both prices came after a February 2024 change in the law on protected areas that opened certain protected zones to luxury hotels. [cite:wb_afp_timeline] [cite:wb_reuters_shehu] Albania's anti-corruption prosecutor has since frozen the sale money. Its case files, as Reuters reported in July 2026, accuse the seller's network of buying the land with drug money and forged deeds, which the seller denies. The files make no allegation against Kushner or the other investors. As of August 2026, the latest reporting found, the money remained frozen, the seller faced charges he denies, the investors said they were not under investigation, and the government said the project would go ahead. [cite:wb_reuters_shehu] [cite:wb_occrp_freeze] [cite:wb_deepdive_aug] [cite:wb_protothema_aug]

None of these rules existed in 1810. The principle behind them did: a fortune seen from outside is only the part that its owners choose, or are made, to show. The economist Thomas Piketty's argument that the return on capital tends to outrun the growth of the economy as a whole explains why that hidden part matters over time, and the section returns to it below. [cite:wc_ted_piketty]

The earlier sections of this chapter follow what the Rothschild houses did in public in the nineteenth century: the loans to Prussia and to Britain, the Suez advance, the money lent to the Pope. This section turns to the structure underneath those transactions, which was private by design. It asks four questions in order. How was the business held, and who could see inside it? How did the partners talk to one another? How large did the fortune become, and in what forms was it kept? And did it simply grow, as compound interest says money should, or did something pull it apart? Each answer rests on documents that are now open, most of them in the family's own archive, and each comes with the points that the record still leaves unsettled.

A partnership closed to outsiders. The business began as a written partnership, not a company with shareholders. On 27 September 1810 Mayer Amschel Rothschild and three of his sons, Amschel, Salomon and Carl, signed the first agreement, under the name Mayer Amschel Rothschild & Sons. James, still under age, was to join when he came of age. Nathan, already established in London, was not a party to it. The agreement ran for ten years and could be renewed, and five signed and sealed copies were made. [cite:wa_ra_partnership1810] [cite:wa_ra_mars] The capital was set out in gulden: 370,000 from the father, 185,000 each from Amschel and Salomon, and 30,000 each from Carl and James, James's share held without interest until he came of age. Profits were divided in fifty parts, twenty-five to Mayer Amschel and twelve each to Amschel and Salomon. [cite:wa_ra_partnership1810]

The terms that mattered most were the ones that kept the business closed. The heirs of a partner who died could not demand to see the books. They had to accept the last balance sheet, and they were paid their share in company stock, not in cash. Balance sheets themselves were to be drawn up, in the Archive's summary, only as soon as business allowed. No partner could trade on the Stock Exchange without the others knowing first. Disputes did not go to a court: they went to Mayer Amschel as arbitrator, and after his death to two arbitrators and a chairman, with a penalty of 20,000 thalers for any breach, payable within twenty-four hours. [cite:wa_ra_partnership1810] The family's own archive states the reason for one further rule. Partners were male family members only. Women advised and used their social connections, but they could not formally join, so that a son-in-law could never claim a controlling interest. [cite:wa_ra_partnership1810]

Mayer Amschel died on 19 September 1812. His will, as History Today summarizes it, laid down that only his sons should own the business; his daughters, their husbands and their children were excluded. [cite:wa_ht_founder] A 2022 review of Natalie Livingstone's history of the family's women says the will explicitly barred female descendants, daughters and the wives of male descendants alike, from holding shares or having a say. [cite:wa_hadassah] Reviewing Niall Ferguson's authorized history, Robert Skidelsky called the founder's insistence on a family partnership "law for a century," and noted Ferguson's count that of twenty-one marriages involving his descendants between 1824 and 1877, fifteen were between direct descendants. [cite:wa_skidelsky] [cite:wa_pw_vol1] Several further provisions are often attributed to the will: that it forbade any public inventory of the estate, barred lawsuits, and named the eldest son of the eldest son as head of the family. None of the sources consulted states them, and they are set aside here as unverified until they can be checked against Ferguson's first volume or Amos Elon's biography of the founder. [cite:wa_ht_founder] [cite:wa_ra_mars]

Later partnership agreements exist, but their terms were not found in the sources consulted. The Archive's own list of acquisitions records "Family partnership agreements, 1815-1862" and balance sheets from 1828 to 1910, and its history of the London house says that Lionel de Rothschild modified the partnership agreement in 1838 to keep the London house's relative autonomy. [cite:wa_ra_acq2002] [cite:wa_ra_nmr_london] Nathan's own will, drawn up in his last days in July 1836, told his four sons to work with their mother, Hannah, and not to undertake "any transactions of moment" without first asking her advice. [cite:wa_ra_nmr_person] The Foreign Affairs review of Ferguson describes the result: a transnational partnership that held together for five generations through family loyalty, written agreements and intermarriage. [cite:wa_fa_cooper]

The partnership was not a temporary arrangement. The London house remained one for a century and a half. In 1941 it formed a corporate partner, Rothschilds Continuation Ltd, to ensure continuity in wartime. It admitted its first partner from outside the family in 1960. It ceased to be a partnership and became a limited company, N M Rothschild & Sons Limited, only on 30 September 1970. [cite:wa_ra_nmr_london] [cite:wa_ra_timeline] The Paris house became a limited company at about the same time, on dates the Archive and a reference summary give as 1967 and 1 January 1968. [cite:wa_ra_paris] [cite:wa_wiki_banque] One review of Ferguson states that "the partnership system ended in 1905"; what it refers to is unclear, and it conflicts with the Archive's date for London, so it is noted and not relied on. [cite:wa_skidelsky]

A private channel. The houses ran on information, and the information travelled in a form outsiders could not easily read. The brothers wrote to one another in Judendeutsch, which the Archive defines as German written in Hebrew characters. The Archive is careful to add that this was "not in itself a code," but it was hard to decipher, and so it protected the letters from prying eyes. [cite:wa_ra_judendeutsch1816] [cite:wa_ra_breakseal] The letters went almost daily, to keep up the cooperation the partnership agreements required, and the family's couriers delivered news faster than almost anyone else in Europe could. The Archive's account also mentions private code names. It gives no example of one, and no other source consulted does either. [cite:wa_ra_breakseal]

That correspondence survives in the Rothschild Archive in London: about 20,000 letters between the brothers, from 1811 to 1868, in some 142 boxes, though the Archive says no precise count has been made. James, the last surviving brother from 1861, went on writing to his London nephews in Judendeutsch until his death in 1868. Transcription began in 1989, mainly by Mordechai Zucker, and by about 2000 some 2,500 letters had been fully translated. [cite:wa_ra_breakseal] [cite:wa_ra_jewishstudies] The Archive itself was founded in 1978 by Victor, 3rd Lord Rothschild, and passed to a trust in 1999. Ferguson had free access to all of it except the most recent thirty years, without censorship, according to Jonathan Steinberg's review in the London Review of Books. [cite:wa_ra_about] [cite:wa_steinberg_lrb] The Archive describes the letters as more revealing of their writers than comparable correspondence of the period. [cite:wa_ra_breakseal]

There is a hole in the record. "There is a break in the letters from 1818 to 1825 for reasons which are not clear," the Archive writes, and it offers accidental destruction as the likeliest explanation. [cite:wa_ra_breakseal] Nothing in the sources shows anything more deliberate, and nothing rules it out. A second family archive had a stranger history. Founded by Salomon in Frankfurt in 1844 and moved to Vienna in 1927, it was seized after the German annexation of Austria, inventoried on 2 April 1938, sent to a Gestapo store in Berlin, taken by the Red Army to a special archive in Moscow in 1945, opened there in 1990, and returned to the Viennese branch's descendants in 2001. [cite:wa_provenienz] [cite:wa_trentmann] The letters were private for a century because the family kept them so. Their opening, when it came, came from the family's own decision in London and from war and its aftermath in Vienna.

How large it became. Because the houses published no accounts, their size has to be reconstructed from their own balance sheets, and the most complete reconstruction is Ferguson's. On his figures, as his reviewers report them, the combined capital of the houses grew from £10,000 in 1797 to £1.77 million in 1818. [cite:wa_skidelsky] By 1825 it was about nine times the capital of Baring Brothers, the leading London rival. [cite:wa_wiki_nmr] [cite:wa_steinberg_lrb] It reached £7.78 million in 1844, ten times the Barings'; £9.5 million in 1852; £22 million in 1862; and £34 million in 1874. [cite:wa_skidelsky] [cite:wa_wiki_nmr] By 1899 it stood at £41 million, more than the five largest German joint-stock banks combined. [cite:wa_wiki_nmr] For most of the nineteenth century, Steinberg writes, the Rothschilds were "the biggest bank in the world by a wide margin." [cite:wa_steinberg_lrb]

Two further numbers from the same history give the scale of individual fortunes. Ferguson puts Nathan's net worth at his death in 1836 at 0.62 percent of British national income, and James's at his death in 1868 at just over 4 percent of French national output. [cite:wa_wiki_nmr] [cite:wa_skidelsky] No probate figure for Nathan's estate, or for that of his grandson Natty, the first Lord Rothschild, who died in 1915, has been found. [cite:wa_ra_nmr_person] [cite:wa_wiki_natty]

A capital figure is a number on a balance sheet. Much of the family's wealth took forms that never appear as one. Nathaniel de Rothschild bought the Bordeaux estate of Brane-Mouton in 1853 and renamed it Château Mouton Rothschild, for 1,175,000 francs according to a reference summary. [cite:wa_ra_timeline] [cite:wa_wiki_nathaniel] Agents of James de Rothschild bought Château Lafite at auction in Paris on 8 August 1868 for 4,140,000 francs, with a further 300,000 francs for the neighbouring Carruades estate; James died on 15 November 1868, the Archive notes, "never having set foot on the property." [cite:wa_ra_lafite] Ferdinand de Rothschild bought the Waddesdon estate in Buckinghamshire, about 2,700 acres, from the Duke of Marlborough in 1874 for about £200,000, and built the house between 1874 and 1885, with a wing added in 1889. [cite:wa_waddesdon] James began the Château de Ferrières in 1853, and Joseph Paxton designed Mentmore for Mayer de Rothschild. [cite:wa_ra_timeline]

Assets of this kind have no daily price. A vineyard, a country house or a collection of paintings has a value only when someone sells it, and until then any figure is an estimate. Family spending also drew on the houses' profits. Skidelsky, summarizing Ferguson, attributes part of the halt in capital growth after 1874 to profits withdrawn for family spending. [cite:wa_skidelsky] Some of what was bought later passed to the public. James de Rothschild bequeathed Waddesdon, its main collections and its grounds to the National Trust in 1957, and it opened to visitors in 1959; Ferdinand's Renaissance collection had already gone to the British Museum as the Waddesdon Bequest. [cite:wa_waddesdon] Guy and Marie-Hélène de Rothschild gave Ferrières to the University of Paris in 1975. [cite:wa_ra_timeline] The Archive's chronology credits James's widow, Dorothy, with the Waddesdon gift; Waddesdon's own history says James bequeathed it and Dorothy chaired its management committee until 1988. [cite:wa_ra_timeline] [cite:wa_waddesdon]

Lending to governments: power in both directions. The houses' largest business was lending to states, and those loans were an exchange that ran both ways. The clearest documented case is in London. In November 1875 N M Rothschild & Sons advanced the British government £4,000,000 to buy 44 percent of the Suez Canal Company's shares while Parliament was not sitting. The Archive calls it a technically unsecured loan, and it was repaid within five months. [cite:rs_rd0_hefler_2023] [cite:rs_rd0_rotharchive_suez] The government gained a strategic asset its prime minister defended as a highway to India; the house earned a commission that critics in Parliament said amounted to 15 percent interest, a claim Disraeli rejected. [cite:rs_rd0_disraeli_1876] The full story is told in [scene:bc_s02|the section on the London house].

The papal loans show the same exchange in Rome. The Archive records that the papal administration sought the Naples house's services and that five loans were issued between 1831 and 1850, without describing their terms. [cite:wa_ra_naples] A reference summary gives the first as a £400,000 loan in 1832 on a 5 percent contract signed by Rothschild and Torlonia. [cite:wa_wiki_papal] The economic historian Daniela Felisini describes that first loan as made in partnership with James de Rothschild of Paris, and as one of the most controversial operations of its period, both for its financial conditions and because the lender was a Jewish banker; the reference summary names Carl of Naples instead. [cite:wa_felisini] [cite:wa_wiki_papal] The most striking term attributed to the loan, a clause requiring the Holy See to tell the Rothschilds before approaching any other lender and to give them preference on equal terms, is footnoted in that summary to a popular book of 1913, Ignatius Balla's The Romance of the Rothschilds, and to no other source this book could find. Until it is confirmed from the contract or from Ferguson, it should be treated as unconfirmed. The papal loans are told in full in [scene:bc_s03|the section on the Pope's bankers].

Compounding, and what pulled against it. A fortune that is never sold is never priced, but it is not standing still. Money that earns a return and is left to earn again grows on itself. The economist Thomas Piketty, in Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2014), argues that the return on capital has usually run ahead of the growth of the economy as a whole, and that as the gap widens, wealth concentrates faster; on the figures summarized from his book, the return on capital held at roughly 4 to 5 percent for most of history and rose above 5 percent during the Industrial Revolution. [cite:wc_ted_piketty] The arithmetic is simple to state. A pound that compounds at 5 percent a year for two hundred years becomes about £17,000; at 3 percent, about £370. That is an illustration of the mechanism, not a measurement of any family's fortune.

The Rothschild record shows the mechanism at work and the forces that worked against it, side by side. On one side stood everything this section has described: the family-only partnership, books closed to heirs, intermarriage that kept the capital inside the family, and a correspondence outsiders could not easily read. On the other stood division among heirs, spending, revolution, war and the state. The capital stopped growing after 1874, by Skidelsky's account of Ferguson partly because profits were drawn off for family spending, and the London house's return on capital fell from 9.8 percent in the 1870s to 3.9 percent in the 1900s, while Barings earned 27.1 percent in the later decade. [cite:wa_skidelsky]

The houses closed one after another. In Naples, the house Carl had founded in 1821 stopped trading in 1863, after Garibaldi took the city in September 1860; Adolphe Carl would not work with the new regime and left the partnership, and the other houses would not back the deposed king. [cite:wa_ra_naples] [cite:wa_ra_naples_soulie] In Frankfurt, after the death of the last partner, Wilhelm Carl, in 1901, the family voluntarily liquidated the founding house, with no male heir and no family member willing to move there; its remaining business went to the Disconto-Gesellschaft of Berlin, and the Naples records were destroyed in the liquidation. [cite:wa_ra_mars] [cite:wa_ra_naples_soulie]

In Vienna the end came by force. The Archive says the house closed in March 1938. Louis von Rothschild was arrested after the annexation, held first in prison and then in the Hotel Metropole, and released, by the Archive's account, in July 1939, after family negotiations and a payment it gives as $21,000,000; his assets were put under a German commissioner. [cite:wa_ra_timeline] [cite:wa_ra_louis] The sources differ on the date of his release: the Jewish Telegraphic Agency reported on 14 May 1939 that he had already reached Switzerland. [cite:wa_jta1939] Austria's commission for provenance research recorded in 2013 that the Nazi authorities moved to expropriate the Viennese branch immediately after the annexation. [cite:wa_provenienz] In Paris, Banque Rothschild was one of thirty-nine banks nationalized by the Mitterrand government, in 1982 by the Archive's dating; the family started again as P.O. Gestion in 1983 and regained the use of its name in October 1986. [cite:wa_ra_timeline] [cite:wa_ra_paris] [cite:wa_wiki_banque]

What the record shows, and what it does not. The documented finding is narrow and firm. The Rothschild houses kept their affairs private by design: through a written partnership that barred outsiders, closed the books to heirs and sent disputes to private arbitration; through a correspondence in a script few outsiders could read, carried by the family's own couriers; and through wealth held in forms that are never priced until they are sold. The partnership form lasted, in London, until 1970, and the company stayed private after it. It was not unusual in kind. Private partnerships among bankers were common in the period, and other banking families worked on similar lines, as an earlier section of this chapter sets out. What set this one apart was its scale and how long it lasted.

The record does not show what a long tradition of hostile writing has claimed: a hidden family hand directing the affairs of states. The houses' great loans are known because governments had to raise, vote and repay them in public. The privacy covered the family's own books, not the policy of the states it lent to. The record also leaves real gaps. The terms of the partnership agreements after 1810 were not found in the sources consulted. The papal exclusivity clause rests on a single popular source. The letters of 1818 to 1825 are missing. No probate figure was found for the estates of Nathan in 1836 or Natty in 1915. And no one outside the family can price the land, houses and art that were never sold. A careful reader can hold both conclusions at once: the fortune was larger and more deliberately private than its public figures suggest, and the documents that are open describe a family business, not a secret government.
