Eleusis: The Secret That Was Kept

The Eleusinian Mysteries were, in Britannica's words, the "most famous of the secret religious rites of ancient Greece". They were built on the myth told in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, in which the goddess searches for her daughter Persephone, carried off to the underworld by Hades. Candidates were purified at the Lesser Mysteries in late winter and initiated at the Greater Mysteries at Eleusis in the autumn, after a ritual sea bath and days of fasting. [cite:ts13_brit_eleusis] Initiation had more than one level: those who returned could reach the epopteia, the grade of "those who have seen". [cite:vt33_nwe_eleusis]

What they saw is not recorded. The central rites "were and remain a secret", and there is "no sure evidence of what the rites actually were". [cite:ts13_brit_eleusis] What initiates said they gained is recorded. Cicero, himself an initiate, wrote that the mysteries taught Romans "how to die with a better hope". [cite:vt33_wh_eleusis] That phrase is the most secure statement of what Eleusis offered: not a doctrine, but a changed relation to death, given by an experience.

One thing about the rite is known: a drink. The kykeon, in the Hymn to Demeter a mixture of water, barley and pennyroyal, was used at the climax of the Mysteries to break the sacred fast. [cite:og_wiki_kykeon] In 1978 three writers, R. Gordon Wasson, the chemist Albert Hofmann and the classicist Carl Ruck, proposed in The Road to Eleusis that ergot, a fungus that grows on barley, made the drink psychoactive. [cite:og_prh_road] The idea remains contested. The historian of Greek religion Walter Burkert rejected it, arguing that fasting followed by a sacred meal could produce "communal bliss" without any drug. Archaeologists have since found traces of ergot in a vessel linked to the kykeon at a Greek sanctuary in Catalonia, which shows the fungus was present, not what the initiates drank. [cite:og_wiki_kykeon] The secret is still kept.

Eleusis was not the only mystery. On Samothrace, anyone free of blood guilt could be initiated, "whether enslaved, free, Greek, non-Greek, male, or female", and the cult seems to have "gradually died out" rather than been closed. [cite:vt33_archaeology_samothrace] About forty thin gold tablets, placed in graves from the fourth to the first centuries BCE, tell the dead to drink from the Lake of Memory and to say to its guardians: "I am the son of Earth and Starry Heaven. But my race is heavenly." [cite:vt33_getty_goldtablets]

The end of Eleusis came in two stages, by two actors. Theodosius's laws of 391 and 392 forbade the cult, though the sanctuary may have continued for some years. Then in 395 and 396 the Gothic king Alaric's forces ravaged Attica and destroyed the Telesterion, the hall of initiation, "which was never to be rebuilt". [cite:vt33_livius_eleusis] [cite:vt33_wiki_telesterion] Alaric's Goths were Christians of the non-Nicene kind, and no source says they acted for any bishop. The pagan writer Eunapius, initiated at Eleusis himself, blamed men "clad in black" who came with Alaric, a phrase commonly taken to mean monks. [cite:vt33_wh_eleusis] [cite:vt33_eunapius_hierophant] The full account of the closings is in [scene:vt33_the_closings|The Vatican Architecture: the closings].
