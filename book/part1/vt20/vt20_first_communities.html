The First Communities: Houses, a Common Purse and Gifts Without a Tithe

Ron's thesis for Book Two is that institutional religion built a mediated, taxed and obligatory system on top of teachings that offered direct access to God. The fairest place to begin testing it is with the earliest communities themselves: how they met, who led them, and how money moved among them, in the century and a half before any emperor favoured the movement. The sources are few, mostly written by insiders, and some describe ideals as much as practice. Read together, they show voluntary giving and no tithe system, and they also show leadership offices and rules forming early, well before Rome's bishop had any special standing.

Meeting in houses

The first followers in Jerusalem, as the Acts of the Apostles tells it, attended the Temple "day by day" and broke bread "in their homes". [cite:vt20_esv_acts2] Paul's letters, the earliest evidence, greet congregations by the houses they met in: Prisca and Aquila, his "fellow workers", and "the church in their house". [cite:vt20_esv_rom16] There were no church buildings in this period. The earliest securely identified Christian meeting place, at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates, is a private house built in 232 or 233, probably converted for Christian use around 240 and abandoned in 256. [cite:vt20_wiki_dura] For about two centuries, a "church" (in Greek ekklēsia, an assembly) meant the people who gathered, usually in a member's home.

A Roman governor's report gives an outsider's view. Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus in northern Asia Minor around 111, wrote to the emperor Trajan that the Christians he interrogated met "on a fixed day before dawn", sang a hymn to Christ "as to a god", bound themselves by oath not to commit fraud, theft or adultery, and later shared a meal of "ordinary and innocent food". He had two slave women whom the Christians called deaconesses tortured and found nothing but what he called excessive superstition. Trajan replied that Christians were not to be sought out and anonymous accusations were not to be accepted, but that those convicted were to be punished. [cite:vt20_wiki_pliny]

Women named in the record

The same letter of Paul that greets the house church of Prisca and Aquila commends Phoebe, "a servant of the church at Cenchreae", where the Greek word is the one later used for deacons and several modern translations render it "deacon"; it also greets Andronicus and Junia, who are "well known to the apostles", a phrase that can also be translated "notable among the apostles". [cite:vt20_esv_rom16] With Pliny's two deaconesses, these are documented women in recognised roles within the first eighty years. How far those roles extended, and how the translation choices have been argued over, is a debate this book does not settle; the presence of the women in the sources is the finding.

The common purse in Acts

Acts describes the Jerusalem community as holding property in common. Believers "had all things in common", sold possessions and distributed the proceeds "as any had need"; they were "of one heart and soul", and "there was not a needy person among them". Barnabas, a Levite from Cyprus, sold a field and laid the money at the apostles' feet. [cite:vt20_esv_acts2] [cite:vt20_esv_acts4] This is the community's own account, written by the author of Luke's gospel, most likely between 80 and 90 CE, several decades after the events, and scholars read it as history written with theological aims. [cite:vt20_wiki_acts] It describes an ideal the author wanted remembered as much as an accounting system.

Acts' own next story shows that sharing was not, in its telling, a compulsory levy. When Ananias and Sapphira sell land and secretly keep part of the price, Peter says to Ananias: "While it remained unsold, did it not remain your own?" The fault charged is lying about the gift, not keeping property, though the story's ending, in which both die, shows that the narrator treated the deception with the utmost severity. [cite:vt20_esv_acts5]

Paul's collection: voluntary by his own insistence

At the Jerusalem meeting the leaders asked Paul to "remember the poor". [cite:vt20_nrsv_gal12] He did so by organising a collection among his Gentile churches for the believers in Jerusalem. To Corinth he wrote that on "the first day of every week" each should put something aside "as he may prosper", so that nothing would need collecting when he arrived, and that the gift would be carried to Jerusalem by people the church itself chose. [cite:vt20_esv_1cor16] In 2 Corinthians he insists that it be "a willing gift, not as an exaction": each should give "as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver". [cite:vt20_esv_2cor9] The money went to the poor of another community, not to a priesthood, and Paul did not set a rate.

The Didache: support for prophets, and a rule against charging

The Didache, or Teaching of the Twelve Apostles, is a short church manual most scholars date to around 100 CE, within a range of about 50 to 120. It was lost for centuries and published in 1883 by Philotheos Bryennios from a manuscript copied in 1056. [cite:vt20_ecw_didache] Its rules on money run in two directions. A travelling apostle may stay one day, or two if needed, and "if he ask money, he is a false prophet". A true prophet who settles in a community, however, is "worthy of his support": the community is to give the first-fruits of its winepress, threshing floor and flocks to the prophets, "for they are your high priests", and if it has no prophet, to the poor; first-fruits of money and clothing are to be given "as it may seem good to you". [cite:vt20_na_didache] This is the nearest thing in the early sources to a regular offering for religious leaders. It is a first-fruits gift, not a fixed tenth; its amount for money and goods is left to the giver; and it is matched by a rule that a teacher who asks for money is a fraud.

The Didache also shows structure. It tells communities to gather "every Lord's day" to break bread, and to "appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons worthy of the Lord", men who are meek, truthful and "not lovers of money". [cite:vt20_na_didache] Leaders here are chosen by the community and serve beside the prophets; the text does not place one bishop over a city.

Justin and Irenaeus: still voluntary in the second century

Around the middle of the second century, Justin Martyr described a Sunday gathering in Rome for the emperor's benefit. The "memoirs of the apostles" or the prophets were read, the "president" taught, bread and wine were shared, and then "they who are well to do, and willing, give what each thinks fit". The money was "deposited with the president", who used it for orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. [cite:vt20_justin_67] Around 180, Irenaeus of Lyon drew the contrast with tithes directly: the Jews had "the tithes of their goods consecrated" to God, whereas Christians, having received liberty, "set aside all their possessions for the Lord's purposes". [cite:vt20_na_irenaeus_iv18] Irenaeus's point is that Christian giving should exceed a tenth, not that it was a tax; in his telling, it rested on the giver's freedom.

The Catholic Church's own 1913 encyclopedia agrees that support for clergy "in the beginning" came from "the spontaneous offerings of the faithful", and dates the earliest positive church legislation on tithes to a letter of bishops at Tours in 567 and the Council of Mâcon in 585, with the first civil enforcement under Charlemagne at the end of the eighth century. [cite:vt20_cathen_tithes] On the record, then, there was no tithe system in the first centuries of the movement. How the tithe became a church law and then a state-enforced tax for about a thousand years is the subject of [scene:vt31_tithe|Part Thirty-One].

From assembly to bishop

The record that supports Ron's point on money complicates it on mediation. A generation after the Didache, Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, wrote a series of letters on his way to execution in Rome, traditionally dated around 108 to 110, though some scholars place them in the 140s or later. He is the earliest known writer to insist on a single bishop in each city, assisted by presbyters and deacons, and the first known to call the church "catholic", meaning universal. [cite:vt20_wiki_ignatius] To the church at Smyrna he wrote: "See that you all follow the bishop"; "wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude also be"; and "it is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast". [cite:vt20_na_ignatius_smyrn] That is the first clear statement in the sources that access to the community's central rites runs through an office. It came from Antioch, not Rome, and from a man about to be killed by the empire, not one backed by it.

By the middle of the second century, then, the communities had weekly Sunday gatherings, readings, appointed leaders and, in some cities, a single bishop who controlled the rites, while giving remained voluntary and no tithe was levied.
