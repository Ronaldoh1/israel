Between 1917 and 1922, a wartime promise became a system of government. When this part of the story opens, Palestine is still Ottoman territory. Britain has made overlapping commitments about it to the Arabs, to France and to the Zionist movement, and the Balfour Declaration is only a letter of policy, with no legal force. When it closes, Britain holds a Mandate, a commission to govern the country, approved by the League of Nations, with Balfour's language written into its preamble. The United States Congress has endorsed the Jewish national home that Balfour's letter favored. And Palestine has become a named polity, with an administration in Jerusalem. [cite:mandate_text]

The question here is how that happened, and by what right. The chapters that follow trace who drafted the declaration and why the British Cabinet approved it. They show how the victorious powers gave it the force of law. They record what the people living in Palestine said about it, through their own congresses and through an American commission whose report was shelved, and how the first violence of the Mandate years was investigated and reported. They also follow the institutions that grew up alongside the new government: the High Commissioner's office, a permanent Zionist fundraising fund, and the first organized American campaigns.

By 1922 Britain had already begun to narrow what it had promised. That first retreat, and the long argument that grew from the separation of Transjordan, the land east of the Jordan River, bring this part to a close. [cite:wiki_churchill_wp_1922]
