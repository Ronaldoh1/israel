Who Chose the Bible: The Canon from the Second Century to Trent

A canon, from the Greek word for a measuring rod, is a closed list of books that a community treats as scripture. Christians did not receive one at a single moment. For about three hundred years after the books of the New Testament were written, churches in different cities read overlapping but not identical sets of texts, and bishops wrote lists that differed at the edges. The modern Catholic list was defined as binding only in 1546, and Protestant and Ethiopian churches today use lists of different lengths. This section sets out the documented steps, then tests three claims that circulate widely: that the Council of Nicaea chose the books, that it did so by a vote of "218 to 2", and that the losing books were ordered destroyed. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon]

Lists before councils

The oldest surviving list of New Testament books is the Muratorian fragment, a Latin text. Most scholars date the list it preserves to the late second century, around 170 to 200; a minority place it in the fourth century. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] Either way, it shows a church already sorting books into those read in worship and those not, well before any council addressed the question.

The first list that matches the 27 books of today's New Testament exactly comes from Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, in his 39th Festal Letter of 367. Egyptian bishops wrote such a letter each year to announce the date of Easter, and Athanasius used this one to list the books "included in the Canon": the four Gospels, Acts, the seven general letters, fourteen letters of Paul (counting Hebrews) and Revelation. [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] [cite:vt14_wiki_easterletter] He then named a second group, including the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Teaching of the Apostles and the Shepherd, that were "not indeed included in the Canon" but "appointed by the Fathers to be read" by new converts. A third group he rejected outright: books "called apocryphal", which he described as "an invention of heretics". [cite:vt14_ect_festal39]

The letter warns readers away from those books. It does not order anyone to burn, bury or destroy them. [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] The often-repeated claim that Athanasius "ordered the destruction of non-canonical texts" is an overstatement of what the document says.

The Western councils

A council at Rome in 382 under Pope Damasus is often said to have issued the same list that Trent later adopted. That attribution depends on the so-called Gelasian Decree, a text that Ernst von Dobschütz and others regard as a private compilation of the early sixth century. Geoffrey Hahneman, Edmon Gallagher and Clare Rothschild argue that the list cannot be a decree of the 382 synod, chiefly because Jerome, who was probably present, never mentions one; William Jurgens holds that the canon section is genuinely from 382. The question is open, and a careful account should say so. [cite:vt14_wiki_rome382]

The firmer record is North African. The Council of Hippo in 393 is the first council known to have accepted the present New Testament canon, and a summary of its acts was read and accepted again at the councils of Carthage in 397 and 419. Augustine of Hippo, the leading figure at these meetings, treated the canon as closed. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon] These were regional councils; their lists carried authority because other churches came to accept them, not because a central office imposed them.

The East, Florence and Trent

The Greek-speaking and Syriac-speaking churches were slower and less concerned to draw a sharp line. Revelation was accepted in most of the East only by the fifth century, the Armenian Bible did not include it until about 1200, and the Syriac Peshitta originally left out 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation. [cite:vt14_wiki_ntcanon]

For the Catholic Church the decisive step came in the dispute with the Reformers. The Council of Florence had approved a list in 1442. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] The Council of Trent, at its fourth session on 8 April 1546, issued a decree naming every book of both Testaments, including the books Protestants were questioning (Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch and the two books of Maccabees), and declared anathema anyone who did not accept them "in their entirety and with all their parts". The same decree made the Latin Vulgate the authentic text for public use. [cite:vt14_trent_s4] The decree was not unanimous: it passed by 24 votes to 15, with 16 abstentions. [cite:vt14_wiki_trentcanon] Trent, not Nicaea, is the council at which a vote fixed the Catholic list.

The Protestant and Ethiopian lists

Protestant Bibles have 66 books, 39 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New. This did not come from a single decision by Martin Luther. His complete German Bible of 1534 kept the disputed Old Testament books, printing them in a separate section headed, in translation, "Apocrypha: These Books Are Not Held Equal to the Scriptures, but Are Useful and Good to Read". The English King James Version of 1611 also printed them between the Testaments. [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible] Exclusion was hardened later, in confessions such as the Westminster Confession of 1646, which states that the Apocrypha, "not being of divine inspiration, are no part of the canon of the Scripture", and in the British and Foreign Bible Society's decision of the 1820s to stop funding their printing. [cite:vt14_wcf_ch1] [cite:vt14_wiki_protbible]

At the other end of the range, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church counts 81 books, the largest canon in traditional Christianity. It includes 1 Enoch and Jubilees, which no other major church treats as scripture, and three books of Meqabyan unrelated to the Greek Maccabees. [cite:vt14_wiki_tewahedo] The church reaches 81 in two ways: its own count, from the commentary tradition on its law book, is 46 Old Testament and 35 New Testament books, the 35 including eight books of church order; a narrower reckoning, printed in the imperial Bibles, is 54 and 27. Both total 81. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] [cite:vt27_cowley1974] [scene:vt27_church_and_canon|How the Ethiopian church counts to 81]

What Nicaea did, and the "218 to 2" figure

The Council of Nicaea met in 325 at the summons of the emperor Constantine. At least 200 bishops attended; contemporary reports range from about 250 to 300, and the traditional figure of 318 became standard later. The surviving signature lists contain between 200 and 220 names. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea] Those lists were compiled and copied in different languages and are incomplete; they record who subscribed, not a tally of votes. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea_list] A figure such as "218 bishops signed, 2 refused" therefore takes a number from the range of names on surviving lists and presents it as the result of a ballot. What the record does show is that all but two bishops subscribed the creed: Theonas of Marmarica and Secundus of Ptolemais refused and were excommunicated and exiled with Arius to Illyria. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea]

The council's business was the teaching of the Alexandrian priest Arius about the Son's relation to the Father. It produced the first form of the Nicene Creed, with the term homoousios ("of one substance with the Father"); Eusebius of Caesarea ascribed the term to Constantine himself, while Hosius of Córdoba also backed that side. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea] It also fixed a common method for dating Easter, dealt with the Melitian schism in Egypt and issued about twenty disciplinary canons. There is no record that it discussed which books belong in the Bible. The story that the bishops placed books on an altar and kept those that did not fall off comes from the Synodicon Vetus, a text of about 887, and historians treat it as fiction. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea] The one documented order to burn books in the Nicene story concerned Arius's own writings, and it came from the emperor, not from a list of scripture. [cite:vt14_wiki_nicaea]

Nag Hammadi and the Index

In late 1945 an Egyptian farmer, Muhammad Ali al-Samman, dug up a cache of books near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt containing twelve leather-bound papyrus codices and part of a thirteenth: 52 treatises in Coptic, most of them Gnostic, including the only complete text of the Gospel of Thomas. The manuscripts date mainly from the fourth century and are kept in the Coptic Museum in Cairo; a full facsimile appeared between 1972 and 1977 and an English translation in 1977. [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi] Why they were buried is not known. James Robinson proposed that monks of a nearby Pachomian monastery hid them after Athanasius's letter of 367; others have argued for a private burial with a wealthy owner. The link to Athanasius is a hypothesis, not a finding, and no document records an order to bury these books. [cite:vt14_wiki_naghammadi]

The Index of Forbidden Books is a documented instrument of control, and it should be described as one without being inflated. Pope Paul IV issued the first Roman Index in 1559; a revised Index followed the Council of Trent in 1564, and a Congregation of the Index ran it from 1571 until 1917, when the Holy Office took it over. [cite:vt14_nwe_index] Over four centuries it listed works by Erasmus, Descartes, Hobbes, Hume, Kant, Voltaire, Zola and Sartre, among many others. [cite:vt14_brit_index] Its last edition, in 1948, listed about 4,000 titles. On 14 June 1966 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, in a notification signed by Cardinal Alfredo Ottaviani, ended its force as church law while saying it kept its "moral value". [cite:vt14_nwe_index] [cite:vt14_brit_index] The Index restricted what Catholics might read; it did not and could not remove the listed books from circulation, and almost all of them stayed in print throughout.

The canon was argued over by named bishops across three centuries, closed for Catholics by a contested vote in 1546, and still differs between churches; Rome also kept a list of forbidden books until 1966. Neither fact requires a hidden council. The Holy See's own papers, the subject of the next section, are where claims of hidden history can be checked.
