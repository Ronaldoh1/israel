London is the first of the three cities this volume examines, and the one whose part in the making of Israel is easiest to document. The difficulty is not finding London in the record. It is deciding which London is meant. The word covers at least four different things: a medieval corporation that governs one square mile, a financial market that has outgrown that square mile many times over, the private banking houses that did business inside it, and the British state that sat a mile and a half to the west, in Whitehall and Westminster. Each of the four did something different, and the familiar claim that "the City of London" created Israel runs them together.

This part takes them apart. It begins with the Corporation itself: its charters, its government, its unusual electorate and its officer in Parliament, so that the reader knows exactly what kind of body it is and what kind of power it holds. It then turns to the money: the Bank of England, the chartered companies of empire, and the scale of London's markets today. The Rothschild house appears where the documents place it, inside the City's institutions and on the floor of its gold market. Then come the financial companies of the Zionist movement, which were registered in London, and the plumbing of Mandate Palestine, whose currency was managed and whose reserves were held there. Only after all of that does the part reach the British state's decisions, from Palmerston to Balfour to the White Paper of 1939 and the grudging recognition of 1949.

The question the part answers is the one in this volume's title. In what sense, if any, was London a parent of the State of Israel, and through which of its institutions? The answer, set out in the closing parent test, is that London was a parent in a specific and documented way, and that the familiar version of the claim points at the wrong building.
