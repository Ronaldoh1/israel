After Kafr Qasim: Compensation, the Sulha, Plan Mole and the Long Memory

This section runs well past the period of this chapter, and deliberately so. What the state paid for the killings at Kafr Qasim, and the reconciliation ceremony it staged in November 1957, belong to the months covered here. But the argument over what the killing meant, and in particular whether it was linked to a plan to drive the Arab population of the Triangle across the border into Jordan, was carried on for more than six decades, and it turned in 2022 on trial records that stayed closed until that year. That argument is followed here to its documented end, and nothing beyond the record of compensation, commemoration, the released records and the state's later words is told. The killing itself is in [scene:mg_s01|Kafr Qasim, 29 October 1956], and the trial in [scene:cw3_kafr_qasim_trial|the Kafr Qasim trial].

The price of a life. In November 1957, a year after the killing and while the trial was still being heard, a special commission recommended what the state should pay. According to a JTA dispatch from Jerusalem, published on 15 November 1957, it proposed IL5,000 to the heirs of each man and woman killed, other sums for dependants and for the wounded, and an additional IL1,000 for the heirs of a pregnant woman. Ben-Gurion pledged to honour the recommendation. [cite:rs_cw3_djn_comp] In the commission's terms, the unborn child whom the village counts as its forty-ninth victim appears as an extra thousand pounds. The same dispatch spoke of "the murder of 48 Arabs." [cite:rs_cw3_djn_comp]

The Encyclopaedia Judaica gives the total: a public board under Pinhas Rashish, the mayor of Petah Tikva, decided to pay IL500,000 in all. [cite:rs_cw3_ej_kafr_qasim] What reached individual survivors could be much less. Jonathan Cook reports that one of them, Saleh Khalil Issa, received 700 lira, less than a year's wage. [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] The sources do not say how the half-million was divided among the families, the dependants and the wounded, so Issa's figure cannot be placed within it; it is a single survivor's sum, reported half a century later.

The sulha, 20 November 1957. Five days after the compensation dispatch, the state staged a ceremony of reconciliation in the village. It was called a sulha. According to the historian Shira Robinson, more than 400 guests attended on 20 November 1957, among them cabinet ministers, Mapai legislators, officials of the military government and Histadrut leaders; the event was publicized in the state-sponsored Arabic and Hebrew press. [cite:rs_cw3_robinson_2003]

The ceremony is remembered very differently from the way it was presented. Robinson calls it a "charade" that villagers were pressured to attend. Slyomovics and Khleif, as cited in Wikipedia's account, describe it as "forced upon the villagers." [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_robinson_2003] The villagers were not without means of refusal: PalQuest records that they prevented the accused men from being brought to it. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] The setting itself carried the argument: the ceremony of reconciliation was organized by the state whose police had done the killing, while their officers were still on trial. The state's own aim in using the form, beyond what Robinson's description of the guests and the press coverage shows, is not documented in the sources used here.

Monument and museum. The village's memory was set in stone in 1976, when the first monument was raised. Its inscription called the killings a "painful tragedy" and did not name who had carried them out. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] Thirty years later, on 29 October 2006, a museum opened in Kafr Qasim, and the education minister, Yuli Tamir, ordered schools to commemorate the event. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_jvl_kfar_kassem] At the sixtieth anniversary in 2016, about 4,000 people marched, and Ayman Odeh called on the government to recognize "the terrible massacre in Kfar Kassem and the historical crimes." [cite:rs_cw3_stljl_60th] Palestinian citizens of Israel had by then long remembered Shadmi's token fine as "Shadmi's penny." [cite:conversation_kafr_qasim_60]

Plan Mole: the claim before the records. The question argued longest about Kafr Qasim is whether the curfew and the killing had a purpose beyond enforcing a curfew. Long before any records were opened, the historian Tom Segev wrote that the sealing-off of the Triangle villages was part of "a contingency plan to expel the inhabitants to Jordan under cover of the war." Cook quoted the line in 2008. [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] The plan's name, in the accounts that followed, was Plan Mole, in Hebrew Hafarperet. [cite:rs_cw3_peoplesdispatch] [cite:rs_cw3_mee_kq]

The fight for the records, 2017–2022. The full transcripts of the trial stayed closed for decades. The historian Adam Raz, of the Akevot Institute, applied for their release in about 2017. On 15 December 2021 the Military Prosecution dropped most of its objection. The Military Court of Appeals decided the matter on 31 March 2022, under a gag order; the order was eased on 17 May and lifted on 31 May 2022. The transcripts were published at the end of July 2022. [cite:rs_cw3_akevot_records] [cite:rs_cw3_akevot_mole] [cite:rs_cw3_newarab_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_peoplesdispatch]

What the transcripts contain. The newly released pages contain the testimony of the Border Police commander Chaim Levy. According to Haaretz's reporting of 29 July 2022, as summarized by Peoples Dispatch and Middle East Eye, Levy testified that Shadmi had said "it's desirable that there be some fatalities," and that "the killing of a few people as an intimidation measure can encourage movement eastward." The transcripts also refer to a plan to move the Triangle's Arab population to Jordan in time of war. [cite:rs_cw3_peoplesdispatch] [cite:rs_cw3_mee_kq] This is testimony attributed to Shadmi by another officer, reported through press summaries of a newspaper's reading of the records; Shadmi's own denial at trial, that the question of returning workers had come up at all, stands on the record beside it. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq]

What they do not contain. The text of Plan Mole itself, and related military documents, remained redacted after the 2022 release, according to PalQuest. The New Arab reported that "information on an Israeli plan to deport" Palestinian citizens to Jordan was withheld. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_newarab_kq] What the plan ordered, and whether the killing at Kafr Qasim was meant to set it in motion, therefore rests on testimony about it, not on its own text. [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_newarab_kq]

The dispute. Two readings of the released record can be made, and both should be given in their strongest form. The first holds that the killing was part of, or at least linked to, a plan to expel. Its evidence is Levy's testimony that Shadmi called fatalities "desirable" as a way to "encourage movement eastward," the transcripts' references to a wartime plan to move the Triangle's population to Jordan, Segev's earlier statement of "a contingency plan to expel the inhabitants to Jordan under cover of the war," and PalQuest's account of Plan Mole as intended to terrorize and to deport. [cite:rs_cw3_peoplesdispatch] [cite:rs_cw3_cook_2008] [cite:rs_cw3_palquest_kq] On this reading a curfew announced with thirty minutes' warning, on the first evening of a war, in villages a kilometre from the border, was not an administrative blunder.

The second holds that the released record does not show this. The plan's own text is still redacted, so what it provided for is not known. Shadmi denied at trial that the question of returning workers had come up at all. Two of the three units given the order did not shoot, which is hard to square with a plan whose execution depended on killings. And the 1958 court tried the killing as an unlawful order carried out by named officers, not as an act of state policy. [cite:rs_cw3_newarab_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_djn_verdict] On this reading the documents establish testimony about intent, and the existence of a contingency plan, but not an executed policy of expulsion. The two readings agree that a plan was referred to and that a senior officer was said, in testimony, to have spoken of deaths as useful. They divide on whether that testimony describes what happened at Kafr Qasim, and the redacted pages are where the answer would be.

The state's words. Israel's presidents came to Kafr Qasim slowly, and the change in their language can be measured. In December 2007 President Peres said, in Ynet's rendering, "A most difficult incident happened here, which we deeply regret." [cite:rs_cw3_ynet_rivlin] On 26 October 2014 President Rivlin became the first sitting president to attend the village's ceremony, and said: "It was an atrocious massacre. A severe crime was perpetrated here." [cite:rs_cw3_ynet_rivlin] On 29 October 2021 President Herzog went further: "on behalf of myself and the State of Israel, I ask for forgiveness." [cite:rs_cw3_toi_herzog] [cite:rs_cw3_stljl_60th] In fourteen years the head of state's vocabulary moved from an "incident" regretted, to a "massacre" and a "crime," to a request for forgiveness in the state's name.

The legislature did not follow. The Knesset voted down a Joint List bill for the state's formal recognition of the massacre, by 93 votes to 12. Wikipedia, citing Haaretz, dates the vote to 26 October 2021; the Times of Israel places it on the day before Herzog's visit. [cite:rs_cw3_wiki_kq] [cite:rs_cw3_toi_herzog] So in the same week of October 2021 the president asked forgiveness in the name of the state, and the parliament declined to recognize, in law, what he had asked forgiveness for. Kafr Qasim's dead, counted among the documented lives lost to the conflict, appear in this library's [scene:pm_s02|ledger of lives and displacement].

Several things remain unsettled. The text of Plan Mole has not been published, and until it is, whether the killing was meant to trigger it cannot be decided from documents. The testimony about Shadmi's words reaches this book through press summaries of Haaretz's reporting rather than the transcript itself. How the IL500,000 was distributed is not recorded in the sources used here. The date of the Knesset vote differs slightly between the two accounts of it. And no source used here records a formal state act of recognition: as of the latest record here, October 2021, recognition had come from the presidency in words, and been refused by the Knesset in a vote.
