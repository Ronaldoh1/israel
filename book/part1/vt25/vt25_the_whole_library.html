The Whole Library: Every Codex, Every Tractate

This section lists the full contents of the Nag Hammadi find in codex order, from the Jung Codex to the loose leaves called Codex XIII. Each tractate gets a one-line summary of its teaching and two further judgements. The first is whether that teaching conflicted with what the bishops' church taught. The second is whether a bishop, a church writer or a church document is recorded as condemning the text by name or condemning the school it belongs to. The order and numbering follow the standard edition. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl]

How the two judgement columns are made

"Institutional teaching" here means the doctrine the bishops' church set down in writing in the period these books were copied. The creed of the Council of Nicaea in 325 opens with belief in "one God, the Father Almighty, maker of all things visible and invisible", and says that the Son "was incarnate and was made man. He suffered and the third day he rose again." [cite:vt25_nicaea_creed] Irenaeus of Lyon, around 180, held that there could be only four gospels. [cite:vt25_iren_311] The same church baptised with water, expected the bodily resurrection and was governed by bishops and deacons. A tractate is marked as conflicting when its teaching contradicts one of these points. Most often the conflict is a lower creator separate from the true God, a Christ who only seemed to suffer, a resurrection already completed in spirit, a rejection of water baptism, or an attack on church offices. A text that teaches in the familiar language of Christian or philosophical morality is marked "no".

The condemnation column names only recorded acts. One general condemnation covers all of these books and is not repeated in every row. In 367 Athanasius of Alexandria warned the churches of Egypt against books "called apocryphal", which he called "an invention of heretics". [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] A row says "none by name" when no surviving bishop or church document names that particular text, even if the school it belongs to was attacked. For school-level condemnations, the reference is Irenaeus's Against Heresies (c.180), written to expose what he called "knowledge falsely so called" and aimed first at the Valentinians, and Tertullian's Against the Valentinians (early third century). [cite:vt24_ah1_pref] [cite:vt24_tert_adval] The Decretum Gelasianum, a sixth-century list later issued under papal names, names Valentinus and Basilides among heretics and closes with an anathema on all the books it lists. [cite:vt25_decretum]

What the catalogue shows

Counted by tractate, the conflict is real and it is the majority. About two thirds of the tractates teach something the creed of 325 or Irenaeus's rule excluded. The most common points are a creator who is not the highest God, a divine Mother or Sophia whose error produced the world, a Christ who did not truly suffer, or a resurrection already completed in spirit. [cite:vt25_nicaea_creed] [cite:vt25_wiki_apjohn] [cite:vt25_wiki_2seth] [cite:vt25_wiki_treatres] Several texts openly reject what the bishops' church practised: water baptism in the Paraphrase of Shem and the Testimony of Truth, the authority of bishops and deacons in the Apocalypse of Peter, and fasting, prayer and alms in Thomas. [cite:vt25_wiki_shem] [cite:vt25_wiki_testtruth] [cite:vt25_wiki_gapocpeter] [cite:vt25_thomas_lambdin] These are the passages most consistent with the reading that these texts offered direct access to God without the church as intermediary, and Part Thirty-One sets them against the institution's own rules. [scene:vt31_two_economies|Part Thirty-One: Two Economies of the Sacred]

The catalogue also complicates that reading in three ways. First, the library is not one teaching. It holds Plato, Hermes, the moral maxims of Sextus and the orthodox-sounding Teachings of Silvanus beside Sethian myth. Melchizedek insists that Jesus truly suffered, and the Testimony of Truth condemns other Gnostic schools as harshly as any bishop did. [cite:vt25_wiki_nhl] [cite:vt25_wiki_melch] [cite:vt25_wiki_testtruth] Second, the Valentinian texts carry their own rites (anointing, baptism, eucharist, bridal chamber), so these were not religions without ritual. [cite:vt25_wiki_valexp] [cite:vt25_wiki_gphil] Third, only a minority of the tractates were ever condemned by name. The Gospel of Truth was condemned by Irenaeus. Thomas was named by Hippolytus, Cyril and the Decretum, and the "Proverbs" of Sixtus by the Decretum. The Allogenes and Zostrianos revelations were attacked by the pagan Plotinus and his pupils and by the bishop Epiphanius. [cite:vt25_iren_311] [cite:vt25_hipp_ref5] [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] [cite:vt25_decretum] [cite:vt25_ecw_allogenes] [cite:vt25_bmcr_plotinus] Most of the others were condemned only as part of a school, or not at all, and the earliest attacks on two of them came from a pagan philosophical school rather than from a church.

The condemnations that are recorded follow the same pattern as in the previous sections. Bishops and heresy writers from Lyon, Rome, Jerusalem, Alexandria and Salamis, writing between about 180 and the sixth century, warned against these books, excluded them from church reading and anathematised them. [cite:vt25_iren_311] [cite:vt25_cyril_cat4] [cite:vt14_ect_festal39] [cite:vt25_decretum] Most of these writers were not bishops of Rome, and the one document issued under papal names, the Decretum, is a later compilation. None of the sources gathered here contains an order to burn the Nag Hammadi books. The copies survived because someone in fourth-century Egypt valued them enough to bind them in leather, collect them and put them in the ground. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] Part Twenty-Six turns to three texts that were not in the jar but tell the same story from other manuscripts: the Gospel of Mary, the Gospel of Judas and the Pistis Sophia. [scene:vt26_gospel_of_mary|Part Twenty-Six: Outside the Jar]
