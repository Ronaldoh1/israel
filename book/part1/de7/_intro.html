Part Seven gives the Dark Enlightenment its strongest hearing. It separates the texts' diagnosis, that American democracy no longer does what it claims, from their cure, and tests the diagnosis against the record: the collapse of public trust since the 1960s, the government's own audits of failed delivery, the growth of rules written by agencies, the long dispute over whether policy follows voters or the affluent, and Franklin Roosevelt's 1933 inaugural, the precedent Curtis Yarvin cites for an executive who takes power from Congress. It then reads the same symptoms the other way, as capture by concentrated money and by platforms that charge rent, and asks what each reading implies for a cure that would concentrate power further.
