Cleaving: the Orchard Opened

This section is a reading. It makes no claim that the record proves.

The word devekut comes from Deuteronomy's command to "hold fast" to God. Britannica defines it as "an adherence to or communion with God that stops short of mystical union". [cite:kb_brit_devequt] In the Kabbalah it was one of the mystic's three highest values, sometimes equated with ecstasy, and "a privilege of the spiritual aristocracy". In Hasidism, in its lower stage, it "found expression in the social sphere" and was, in principle, "open to every Ḥasid". [cite:kb_brit_devequt]

The inner reading takes this as the turn the whole shelf has been leading to. Every tradition here has had a gate: a list, a jar, a monastery, a team, an age limit. And in every tradition the reading has found the same teaching: that the holiest place is within. Hasidism is the case where the tradition itself drew the conclusion. If closeness to God is within, it cannot belong only to the learned, the old or the initiated; it belongs to anyone who turns toward it, in ordinary life.

The orchard story is the right warning to keep beside the opening. Of four who entered, three were harmed, and only Akiva came out unharmed; the danger the rabbis named was real. [cite:kb_mjl_danger] The reading does not say the gate was foolish. It says the gate guarded the right thing in the wrong way. The safeguard the rabbis required, learning and a grounded life, a stomach full of bread and meat, is the safeguard every inner path needs. [cite:kb_ou_age40] What Hasidism added is that grounding can be found in ordinary life itself, in work, prayer and community, and need not be bought with forty years of waiting.

The record limits the reading. Britannica is careful that devekut "stops short of mystical union": the tradition kept a distinction between closeness to God and becoming God, which the inner traditions on other shelves do not always keep. [cite:kb_brit_devequt] The Hasidic opening also had its own leaders and dynasties, with gates of their own. The reading that the orchard belongs to everyone is the reader's conclusion, drawn from the tradition's own turn.
