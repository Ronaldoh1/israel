The Master Ledger: Fifteen Mechanisms, Told and Recorded

The Dream as taught is a chain of promises, each resting on a mechanism. Work earns a fair share of what it produces; a home builds wealth; a degree lifts its holder; a job brings health coverage; savings and Social Security carry a family through old age; and the rules apply to everyone alike. Each earlier part of this book tested one link. This section sets every link in one ledger, so that a reader can see at a glance which promises the record keeps, which it keeps for some, and where it measures a shortfall. The terms are those set out in Part Two: absolute mobility is the share of children who out-earn their parents, relative mobility the odds of moving between rungs; a median is the household in the exact middle; real dollars are adjusted for inflation. [scene:ad2_how_to_measure|Part Two: How to measure]

Each row has six entries. "As taught" gives the promise in the words of a curriculum, a citizenship test or a president, sourced in Part One and the part named. "The record" gives the key figure. "Friction" says whether the gap is designed, a feature someone wrote into law or practice on purpose, or captured, a documented departure from the design such as a settled case of discriminatory pricing; many rows are mixed. "Who gains, who pays" names industries, institutions and positions in the distribution, never groups as such. "Register" says how firmly the record holds: documented, where primary data agree; disputed, where named researchers with credible methods disagree about size or cause; not supported, where a circulating claim fails against the record.

Work and income

The first five rows are the core of the taught Dream: that effort is rewarded and that each generation does better than the last. The mobility record confirms the promise for the generation born around 1940, when about nine in ten children out-earned their parents, and qualifies it for every cohort since; for children born in the early 1980s the share was about half. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] Critics who adjust for household size and use a different price index put the share at 60 to 68 per cent, but accept the decline. [cite:ad2_reeves_winship] The pay record shows the same pattern: productivity and typical pay rose together until the 1970s and then parted, by an amount that depends on the price index used. [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] [cite:ad4_bls_btn2017]

The cost of the Dream's assets

The next five rows concern the things the Dream promises a working family can buy: a home, a degree, health care, credit on fair terms and a secure old age. Here the record is most mixed, because each of these markets was shaped by federal law and each has both a documented success and a documented failure. The long, fixed-rate mortgage built by federal agencies in the 1930s is still the standard loan, and about 65 per cent of households own their homes, the same share as in 1980. [cite:us15_census_hvs_q226] [cite:ad1_census_hvs04] Yet the price of a typical home has risen from about 3.2 to 5.0 years of median household income since the 1990s. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] The degree still pays: bachelor's holders earn about 75 per cent more over a working life than high-school graduates. [cite:ad7_cew_payoff] But the largest federal grant, which once covered more than three-quarters of a public college year, now covers about a quarter. [cite:ad7_nasfaa_pell]

Distribution and the rules

The last five rows ask who ends up with what and who writes the rules that decide it. Here the taught version holds most plainly on one point: the federal tax system is progressive, taking 1.4 per cent of the lowest fifth's income and 31.5 per cent of the top 1 per cent's in 2022. [cite:ad11_tf_cbo2022] It holds least on the gap between what families earn and what they own: in 2025 the median white family earned about 1.9 times the median Black family's income and held about 9.0 times its wealth. [cite:ad2_scf_t1] [cite:us8_scf2025]

What the ledger shows, read across

Three patterns run across the fifteen rows. The first is that almost every gap has a date and a designer. The pay floor was written as a fixed dollar sum in 1938 and has not moved since 2009; the tie between job and health coverage was written into the tax code in 1954; the bankruptcy treatment of student loans was set in a sequence of laws from 1976 to 2005; the 401(k) entered the tax code in 1978 and employers then chose it over the pension. [cite:ad5_epi_real2025] [cite:ad8_tpc_esi] [cite:ad7_crs_rs22699] [cite:ad10_napa_1978] These are designed frictions in the protocol's sense: intended features, debated in public and enacted by Congresses and presidents of both parties. Captured frictions, documented departures from the design, are fewer and more specific: pricing that, the Justice Department alleged, charged Black and Hispanic borrowers more than white borrowers with similar credit, in cases two of the largest lenders settled; contract sales in Chicago; Ferguson's courts run for revenue. [cite:ad12_doj_countrywide] [cite:ad6_duke_plunder] [cite:ad11_doj_ferguson]

The second pattern is that the record turns on the difference between a level and a share. On levels, the taught version often holds: real median household income reached $87,460 in 2025, the highest on record, and the typical worker's pay rose by about a third since 1979. [cite:us14_census_story2025] [cite:ad4_epi_gap2026] On shares, it often does not: the proportion of children who out-earn their parents halved, the share of the economy's gains reaching typical pay narrowed, and the bottom half of households holds 2.3 per cent of the country's net worth. [cite:ad3_fading_paper] [cite:ad12_ad_bottom50] A reader who asks whether life got better and a reader who asks whether it got fairer will both find their answer in the same tables.

The third pattern is that the gaps fall on positions, not on character. The burden of each mechanism lands where the ledger says it lands: on renters and young buyers, borrowers who did not finish college, the underinsured, revolving borrowers, low-paid workers without a retirement plan, and the lowest fifth through state and local taxes. Where a gap follows race or origin, the record ties it to named programmes and practices, from FHA underwriting to the 1935 exclusions, and economists dispute how much of today's gap those histories explain. [cite:ad6_fha_manual] [cite:ad13_dewitt] [cite:ad13_barsky] [cite:ad13_dkks] Part Seventeen followed them into household budgets: the middle fifth's average household took in $74,474 in 2024 and kept about $4,410 after taxes and spending, roughly 5.9 cents per dollar, while the two lower fifths reported spending more than their income in every year from 2020 to 2024. [cite:us22_bls_ce24_q] [cite:ad17_fred_q1exp] [cite:ad17_fred_q2exp] [scene:ad17_three_families|Part Seventeen: Families Through the Machine]

Where the promise works as taught

The ledger also records where the promise is kept. Social Security cut old-age poverty from 35.2 per cent in 1959 to 9.9 per cent in 2024, and moved 28.8 million people above the poverty line in 2025. [cite:ad10_census_a3] [cite:us18_census_p60290] Children of immigrants have out-climbed the children of American-born parents for more than a century. [cite:ad2_abramitzky_aer] The uninsured rate in 2025 was less than half its 2009 level. [cite:us16_census_2025] [cite:us16_khn_uninsured] The degree still pays. Overdraft revenue at large banks halved between 2019 and 2023, largely through the banks' own changes, and overdose deaths fell for a third straight year in 2025. [cite:us22_cfpb_od23] [cite:ad16_nchs_2025] These are not footnotes to the ledger; they are rows of it, and any reading of the whole has to account for them.

Claims that did not survive the record

Several widely repeated claims failed when tested against primary data, and they belong in the ledger as findings in their own right. A widely repeated figure holds that house prices have doubled against incomes; the Harvard housing centre's series shows a rise of roughly half since the 1990s. [cite:us14_jchs_pti] Another holds that tuition has risen far faster than every other cost; real published tuition at public four-year colleges roughly doubled from 1995–96 to 2025–26, and net tuition after grants has fallen since 2012–13. [cite:ad7_cb_trends25] A third holds that medical debt is the leading cause of bankruptcy; the two main studies answer different questions, contribution against cause, and the dispute is open. [cite:us22_dobkin2018] [cite:us22_himmelstein2019] A fourth, that homelessness is maintained on purpose, rests on motive with no document behind it; regional rates track the cost and availability of rental housing rather than addiction, poverty or weather. [cite:ad14_ucp_colburn] A fifth, that the poor defend the system most, did not survive a large-scale test. [cite:ad16_forrt_brandt]

Read as a whole, the ledger does not describe a promise that was never kept, nor one kept for everyone. It describes a set of mechanisms, each built at a known time by known hands, that delivered the promise broadly to the generation born before 1950, kept parts of it for most families since, and moved others out of reach for families at specific positions in the distribution.
