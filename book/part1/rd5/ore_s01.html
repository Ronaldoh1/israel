The New CIA's First Warning: ORE-55

When the State Department and the Joint Chiefs of Staff warned against partition in September and October 1947, the Central Intelligence Agency was only weeks old. Ten weeks after it came into being, it delivered a formal estimate of its own, dated the day before the General Assembly vote. It predicted war if partition passed, and it warned that American backing for partition was already costing the United States prestige and would endanger its interests. A common assumption holds that American intelligence either never assessed partition or supported it. The declassified record shows the opposite: the intelligence service assessed it, in writing, and advised against what the government was about to do.

Who ran American intelligence. Before 1947, American foreign intelligence ran through the wartime Office of Strategic Services, which operated from 1942 and led by William J. Donovan, whom Roosevelt appointed. The OSS was dissolved in October 1945, and its analysts and operatives fed the agencies that followed. In 1946 an interim body, the Central Intelligence Group, carried the work forward. [cite:w_oss] The OSS also had a Palestine desk during the war; that history, and the Zionist intelligence service that operated on American soil in the same years, are told with the July 1945 Sonneborn meeting in [scene:s07d|the network founded in 1945].

The National Security Act of 1947 put the system on a permanent footing. It created the Central Intelligence Agency, the National Security Council, and the structure that became the Department of Defense; the CIA came into existence on 18 September 1947. [cite:w_nsa1947] Its first Director of Central Intelligence was Rear Admiral Roscoe Hillenkoetter, appointed by Truman. [cite:w_hillenkoetter] James Forrestal, the first Secretary of Defense, oversaw the department that grew from the same Act, so his warning on oil and the agency's estimate on partition came from two parts of one newly built national-security structure. The CIA was born four days before Loy Henderson sent his "not workable" memorandum to Secretary of State Marshall.

What ORE-55 said. The agency's Office of Reports and Estimates issued ORE-55, titled "The Consequences of the Partition of Palestine," dated 28 November 1947, one day before the General Assembly voted on partition. The declassified document is held in the CIA's own reading room. [cite:cia_ore55] Its summary makes five points. Armed hostilities between Jews and Arabs would break out if the General Assembly adopted partition. The United Nations would be morally bound to enforce its decision. Jewish forces would at first prevail, because of superior organization and equipment. The United States, by backing partition, had already lost much of its prestige in the Arab world. And imposed partition would place American commercial and strategic interests in serious danger. [cite:cia_ore55]

The first prediction was borne out quickly: sustained fighting began as a two-sided civil war inside Palestine within days of the 29 November vote, five and a half months before the armies of neighbouring Arab states crossed any border. [cite:explaininghistory_un_partition] The prediction of early Jewish advantage through organization and equipment belongs to the long argument over the balance of forces taken up later in this chapter. The estimate's concern for American standing in the Arab world restated, in the language of intelligence, what Henderson had put to Marshall and the Joint Chiefs had put in "The Problem of Palestine." Its summary, as recorded here, does not propose an alternative policy.

A chorus of four. Within ten weeks, four separate parts of the American government warned in writing against American sponsorship of partition: the State Department's Near East office, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the Secretary of Defense, and the new CIA. Each of the first three is set out in [scene:s07j|the warnings of 1947]. They did not form one coordinated campaign. Each institution reached its conclusion through its own work, and the agreement among them is the finding.

Overruled. The President's domestic political advisers, led by Clark Clifford and David Niles, argued the opposite case, and their view prevailed, first in the partition vote and then in the recognition of Israel eleven minutes after its independence was proclaimed on 14 May 1948. The record shows that the warnings were made and read. It does not show them shaping the outcome. [cite:forward_state_dept_told_truman]

A later chapter of this library carries the story into 1948 and beyond, and adds claims that should be read with care. It states that declassified CIA and State Department documents from 1947 and 1948 warned consistently against recognizing Israel on strategic grounds; that CIA assessments noted Zionist arms-procurement networks operating across Europe and the United States and predicted that recognition would at once produce armed conflict with Arab states; that the State Department under Marshall opposed recognition on the same grounds; and that Truman's recognition came at 6:11 p.m. on 14 May 1948, eleven minutes after the declaration, the fastest diplomatic recognition in American history, so fast that his own State Department was caught unprepared. That account cites no specific document for these points, and they are given here as its claims. It goes on to describe the intelligence relationship with Israel that grew out of 1948, a later story told in [scene:cia_s02|1948: the CIA opposes].

One claim in that account does not match the document examined here. It states that a CIA report predicted that an arms embargo would lead to "peaceful coexistence." No such prediction appears in ORE-55's summary as recorded in this book, which predicts the reverse: armed hostilities if partition passed. If such a report exists, it is a different document, and it has not been identified or fetched for this book.

What the record shows, and what it leaves open. The answer to "didn't they know?" is that they did. American intelligence assessed partition, did so in writing, and predicted war and damage to American interests; the decision was taken anyway. [cite:cia_ore55] What the record does not establish is how much weight the President gave the estimate, which arrived on the eve of a vote whose American position had been announced seven weeks earlier. A document dated 28 November could inform the vote on 29 November only if the decision were still open, and the 11 October announcement suggests it was not. Nor does the summary say what Hillenkoetter or Truman did with it once it was delivered. The estimate stands as a record of what the government's new intelligence service believed, not as evidence of what it changed.
