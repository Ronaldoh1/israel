Henry Wallace and Zionism

In 1944 the Democratic Party replaced Vice President Henry Wallace on its ticket with Senator Harry Truman, the man who would become president within a year and who, in 1948, would recognize the state of Israel. Seen backwards from 1948, the swap invites a suspicion: that Wallace's views on Palestine had something to do with his removal. A version of the story circulates in which Zionist-aligned donors and organizations preferred Truman after Wallace said something unfavorable about Palestine or about Jews. The documented record answers the question directly, and at each point it runs the other way.

Start with Wallace himself. His interest in Zionism began in the 1930s, while he was serving as Roosevelt's Secretary of Agriculture, and it came through a working relationship rather than a political one. The soil scientist Walter Clay Lowdermilk had examined agricultural conditions in British Mandatory Palestine, and Wallace absorbed his enthusiasm. Wallace later recalled of Lowdermilk: "I don't think he has any Jewish blood, but he was the most complete Zionist convert anyone could ask for." Through that influence Wallace came to see the Jewish settlers in Palestine as frontiersmen on the American pattern. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] [cite:algemeiner_wallace_zionist] The point that matters for 1944 is the date. Wallace's sympathy was a personal conviction that he described in his own later recollection, and it predated the convention fight by roughly a decade. That fixes where the conviction came from. It does not prove that every later public statement he made on Palestine was free of political calculation, and the sources do not claim that it was.

Then the removal. Historical accounts state plainly that Roosevelt grew wary of Wallace's left-wing, pro-Soviet political tilt, and that this broader concern, not any statement about Palestine, Jews or Zionism, drove the push to replace him. [cite:wiki_1944_dnc] [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] Nor was the decision quiet or uncontested. Wallace kept real support to the end. Labor organizations, including the CIO and the UAW, campaigned for him at the convention itself, and a Gallup poll taken on the day of the convention showed 65% of delegates still favoring Wallace over Truman. [cite:wiki_1944_dnc] That figure describes a contested, actively fought political battle over the general ideological direction of the party, with the man who lost it still the delegates' preference on the day. That is hard to square with the picture of a quiet substitution arranged to please one constituency.

Why the choice of running mate carried so much weight in 1944 is told in [scene:s07c3|Roosevelt's hidden decline]: the president's doctors knew how ill he was, the voters did not, and the vice-presidential slot was in practice a choice of the next president. The same section sets out the documented reasons Truman was chosen in Wallace's place. What concerns this section is narrower. The sources identify the general pattern historians treat as the main driver of Wallace's removal, and that pattern has no documented connection to Palestine or Zionism. They do not claim an exhaustive account of every reason every delegate or party official opposed him, and a minor factor cannot be ruled out from them. What they do show is that the Palestine explanation has no documentary basis, while the ideological one does.

Wallace did not leave the story in 1944. Four years later, running for president as the candidate of the Progressive Party, he returned to the Palestine question from the side opposite to the one the common assumption gives him. The events belong to the period after this chapter, and only their outline is needed here. His campaign literature included a booklet, Meet Henry Wallace, written by James Waterman Wise, the son of Rabbi Stephen Wise, the Zionist leader whose wartime dealings with Roosevelt run through this chapter. The booklet carried a half-page photograph of Wallace "helping the Jewish settlers in Galilee harvest their fields." At rallies Wallace accused Truman of "playing politics with the lives of the people of Israel," aiming directly at Truman's arms embargo on the newly declared state. That attack was the center of his appeal to Jewish voters. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] It was not the center of his campaign as a whole, which was built mainly on opposition to Cold War policy and support for continued engagement with the Soviet Union. Israel was one real, deliberate element of a broader platform, not its foundation.

The appeal had a measurable effect. New York newspapers reported widespread talk of Jewish voters leaving Truman for Wallace out of dissatisfaction with Truman's Palestine record, and the result was documented as the lowest share of the New York Jewish vote for any Democratic presidential candidate in modern history, except Jimmy Carter's. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist] [cite:algemeiner_wallace_zionist] The sources credit Truman's Palestine record as a contributing factor in that decline, not its only cause. Other parts of Wallace's campaign, and general dissatisfaction with Truman, likely contributed as well.

Put together, the record reverses the assumption twice. In 1944 Wallace was removed for reasons that had nothing documented to do with Palestine, and he was himself a long-standing sympathizer of the Zionist project. In 1948 he became one of Truman's most direct critics from the pro-Israel side, and that criticism cost Truman votes in New York. If the episode points to any electoral incentive, it points the opposite way from the one usually assumed: in the 1948 record it is being seen as insufficiently pro-Israel, not too closely tied to the cause, that carried a cost. That is an inference from one election in one state, and it should be held at that scope. [cite:aish_wallace_zionist]

Several things the record here does not settle. The sources give the New York result only as a ranking, not as a percentage, so the size of the shift cannot be stated from them. They do not separate how much of it came from the Palestine issue and how much from Wallace's wider campaign. And while they find no Palestine dimension to the 1944 decision, they do not record what, if anything, Wallace said in public about Palestine during his vice presidency. What is established is narrower and firmer: the belief that Wallace was pushed aside over Zionism has no documentary support, and the documents that exist point in the other direction.
