The Name in the Record

This part of the volume steps aside from the political history told in Parts One to Twelve to look at the word at the centre of it. Christian Zionism reads a promise made to "Israel" as a promise about a modern state and its land. Other readers, Jewish and Christian, have read the same name as describing something that happens inside a person. Before either reading can be weighed, the documented record of the name has to be set out on its own: where it first appears, what the Hebrew Bible says about it, and what scholars of Hebrew and the ancient Near East say it means. This section does that and nothing else. The interpretive readings follow in the sections after it, labelled as readings.

The earliest attestation: an Egyptian victory stele

The oldest securely dated mention of Israel is not in the Bible. It is on a black granite slab more than three metres high, carved in the fifth regnal year of the pharaoh Merneptah, about 1208 BCE, and found by the British archaeologist Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896. The text, 28 lines long, was cut into the back of an older stele of Amenhotep III and is now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo (JE 31408). [cite:ts13_wiki_merneptah] Britannica dates the campaign it celebrates, a victory over a Libyan invasion, to around 1209 BCE and describes the stele as containing "the earliest-known reference to Israel, which Merneptah counted among the peoples that he defeated." [cite:ts13_brit_stela]

The line reads, in the usual translation: "Israel is laid waste—its seed is no more." It is a stock boast that Egyptian kings made about defeated enemies. What makes it informative is the sign that follows the word. Egyptian scribes added a silent classifier, a determinative, to names. The Canaanite towns in the same passage, Ashkelon, Gezer and Yanoam, carry the sign for a city or foreign land. "Israel" carries the sign of a seated man and woman with a plural marker, the sign for a people. [cite:ts13_wiki_merneptah] Most Egyptologists read the word as Israel and place this group in the central hill country of Canaan. Minority readings exist, among them Othniel Margalith's "Jezreel" and Alessandra Nibbi's "wearers of the sidelock", which would refer to Libyans, and the Egyptologist John A. Wilson, while accepting the reading as a people, cautioned about "the notorious carelessness of Late-Egyptian scribes and several blunders of writing in this stela." [cite:ts13_wiki_merneptah] Read on the majority view, the first time "Israel" appears in any surviving document, it names a population, not a territory and not a state.

The name in Genesis

Within the Hebrew Bible the name begins with one man. Genesis 32:22–32 tells how Jacob, on his way back to meet the brother he had cheated, crossed his family over the ford of the Jabbok and stayed behind. "And Jacob was left alone. And a man wrestled with him until the breaking of the day." The stranger, unable to win, struck Jacob's hip out of joint; Jacob would not let go: "I will not let you go unless you bless me." [cite:ts13_esv_gen32] The stranger then renamed him: "Your name shall no longer be called Jacob, but Israel, for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed." Jacob called the place Peniel, "face of God", saying "I have seen God face to face, and yet my life has been delivered", and the passage closes on an image that later readers have returned to many times: "The sun rose upon him as he passed Penuel, limping because of his hip." [cite:ts13_esv_gen32] Genesis gives the renaming a second time, in a different setting, at 35:10. [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname]

The prophet Hosea, writing centuries later, recalled the same story and read it as a sign of character: "In the womb he took his brother by the heel, and in his manhood he strove with God." The next verse says that he strove with the angel and prevailed, and that he wept and sought his favour. [cite:ts13_esv_hos12] Two points follow from the texts themselves. The figure Jacob fought is called a man in Genesis, an angel in Hosea and God in Jacob's own words at Peniel, and the tradition never settled on one identity. The birth name and the new name are set against each other: Jacob, the one who held the heel, becomes Israel, the one who strove and prevailed. The account of the two names in [scene:s00f|the opening chapter] covers the same ground for the library's first volume.

What the philology says

The second element of Yisra'el is not in dispute. It is el, the common Northwest Semitic word for God, which was also the name of a Canaanite deity of importance in early Israelite religion. [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname] The first element is a verb, and here scholars give several options. One candidate root is sarah, glossed in the lexicons as to contend, have power, persist or persevere; another is sarar, to rule, be prince, prevail. [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname] Michael Heiser, a scholar of Hebrew and Semitic languages, lists a third, yashar, "upright" or "just", which some readers hear in the name. [cite:ts13_heiser_maxwell]

Grammar matters as much as the root. The name can be read with God as the subject of the verb, giving "God strives", "God persists" or "God rules", or with God as the object, which is how Genesis 32:28 explains it: Jacob is the one who strove with God. [cite:ts13_heiser_maxwell] [cite:ts13_esv_gen32] The two readings are not equivalent, and the scholarly proposals below lean to the first, which means the Genesis explanation is best treated as the story the text tells about the name, not as a settled account of its origin. John Day has argued that the name probably meant "El will rule", a reading he finds consistent with the Ugaritic texts, and Mark S. Smith argues that the name is built on El, not on Yahweh, and that "El was the original chief god of the group named Israel." [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname] Forms of the name are attested at Ebla and Ugarit in the Bronze Age, which suggests it was a personal or group name in use before the biblical narratives were written. [cite:ts13_wiki_israelname]

Layers of the one word

The record therefore shows a name with several layers, each added at a different time. In the surviving documents it first names a people in Canaan in about 1208 BCE. In Genesis it names one man after one night, and then the descendants of his twelve sons. Later biblical books use it for a kingdom and for the land. Much later it became the name of a modern state, proclaimed in May 1948. [cite:ts13_wiki_merneptah] [cite:ts13_esv_gen32] None of those layers cancels the others, and none of them is identical with another. The rest of this volume has shown what happens when one of them, the promise to a people about a land, is read by a Protestant prophetic system as a forecast about a modern government.
