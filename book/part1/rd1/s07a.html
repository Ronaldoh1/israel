Five Dates Before 1933

A familiar account holds that the demand for a Jewish state grew out of the Holocaust, that "never again" was its direct cause. The question of sequence can be tested against the calendar, and five dates, each documented, settle it. All five fall between 1896 and 1922. All five predate Hitler's rise to power in 1933 by at least a decade, and the Holocaust itself by at least sixteen years.

The first is 1896. In that year Theodor Herzl published Der Judenstaat, The Jewish State, which explicitly weighed Palestine against Argentina as candidate territories for a political solution to European antisemitism.[cite:herzl_judenstaat] This was a political program, dated and in print, thirty-seven years before Hitler took power in Germany. It was not a reconstruction assembled after the fact. The arithmetic alone rules out the later catastrophe as its cause. It does not follow, however, that everything in Zionist history flowed in a straight line from this one pamphlet. The decades that followed were marked by real internal contest, splits and changes of direction, not a single unbroken plan.

The second date is really a pair: 1903 and 1905. The Sixth Zionist Congress voted in 1903 to investigate British East Africa; the Seventh Congress formally rejected it in 1905.[cite:wiki_seventh_zionist_congress] The point matters because it shows an active choice rather than a default. Choosing a place because nothing else is on offer is different from choosing it after seriously weighing a real alternative and turning it down. Palestine's place at the center of the movement was confirmed by a contested, two-stage decision, and that decision produced its own split, the breakaway Jewish Territorial Organization. It was not the last disagreement the movement would face.

The third is 1917. In November of that year Britain committed in writing to "the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people."[cite:balfour_letter] The commitment came sixteen years before Hitler's rise and twenty-four years before the systematic phase of the Holocaust began. A letter published that early cannot have been a response to what came later; the dates do not allow it. Nor did the letter by itself guarantee a state. Decades of uncertain, incremental and contested development lay between it and 1948, as later parts of this book show.

The fourth is 1920. At San Remo the Allied powers formally assigned Britain the Mandate for Palestine and incorporated the Balfour language into an international agreement.[cite:san_remo_wiki] This was more than repetition. A promise made by one government had become part of a multilateral settlement, and its legal weight changed accordingly. That does not mean every Allied power shared the same enthusiasm or the same motives; different national interests stood behind the same formal text.

The fifth is 1922. The League of Nations Mandate for Palestine was approved in July 1922 and took legal force in September 1923. Its Article 6 committed Britain to facilitate Jewish immigration and "close settlement by Jews on the land."[cite:mandate_palestine_wiki] By then a binding international legal framework existed, eleven years before Hitler took power and before the word "Holocaust" described anything at all. It was not applied consistently. Within the same decade, the Churchill White Paper narrowed the commitment, as [scene:s06a|the Churchill White Paper] shows.

Taken together, the five dates describe what the movement already possessed when Hitler came to power: a political program thirty-seven years old, a territorial question decided twice in its own favor, a great-power commitment, international ratification of that commitment, and a binding legal mandate to build toward it. The funding institutions, the political promises and the legal framework were in place.

None of this diminishes the Holocaust or its effect. It intensified the urgency and reshaped the world's willingness to act. The clearest evidence of that acceleration is the Biltmore Program of May 1942, with its open demand for a "Jewish Commonwealth," the movement's first explicit, public call for statehood.[cite:biltmore_wiki] [cite:biltmore_medoff] That demand was real and significant. But it was the culmination of forty-six years of institution-building and political commitment, not their origin; the full story is told in [scene:s07b|the Biltmore Program]. "Never again" explains why the world finally acted. It does not explain when the planning began.
