Keren Hayesod: Fundraising Becomes Permanent

On December 24, 1920, at a Zionist conference in London, the World Zionist Organization founded Keren Hayesod, the Palestine Foundation Fund. It was to be the movement's central and permanent fundraising arm. Three years earlier the Balfour Declaration had given Zionism a political opening; this was the institution built to pay for it.

Its originators were Hillel Zlatopolsky and Isaac Naiditch. The fund was registered in Britain in 1921, and in 1926 its headquarters moved to Jerusalem. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] Two features defined it. It was open to non-Zionist donors as well as Zionists. And it relied on a self-imposed, tithe-style system of giving. Its founders contemplated raising £25,000,000 within five years. The records cited here document that figure as an aim; they do not show whether it was reached.

It is often said that Zionist fundraising stayed informal and improvised until American donors organized it in the late 1930s. Keren Hayesod's founding record tells a different story. By the time the United Jewish Appeal formed in 1939, Keren Hayesod had already been operating as a structured, international fundraising institution for nearly two decades. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]

The fund also drew on public figures who agreed on little else. Among the named early figures associated with its fundraising were Chaim Weizmann, president of the World Zionist Organization; Albert Einstein, the most famous scientist of the era; and Ze'ev Jabotinsky, who would lead the movement's more militant Revisionist wing. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] The combination suggests a fund designed to reach across the movement's factions. It does not show that the three men worked closely together or agreed on strategy. Their disagreements in this period were real and substantial.

What the money paid for is as revealing as how it was raised. Histories of the era tend to dwell on land purchase, but Keren Hayesod's spending reached well beyond it. It financed the absorption of immigrants, settlement, defense and water development. It backed major enterprises, including the Palestine Electric Company and the Palestine Potash Company. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki] These were the pieces of a national economy: power, water, industry and the means to receive new arrivals. The available record names these categories of investment, but it gives no itemized account of how much went to each.

At the end of the decade the fund's importance grew again. In 1929 Weizmann and Louis Marshall launched an enlarged Jewish Agency for Palestine, which gave non-Zionists equal representation alongside Zionists. Keren Hayesod became its financial instrument. [cite:yivo_weizmann_encyclopedia] [cite:israeled_jewish_agency_expands] The fund was thus folded directly into the body that would act as a government-in-waiting before statehood.

Marshall died in September 1929, within weeks of the Agency's launch. His successor as leader of the Agency's non-Zionist wing in the United States was Felix Warburg. Warburg became chairman of its Administrative Committee and Weizmann's senior non-Zionist counterpart. [cite:britannica_jewish_agency] He had earlier been a prominent, on-record opponent of Zionism. His new role was less a reversal of that position than an expression of the Agency's design. Its non-Zionist seats were meant for people who supported the development of Palestine without fully endorsing political Zionism.

From a London conference room in 1920 to the financial center of the enlarged Jewish Agency in 1929, the arc shows how quickly the movement's fundraising became infrastructure. A fund with named founders, a stated target, a registered legal form and a headquarters in Jerusalem was a different kind of instrument from a collection plate. The first American campaigns, described in [scene:us_s01|the following section], show how hard its leaders fought over who would control it. [cite:keren_hayesod_wiki]
