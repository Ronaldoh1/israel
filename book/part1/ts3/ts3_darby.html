John Nelson Darby and the Brethren

The British restorationists described in Part Two had read a Jewish return to Palestine into prophecy for two centuries before John Nelson Darby began to write. [scene:ts2_shaftesbury|Part Two: Shaftesbury and the restorationists] What Darby added was not the hope itself but a system: a way of dividing the whole Bible, and the whole of history, into separate stages with separate peoples, in which Israel and the church never merge. That system, later called dispensationalism, is the theology that most of the American organisations in later parts of this book grew out of. This section sets out who Darby was, how the movement he led began, and the long argument over where his most famous doctrine came from.

Lawyer, curate, separatist

Darby was born in Westminster on 18 November 1800, to an Anglo-Irish family, and was educated at Westminster School and Trinity College Dublin, where he graduated in 1819 as Classical Gold Medallist. He entered an inn of court to train for the law, then concluded that practising it conflicted with his religious beliefs. He was ordained a deacon of the Church of Ireland in 1825 and a priest in 1826, and served as curate of Delgany in County Wicklow. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby]

His break with the established church came in stages. He resigned his curacy in protest when William Magee, Archbishop of Dublin, required converts to swear allegiance to King George IV. Around 1827 and 1828 he joined a small interdenominational circle in Dublin, among them the missionary Anthony Norris Groves and John Gifford Bellett, that met to "break bread" without clergy or denominational rules. By 1832 the group regarded itself as a distinct assembly, and similar meetings at Plymouth in England gave the movement its common name, the Plymouth Brethren. He is believed to have left the Church of Ireland around 1831, though the year is uncertain. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby]

The movement divided in 1848 in a quarrel over whether one assembly's discipline bound the others, a dispute in which Darby's former ally Benjamin Wills Newton was a principal party. The result was two streams: the Open Brethren, whose congregations govern themselves, and the Exclusive Brethren, who followed Darby's view that assemblies must act together. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] Darby spent the rest of his life travelling, writing and translating; he produced Bible translations in German (the Elberfeld Bible), French and English, the last finished after his death. He died at Bournemouth on 29 April 1882. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby]

Powerscourt, 1831 to 1833

The ideas took public form at the Powerscourt conferences, gatherings on prophecy held annually from 1831 to 1833 at the County Wicklow estate of Darby's friend Theodosia Wingfield, Lady Powerscourt, a wealthy widow. The Dictionary of Premillennial Theology records that Darby set out his views on the church and on the end times there, "including the pre-tribulation rapture." [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] That doctrine holds that true Christians will be taken up from the earth to meet Christ before a final period of tribulation, rather than living through it.

The conference record is thinner than the summary suggests. Notebooks from the 1831 meeting survive, and the historian James Fazio has argued from them that Darby was still resisting pre-tribulational ideas at that conference; others read the evidence differently. [cite:ts3_brarchive_powerscourt] Darby himself dated his turn earlier. Around 1827, while recovering from a riding accident, he came to believe that the "kingdom" promised in Isaiah and the other prophets was something entirely different from the Christian church. [cite:ts3_wiki_darby] In a letter of 1879 he wrote: "What set me free in 1827 is still the theme on which my soul dwells." [cite:ts3_ice_darby]

The Margaret MacDonald dispute

Where Darby's rapture doctrine came from has been argued over since the nineteenth century, and the argument still circulates. Its fullest form concerns Margaret MacDonald (1815–1840), a young woman of Port Glasgow in Scotland who, around 1830, during a wave of charismatic revival linked to the London preacher Edward Irving, gave a series of prophetic utterances about the coming of Christ. Her words were first published in 1840. [cite:ts3_wiki_macdonald]

Two critics built the case. The biblical scholar Samuel Prideaux Tregelles alleged that the idea of a secret rapture came from a charismatic "utterance" in Irving's church; in an account Thomas Ice summarises, Tregelles dated it "about the year 1832." [cite:ts3_wiki_macdonald] [cite:ts3_ice_darby] In the 1970s the American writer Dave MacPherson went further, arguing in The Unbelievable Pre-Trib Origin (1973) and later books that MacDonald's 1830 vision was the source, and that Darby had taken the doctrine from it during a visit to the MacDonalds in 1830. [cite:ts3_strand_auss] [cite:ts3_wiki_macdonald]

The case has not persuaded historians on either side of the theological argument. Darby and Newton, sent by the Brethren to investigate the 1830 manifestations, concluded that they were demonic, which makes borrowing from them unlikely; according to John Bray (1992), Darby had written down pre-tribulational views by January 1827, three years before the events in Scotland; and MacDonald's own text, read in full, places believers inside the "fiery trial," a posttribulational view, while the version printed in 1861 left out two passages that make this plain. [cite:ts3_wiki_macdonald] The sharpest assessment came from a reviewer outside Darby's tradition. Kenneth Strand, writing in the seminary journal of the Seventh-day Adventist Andrews University, found that MacPherson's chief witness attributed the idea to Irving, "not Margaret Macdonald," that "It is impossible to prove that Darby adopted" the view from her, and that "the case for the 'unbelievable pre-trib origin' falls apart." [cite:ts3_strand_auss]

Defenders of the doctrine add their own argument, which is stated here as theirs. Thomas Ice of the Pre-Trib Research Center holds that Darby reached his view by December 1826 or January 1827 from his reading of the church as distinct from Israel, and that MacDonald's text "does not come close to even teaching a pre-trib rapture." [cite:ts3_ice_darby] The fairest summary is that the MacDonald origin is unproven and, on the text, unlikely; that Darby's own account dates his central idea to 1827; and that whether he had fully worked out the rapture by the 1831 conference remains a matter on which specialists differ. The doctrine's novelty is not in dispute: Weber notes that earlier premillennialists placed the rapture at the end of the tribulation, and that the Princeton theologian A. A. Hodge called the new version "an unscriptural and unprofitable theory." [cite:ts3_weber_ct1999]
