The Lobbying: A Small Budget and a Large Reputation

AIPAC calls itself a lobby, and lobbying is what its name describes. Yet in the one channel built to measure lobbying, the quarterly disclosures every lobbying organization must file with Congress, its spending has always been modest. That gap, between a small lobbying budget and a reputation as one of the most powerful lobbies in Washington, is the best clue to how the network actually works, and it has been pointed out as often by AIPAC's defenders as by its critics.

The disclosed figures. In 2018 AIPAC reported $3,518,028 in lobbying, according to OpenSecrets figures compiled by Tablet magazine, which ranked it about 147th among all lobbying organizations that year. All pro-Israel groups together spent about $5.0 million. The largest lobbying spenders were in another league: the US Chamber of Commerce reported $94.8 million and the National Association of Realtors $72.8 million, and companies such as Boeing, Amazon and Exxon Mobil each spent between three and four and a half times what AIPAC did. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] AIPAC's quarterly spending rose after the attacks of 7 October 2023, when it reported more than $800,000 in a single quarter, [cite:pm_cns_2024] and its annual figures for the two years that followed were about $3.3 million in 2024 and almost $3.8 million in 2025. [cite:pm_cjn_lobby2025]

Two cautions apply to the table. First, several of these organizations lobby on many subjects, from social services to civil rights, and their totals are not all Israel-related. Second, lobbying disclosures record what an organization pays its own lobbyists and outside firms to lobby; they do not record the time its members spend calling and meeting their own representatives, which for a membership organization like AIPAC may be a large part of the effort and is not measured anywhere.

What it lobbies for. AIPAC describes its aim, in its own words, as encouraging and persuading "the U.S. government to enact specific policies" that support the relationship with Israel. In its account of the 2023–24 Congress it pointed above all to money: an emergency package of $14.3 billion and what it called the largest funding package in Israel's history, worth $18.1 billion. [cite:pm_aipac_memo_2024] Its long-standing positions include strong support for military aid to Israel and opposition to the 2015 nuclear agreement with Iran, which it supported the United States leaving in 2018. [cite:pm_wiki_aipac] The aid itself, how it is structured and where it is spent, is the subject of other volumes in this library; this one follows the money that moves the other way, from American donors into American politics.

Where the influence comes from. If not from lobbying dollars, from where? Writing in Tablet in 2019, before AIPAC gave a single dollar to a campaign, Armin Rosen argued that its strength lay in a network: AIPAC-connected activists who give to future members of Congress early in their careers, and whose support signals to other pro-Israel donors which candidates are worth backing. [cite:pm_tablet_lobbying] On this account, AIPAC's real asset was never its lobbying budget but its ability to mobilize the donations of its members, which at that time flowed through independent PACs and individual checks. The decision AIPAC took two and a half years later, described in the next section, can be read as a decision to bring that network's money under its own name and control.

The gap between the lobbying figures and the reputation therefore does not show that the reputation is exaggerated, nor that it is deserved. It shows that the reputation does not rest on lobbying. The disclosed lobbying is real but small; the campaign money, which Parts Three to Five count, is large; and the trips, which Part Seven follows, have reached hundreds of congressional offices: the foundation sponsored 868 trips for 463 offices from 2012 to 2023. [cite:cnsmd_aief_explorer] A lobby's power, on this evidence, is better measured by all six channels together than by the one that bears its name.
