Country by Country: How the Jews of the Arab World Left

The departure of the Jews of the Arab world is usually told as one event with one number attached. It was a series of departures, country by country, each with its own dates, its own laws and its own violence, and each recorded by different sources with different degrees of certainty. The previous section followed Iraq, the largest single case, and the arrival camps in Israel [scene:s00e2|the exodus and its aftermath]. This section takes the other communities in turn: what happened in each place, who decided it, and where the people went. It ends with the two arguments that still surround the whole movement: how many left, and whether they should be called refugees.

Two common shortcuts fail on the record. One says that Israel's ties to Yemen began with the Houthis and that its ties to Morocco began with the agreements of 2020. In both countries the first connection was a community of people who left decades earlier. The other shortcut makes every departure an expulsion, or every departure a voluntary emigration organized from Israel. The country record shows both, often in the same place.

Libya. Libya had about 21,000 Jews in 1911, about 4% of a population of 550,000, and 30,387 on the eve of the Second World War. Italy's racial laws of 1938 were enforced in Libya from 1940. By June 1942 some 2,584 Jews had been deported to the camp at Giado, where 562 of them died.[cite:rs_ch01_yv_libya] More violence came after the war, under British military administration. In November 1945 a three-day attack on the Jews of Tripoli killed 120 Jews and destroyed at least five synagogues. Riots in 1948 killed 12 more and destroyed 280 homes.[cite:rs_ch01_yv_libya] According to Yad Vashem, 30,972 Libyan Jews emigrated to Israel in the wake of the violence around 1948. Those who stayed were pushed to the margins by law: a 1961 law denied nearly all Jews proof of citizenship. After riots in 1967 about 6,000 were airlifted to Rome, and by 2004 no Jews remained in Libya.[cite:rs_ch01_yv_libya] In Libya, then, the sequence ran from a fascist camp to a postwar pogrom under a European administration, then a mass departure to Israel, and finally legal exclusion. The Tripoli killings of 1945 came before the partition vote and before Israel existed.

Egypt. Egypt's community numbered an estimated 75,000–80,000 at its height in 1948. That figure is an Israeli Foreign Ministry estimate, cited by the University of Washington's Stroum Center. Bombs in Cairo's Jewish quarter between June and November 1948 killed more than 70 Jews, according to the same essay.[cite:rs_ch01_uw_egypt1956] A second break came with the Suez war. From 29 October 1956 the Egyptian government treated Jews as enemies of the state. About 1,000 were arrested in Cairo and 450 Jewish-owned businesses were confiscated. From 15 November, Jews leaving the country had to sign forms renouncing their property and their Egyptian citizenship. These details rest on the historian Joel Beinin and on an American Jewish Committee factsheet of March 1957.[cite:rs_ch01_uw_egypt1956] According to figures cited by the Stroum Center, about 25,000 Jews left Egypt in 1956, leaving roughly 15,000 in the country in 1957.[cite:rs_ch01_uw_egypt1956] Egypt's departures therefore came in at least two waves, eight years apart, with different triggers: the violence of 1948, and state measures taken during the Suez war. A third episode belongs to Egypt's story, the 1954 Israeli sabotage operation in which Egyptian Jews were recruited; it is told in the previous section.

Aleppo, Aden and Bahrain: the partition vote. The United Nations voted to partition Palestine on 29 November 1947. Within a week, Jewish communities far from Palestine were attacked. According to the Center for Israel Education, rioters in Aleppo on 30 November damaged the Great Synagogue and the Aleppo Codex, a manuscript of the tenth century. They destroyed some 150 Jewish homes, 50 shops, five schools and 18 synagogues.[cite:rs_ch01_cie_nov1947] In Aden, then under British rule, rioting from 2 December 1947 killed 82 Jews and wounded 76. In Manama, Bahrain, on 5 December, the synagogue was destroyed and one Jewish woman was killed, according to the same source.[cite:rs_ch01_cie_nov1947] These three accounts rest on one outlet, and the figures are given here as it reports them. The sources available for this book do not record how many Jews left each of these places afterwards, or where they went.

Yemen. Yemen's Jews were among the oldest Jewish communities in the world and had lived there for well over a thousand years. Between June 1949 and September 1950 Operation Magic Carpet, also called "On Wings of Eagles", airlifted about 49,000 of them to Israel. It was organized with the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee and flown by Alaska Airlines and other carriers. Many of the emigrants first walked for weeks to reach the British colony of Aden, where the flights began.[cite:w_magic_carpet] Some accounts round the total to nearly 50,000. In Israel many were placed in transit camps, where hundreds of children were reported dead or missing. This became the Yemenite Children Affair, for which Israel expressed "regret" and paid compensation in 2021 [scene:mz_s02|the Yemenite Children Affair]. So Israel's first tie to Yemen was a people moved by airlift, some seventy years before the missiles.

Morocco. Morocco's Jewish community had been rooted there for two millennia and was among the largest in the Arab world, numbering in the hundreds of thousands in the 1940s. Most emigrated after 1948, the majority to Israel. Between 1961 and 1964 a secret operation, Operation Yachin, brought about 97,000 Moroccan Jews to Israel with the Moroccan government's quiet consent. Historians record that Israel, together with American Jewish organizations, made payments to the Moroccan government to allow the departures.[cite:w_yachin] The money chain here ran in the opposite direction from Egypt's confiscations. A foreign state and diaspora organizations paid a sovereign government, and that government allowed its citizens to leave. The records available here do not give the amount. Israelis of Moroccan descent became one of Israel's largest communities. The two states' official ties came much later and did not last at first: Morocco and Israel opened low-level liaison offices in 1994, after the Oslo agreements, and closed them in 2000 during the second intifada.[cite:w_isr_morocco] The relationship formalized in 2020 rested on people and quiet arrangements that were decades older.

Algeria. Algeria shows how much the map depended on which empire held a country. The Crémieux Decree of 24 October 1870 made Algeria's Jews French citizens. The Vichy regime withdrew that citizenship on 7 October 1940, and it was restored on 20 October 1943. Rioting in Constantine in 1934 killed 25 Jews.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_algeria] According to the Jewish Virtual Library, Algeria had about 140,000 Jews in the mid-1950s, and almost 130,000 of them moved to France when Algeria became independent in 1962. Over the whole period from 1948, 25,681 Algerian Jews immigrated to Israel.[cite:rs_ch01_jvl_algeria] Most of Algeria's Jews, in other words, went to the country whose citizens they already were, at independence, more than a decade after 1948.

Read together, the cases show three separate mechanisms, often working at once. The first was violence by crowds, sometimes in places under British rule, as in Tripoli in 1945 and Aden in 1947. The second was the action of states: Egypt's renunciation forms in 1956, Libya's citizenship law of 1961 and, in Iraq, the denaturalization law told in the previous section. The third was organized emigration arranged from outside: airlifts by Israel and Jewish organizations in Yemen, and a paid, secret agreement in Morocco. Who could act also differed from place to place. In Morocco and Iraq governments negotiated the terms of exit. In Egypt in 1956 the government set them alone. In Algeria the decisive fact was a French citizenship that dated from 1870.

How many? The total is disputed, and the figures come from sources with different purposes. The account of the exodus in this book uses roughly 850,000 Jews leaving Arab and Muslim countries between 1948 and 1972. A survey of the regional aftermath gives estimates "commonly in the range of 800,000–900,000" for Arab countries and Iran from 1948 to the 1970s.[cite:w_jewish_exodus_arab] Avi Beker, writing in the Jewish Political Studies Review, gives about 900,000 Jews "forced out" of Arab countries, of whom about 650,000 went to Israel.[cite:rs_ch01_beker_jpsr] Beker writes as an advocate of one side of the argument that follows, and his figure is a position in that argument, not a settled count. The figures also differ in scope: one includes Iran, their end years differ, and Beker's counts those he describes as forced out.

Refugees? The sharper dispute is over the word itself. One position holds that these Jews were refugees in the same sense as the Palestinians of 1948. Beker sets out this "dual refugee problem" case, which the United States House of Representatives took up in H. Con. Res. 311 in October 2003. In this view about 900,000 Jews were forced out of Arab countries, and their case has been forgotten through international indifference and Israel's own reluctance to call it expulsion.[cite:rs_ch01_beker_jpsr] The strongest form of the case rests on records like those above: the Giado camp, Tripoli, the partition-vote riots, the Egyptian renunciation forms, and laws that stripped people of citizenship and property.

The opposing position holds that the comparison does not fit. The Israeli sociologist Yehouda Shenhav argued in Haaretz in 2006 that Palestinians did not leave of their own volition, while Arab Jews moved to Israel under initiatives by the state and Jewish organizations, some willingly and some not. The Palestinian writer Khalid Amayreh, in an opinion piece in Ahram Online in 2012 that quotes Shenhav, called the equation propaganda meant to offset the Nakba.[cite:rs_ch01_ahram_amayreh] The strongest form of this case also rests on the record: the organized airlift from Yemen, the payments to Morocco, and the recruitment operations described in the previous section.

A middle line accepts parts of both. The Stroum Center essay treats the Egyptian departures of 1956 as an expulsion. It also warns, citing the historian Eyal Bizawe, against using Mizrahi history to cast Arab societies as permanent enemies, and asks that the history of coexistence be taught alongside the history of the expulsions.[cite:rs_ch01_uw_egypt1956] This book's reading of the country record is close to that line. Some communities were expelled or driven out by violence and law, some left through organized operations, many left under both pressures at once, and the balance differed by country and by year. Whether the Jewish departures and the Palestinian displacement of 1948 can be set side by side as an "exchange" is a separate question, taken up in [scene:s00e3|the next section].

Much remains unsettled. The overall total is a range, and different authors set its ends for their own reasons. For Aleppo, Aden and Bahrain this book has the violence but not the departures that followed. For Egypt it has neither the destinations nor a full count of the 1948 wave. The amount paid to Morocco under Operation Yachin is not given. The sources also leave open the question each family faced: how far a given departure was flight, how far it was choice, and how far it was the result of someone else's negotiation. The record shows only that all three happened.
