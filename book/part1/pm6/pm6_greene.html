Marjorie Taylor Greene: The Break, the Attacks and the Resignation

Marjorie Taylor Greene of Georgia was, until 2025, a longtime ally of President Trump in the House. [cite:pm_reuters_trump_mtg] Within a few months of that year she broke with the pro-Israel network, was attacked by it in terms rarely used against a Republican, broke with the president, lost his endorsement, and announced her resignation from Congress. Because her departure is often described as the clearest case of a member driven out by pro-Israel pressure, and as often as the departure of a member who had made antisemitic statements, this section sets out the documented sequence in order, with each side's words, and keeps the two forces in the story, the pro-Israel network and the president, side by side.

The break. In late July 2025 Greene wrote that the attack of 7 October 2023 was horrific and the hostages must be returned, but that "so is the genocide, humanitarian crisis, and starvation happening in Gaza." Reuters later described her as the first Republican member of Congress to call the crisis in Gaza a genocide. [cite:pm_aljaz_aipac_mtg] [cite:pm_reuters_trump_mtg] In the same month she offered an amendment to the 2026 defense appropriations bill to strip $500 million a year in cooperative missile-defense funding for Israel, the money for the Iron Dome, Arrow and David's Sling systems. She called it "money we don't have" and said Israel was "very capable of defending themselves." The House rejected the amendment by 422 votes to 6; the six in favor were Greene, Thomas Massie, Ilhan Omar, Al Green, Summer Lee and Rashida Tlaib. [cite:pm_ji_mtg_amendment] She had taken no money from AIPAC; a later review by a conservative news service found no public record of any direct AIPAC donation to her. [cite:pm_dcnf_mtg_aipac]

The attacks. AIPAC answered on 7 August 2025. It called her accusation of genocide "disgusting" and said: "Let's call this what it is: Marjorie Taylor Greene is the newest member of the anti-Israel Squad." Greene replied: "Bring. It. On.," adding, "I can't be bought and I'm not backing down!!" [cite:pm_aljaz_aipac_mtg] The criticism that followed went beyond her positions on Israel to her character. Jewish Insider, a publication covering Jewish and Israel-related politics, published an account of what it called her amplification of antisemitic rhetoric. It cited, among other things, an earlier claim of hers that space-based weapons connected with the Rothschild family had caused California wildfires; a post saying "I'll never take 30 shekels," which it read as invoking Judas and Israel's currency; and posts suggesting that a foreign government or "powerful people" might target her. It quoted AIPAC's spokesman calling her statements "rancid anti-Israel and unhinged raving," and reported that the Republican Jewish Coalition had endorsed challengers to defeat her in her primary. [cite:pm_ji_mtg_rhetoric] The same account recorded her own demands: that AIPAC and other pro-Israel groups register as foreign agents, and her description of the Anti-Defamation League as a "dangerous hate group." [cite:pm_ji_mtg_rhetoric]

The president. The decisive break came from another direction. Greene had also broken with the White House on tariffs, on inflation and health-care costs and on foreign policy, and she had signed the petition to force a House vote on releasing the Justice Department's files on Jeffrey Epstein. On 14 November 2025 President Trump withdrew his endorsement on Truth Social, saying that all he saw her do was "complain, complain, complain," and that he would back the "right candidate" against her in a primary. She said he had "attacked me and lied about me," and linked the attack to her push for the Epstein files. [cite:pm_reuters_trump_mtg]

The resignation, in her words. A week later, on 21 November, Greene announced that she would resign from Congress on 5 January 2026. Her stated reasons were about the president. Her disagreements with him, she wrote, "should not result in me being called a traitor and threatened by the President of the United States"; she would not "endure a hurtful and hateful primary against me by the President we all fought for"; and, in the sentence most quoted, "I refuse to be a 'battered wife,' hoping it all goes away and gets better." [cite:pm_npr_mtg_resign] NPR's account described the Epstein files as the final straw and noted her opposition to the strikes on Iran and to support for Israel's war in Gaza. [cite:pm_npr_mtg_resign] Her letter did not name Israel; it spoke of "foreign interests" and of her opposition to foreign wars and foreign aid. [cite:pm_ji_mtg_resign] When the president later called her a "traitor," she answered by posting an image showing $0.00 in AIPAC money beside her name and more than $230 million beside his, Track AIPAC's figure for pro-Israel spending benefiting him since 2020, with the words "I AM AMERICA FIRST." [cite:pm_dcnf_mtg_aipac]

The reaction. The pro-Israel network's response to her resignation was open satisfaction. The Republican Jewish Coalition posted a clip of the president waving, captioned "And we say bye bye." Representative Randy Fine of Florida wrote: "One antisemite down. One to go." [cite:pm_ji_mtg_resign]

What the record shows. The documents support three statements and rule out a fourth. First, Greene broke with the pro-Israel network over Gaza and military aid in the summer of 2025 and was attacked by its leading organizations in the strongest terms, including by AIPAC by name and by the Republican Jewish Coalition through its support for primary challengers. Second, the attacks included accusations of antisemitism that cited specific statements of hers, some made years earlier; whether those statements were antisemitic, as her critics say, or whether the accusation was used to discredit her criticism of Israel, as her defenders say, is a judgment the reader can make from the statements themselves. Third, the event that preceded her resignation, and the one she gave as its reason, was the president's withdrawal of support and his promise to back a challenger. What the record does not support is the claim that pro-Israel pressure alone drove her from office. By her own account the decisive pressure came from the president. Her critics, for their part, pointed to her own statements. The two forces converged, as they did against Thomas Massie in the same months, and the record does not let a reader weigh one against the other.
