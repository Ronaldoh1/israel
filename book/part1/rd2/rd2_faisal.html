The Faisal–Weizmann Agreement, January 1919

Thirteen months after Allenby walked into Jerusalem, two men in London signed a short agreement that has been used as evidence in the argument over Palestine ever since. It is dated 3 January 1919. It was signed by Amir Faisal, on behalf of the Arab Kingdom of Hejaz, and by Dr Chaim Weizmann, on behalf of the Zionist Organization. [cite:rs_fw_unispal_text] Pro-Zionist writers have cited it as evidence that an Arab leader accepted the Zionist project at the start. Others have cited it to argue that he never consented to a Jewish state. Both uses depend on the same few lines of text, and on a condition that Faisal attached to it.

The two men had met before. Their first meeting took place in June 1918 at Faisal's camp in Transjordan. According to minutes kept by Lieutenant Colonel P. C. Joyce, as reported by the writer Rick Richman, Faisal "personally accepted the possibility of future Jewish claims to territory in Palestine," but said that only his father could make political commitments. [cite:rs_fw_jrb_richman_2018]

What the agreement said. The text treats an "Arab State" and "Palestine" as two separate entities, whose relations it sets out to regulate. Article II provided that, after the Peace Conference, "the definite boundaries between the Arab State and Palestine shall be determined by a Commission" agreed by the parties. Article III required that Palestine's constitution and administration afford "the fullest guarantees for carrying into effect the British Government's Declaration of the 2nd of November, 1917," the Balfour Declaration. [cite:rs_fw_unispal_text] Article IV stated that "all necessary measures shall be taken to encourage and stimulate immigration of Jews into Palestine on a large scale," while protecting the rights of Arab peasant and tenant farmers. Article V guaranteed the free exercise of religion; Article VI provided that "the Mohammedan Holy Places shall be under Mohammedan control"; and Article IX referred any disputes to the British Government for arbitration. [cite:rs_fw_unispal_text]

Read on its face, this is a striking document. An Arab leader, signing for the Kingdom of Hejaz, endorsed the Balfour Declaration by name, accepted large-scale Jewish immigration, and agreed that Palestine's boundaries with the Arab state would be fixed by a joint commission. The arbiter of disputes was to be Britain, the power that had made the wartime promises to both sides (see [scene:s06|the three promises]).

The condition. Beneath the articles, Faisal appended a reservation. The text held by the United Nations records that he wrote it in Arabic, beside his signature. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text] In the translation printed on the United Nations' UNISPAL site, where it is listed as a non-UN document, it made his agreement conditional: "Provided the Arabs obtain their independence as demanded in my Memorandum dated the 4th of January, 1919." If any departure were made from that, the agreement would be "void and of no account or validity." [cite:rs_fw_unispal_text] [cite:rs_fw_jvl_text] The Jewish Virtual Library prints the same text, reservation included, and gives as its source George Antonius's The Arab Awakening (1938), pages 437–439. [cite:rs_fw_jvl_text] Both the UNISPAL and the Jewish Virtual Library versions thus derive from Antonius's translation.

The exact wording of the condition is itself in question. A 2019 history-education article for EuroClio by Bob Stradling renders it in a softer form: "If changes are made I cannot be answerable for failing to carry out this agreement." [cite:rs_fw_euroclio] The difference matters. In the Antonius-derived version, a breach of the condition voids the agreement automatically. In the EuroClio version, Faisal merely disclaims responsibility for carrying it out. The sources used here do not resolve which rendering is closer to the original.

Whether the condition traveled with the agreement is a separate question. According to two accounts, the Zionist Organization submitted the agreement to the Paris Peace Conference without the caveat. [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] [cite:israeled_faisal_weizmann] If so, the delegates saw Faisal's signature without the terms on which he had given it. The sources do not say who decided to leave it out, or why.

Faisal in Paris. Faisal took his case to the Peace Conference, where the future of the Ottoman Arab lands was being argued over (the conference's handling of Palestine is told in [scene:pc_s01|the Paris Peace Conference]). Two of his statements from these weeks pull in different directions. The UN study The Origins and Evolution of the Palestine Problem quotes a memorandum of his dated 1 January 1919: "In Palestine, the enormous majority of the people are Arabs." [cite:rs_fw_un_origins] Two months later, on 3 March 1919, he wrote from the Hejaz delegation in Paris to Felix Frankfurter. In that letter he called the Zionist proposals "moderate and proper" and wrote that the Arabs "will wish the Jews a most hearty welcome home." [cite:rs_fw_jvl_frankfurter] The two statements are not necessarily in conflict. One describes who lived in Palestine; the other welcomes Jewish settlement there.

The memorandum dates also need care. The reservation refers to a memorandum of 4 January 1919, and the UN study quotes one of 1 January 1919. The sources used here do not say whether these are the same document under different dates or two separate papers.

What happened to the condition. Events soon tested it. In July 1919, according to Richman, the Syrian Congress rejected French claims, claimed Palestine as part of Syria and opposed Jewish immigration. [cite:rs_fw_jrb_richman_2018] At San Remo in April 1920 the Allies assigned Syria and Lebanon to France, and Iraq and Palestine to Britain. After the battle of Maysalun on 24 July 1920, Faisal's government in Damascus came to an end. [cite:rs_fw_hnn_black_2004] The independence Faisal had demanded, at least in Syria, did not come. The accounts that report the conference copy's missing caveat put the failure in the same terms: the mandates were divided along the lines of Sykes–Picot, the condition failed, and an Arab congress in Damascus repudiated Faisal's authority to sign the agreement at all. [cite:un_faisal_weizmann_text] [cite:wiki_faisal_weizmann] They do not say whether that congress was the Syrian Congress of 1919.

Did it lapse? Three readings. Whether the agreement ever took effect is disputed, and the dispute has three positions, each resting on documents.

The first holds that the agreement lapsed by its own terms. Faisal's condition was Arab independence as set out in his memorandum. Once France took Syria in 1920, that condition was not met, and the agreement became void. Stradling's EuroClio article concludes that the Allied allocation of Syria to France made it "null and void as far as Faisal was concerned." [cite:rs_fw_euroclio] [cite:rs_fw_hnn_black_2004]

The second holds that the condition was fulfilled in substance. In 1947, according to Richman, Weizmann told the United Nations that Faisal's condition, independence for the Arab territories outside Palestine, had been met. [cite:rs_fw_jrb_richman_2018] On this reading the agreement stands as evidence that a recognized Arab leader accepted Jewish immigration and the Balfour Declaration at the outset. Pro-Zionist writers have used it in exactly this way. Those making the case can point to the text that Faisal signed and to his letter to Frankfurter.

The third holds that, whatever its status, the agreement could not bind the Arabs of Palestine. The UN study argues that Faisal "in no manner consented to the establishment of a Jewish State." [cite:rs_fw_un_origins] Arab bodies took a different line from his; among them, the Syrian Congress of 1919 rejected Zionist claims. [cite:rs_fw_jrb_richman_2018] Faisal signed for the Kingdom of Hejaz, not for the Arabs living in Palestine, who had no part in it. On this reading, even an agreement fully in force would have been a commitment by one Arab leader about a country whose own population had not been asked.

Each reading has weight. The lapse argument has the plain logic of the reservation on its side, if the Antonius wording is right. The fulfillment argument rests on reading the condition as independence for Arab lands outside Palestine, despite the fall of Faisal's own government at Maysalun. The limited-scope argument has the signature line itself: the agreement was made by the Hejaz, not by Palestine.

The record leaves several things open. The sources used here do not reproduce the memorandum of 4 January 1919, so the exact independence Faisal demanded cannot be checked against what followed. They do not settle the original wording of the reservation, which reaches readers through Antonius's 1938 translation and through a softer modern rendering. They do not say who left the condition out of the copy submitted in Paris. And they do not show whether Faisal's father, whose authority Faisal himself invoked in 1918, ever endorsed the agreement. The later course of the Hashemite family, and Faisal's own new throne in Iraq, are told in [scene:me_s05|the Cairo Conference of 1921].
