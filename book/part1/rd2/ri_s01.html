First Riots, First Militia, and Two British Inquiries

Less than three years after the Balfour Declaration, violence broke out in Palestine under British rule. It is sometimes remembered as if it came from nowhere, or as if it had one simple cause. The men Britain sent to investigate it, writing at the time, saw something more tangled. They recorded crowds attacking Jews, and they recorded the political fear that lay underneath. They also recorded the first armed Jewish answer. Between the first outbreak in Jerusalem in April 1920 and the second, which began in Jaffa in May 1921, the Haganah, the defense organization of the Yishuv, the Jewish community in Palestine, was founded. Britain produced two inquiries into the violence. One was published. The other, which laid the heavier blame on the Zionists, was not.

The first outbreak came in Jerusalem during the Nebi Musa festival. The pilgrimage began on 4 April 1920, which that year was Easter Day. [cite:rs_hg_palin] Over the days from 4 to 7 April, speeches by Arab religious leaders were followed by attacks on the city's Jews. [cite:jvl_arab_riots_1920s] The toll differs from source to source. Some accounts give five to six Jews and four Arabs killed and some 200 injured. [cite:jvl_arab_riots_1920s] The military court that investigated the events kept its own count. It recorded 5 Jews killed, 18 dangerously wounded and 193 wounded, 216 Jewish casualties in all. It counted 4 Muslims killed, 1 dangerously wounded and 20 wounded, 25 in all, as well as 5 Christians and 7 soldiers wounded. The report gives the overall total as 251. [cite:rs_hg_palin]

The record of those days is not only one of violence. The sheikhs of 82 villages issued a document protesting the attacks on Jews. [cite:jewishpress_nebimusa_palin] The court also found that the attack fell on one community: "the incidence of the attack was against the Jews," it concluded. [cite:rs_hg_palin]

Jabotinsky had organized Jewish self-defense in Jerusalem during the riots. [cite:jvl_arab_riots_1920s] He was arrested with 19 others, most of them former soldiers of the Jewish Legion. A military court sentenced him to 15 years' hard labor and each of his 19 comrades to three years. [cite:rs_hg_ej_jabotinsky] [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] The court of inquiry took a different view of these men from the one the military court had taken. It found the intention of the Jewish self-defense organizers "purely defensive," and it wrote that "the arrest and prosecution of Mr. Jabotinsky was ungenerous." [cite:rs_hg_palin]

What happened to the 15-year sentence is reported in two ways, and both appear on the same reference page. In J. B. Schechtman's account in the Encyclopaedia Judaica, the new High Commissioner, Herbert Samuel, amnestied those imprisoned over the Jerusalem riots in July 1920, Jews and Arabs alike. Jabotinsky left Acre prison and in September 1920 had the sentences quashed in London. The Encyclopedia of World Biography entry on that page says instead that the sentence was commuted to one year with banishment from Palestine. [cite:rs_hg_ej_jabotinsky] The two accounts cannot both be right, and this book does not choose between them. An Etzel history site adds that Jabotinsky objected to an amnesty that put defenders and aggressors in the same category, and that he kept pressing until the War Office revoked the sentences. [cite:rs_hg_daat]

Amin al-Husseini was sentenced too. His biographer Walid Khalidi writes that he was sentenced "for having led demonstrations," which places the April events in a different frame: political protest for Syrian unity. [cite:rs_hg_palquest_khalidi] The court of inquiry described an attack directed mainly against Jews and a Jewish self-defense that was defensive. [cite:rs_hg_palin] Khalidi's framing and the court's are both part of the record, and they describe the same days from different sides. Amin al-Husseini's conviction, pardon and rise to religious office are told in [scene:rd2_mufti|the appointment of the Mufti].

Britain's first inquiry was a military Court of Inquiry, known as the Palin court. It was convened on 12 April 1920, heard 152 witnesses, and reported from Port Said on 1 July 1920. [cite:rs_hg_palin] Its findings went further than the riots themselves. It called the Balfour Declaration "the starting point of the whole trouble." It criticized the Zionist Commission for taking on administrative functions that ran alongside the government's own. [cite:rs_hg_palin] It blamed the Zionists, "whose impatience to achieve their ultimate goal and indiscretion are largely responsible for this unhappy state of feeling." The report was never published. [cite:jewishpress_nebimusa_palin]

The Jewish units that had fought in the war were by then almost gone. The Jewish Legion's battalions had numbered over 5,000 men by early 1919. In the second half of 1919 they were cut to one battalion, and by spring 1920, in Schechtman's words, "a mere 300 to 400 men remained." Jabotinsky himself had been forcibly demobilized in August 1919. [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] Meanwhile the labor movement built its own body. In June 1920 an Ahdut ha-Avodah conference at Kinneret accepted the decision of Ha-Shomer to disband. It chose a committee, including Israel Shohat and Eliyahu Golomb, to organize a defense body. That body was the Haganah. [cite:rs_hg_ej_haganah] [cite:wiki_israel_timeline] Its purpose was to defend Jewish settlements. [cite:wiki_israel_timeline]

The Haganah's place in the labor movement is described in different terms. At its founding convention in December 1920, the Histadrut took on matters of guard and defense. Y. Slutsky, writing in the Encyclopaedia Judaica, says the Histadrut "accepted responsibility" for them. P. Atlas, in the Encyclopedia of the Modern Middle East, goes further and says the Haganah was placed under the Histadrut's direct control. [cite:rs_hg_ej_haganah] The founding of the Histadrut belongs to the next stage of this book. Slutsky also records how small the force was: through the 1920s, Haganah volunteers "did not exceed a few hundred," and they were armed mainly with revolvers and grenades. [cite:rs_hg_ej_haganah]

The second outbreak began in Jaffa in May 1921. It started after a clash between two rival Jewish May Day marches and spread to Petah Tikva, Hadera and Rehovot. [cite:wiki_jaffa_riots_1921] On 2 May 1921 Haganah members launched the first reprisal attacks on Arabs. [cite:wiki_jaffa_riots_1921] The Legion was also caught up in Jaffa. On 1 May 1921 the Legion officer Margolin led armed men into the town without military permission. He was charged with breach of discipline and forced to resign. The last 400 Palestinian Legionnaires joined a mixed Arab-Jewish militia that Samuel created. [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] Samuel also suspended Jewish immigration after the Jaffa riots, which is covered in [scene:rd2_aliyah|the Third Aliyah].

The second inquiry, the Haycraft Commission, was published. Its findings ran in two directions. It found the "fundamental cause" of the violence in Arab "discontent with, and hostility to, the Jews, due to political and economic causes, and connected with Jewish immigration," and with Zionist policy as stated by its exponents. [cite:jvl_haycraft_report] It also found that "the Arab majority, who were generally the aggressors, inflicted most of the casualties." Most Arab casualties came from British forces restoring order. [cite:jvl_haycraft_report]

Put side by side, the two reports do not tell two separate stories. Each holds both halves of the same one. Palin found that the attacks fell on Jews and that the Jewish organizers acted in defense, and still put the political weight on Zionist impatience and on the Balfour Declaration. Haycraft found the Arab side generally the aggressors, and still traced the cause to Arab hostility connected with Jewish immigration and Zionist policy. From its first year, then, Britain's own record held both the attacks by Arab crowds on Jews and the Arab fear of the Zionist program as the cause beneath them. What differed was which report was published. The report that leaned toward Zionist responsibility stayed unpublished in 1920. That was the same year the King–Crane report, discussed in [scene:pc_s01|the Paris Peace Conference], was set aside.

The structure of power was already visible in these thirteen months. Britain's military courts decided who was tried and how severely. Its High Commissioner could amnesty Jews and Arabs alike with a single act in July 1920, and London could quash sentences. [cite:rs_hg_ej_jabotinsky] Its government decided which inquiry the public would read. As Britain cut the Jewish battalions from over 5,000 men to a few hundred, the labor movement built a defense body of its own. [cite:rs_hg_jvl_legion] [cite:rs_hg_ej_haganah]

Some things these sources leave unsettled. They do not give one agreed death toll for April 1920. The court's own figures, which come closest to a count, sit beside later accounts that differ. The sources do not agree on whether Jabotinsky's sentence was quashed outright or commuted with banishment. They do not settle how directly the Histadrut controlled the Haganah in its first years. They give the reasoning of both inquiries but not, from the material here, why the Palin report in particular was kept unpublished. And they leave open the larger question these two reports first raised, one that every later inquiry would face again: how much weight to give the attacks themselves, and how much the political fear behind them.
