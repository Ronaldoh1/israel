Four Questions

1. Would an Islamist movement exist in Gaza? Very likely, and this is the part least tied to Israel. Hamas grew out of the Muslim Brotherhood, a religious and political movement from Egypt whose Gaza branch was building a social network of charities, schools and mosques long before 1987. [cite:hm_pbs_baconi] [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] The religious and social network that became Hamas was not itself a product of the occupation.

2. Would that movement have taken up arms against Israel? Here the record points the other way. For most of the 1970s and 1980s the Brotherhood in Gaza built schools, clinics and mosques, under an Israeli licence granted in 1979, and fought the secular Palestinian nationalists of Fatah rather than Israel; when Ahmed Yassin was caught with a cache of weapons in 1984, he told his interrogators they were meant for his Palestinian rivals. [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] It was in December 1987, in the first weeks of the uprising against twenty years of Israeli occupation, that the movement took the name the Islamic Resistance Movement, Hamas by its Arabic initials, and turned to armed struggle. [cite:hm_avalon_charter] The historian Tareq Baconi describes the change as a response to the uprising: the movement came to believe that "the only way that Israel can be compelled to end its occupation" was armed force. [cite:hm_pbs_baconi] Its first suicide bombing inside Israel, at Afula in April 1994, was announced as revenge for the massacre of 29 Muslim worshippers in Hebron by a settler six weeks before; an adviser to Israel's own Shin Bet said Hamas had refrained from attacking civilians inside Israel until then. [cite:hm_wiki_afula]

3. Would Hamas have grown as it did without Israel's early tolerance? Israeli officials who served in Gaza disagree among themselves. Avner Cohen, who handled religious affairs there, said: "Hamas, to my great regret, is Israel's creation." A former military governor, Yitzhak Segev, said the government gave him a budget and "the military government gives to the mosques." Others, such as Brigadier General Shalom Harari, said Israel's failures were neglect, not design, and that Israel never financed or armed Hamas; Arieh Spitzen doubted political Islam could have been checked sooner. [cite:hm_wsj_spawn] [cite:hm_intercept_2018]

4. Would Hamas be what it is without the split and the separation? Israel restricted movement between Gaza and the West Bank from 1991, and kept restricting it after the Oslo agreement of 1993 called them "a single territorial unit"; more than 500,000 exits from Gaza were recorded in September 2000, against about 3,000 permits a month in 2013. [cite:hm_aj_separation] Hamas won the Palestinian election of 2006 across both territories before the split, and took Gaza by force in 2007. [cite:gz_wiki_battle2007] After that, Bezalel Smotrich, then a Knesset member, said in 2015 that "the Palestinian Authority is a burden, and Hamas is an asset", and Israeli governments let Qatari money reach Hamas's government while blockading Gaza. [cite:wb_wiki_smotrich] [cite:gz_week_qatar] Netanyahu denies the policy aimed to strengthen Hamas and says the money prevented a humanitarian collapse. [cite:hm_ynet_biglie] What Hamas would have become in a Gaza connected to the West Bank and open to the world cannot be known; what is documented is that its rule hardened inside a closed strip.

Two Readings

Scholars read this history in two ways, and the record holds both. One reading, Baconi's, sees Hamas as a Palestinian national movement with an Islamic identity, engaged in "armed resistance against a colonial force"; treating it only as a terrorist group, he argues, lets governments avoid the political question of Palestine. [cite:hm_pbs_baconi] The other, set out by the terrorism scholar Bruce Hoffman, holds that Hamas's aims come from its ideology, not the occupation: its 1988 charter, he writes, "spells out clearly Hamas's genocidal intentions," and its 2017 document changed the language without changing the goal. [cite:hm_atl_hoffman] The charter itself gives Hoffman's reading its evidence: it calls all of Palestine an Islamic trust, says "there is no solution for the Palestinian question except through Jihad," and repeats antisemitic conspiracy claims; Part Two quotes it. [cite:hm_avalon_charter] [scene:hm2_charter|The charter]

On the documents, the armed organization called Hamas was born of the uprising against the occupation, and its first attacks on civilians inside Israel followed a massacre of Palestinians; its ideology, written into its charter, reached beyond ending the occupation to the whole land. Both are true. The rest of this book follows what it did with both.
