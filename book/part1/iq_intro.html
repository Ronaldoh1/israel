"The Inquisition" is usually spoken of as one thing. The documents show several. In the thirteenth century it was a set of commissions that popes handed to individual friars. From 1478 it was a royal court in Spain, licensed by a pope and staffed by men the king chose; from 1536 a second royal court in Portugal, which in 1560 opened a branch in Goa; and from 1542 the pope's own tribunal in Rome, which still exists under another name. [cite:vt28_wiki_greg9] [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] [cite:vt34_wiki_portinq] [cite:vt34_va_cdf_profile] This book follows each of them, and then the witch trials, which most people blame on the Inquisition and which the record mostly does not.

The record is grim and specific. In 1252 a pope authorised torture in heresy trials and split the property of the condemned between the city, the officials and the inquisitors. [cite:vt28_wiki_adextirpanda] At the first Spanish auto de fe, in Seville in 1481, six people were burned alive. [cite:vt34_wiki_spaninq] In Rome in 1600 Giordano Bruno was burned alive with his tongue held in a gag. [cite:vt29_wiki_bruno] Across Europe between about 1450 and 1750 courts executed somewhere between 40,000 and 60,000 people for witchcraft. [cite:vt30_wiki_witchtrials]

The record also corrects the legend in both directions. It does not support the thirty thousand Spanish burnings claimed in 1817, or the nine million witches claimed in 1784; it does show tens of thousands of trials, thousands of deaths and a machinery that searched for dissent instead of waiting for it. [cite:vt34_eretico_victims] [cite:vt30_luebke_ninemillion] This book also follows the people that machinery questioned: village healers, a miller with his own theory of creation, and Friulian peasants who said they left their bodies at night to fight for the harvest. The closing part, The Inner Reading, reads what survived the fires.
