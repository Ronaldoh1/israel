What Survived, and What Stays Open

A ledger of suppression is also, read the other way, a record of survival. Almost every tradition this book has followed reached the present, in some form, by one of four routes: buried in the ground, preserved in its enemies' books, kept alive by a community that never stopped using it, or revived by later readers who found it again. This section sets out what came through each route, then the questions the record still leaves open, and ends the volume.

Buried and found

The most famous survivals came out of the Egyptian ground. The thirteen Nag Hammadi codices, with 46 different works, were dug up at the foot of the Jabal al-Tarif in December 1945; they had been made about 350 and buried in the following decades, by whom and why is still argued. [cite:vt25_enc_nh] [cite:vt25_lj2015] [cite:vt25_lewisblount2014] The Berlin Codex, with the only extended text of the Gospel of Mary, was bought in Cairo in January 1896 and reportedly came from a burial site at Akhmim; Codex Tchacos, with the Gospel of Judas, was found near Maghagha in the 1970s and passed through dealers, a theft and a bank box on Long Island before its publication on 6 April 2006. [cite:vt26_wiki_berlincodex] [cite:vt26_natgeo_journey] The Greek fragments of the Gospel of Thomas came out of the ancient rubbish of Oxyrhynchus, and the Aramaic fragments of Enoch out of Qumran's Cave 4. [cite:vt20_ecw_thomas] [cite:vt27_wiki_enoch] Two codices left Egypt earlier, by purchase: the Askew Codex with Pistis Sophia, in the British Museum since 1785, and the Bruce Codex with the Books of Jeu, bought by James Bruce about 1769 and in the Bodleian since 1848. [cite:vt26_wiki_askew] [cite:vt26_wiki_bruce] None of these books was hidden from Rome, and none was found by Rome; most were found by chance, sold through the antiquities trade, and published decades later.

Kept by their enemies

The second route is the strangest. Much of what survives of the condemned traditions survives because their opponents copied it in order to refute it. Ptolemy's Letter to Flora, one of the few complete Valentinian works known before 1945, exists only because Epiphanius quoted it whole in his catalogue of heresies. [cite:vt24_wiki_flora] Irenaeus's summaries remain the main source for Basilides and the Barbelo myth, and the Refutation of All Heresies, recovered from a Mount Athos manuscript in 1842, preserves accounts of groups known from nowhere else. [cite:vt25_iren_129] [cite:vt24_wiki_refutation] The pattern repeats in the Middle Ages. The voices of the last Cathars of the Pyrenees survive in the register of Jacques Fournier, bishop of Pamiers and later Pope Benedict XII, whose 578 interrogations of 1318 to 1325 are kept in the Vatican Library and became the basis of Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's Montaillou. [cite:vt28_wiki_fournier] The Chinon parchment of 1308, which records the Templar leaders' absolution, lay in the Vatican's archive until it was identified in 2001. [cite:vt29_wiki_chinon] The archive of the institution that condemned them is now one of the main witnesses to what they believed; the archives themselves are described in [scene:vt14_apostolic_archive|Part Fourteen].

A few of the groups' own texts came through as well. The Cathar Book of Two Principles, the Occitan ritual of Lyon and the Bogomil Secret Supper, the Interrogatio Iohannis, all survive in manuscript. [cite:vt28_wiki_catharism] [cite:vt28_lyons_traditio]

Kept alive

The third route is continuous use, and here the survivors are living communities rather than manuscripts. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, which has never been under Rome's jurisdiction, keeps a Bible its own website counts at 81 books, including 1 Enoch, Jubilees and the three books of Meqabyan; it became self-governing under its own patriarch in 1959 and counts its members in the tens of millions. [cite:vt27_eotc_books] [cite:vt27_wiki_basilios] [cite:vt27_wiki_eotc] Its canon is its own, not a museum of a lost Bible, and it accepts the Council of Nicaea. [cite:vt27_eotc_chalcedon] The Mandaeans, who honour John the Baptist as their greatest teacher and practise repeated baptism in running water, are the one living community that scholars commonly class among the Gnostic religions; they are not Christians, their origins are disputed, and the war in Iraq after 2003 killed or drove out about nine in ten of the Iraqi community, leaving 60,000 to 100,000 worldwide. [cite:vt24_wiki_mandaeans] In Ethiopia, Rome's one sustained attempt to change a church's practice, the Latinisation of 1622 to 1632, was reversed by the same emperor who had imposed it. [cite:vt27_wiki_mendes]

Found again

The fourth route is revival. Marsilio Ficino translated the Hermetic writings for Cosimo de' Medici in 1462 or 1463, and Renaissance readers took them for a wisdom older than Plato until Isaac Casaubon dated them to the Christian era in 1614. [cite:ts13_wiki_hermetica] The Rosicrucian manifestos of 1614 to 1616 announced a brotherhood for which there is no evidence; real Rosicrucian orders appeared only in the eighteenth century, and AMORC, among the best known today, was founded in the United States in 1915. [cite:ts13_wiki_rosicruc] [cite:ts13_wiki_amorc] Jules Doinel founded the Église Gnostique in Paris in 1890, the first of the modern Gnostic churches. [cite:vt24_wiki_doinel] G. R. S. Mead's English Pistis Sophia of 1896 and Jung's writing carried the word "Gnosis" into the twentieth century, and Elaine Pagels's The Gnostic Gospels of 1979 carried the Nag Hammadi texts to a general readership. [cite:vt26_wiki_mead] [cite:vt24_enc_pagels] Freemasonry, organised in its modern form by the Grand Lodge founded in London on 24 June 1717 and condemned by Rome since 1738, survives as a fraternity whose relations with the Church are set out in [scene:vt30_rosicrucians_masons|Part Thirty]. [cite:vt30_wiki_pgle] [cite:vt30_vn_ddf2023] Each revival describes itself as the return of something old; the record usually shows a modern founding that drew on old texts, which is itself a kind of survival.

The institution has revived some of what it once set aside. Mary Magdalene, fused with a sinful woman by Gregory I about 591, was separated from her in the Roman calendar in 1969 and given a feast as "apostle of the apostles" on 3 June 2016. [cite:vt26_pearse_greg33] [cite:vt26_va_feast2016] Galileo's case was reopened by a papal commission and closed by John Paul II's address of 31 October 1992. [cite:vt29_coyne_galileo]

Parallels beyond Christianity

The tension between inner knowledge and religious authority is not only a Christian story. In Judaism, the Kabbalah developed a map of the divine in ten sefirot, aspects through which the divine is manifest, as an inner tradition within rabbinic Judaism rather than against it; the readings of Jacob's struggle as an ascent of the soul, from Philo to the Kabbalah, are set out in [scene:ts13_jacob|The Theology Shield]. [cite:ts13_wiki_tiferet] In Islam, the Sufi teacher al-Hallaj was executed in Baghdad in March 922 with the caliph's approval, charged in terms that presented him as a rebel against the Kaaba; one of his sayings put the true pilgrimage around "the Kaaba of one's heart". [cite:vt32_brit_hallaj] [cite:vt32_wiki_hallaj] The ancient mysteries and the ascent pattern they share with these traditions are traced in [scene:ts13_mysteries|The Theology Shield's chapter on the mysteries]. The parallels show a recurring pattern, not a single lineage: no documented line of transmission joins the Valentinians, the Sufis and the Kabbalists, and none is claimed here.

The end of the record

Book One of this volume asked whether the Holy See's refusal of Zionism was a front, and found that it was not. Book Two asked what the institution took, buried and kept, and its answer is less tidy. The institution did build a mediated, obligatory and, from the sixth century, taxed system over teachings that had offered a direct path; it did condemn, exclude and, in the Middle Ages, burn the people who kept such teachings; and it did so under its own canons, as its own leaders acknowledged in 2000. [cite:vt24_ce_tithes] [cite:vt28_fordham_lat4c3] [cite:vt28_america_2000] It did not choose its Bible at Nicaea, did not order the Gnostic gospels burned in any document yet found, did not suppress the Ethiopian Bible, and did not act alone. [cite:vt22_canons] [cite:vt27_hessayon] [cite:vt21_fc_laws] What it excluded did not disappear. It survived in a jar, in a bookseller's codex, in its enemies' refutations and its own inquisitors' registers, in a church in the Ethiopian highlands and a people baptising in the rivers of Iraq, and it is now printed, translated and read more widely than at any time since it was written. The record does not say whether that recovery was an accident or a pattern; it says what was done, and what was kept.
