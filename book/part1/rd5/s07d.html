The Meeting in Sonneborn's Apartment, July 1945

A common account of Israel's founding war holds that its weapons arrived only after May 1948, once the state existed and could ask the world for help. The documents place the start of the supply effort almost three years earlier, in a private apartment in Manhattan, in the summer that the war in Europe ended.

By July 1945 the fighting in Europe was over, and the full scale of the Holocaust was becoming documented and public. David Ben-Gurion, chairman of the Jewish Agency Executive and the de facto head of the Yishuv, the Jewish community of Palestine, had travelled from Palestine to New York with a specific purpose. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] His assessment, made three years before independence and not in response to any declared war, was that the Arab states would go to war against the Jews of Palestine, and that the Haganah, the Yishuv's underground defense force, had neither the weapons nor the money to survive such a war. [cite:sonneborn_toi] He needed a supply and financing pipeline in America, and it was a task he could not ask the Jewish Agency to carry out openly. [cite:mexico_arms_1948]

The meeting took place in the Manhattan apartment of Rudolf Sonneborn, a wealthy New York industrialist and scion of an oil-and-chemical business, who had travelled to Palestine himself and formed a friendship with Ben-Gurion. [cite:sonneborn_toi] [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The sources differ slightly on who called it. One account has Ben-Gurion convening the meeting; another has Sonneborn convening it at Ben-Gurion's request. [cite:sonneborn_toi] [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The two are compatible: the request was Ben-Gurion's, the room and the guest list Sonneborn's. The man who gathered the guests was Henry Montor, who by 1948 was executive vice-chairman of the United Jewish Appeal, a connection that would matter again in 1948 when the same Montor arranged Golda Meyerson's appeal to the American federations, told in [scene:us_s07|Golda Meyerson's American tour]. [cite:jta_golda_1948]

The sources also differ on the date and the number present, in small but real ways. One account dates the meeting to 1 July 1945; another says only that it took place on a July afternoon. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] [cite:sonneborn_toi] Accounts describe roughly seventeen prominent Jewish businessmen in the room. [cite:sonneborn_toi] One account gives seventeen American businessmen in one place and seventeen Americans and one Canadian in another. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] Ben-Gurion warned them of the coming war and of the Haganah's poverty, and the men in the room, moved, by later accounts, by the warning together with the recent revelation of the Holocaust's scale, agreed that same afternoon to build what became a Jewish-American arms underground. [cite:sonneborn_toi]

How much weight that last detail can bear needs saying. Firsthand descriptions of the meeting, including Leonard Slater's 1970 book The Pledge, were written decades afterwards, sympathetically, by an author who interviewed participants long after the fact. [cite:sonneborn_toi] The meeting itself, its date, its convener and the organization that came out of it are well documented. Any single participant's stated motives, recorded a quarter-century later, deserve the caution owed to any retrospective account. The record also does not show that every donor or participant fully understood, at the time, that the network would be operating illegally. [cite:sonneborn_toi]

What emerged from the meeting was the Sonneborn Institute, a network that became the financial and logistical backbone of the Haganah's transatlantic supply effort. [cite:sonneborn_jps] [cite:ampalestine_haganah] It was not a fundraising drive in the ordinary sense. It was the operating base of an armed procurement effort that ran for three years outside any registered or public channel, and by one account continuously until 1949. [cite:sonneborn_toi] Ben-Gurion directed its formation; the day-to-day American procurement work was run by Sonneborn and a small operational circle. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] Sonneborn received no formal recognition for his part beyond a private letter of thanks from Ben-Gurion after Israel was founded. [cite:sdjw_winters]

The network worked through a front company, and here the sources disagree on its name. One historical account calls it Materials for Palestine; another calls it Materials for Israel. [cite:sonneborn_jps] [cite:mexico_arms_1948] They also describe its cover somewhat differently. In one account it operated under the guise of an ordinary commercial and charitable enterprise, providing cover for buying and shipping machinery, tools and industrial materials, legally purchasable surplus, destined for Ta'as, the Yishuv's clandestine arms-production effort. [cite:sonneborn_jps] In the other, the network operated under the legal cover of a charity for European Jewish relief, raising funds and moving industrial equipment, machine tools and eventually munitions toward Haganah forces in Palestine. [cite:ampalestine_haganah] [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The two descriptions are not contradictory so much as describing different stages: the first stresses the legal machinery bought for production inside Palestine, the second the move toward finished munitions as the war drew closer.

The methods are documented end to end. Donors gave money for which no receipts were issued. The front company bought under its commercial or charitable cover. Shipments moved under falsified manifests and disguised invoices, routed through Panama, Mexico, Czechoslovakia, Canada and France to evade American export controls, and arrived with Haganah forces in Palestine. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] By the time the United States formally tightened arms-export enforcement under the Neutrality Act in December 1947, the network already had two years of working tradecraft in place. [cite:mexico_arms_1948] The scale it reached, about 2,400 tons in some 800 consignments by one count, and the routes, aircraft and trials of its working years are told in [scene:s07e|the arms network in operation]. [cite:mexico_arms_1948]

The structure was designed around one legal fact. What the network did was, under American law, illegal arms export, and the people who built it arranged matters so that the official Jewish Agency would not be directly exposed. Historians who have documented the network note that it was kept deliberately separate from the Agency, and that this compartmentalization was a choice, not an accident of organization. [cite:sonneborn_jps] They also note that the separation was not total. Abba Hillel Silver, chairman of the Jewish Agency's American section, served as a liaison between the two. [cite:sonneborn_jps] The record therefore shows both things at once: a structure built to insulate the Agency, and a named senior figure connecting it to the Agency anyway. It does not show that the Agency was unaware of what the network did, nor does it give an account of what each Agency official individually knew.

Where the money came from, and how much there was, is less well recorded than how it moved. Donors were issued no receipts. The sources give no total for what the Sonneborn circle raised or spent, and no ledger of individual shipments or transactions. [cite:sonneborn_jps] The panel below places the network in the longer sequence of channels that paid for the Yishuv's defense, from the Jewish Agency's own budget and a community levy of the 1930s to the American appeals of 1948.

That sequence matters because the Sonneborn network sat in the middle of it, and because the same people moved along it. Montor's role in both the 1945 meeting and the 1948 appeal shows that the arms network and the open federation-and-appeal fundraising overlapped directly through named individuals. [cite:jta_golda_1948] Among the network's key financiers was Abraham Feinberg, who met repeatedly with Sonneborn and later founded Americans for Haganah. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file] In 1952 the United States government would order him to register as a foreign agent, a later chapter of his story told in [scene:s07f2|Abraham Feinberg]. [cite:feinberg_fbi_file]

The procurement network had an intelligence counterpart. The first Jewish intelligence organization in Palestine had been established under the Haganah in 1929, and the Shai (Sherut Yediot, the Information Service) was set up in 1940 as the Haganah's intelligence and counter-espionage arm. [cite:shai_wiki] By 1948, according to the account this book draws on, the Shai had 68 staff, 60 British and Jewish agents and 80 Arab agents, and spent nearly $700,000 a year. Its stated objectives were to promote an independent Israeli state, to infiltrate British Mandate offices, to collect intelligence for propaganda, to penetrate Arab factions, and to secure the Rekhesh arms-smuggling operations. It had American branches, Zionist networks in New York and Detroit that served as procurement and intelligence points, and these were not registered under the Foreign Agents Registration Act. [cite:shai_wiki] [cite:ips_espionage] After 1948 the Shai became the seed from which Israeli military intelligence grew, and its American networks formed the early North American foundation of the Mossad. [cite:shai_wiki]

Rekhesh, Hebrew for "acquisitions," was the Haganah's arms and munitions procurement branch in the United States in the mid-1940s. It built an extensive illegal procurement network despite the arms embargo on the Palestine region. Its operations were funded by American Jewish donors and involved buying surplus military equipment from demobilized United States Army stocks and from black-market dealers, then smuggling it out through covert shipping routes, often with the help of organized crime to get around export controls. [cite:ips_espionage] [cite:medium_zionist] The account this book draws on describes the networks as violating both American export law and the British arms embargo. [cite:forward_oss]

The federal government knew. The FBI documented these operations and repeatedly recommended that key figures be prosecuted or required to register as foreign agents; the Justice Department declined each time. [cite:forward_oss] [cite:medium_zionist] One case shows the pattern in detail. The attorney William Bernstein, according to the FBI, trained Mossad agents, incorporated companies that smuggled arms for Zionist groups, and conducted surveillance of Arab delegates at the United Nations; the Bureau linked him to sophisticated technical surveillance of Arab officials and to break-ins at consular offices. The Justice Department declined every FBI recommendation that Bernstein be prosecuted or made to register. [cite:ips_espionage] [cite:medium_zionist] The enforcement record, set side by side, is uneven rather than empty. Federal agencies, the FBI and Customs among them, were aware of the network while it operated. No prosecution of its top organizers, Sonneborn or Feinberg, is documented. At least one participant, Winters, was convicted and sentenced to eighteen months and a $5,000 fine. And the operation was not shut down before the 1948 war ended; it continued through the conflict. [cite:mexico_arms_1948]

A second government agency was watching from a different angle. During the war the Office of Strategic Services, the predecessor of the CIA, ran a Palestinian Desk in its Foreign Nationalities Branch that tracked Zionist activity in the United States. [cite:forward_oss_palestine_desk] A 2016 review of declassified documents by the Forward found that much of its information came from Jewish officials, some highly placed in Jewish organizations, who volunteered it as a wartime patriotic duty and not under coercion. [cite:forward_oss] [cite:forward_oss_palestine_desk] Its target list reportedly included Moshe Shertok, later Moshe Sharett, Israel's second prime minister. [cite:forward_oss_palestine_desk] The desk built a picture of which American Jewish leaders were aligned with which Zionist factions; it took no action against the Rekhesh procurement networks. [cite:forward_oss] The two agencies should not be merged into a single "intelligence community." The OSS's wartime foreign-intelligence interest and the FBI's domestic-security interest had separate rationales and separate reporting chains, and they are documented independently. [cite:forward_oss_palestine_desk]

Why the Justice Department declined is the question on which the sources divide. One account, built on FBI records reported by the Forward and on later analysis, argues that the non-enforcement was deliberate and political: the Truman administration was moving toward support for Jewish statehood, and prosecuting the future state's arms networks, or requiring its American political organizations to register as foreign agents, would have been incompatible with that course. On this reading the decision not to enforce was a foreign-policy decision in the form of prosecutorial discretion, and it set a precedent, never reversed, that later shaped pro-Israel lobbying in the United States; the same account traces it to 1963, when Isaiah Kenen, himself formerly registered as a foreign agent, moved the lobbying function from the American Zionist Council to the newly created AIPAC. [cite:forward_oss] [cite:medium_zionist] [cite:ips_espionage] The same sources, in their own accounting, mark the central link as contested. That the FBI recommended prosecution or registration, that the Justice Department declined, and that the procurement operations broke American law are, in their judgment, documented. Whether the Justice Department acted on explicit direction from the Truman White House, or exercised its own discretion, is not established. [cite:forward_oss] The reading of a fixed political will also has to account for the rest of the record of these years: the same administration's State Department opposed early recognition of a Jewish state, as the opening section of this chapter shows, so "the government" did not speak with one voice on Palestine.

The case for the network, as its founders and sympathetic historians put it, also deserves its strongest form. In July 1945 Ben-Gurion expected a war that he believed the Haganah could not survive without weapons it did not have, and a British arms embargo stood between it and the means to get them. On that view the procurement effort was the preparation of a community's defense, organized by American donors who had just learned the full scale of the Holocaust. [cite:sonneborn_toi] [cite:forward_oss] The case against it, as stated in the sources critical of it, is that it was a foreign-directed intelligence and arms operation on American soil that broke American law and was allowed to continue because of whom it served. [cite:forward_oss] [cite:medium_zionist] Both cases rest on the same documented facts: the meeting, the front company, the deliberate separation from the Agency, the false paperwork, and the decisions not to prosecute.

What the record leaves unsettled is substantial. It does not give the total sums raised or spent, or a ledger of shipments. It does not establish what each donor understood about the law when he gave. It records the name of the front company two different ways. It does not show whether the Justice Department's restraint came from the White House or from its own discretion. And by the sources' own account, the full extent of Zionist intelligence operations on American soil in the 1940s is only partly documented, with part of the record still classified. [cite:forward_oss] What it does establish is the starting point: a working, financed, deliberately compartmented supply network on American soil from 1945, three years before the state it was built to arm.
